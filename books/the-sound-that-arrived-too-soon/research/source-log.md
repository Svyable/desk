# Source Log

## Core peer-reviewed sources

### Colin S. L. Keay, 1980 — anomalous sounds from meteor fireballs

Keay, C. S. L. “Anomalous sounds from the entry of meteor fireballs.” *Science* 210(4465), 11–15 (1980). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.210.4465.11

PubMed record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17751127/

Use: originating modern physical model tying the 1978 New South Wales fireball to ELF/VLF radiation and local sound transduction. The abstract explicitly reports a bright 1978 fireball, a VLF mechanism proposal, and human-subject/transducer threshold work. Do not cite this paper as proof that fireballs generally produce the required VLF fields; it is partly theoretical interpretation of an event and laboratory work.

### Colin S. L. Keay, 1992 — large-fireball electrophonic review

Keay, C. S. L. “Electrophonic sounds from large meteor fireballs.” *Meteoritics* 27(2), 144–148 (1992). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1992.tb00741.x

Use: mature version of Keay's local-transduction framework and phenomenology. Important for the claim that only a minority of observers perceive the sounds and for the distinction between source and local receiver.

### Neven Zgrablić et al., 2002 — first instrumental audio recordings

Zgrablić, N., Vinković, D., Gradečak, S., Kovačić, D., Ćirić, D., Grbac, N., Andreić, Ž., and Garaj, S. “Instrumental recording of electrophonic sounds from Leonid fireballs.” *Journal of Geophysical Research: Space Physics* 107(A7) (2002). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1029/2001JA000310

Full text: https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2001JA000310

Use: strongest instrumental evidence in the book. Two events associated with 1998 Leonids were recorded acoustically and heard by multiple observers under a purpose-built campaign. Event one: approximately magnitude −6.5, ~0.12 s sound, spectral weight mainly below 250 Hz, two witnesses. Event two: approximately magnitude −12, ~0.074 s sound, six witnesses, strong low-frequency content around 37–44 Hz. The same paper reports no simultaneous ELF/VLF signal for those two acoustic events in receivers insensitive below roughly 500 Hz. Preserve that null.

### Douglas O. ReVelle and R. W. Whitaker — ordinary Leonid infrasound

ReVelle, D. O., and Whitaker, R. W. “Infrasonic detection of a Leonid bolide: 1998 November 17.” *Meteoritics & Planetary Science* 34(6), 995–1005. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1999.tb01419.x

Use: control case for normal delayed meteor acoustics/infrasound. Do not conflate this with concurrent sounds.

### Colin Price and Moshe Blum — low-frequency meteor signals

Price, C., and Blum, M. “ELF/VLF radiation produced by the 1999 Leonid meteors.” Research record: https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/elfvlf-radiation-produced-by-the-1999-leonid-meteors/

Use: positive meteor-correlated low-frequency electromagnetic evidence in the literature. Treat the exact source mechanism and event matching as an experimental claim requiring protocol-level reconstruction in the next pass.

### D. M. Suszcynsky et al., 1999 — meteor/sprite/jet coincidence

Suszcynsky, D. M., Strabley, R., Roussel-Dupré, R., Symbalisty, E. M. D., Armstrong, R. A., Lyons, W. A., and Taylor, M. “Video and photometric observations of a sprite in coincidence with a meteor-triggered jet event.” *Journal of Geophysical Research* 104(D24), 31361–31367 (1999). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1029/1999JD900962

Open institutional record: https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/physics_facpub/1240/

Use: evidence that at least one meteor was observed in a tightly coupled transient-luminous-event sequence. The authors discuss meteor triggering. Do not generalize to a demonstrated population-level meteor→sprite mechanism.

### Richard Spalding et al., 2017 — photoacoustic mechanism

Spalding, R., Tencer, J., Sweatt, W., Conley, B., Hogan, R., Boslough, M., Gonzales, G., and Spurný, P. “Photoacoustic Sounds from Meteors.” *Scientific Reports* 7, 41251 (2017). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/srep41251

Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5286498/

Publisher: https://www.nature.com/articles/srep41251

Use: controlled laboratory and modeling evidence that rapidly modulated light can generate faint acoustic signals in materials such as dark cloth, wood, hair-like fibers and related dielectrics. The paper estimates that sufficiently bright, rapidly modulated fireballs can yield audible local sound near ~25 dB SPL under favorable conditions. Establishes physical possibility, not event-specific causation.

### Michael C. Kelley and Colin Price, 2017 — E-region/Hall-current source model

Kelley, M. C., and Price, C. “On the electrophonic generation of audio frequency sound by meteors.” *Geophysical Research Letters* (2017). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/2017GL072911

Abstract: https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/2017GL072911

Use: explicit electrodynamic alternative in which differential plasma motion produces an ambipolar electric field, maps to the E region, drives a Hall current and launches electromagnetic energy. Treat calculated ground flux as a model result, not a direct field measurement during an audible meteor.

## Modern event context

### Chelyabinsk eyewitness work

Peer-reviewed Chelyabinsk physical overview and eyewitness literature should be reconstructed directly in the expansion pass. A useful secondary lead is the electrophonic section of interview/survey work summarized in the public paper “Eye-witness interviews of the Chelyabinsk airburst,” which reports 198 of 1,674 survey respondents describing sound before the shock. Current draft treats this as retrospective testimony only and does not use the number as instrumental evidence.

Do not rely on popular retellings for mechanism. The ordinary delayed shock sequence is independently visible in synchronized public recordings; concurrent hissing/crackling reports remain testimony unless paired with calibrated audio.

## Historical leads

Zgrablić et al. (2002) provides a literature trail to Montanari (1676, later cited by Halley), Halley (1714), Denison Olmsted's 1833 Leonid work, Romig and Lamar, Kaznev and Keay's catalogs. These are leads for page-indexed archival reconstruction. Until that is done, historical descriptions in the manuscript stay generalized and attributed to later reviews rather than quoted as primary text.

## Negative / adversarial evidence to preserve

- The 1998 Mongolia audio events did **not** have coincident ELF/VLF detections in the receiver's measured band.
- The same campaign observed many bright Leonids without electrophonic sound.
- Prior ELF/VLF meteor searches include null or inconclusive campaigns.
- The 2017 photoacoustic paper explicitly argues that the RF mechanism had not been adequately validated experimentally.
- Meteor-triggered TLE observations do not establish a general causal relationship between meteor flux and sprites.
- Historical witness reports cannot by themselves establish simultaneity to subsecond precision.

## Research handling rule

For every event, keep source, transmission channel, local transducer and percept separate. A source can be real while a proposed channel is wrong. A laboratory transducer can work while the natural source never supplies enough energy. A witness can report the correct endpoint while inferring the wrong origin. The manuscript should never allow evidence from one stage to silently validate the others.
