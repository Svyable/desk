# Chronology

**1676** — Geminiano Montanari is credited in later electrophonic literature with recognizing a timing anomaly between a distant fireball and an apparently immediate sound. This needs primary-text reconstruction before quotation.

**1714** — Edmond Halley discusses meteor phenomena in a historical record later cited by electrophonic researchers. Primary text and exact wording remain an expansion task.

**1833** — The great Leonid storm generates extensive U.S. observations. Denison Olmsted's collection includes reports later discussed in the electrophonic literature as possible pop-like simultaneous sounds.

**Late nineteenth to twentieth century** — Fireball catalogs accumulate hissing, crackling, swishing and popping reports. The scientific problem remains dominated by witness testimony and the contradiction with ordinary acoustic travel time.

**1978** — A very bright fireball over New South Wales produces anomalous-sound reports that Colin Keay investigates.

**1980** — Keay publishes “Anomalous sounds from the entry of meteor fireballs” in *Science*, proposing ELF/VLF generation in a fireball plasma/wake and local transduction near observers.

**1991** — Keay and Peter Ostwald publish laboratory work on production of electrophonic sound in materials, strengthening the receiver side of the hypothesis.

**1992** — Keay publishes a mature review of electrophonic sounds from large fireballs and the natural-transducer interpretation.

**1990s** — Several groups search for meteor-correlated ELF/VLF signals with mixed positive and null results. Competing source mechanisms appear for different fireball classes.

**August 1998** — The SPRITES '98 campaign records a meteor, a nearby sprite and a subsequent jet-like luminous feature along the meteor trail. Suszcynsky et al. later publish the event and discuss possible meteor triggering.

**16–17 November 1998** — A dedicated expedition in Mongolia observes unusually bright Leonid fireballs. Two events produce short low-frequency sounds recorded on acoustic channels and heard by multiple observers. No coincident ELF/VLF signal is detected for those events in the receiver band above roughly 500 Hz.

**1999** — Additional Leonid observing campaigns produce low-frequency electromagnetic data. Price and Blum later report ELF/VLF radiation associated with 1999 Leonid meteors.

**2002** — Zgrablić et al. publish the Mongolia results in *Journal of Geophysical Research*, describing them as the first instrumental detections of electrophonic sounds from meteors and explicitly acknowledging that existing theories do not fully explain the events.

**15 February 2013** — The Chelyabinsk superbolide produces extraordinarily well-documented optical and delayed shock phenomena. Retrospective surveys also collect numerous reports of weaker sounds during the visual event, renewing interest in concurrent-sound mechanisms without supplying a clean synchronized instrumental test.

**1 February 2017** — Spalding et al. publish “Photoacoustic Sounds from Meteors” in *Scientific Reports*. Laboratory measurements and thermal/acoustic modeling show that rapidly modulated bright light can make common absorbing materials produce faint local sounds.

**23 March 2017** — Kelley and Price publish an alternative electrodynamic source model in *Geophysical Research Letters*, proposing an ambipolar-field/E-region Hall-current pathway capable, in their calculation, of supplying electromagnetic power for local electrophonic transduction.

**Present research state** — Concurrent meteor sound has stronger physical support than it did before the Mongolia recordings, but no single source/transduction mechanism has been demonstrated as universal. A decisive modern test requires synchronized high-speed photometry, broadband fields, calibrated audio and controlled local transducers across positive and silent bright-fireball events.
