## Reader promise

For centuries, people have occasionally reported hearing bright meteors at essentially the same moment they saw them: hissing, crackling, popping, rustling, a sound like frying or high-voltage wires. Ordinary acoustics says this should not happen. A fireball tens of kilometres overhead can produce a delayed boom or infrasound, but that sound needs minutes to reach the ground. Light arrives almost immediately. The timing problem is so simple that it turned many reports into examples of unreliable perception.

Then the problem stopped being only about witnesses. Colin Keay proposed that bright fireballs could generate low-frequency electromagnetic energy that reached the observer at nearly light speed and was converted into audible vibration by nearby objects. In 1998 a team in Mongolia recorded two low-frequency sounds associated with Leonid fireballs while multiple observers heard them. The same experiment did not record a simultaneous ELF/VLF signal in its measured band. In 2017 Sandia researchers published a different mechanism: rapidly modulated fireball light can heat hair, leaves, dark cloth and other materials quickly enough to create faint local sound. That same year, Michael Kelley and Colin Price proposed another electrical route through meteor-plasma coupling to the ionosphere.

This book follows an anomaly that became more physical as its explanation became less tidy. Its thesis is not that old eyewitness reports were all correct, or that one favored theory has solved the phenomenon. It is that a class of reports rejected under one propagation model can become scientifically productive when the timing is treated as data rather than embarrassment. The useful question is not merely whether a witness heard a meteor. It is what channel could make a local sound with almost no acoustic travel delay.

The distinct contribution is to put the history of testimony, ordinary meteor acoustics, Keay's electromagnetic hypothesis, the 1998 instrumental recordings, the missing coincident VLF signal, meteor-ionosphere coupling, the 2017 photoacoustic experiments and modern fireball observation into one evidentiary sequence. The strongest competing explanation is plural and deliberately unglamorous: some reports are expectation, memory, coincidental environmental noise or delayed sound mis-timed in recollection; among genuine concurrent events, more than one local transduction mechanism may operate.

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified/documented facts:** ordinary meteor acoustic waves arrive with substantial delay; Keay published an ELF/VLF transduction model and laboratory work; two 1998 Leonid events were instrumentally recorded as low-frequency sounds while observers reported hearing them; the same setup did not detect a simultaneous ELF/VLF signal above its lower instrumental band; controlled 2017 experiments demonstrated photoacoustic sound generation from modulated light in common materials; meteors can measurably interact with ionospheric and upper-atmospheric electrical phenomena.
2. **Credible but incomplete reporting/testimony:** historical and modern witness reports of concurrent meteor sounds; Chelyabinsk reports collected after the event; reports identifying nearby wires, hair, vegetation or structures as apparent sound sources; sparse meteor-correlated low-frequency electromagnetic detections.
3. **Disputed allegations or unresolved claims:** that Keay's turbulent-wake mechanism explains most electrophonic sounds; that any particular historical report was genuinely concurrent; that reported Chelyabinsk hissing was electrophonic rather than expectation or environmental sound; that one 1998 meteor-triggered sprite/jet event establishes a general meteor-triggering mechanism.
4. **Speculative synthesis/open conjecture:** that electrophonic meteor sounds are a family of effects rather than one phenomenon; that different fireball classes preferentially drive optical, local electrostatic, ELF/VLF or ionospheric pathways; and that the history offers a general lesson about anomalies whose impossibility depends on assuming the wrong transmission channel.
