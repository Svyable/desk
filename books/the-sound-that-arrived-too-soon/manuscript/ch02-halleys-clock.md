# Halley’s Clock

The timing problem is older than the equipment required to solve it. In the early eighteenth century, Edmond Halley discussed accounts of meteoric phenomena in a world that did not yet understand meteors as extraterrestrial bodies ablating through the atmosphere. Even without a modern meteor model, observers could notice a simpler contradiction: some reported noises appeared too closely synchronized with the visible event.

The literature on electrophonic sounds often reaches back to Geminiano Montanari in the seventeenth century and to Halley's later discussion of meteor reports. Ancient and medieval chronicles contain descriptions that modern readers can make sound electrophonic if they try hard enough. That is dangerous territory. Translation, genre, metaphor and retrospective selection can turn a poetic noise in the heavens into a technical observation it never was. The useful historical point is narrower. Once people began comparing the apparent distance of fireballs with the timing of reported sounds, the speed mismatch became visible as a problem in its own right.

The nineteenth century made the problem worse by giving observers spectacular meteor storms. During the 1833 Leonids, Denison Olmsted gathered reports as part of the effort to understand the shower. Some witnesses described small explosive or popping noises associated with meteors. Again, the documents do not give us modern synchronized timestamps. They give us an evidentiary category: observers sometimes associated faint, immediate noises with luminous meteors, while the physical distance of the events made ordinary acoustic simultaneity implausible.

Scientists had a perfectly respectable reason to distrust this. Astronomy learned to become powerful by disciplining perception. The telescope mattered, but so did the culture of separating what an observer thought he saw from what an instrument could preserve. A rare, subjective auditory event attached to an emotionally overwhelming fireball is almost designed to trigger skepticism. The sound may be faint. Only one person in a group may hear it. The apparent source may be local. The event cannot be summoned on demand.

For a long time, those features made the reports look less like a physical phenomenon and more like a museum of human suggestibility.

There is a trap here for anyone writing history after the later experiments. Once a modern mechanism exists, old reports start to look vindicated. They are not. A physically plausible pathway can raise the prior probability of a historical claim without giving that claim a timestamp, a spectrum or a calibrated microphone. The correct move is to preserve the asymmetry. The old reports motivated questions. They did not answer them.

What they did preserve unusually well was phenomenology. The vocabulary repeats across eras: hiss, swish, crackle, pop, frying, rustling. Some reports describe sound as if it came from the immediate environment rather than from the meteor's position in the sky. Later, that local quality would become important because both electromagnetic and photoacoustic hypotheses depend on transduction near the observer.

This is one reason strange reports sometimes survive by being annoyingly specific. A generic claim such as “the meteor roared” is easy to absorb into expectation. A claim that hair, leaves or a metal object seemed to hiss during the visual passage is harder to classify. It can still be wrong. But it contains a clue about mechanism.

The history also teaches a harder lesson about scientific patience. A contradiction can remain stable for centuries without being important. Longevity is not evidence. Astrology is old. Folklore is old. Error can reproduce indefinitely. The thing that changes an anomaly's status is not the number of years people have repeated it. It is the arrival of a measurement that excludes enough ordinary alternatives to justify more work.

Electrophonic meteors took a long time to reach that threshold. Before they did, the central fact was almost embarrassingly simple. If the meteor was high and the sound was immediate, the sound could not have come through the air from the meteor in the ordinary way.

Halley's clock was not a theory. It was a refusal to let two arrival times occupy the same causal channel.
