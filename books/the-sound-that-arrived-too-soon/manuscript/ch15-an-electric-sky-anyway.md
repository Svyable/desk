# An Electric Sky Anyway

The photoacoustic hypothesis has an elegance that tempts a premature verdict. The meteor sends light. Light reaches the observer immediately. Common materials can convert modulated light into faint sound. Why keep the electromagnetic story at all?

Because nature has not provided that clean a dismissal.

Meteor trails are electrically and electromagnetically active. Radar routinely detects their ionization. Low-frequency observations have reported signals correlated with meteors. Upper-atmospheric experiments have documented unusual interactions between meteors and transient luminous phenomena. Kelley and Price's 2017 model supplied a specific pathway for a meteor-associated electric field to drive a Hall current in the E region and radiate electromagnetic energy to the ground.

The right comparison is not elegance versus complication. It is predictive success across well-characterized events.

Suppose a concurrent sound occurs with a meteor too dim for the measured optical waveform to supply the photoacoustic energy budget, while a broadband ELF/VLF receiver records a synchronized field pulse with the correct polarization and amplitude. That would strongly favor an electromagnetic pathway. Suppose instead a brilliant rapidly flickering bolide generates local acoustic responses from black cloth and hair-like instrumented fibers while field sensors remain quiet across the relevant band. That would favor photoacoustics.

Theories earn territory event by event.

There is also no law requiring exclusivity. A large fireball can emit modulated light and disturb plasma at the same time. Different local objects can respond to different inputs. The historical category may contain optical and electromagnetic subclasses that feel identical to the witness.

Plural mechanisms are less satisfying to the human appetite for a solved mystery, but they are common in sensory phenomena. A person can hear a click because a pressure wave reached the ear, because bone conducted vibration, because an electrical stimulus activated the auditory pathway, or because a nearby object emitted the sound. The percept does not label its own cause.

This is why the electrophonic literature should be reorganized around measured channels rather than colorful witness adjectives. Hiss and crackle may contain clues, but waveform, optical modulation, field strength, meteor altitude and local transducer properties are better discriminators.

The electrical hypothesis also benefits from the broader maturation of ionospheric science. Sprites, elves, jets and other transient luminous events were themselves once reported by pilots and observers long before routine imaging made them respectable. That history can be abused as a generic argument that mocked anomalies are usually real. They are not. The actual lesson is narrower: rare upper-atmospheric events can evade ordinary observing systems, and new instrumentation can reveal categories that testimony alone cannot establish.

Electrophonic meteors occupy a similar but not identical space. The acoustic endpoint has crossed the instrumental threshold. The source pathway has not crossed it consistently enough to support one universal mechanism.

An electric sky remains in the case because there are measurements and quantitative models worth testing, not because electricity is a convenient synonym for mystery.

The burden now belongs to coordinated observation.
