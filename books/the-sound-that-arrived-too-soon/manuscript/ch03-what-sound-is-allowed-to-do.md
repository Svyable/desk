# What Sound Is Allowed to Do

A meteor at eighty kilometres altitude can seem close because the sky has no scale. That visual intuition is one reason the timing problem needs arithmetic.

Near the ground, sound travels roughly a third of a kilometre per second, with the exact speed depending mainly on temperature. The atmosphere is stratified, winds matter, shock paths refract, and a real bolide is not a stationary point source. None of that rescues simultaneity. If a pressure disturbance must travel tens of kilometres from the luminous path to the observer, the delay is on the order of minutes, not fractions of a second.

Fireball acoustics has a rich vocabulary precisely because different pressure phenomena propagate and evolve in measurable ways. A large meteoroid moving hypersonically generates a cylindrical shock along portions of its trajectory. Fragmentation can create stronger, more nearly spherical disturbances. At sufficiently large distances and low frequencies, the signal becomes infrasound. Networks can detect it after the light has vanished, sometimes at ranges where nobody heard anything at all.

The delayed wave is not a nuisance. It is a remote sensor. Researchers can compare arrival times across stations, model atmospheric propagation, estimate source energy and constrain trajectories. A well-behaved delay contains geometry.

Now consider a witness who reports a faint hiss while the fireball is still moving. If the report is genuinely simultaneous, the ordinary acoustic path is gone. A second meteor could have generated an earlier delayed sound at exactly the right moment. A nearby branch could have moved. A car tyre could have crossed gravel. The witness could have misremembered the order. Those are all ordinary explanations for individual cases. What they cannot do is create a physical theory for a reproducible class of synchronized events.

This distinction mattered in the 1998 Leonid observations. The fireballs involved were high-altitude Leonids, extraordinarily fast and fragile. The instrumented low-frequency sounds were recorded within the optical event, whereas ordinary acoustic signals from such heights would arrive much later. The paper reporting the recordings explicitly separated electrophonic sounds from normal fireball acoustics.

The word electrophonic was an attempt to encode that separation. It proposed that electromagnetic energy travelled from the meteor region at nearly the speed of light and then became sound near the observer. The label was useful, but it later became a source of confusion because it smuggled a mechanism into the category. Once photoacoustic coupling became a serious alternative, a genuinely concurrent meteor sound no longer had to be electrophonic in the strict sense.

We therefore need two terms. Concurrent sound describes the timing fact. Electrophonic sound describes a proposed electromagnetic-to-acoustic route. They overlap historically, but they are not identical.

The same caution applies to the word meteor. A witness can only report correlation between a sound and a visible event. Establishing causation requires synchronized instruments, exclusion of environmental sound, spectral characterization and ideally a candidate energy channel. The sky is full of coincidences if you stare at it long enough.

This is where the problem stops being quaint. A rare event with no experimental control can remain permanently ambiguous. To learn anything, researchers have to turn an unrepeatable natural event into something closer to a laboratory trial. That means choosing a meteor shower likely to produce many bright targets, moving to a quiet site, recording the optical signal, recording audio, recording low-frequency electromagnetic fields, and deciding in advance what counts as an event.

Before anyone did that successfully, however, Colin Keay took a different route. He asked whether the part of the story that sounded most absurd could be built on a bench.

Could an electromagnetic field make an ordinary object audibly respond?

If it could, then the speed-of-sound objection would no longer kill the entire category. It would merely kill one transmission path.
