# Hair, Leaves, and the Full-Moon Problem

A mechanism becomes believable when its numbers stop being decorative.

The photoacoustic proposal lives or dies on an energy budget. The relevant quantity is not the meteor's total radiated power but the optical irradiance at the observer, how rapidly it varies, how strongly a local material absorbs it, and how efficiently that periodic heating becomes pressure variation near the ear.

The Sandia-led experiments used controlled modulated light and measured sound from candidate materials. Their modeling suggested that a very bright fireball with sufficiently rapid optical fluctuations could drive audible sound around twenty-five decibels under favorable conditions. That is not loud. A quiet rural night can accommodate it. Traffic, conversation, wind or a vehicle interior can erase it.

This gives the theory a strong environmental prediction. Reports should cluster in quiet settings and around good transducers. That pattern appears qualitatively in some witness literature, but a proper test needs more than anecdotes. Future fireball networks could pair photometry with standardized acoustic stations surrounded by known materials. The experiment should know the thermal properties and geometry of its receivers instead of reconstructing them from memory after the meteor is gone.

Hair is especially memorable because it sounds ridiculous until the heat-transfer calculation is considered. Fibers offer high surface area and can sit centimeters from the ear. A tiny pressure field generated there does not need to fill a landscape. The observer's head becomes part of the detector.

This feature also explains why a group of people under the same meteor might disagree honestly. One person has dark, dry, exposed hair. Another wears a hat. One stands beside dry leaves. Another stands on bare ground. One is near a dark painted surface. One is talking.

Again, explanatory flexibility must be disciplined. If every null can be blamed on the observer's wardrobe, the theory will never lose. A serious test should measure the incident optical waveform and predict, before listening to the microphone, which instrumented materials ought to produce what acoustic spectrum.

The brightness threshold creates another constraint. Reports associated with relatively modest fireballs are more difficult for a pure photoacoustic account unless the local conversion is much more efficient than the baseline model or the meteor's instantaneous flares are much brighter than its average magnitude suggests. Conversely, extremely bright bolides with strong rapid modulation should be excellent candidates.

The full-moon comparison is useful because it gives human scale to irradiance. Moonlight does not ordinarily hiss in your hair because it is not modulated at audio frequencies with sufficient amplitude. The meteor is not simply a brighter lamp. It is a rapidly varying optical source. Frequency content is the mechanism.

This helps untangle a persistent intuition error. People tend to ask whether a meteor is bright enough to make sound as if brightness were a single number. The correct question is whether enough optical power sits in the temporal frequencies that a local object can convert into pressure oscillation.

That is the same conceptual move the electromagnetic theory requires. Total meteor energy is irrelevant until it is placed into the correct spectral channel.

Both major physical hypotheses therefore converge on a deeper rule: the anomaly is a problem of coupling. Energy is abundant. The mystery is how a tiny fraction of it crosses from the meteor into the acoustic band at the observer.
