# Impossible Witnesses

The most durable fact in this story is not that witnesses were right.

Some were certainly wrong.

People mis-time experiences. They attach unrelated sounds to dramatic sights. Historical reports are selected after the fact. Survey respondents influence one another. A theory of electrophonic meteors that needs every witness to be reliable would deserve to fail immediately.

The durable fact is that the timing complaint survived the unreliable witnesses.

Ordinary acoustic propagation could not explain a genuinely simultaneous sound from a distant fireball. That much was always available to anyone willing to do the travel-time calculation. For a long time the contradiction mostly counted against the report. Keay changed the direction of inference by asking whether sound could be produced locally from a fast electromagnetic signal. Laboratory work showed that this kind of transduction was physically possible. The 1998 Leonid campaign then recorded two low-frequency acoustic events in close association with fireballs. The expected electromagnetic driver was not detected in the measured band. The result strengthened the phenomenon and weakened the simplest version of its explanation at the same time.

That is good science.

The 2017 photoacoustic work reopened the mechanism from another direction. Light, already guaranteed to arrive quickly, can drive sound locally when its intensity is modulated rapidly and the receiving material has favorable thermal properties. Kelley and Price, in the same year, argued that meteor plasma could drive ionospheric currents capable of supplying an electromagnetic field. Neither paper converted all historical reports into solved events. Together they made a plural-mechanism future harder to avoid.

The phrase impossible witness is therefore slightly unfair. The witness did not violate physics. The interpretation did. A person reported a hiss at the same time as a streak of light; everyone else supplied the assumption that the hiss had travelled through the air from the streak.

This distinction scales beyond meteors, but it should not be turned into a cheap sermon about believing extraordinary claims. Most extraordinary claims do not become stronger when the transmission channel is changed. Some become weaker. The lesson is procedural: identify the exact point at which a report conflicts with the model. Then ask whether the conflict belongs to the observation, the assumed causal path, or both.

Electrophonic meteors are unusually clean because the contradiction is temporal. Time-of-flight calculations are unforgiving. They let us reject one mechanism without pretending we have identified the correct one.

The subject also shows why instrumented anomalies can become more ambiguous before they become less. A microphone can prove that a sound occurred without telling you why. A field receiver can miss the band that mattered. A laboratory can validate a mechanism that never occurred in the wild. More data creates more joints in the causal chain, and each joint has to be tested separately.

There is no shame in ending with competing mechanisms if the alternatives are explicit enough to lose future experiments. Photoacoustic coupling makes predictions. Electromagnetic transduction makes predictions. Ionospheric trigger models make predictions. Environmental coincidence makes predictions about false-positive rates. Witness error makes predictions about dependence on retrospective prompting and expectation.

The mystery is no longer a permission slip for imagination. It has become an experimental program.

That may be the best outcome an anomaly can earn.

A streak appears high above the observer. The light arrives. Something nearby makes a faint sound. Minutes later, if the fireball was large enough and the geometry kind, the real boom comes through the atmosphere and shakes the windows.

Two clocks. Two channels. One event.

The mistake was thinking the first sound had to travel the same way as the second.
