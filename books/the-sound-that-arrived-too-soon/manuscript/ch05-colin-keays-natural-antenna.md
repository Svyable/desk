# Colin Keay’s Natural Antenna

Colin Keay did not begin with a general theory of impossible sounds. He had a specific fireball to explain.

In 1978 a very bright meteor over New South Wales generated reports of anomalous sound. Keay investigated the event and in 1980 published a mechanism in Science. His proposal was that turbulence in the ionized wake of a large fireball could interact with Earth's magnetic field and radiate energy in the extremely low frequency and very low frequency range. That electromagnetic energy could propagate rapidly to the observer and be converted into acoustic vibration by nearby objects.

The phrase natural antenna became useful because it shifted the location of sound production. The fireball did not need to send an audible pressure wave fast enough. It needed to send an electromagnetic disturbance. The fence, eyeglasses, vegetation or other local object would do the speaking.

Keay's source model involved a turbulent plasma wake. The details mattered. Not every meteor produces the same ionization, brightness, speed, fragmentation or wake structure. A mechanism that works for a large, slow, deeply penetrating bolide may not work for a fast Leonid disintegrating high in the atmosphere. Later authors would divide electrophonic reports into types and propose different source processes for pops and longer hissing sounds.

That is already a warning against the word phenomenon in the singular. Nature often hands us a category assembled by the observer rather than by the mechanism. Headache is one symptom with many causes. A transient radio burst can come from multiple sources. A simultaneous meteor sound might likewise be the perceptual endpoint of several physical routes.

Keay also confronted the other objection: if the electromagnetic field is real, why does everybody not hear it? Experiments suggested large differences in the ability of people and materials to produce or perceive electrically excited sound. Geometry mattered. Material properties mattered. The acoustic environment mattered. That fit the patchiness of the witness record.

Again, fit is not proof. A theory that explains why evidence is missing needs positive predictions as well. The electromagnetic model predicted that bright fireballs capable of producing electrophonic sounds should sometimes be accompanied by detectable low-frequency fields. Researchers began looking.

The search record was mixed. Some studies reported meteor-correlated VLF signals. Other observing campaigns found no convincing change in their monitored bands. The problem is difficult because the natural VLF environment is noisy. Lightning is an enormous transmitter. Human infrastructure leaks electromagnetic energy. A meteor signal can be brief, weak and hard to distinguish from unrelated pulses. A receiver can also be tuned to the wrong part of the spectrum.

The mixed record matters more than the clean story. In popular retellings, electrophonic meteors are often presented as an old mystery solved by Keay: fireball makes VLF, nearby object converts it to sound. The scientific literature never became that simple. The 2002 Leonid paper, which provided the first instrumental audio recordings, found no coincident ELF/VLF signal in the receiver's measured band for those two sound events. The 2017 photoacoustic paper explicitly noted that the RF route had not been adequately validated by observation.

Keay nevertheless changed the problem permanently. Before his work, the timing mismatch encouraged dismissal. After his work, simultaneity could be reframed as evidence about the channel. A report that had looked like bad acoustics became a test of plasma, electromagnetism and transduction.

That is a significant achievement even if the original mechanism turns out to explain only some events or none of the best ones. Science does not owe a theorist victory for making a problem tractable. But tractability is not a small thing.

Keay gave the impossible witness a receiver.
