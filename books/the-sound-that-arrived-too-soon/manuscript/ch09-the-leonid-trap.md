# The Leonid Trap

The Leonid meteor shower offered something close to experimental leverage over an event that could not be manufactured. In November 1998, observers expected unusually high activity over East Asia. A team went to Mongolia with equipment designed specifically to catch electrophonic sounds.

The location mattered. Environmental audio contamination is the enemy of a claim built around faint, rare sounds. The observing site was remote from power lines and ordinary nocturnal machinery. The team used more than one acoustic channel and monitored low-frequency electromagnetic radiation. Video provided the optical chronology.

They also decided what would count before the night delivered its surprises. This is more important than it sounds. Rare-event research is vulnerable to post hoc thresholding because every strange squiggle can become interesting after a spectacular meteor crosses the sky. Restrictive a priori criteria reduce the temptation to promote coincidences into events.

The Leonids cooperated in an unexpected way. The night did not simply supply an overwhelming number of ordinary meteors. It supplied many extremely bright fireballs, effectively compressing years of opportunities for rare electrophonic events into a short observing window.

That setup created a trap for the old debate. If the witnesses were merely projecting sound onto the sky, synchronized microphones should not preserve the effect. If the sounds were distant sonic booms, their timing and spectral behavior should follow acoustic propagation. If the electromagnetic hypothesis was right in the measured frequency band, the field receivers might register a corresponding signal.

Two events survived the criteria.

One was associated with a fireball estimated around magnitude minus 6.5. Two observers reported a short pop. The sound appeared on the acoustic channels and lasted roughly a tenth of a second. The paper reported it slightly before the meteor's light maximum, with most of the recorded spectral weight below 250 hertz.

The second event was brighter, estimated near magnitude minus 12. Six people reported a deep pop. Again the sound was recorded. Its dominant energy was at very low audio frequencies, around the tens of hertz, and the signal lasted only a fraction of a second.

The recordings changed the epistemic category. The question was no longer whether every old witness had imagined a noise. At least under the reported experimental conditions, low-frequency acoustic events occurred in close temporal association with observed Leonid fireballs and were captured instrumentally.

This does not mean the experiment answered every alternative. A field recording is not a laboratory isolation. The authors argued that the remote site, dual channels, timing and lack of similar environmental sounds during the campaign made accidental local noise extremely unlikely. The evidence is strong enough that later literature treats the recordings as instrumental detections of concurrent meteor sounds.

Then comes the part that keeps the story alive.

The electromagnetic receivers did not detect a coincident ELF/VLF signal for those two audio events in their measured band above roughly 500 hertz. The sounds themselves had substantial energy below that lower limit. The authors therefore argued that a lower-frequency electromagnetic driver could have escaped the instrumentation.

That is plausible. It is not the same as detection.

The Leonid trap caught the sound and missed the expected ghost. The anomaly had become more real at exactly the moment its leading mechanism became harder to claim cleanly.
