# Hunting the Radio Ghost

The electromagnetic hypothesis made a prediction that ought to have been gratifyingly concrete: if fireballs sometimes drive electrophonic sounds, low-frequency electromagnetic disturbances should sometimes accompany them.

In practice, this is like trying to hear a whisper in a room where thunderstorms are shouting from the next continent.

The ELF/VLF environment contains natural and human signals from many sources. Lightning produces powerful broadband impulses. Power systems contaminate lower frequencies. Navigation and communication transmitters occupy portions of the spectrum. The ionosphere changes propagation conditions. An observing team searching for millisecond or subsecond meteor-correlated pulses must synchronize optical records with electromagnetic channels and then ask whether an apparent coincidence exceeds the background coincidence rate.

Several groups reported signals they associated with meteors. A Japanese observation discussed in the literature linked a short low-frequency pulse to a Perseid fireball. Later work reported a VLF signal associated with another bright Perseid. Price and Blum analyzed low-frequency observations during the 1999 Leonids and reported meteor-associated electromagnetic pulses, including detections corresponding to meteors too faint to see visually.

Other campaigns failed to find convincing meteor-correlated changes in their monitored bands. That mixed result should be expected to matter more than it often does in popular summaries. A theory that predicts a field needs a denominator: how many suitably bright meteors were observed, at what geometry, with what receiver sensitivity, over what frequency range, and how many produced candidate signals?

Without the denominator, a positive event can become a story about possibility rather than prevalence.

The source theory also evolved in response to meteor diversity. Keay's turbulent-wake model was most applicable to very bright, relatively slow, deeply penetrating bolides. Short pops associated with faster or higher events encouraged alternative mechanisms involving fragmentation or charge separation. Later, Kelley and Price proposed a pathway in which meteor plasma and geomagnetic constraints create an ambipolar electric field that maps into the E region and drives a Hall current.

These models should not be blended into one vague statement that meteors make VLF. They posit different source physics. Their predictions about altitude, frequency content, meteor speed and timing can differ. A serious evidence matrix therefore treats each as a separate claim.

The search for the radio ghost also reveals a methodological asymmetry. An audio event with no detected field can weaken one version of the theory but may not falsify every electromagnetic pathway if the receiver lacked sensitivity below the sound's dominant frequencies. A field event with no audible sound does not necessarily hurt the theory because local transduction may be absent. This asymmetry makes decisive experiments difficult.

The 1998 Leonid campaign tried to address exactly that problem by measuring multiple channels at once. The site was deliberately remote. The team set restrictive criteria in advance. Optical observations, microphones and electromagnetic receivers operated together.

That is the kind of experiment an anomaly needs because it forces several stories into the same timestamp.

When the crucial events arrived, the result was simultaneously stronger and more troublesome than the theory had asked for.
