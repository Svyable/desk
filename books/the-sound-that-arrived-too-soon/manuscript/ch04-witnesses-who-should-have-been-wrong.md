# Witnesses Who Should Have Been Wrong

The easiest anomalous witness to dismiss is the one who reports an effect that violates a known law. The harder case is a witness who reports something impossible only under a hidden assumption.

Electrophonic meteor reports lived in that second category without anyone knowing it. The hidden assumption was that if a person hears a meteor, the acoustic wave must have travelled from the meteor to the ear. Once stated explicitly, that assumption looks less inevitable. Radio already demonstrated a different architecture. Energy can cross a large distance electromagnetically and become mechanical motion at the receiver.

But a radio analogy can mislead too. Human beings do not carry tuned VLF loudspeakers in their skulls. A mechanism needs a source powerful enough to radiate at the relevant frequencies, propagation through the Earth-ionosphere environment, and a local object capable of converting the field into audible vibration at an intensity above the observer's threshold. Each stage can fail.

The witness record nevertheless contained details that made local transduction worth testing. Some people described sounds near vegetation, metal structures, eyeglasses or hair. Others in the same group heard nothing. That variability had usually counted against the phenomenon. If a meteor truly made a sound, why would only one person hear it?

Local transduction flips the inference. Different observers occupy different acoustic microenvironments. One stands beside dry grass; another beside a car body; one wears wire-frame glasses; another has hair that might respond differently; one has a lower hearing threshold in the relevant band; another is masked by wind or conversation. A field spread over a region could therefore produce patchy reports without requiring a narrowly directed sound beam from the sky.

This is a beautiful explanation. It is also exactly the kind of explanation that can become too beautiful. Once a theory can absorb every inconsistency as local variability, it risks becoming unfalsifiable. No sound? Wrong transducer. One witness? Individual threshold. No radio measurement? Receiver band mismatch. Historical report? Natural antenna. Modern null? Geometry.

Keay's work is strongest where it refused to remain at the level of narrative. He and collaborators subjected candidate materials and human listeners to low-frequency electromagnetic fields and investigated whether audible responses could be produced. That does not prove a meteor can supply the field. It does establish that the receiver side of the story is not nonsense.

The distinction between source and receiver is one of the most useful habits in anomaly research. A laboratory can validate a transduction mechanism while the natural source remains unknown. A natural source can be measured while the human percept is mistaken. Evidence has to attach to the stage it actually tests.

Historical witness catalogs blur those stages because they only preserve the endpoint. They tell us somebody heard something. They rarely tell us the spectrum of the field, the thermal properties of nearby materials, the meteor's absolute magnitude or the observer's acoustic threshold. Treating them as if they do would be evidence laundering.

Still, witness language can shape experiments. A theory born from testimony is not automatically contaminated science. The important question is whether it generates measurements that could come out wrong.

By the late twentieth century, electrophonic meteors had reached that point. The witnesses had supplied a phenomenological puzzle. Keay supplied a candidate physical architecture. The next step was to ask whether fireballs actually emitted enough low-frequency electromagnetic energy to drive it.

That question proved much harder than making a bit of foil or hair respond in a laboratory.
