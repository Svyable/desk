# The Sound Before the Boom

On 15 February 2013, the sky over Chelyabinsk became brighter than most people who saw it had any reason to expect the morning sky to become. Cameras caught the fireball because Russia had already solved a different problem with cheap recording technology: too many drivers had dashcams. The result was one of the most thoroughly witnessed asteroid airbursts in history. The videos show the light first. The violent shock arrives later. Windows flex and break after the luminous event has already passed.

That delay is useful because it gives the problem in this book a control condition. Nobody needs an exotic theory for the great delayed booms of Chelyabinsk. A hypersonic body drove shock waves through the atmosphere. The shock propagated at acoustic speed. Buildings and human bodies encountered it later. The sequence is visible in ordinary video: flash, pause, impact.

Yet survey work after Chelyabinsk also collected reports of a weaker class of sound that people said occurred before the main shock arrived, during the bright passage itself. Hissing. Crackling. Whistling. A sound like a Bengal sparkler. Some reports came from people outdoors in quieter settings. A later summary of interviews and internet responses counted nearly two hundred people who described sound well before the shock wave. Those reports do not become measurements merely because there are many of them. The collection happened after a globally famous event. Memory contamination and expectation are obvious hazards. But Chelyabinsk accidentally reenacted an older scientific argument in a setting with unusually good timing evidence for the ordinary acoustic wave.

There were, in other words, potentially two clocks in the same event. The loud clock behaved. The faint one did not.

That distinction is easy to lose because human language is bad at preserving propagation mechanisms. A witness says the meteor made a sound. The sentence fuses source, channel and perception into one package. Physics needs them separated. Did the meteor create pressure waves at altitude that travelled all the way to the ear? Did it create electromagnetic radiation that reached the ground quickly and caused a nearby object to vibrate? Did changing light heat a local material fast enough to create pressure oscillations? Did an ionospheric electrical disturbance alter the immediate environment? Did the witness simply hear something unrelated and bind it to the most astonishing visual event in the sky?

All of those end in the same subjective sentence: I heard it.

The ordinary meteor-acoustics literature is not uncertain about the basic travel-time problem. Fireballs can generate sonic booms and infrasound, and researchers use those signals to infer energy and trajectory. The 1998 Leonid campaign, for example, had a bolide whose infrasonic signal could be studied as a normal delayed atmospheric wave. That branch of meteor science is mature enough to be useful precisely because sound is slow. Travel time is information.

Concurrent sounds invert that advantage. If a person fifty or a hundred kilometres from the luminous path reports a noise within a fraction of a second of the light, ordinary sound propagation is disqualified by geometry before anyone argues about the precise acoustic spectrum. The timing does not prove an alternative mechanism. It simply tells us that one familiar mechanism cannot do the job.

This is the first discipline the subject requires: refusing to let the word sound decide how the energy travelled.

The second discipline is less comfortable. A large fraction of anomalous reports in the world deserve to die at the timing test. Memories drift. Coincidences happen. Expectations are contagious. A category assembled from witness testimony can manufacture apparent regularity. The existence of a physically possible local-transduction mechanism does not retroactively authenticate ancient chronicles or every modern anecdote.

But impossibility cuts both ways. If a proposed explanation violates travel time, it should be discarded. If a dismissal assumes without evidence that only one transmission channel is available, it deserves the same treatment.

Chelyabinsk is therefore a good place to begin because the violent, familiar sound arrived exactly when ordinary acoustics said it should. The reports that interest us belong to the interval before that arrival. They are quieter, messier and easier to dismiss. They also ask the sharper question.

What, exactly, reached the witness first?
