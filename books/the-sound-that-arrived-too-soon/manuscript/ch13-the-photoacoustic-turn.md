# The Photoacoustic Turn

In 2017 a team including researchers from Sandia National Laboratories and the Czech Academy of Sciences published an alternative that was almost offensively simple.

Bright meteors flicker.

Not merely in the slow, visually obvious sense. High-speed photometry shows rapid brightness variations and millisecond flares. If the optical intensity reaching the ground is modulated at audio frequencies, a dark absorbing material can heat and cool in step with that modulation. The surface warms the adjacent air. The air expands and contracts. Sound is produced locally.

The phenomenon is photoacoustic conversion, and it predates the meteor problem by more than a century. Alexander Graham Bell famously experimented with modulated light producing sound in materials while developing the photophone. The 2017 meteor paper took that old physical effect and asked whether natural fireball illumination could drive it strongly enough to match witness reports.

The researchers tested materials including cloth, wood and a synthetic wig under modulated light. They modeled heat absorption and the resulting acoustic pressure. Materials with high optical absorption and low thermal inertia performed better. Fibrous structures such as hair offered a large surface area close to the ear. Dark leaves, clothing and painted surfaces became plausible local transducers.

The calculations suggested that very bright meteors, around the brightness of the full moon or greater depending on modulation and assumptions, could produce faint but audible sounds on the order of tens of decibels in favorable quiet conditions. The predicted phenomenology resembled reports of rustling, hissing and whisper-like sounds.

This theory has several virtues. The transmission channel is unquestionably present: the meteor is visible because optical energy reaches the observer. Light already travels with the required timing. No unmeasured radio burst is required. The transducer variability naturally explains why only some observers hear the effect.

It also has demanding conditions. The light must be bright enough, strongly modulated at suitable frequencies, and incident on material capable of responding. Not every fireball qualifies. Not every nearby object is a good transducer. A city street or moving vehicle can easily mask a twenty-five-decibel local sound.

Most importantly, the laboratory demonstration does not prove that photoacoustics caused the Mongolia recordings or any particular historical report. The meteor's actual high-frequency brightness modulation and the local material environment would have to be reconstructed event by event.

The photoacoustic theory also does not automatically explain short, relatively loud pops associated with meteors that are not exceptionally bright. Those events may require another mechanism or a more efficient local transducer. The literature's historical division between longer hissing sounds and brief popping sounds may turn out to be mechanistically meaningful.

This is where the subject becomes more interesting than a solved mystery. The 2017 paper did not replace magic with physics. Physics had already arrived. It replaced one physical channel with a serious competitor.

That competition is healthy because the theories fail differently. An electromagnetic model can survive weak optical modulation but owes a field measurement. A photoacoustic model can survive an RF null but owes enough modulated irradiance at the observer. A triggered-ionosphere model can supply additional electrical power but owes evidence of the trigger and reservoir.

The sky has given us several ways to make a faint local sound quickly. The task now is to stop asking whether concurrent meteor sounds are possible and start asking which mechanism operated in each class of event.
