# The Missing Signal

A scientific result becomes more trustworthy when it preserves the part that did not cooperate.

For the Mongolia events, the uncooperative fact is straightforward: the experiment did not detect a simultaneous ELF/VLF signal in the monitored band during the two recorded sounds. The electromagnetic receivers were insensitive below about 500 hertz, while the acoustic events contained much of their energy below 250 hertz and, in one case, strongest energy in the tens of hertz. The authors proposed that the driving field might therefore have lived below the receiver cutoff.

That explanation is physically possible. It is not a measurement. The distinction should remain visible every time the experiment is cited.

This is where anomaly research often encounters a temptation to upgrade absence into indirect confirmation. If the detector did not cover the frequency that later seems important, the null is not decisive. But neither is it supportive. It is an unresolved instrumental gap.

The same campaign reportedly detected low-frequency electromagnetic signals associated with other fireballs, and other researchers had reported meteor-correlated VLF events. Those observations keep electromagnetic generation in play. They do not establish that the two recorded acoustic events were caused by the same pathway.

One can imagine several architectures. A meteor produces ELF/VLF energy below the measured band, which local objects transduce. A meteor triggers an ionospheric or atmospheric electrical response that produces the field. A rapidly fluctuating optical signal directly heats nearby material and makes sound. A combination operates, with different mechanisms dominating different events. Or one or both recorded sounds have an unrecognized local cause despite the experimental precautions.

The correct scientific response is not to choose the most narratively satisfying architecture. It is to design measurements that force them apart.

Photoacoustic coupling, for example, predicts dependence on optical modulation and local material absorption. An RF mechanism predicts a measurable field if the receiver spans the relevant spectrum and dynamic range. An electrostatic local mechanism might correlate with field changes without requiring a propagating radio wave. A triggered atmospheric mechanism could depend strongly on ionospheric state and meteor altitude.

This is why full-spectrum instrumentation matters. An experiment can fail by measuring the wrong thing with excellent precision. The Mongolia team measured enough to establish the acoustic event but not enough to close the causal chain.

That incompleteness is intellectually useful because it prevents the story from becoming a morality tale about brave witnesses finally defeating skeptical scientists. Skepticism was necessary. The witness interpretation was too underdetermined. Instrumentation improved the case. Then instrumentation created a new problem.

The missing signal also teaches something about labels. Once a concurrent sound is recorded, calling it electrophonic subtly implies the missing electromagnetic step. Better to separate observation from explanation. The observation is a concurrent low-frequency acoustic event associated with a meteor. The electromagnetic route is one hypothesis.

Words are cheap sensors. They can register a mechanism that no instrument has seen.

The next chapters follow two ways scientists tried to fill that causal gap. One went upward, into the ionosphere and its electrical currents. The other came downward, toward the observer's hair and clothing, and asked whether the meteor's light itself was enough.
