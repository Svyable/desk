# Chapter 2 — A Laser Man in Menlo Park

Menlo Park in the early 1970s is now easy to misremember because Silicon Valley has since colonized the geography of technological imagination. We picture garages, chips, venture capital, and the personal-computer future beginning to hum. That was part of the landscape. It was not the whole thing.

Stanford Research Institute was a contractor’s institution. It lived in the practical middle distance between universities, companies, and government agencies. If a sponsor had a problem and money, SRI could assemble people, apparatus, and a proposal. The problems did not need to be glamorous. Many were technical, incremental, classified, or boring. Some were the opposite.

Hal Puthoff arrived with a laser physicist’s résumé.

The detail matters because the first psychic-research work did not begin inside an organization whose main purpose was proving psychic powers. It began inside an institution with enough breadth to contain electronics, bioengineering, defense analysis, computing, communications, and human-performance research; enough government relationships to attract intelligence attention; and enough managerial flexibility for a physicist to explore an embarrassing possibility without immediately becoming the entire identity of the place.

That institutional ambiguity made everything possible.

It also made the later argument harder. When a respected contract laboratory studies an extraordinary claim, outsiders tend to infer that the claim passed some invisible threshold before the work began. Often the opposite is true. Contract research exists precisely because a sponsor wants a question answered. The decision to investigate can mean we believe this, but it can also mean we cannot afford to ignore this, our adversary may be doing this, somebody senior saw something odd, or simply a small experiment is cheap.

Cold War intelligence was built to take low-probability threats seriously when the downside of being surprised was high.

The Soviet Union was known to sponsor research Americans often categorized under parapsychology, psychotronics, or related terms. U.S. intelligence collected reports about that work. Some reports were exaggerated; some were difficult to evaluate; some reflected a genuine difference in research culture. The strategic question was not whether an American academic department would grant the phenomenon tenure. The question was whether an adversary might discover or exploit an information channel the United States had dismissed.

This is one of the recurring structures of secret research. A program can be rational to start even if its hypothesis is unlikely. The expected value of a small investigation may be positive when the claimed capability, if real, would matter enormously. That arithmetic says nothing about whether the capability actually exists.

Puthoff’s later defenders sometimes cite government sponsorship as if the checks themselves certify the phenomenon. Critics sometimes make the opposite mistake and treat any sponsorship as bureaucratic foolishness. The archive supports a more ordinary explanation. Agencies explore strange things because intelligence failure is expensive.

The earliest funding sequence makes that point better than any general argument.

In Puthoff’s own 1996 retrospective account, the beginning was almost embarrassingly casual. Early in 1972 he was still working on lasers at SRI and circulating a proposal on quantum biology. Ingo Swann, a New York artist already involved in psychical research, reportedly encountered the proposal while visiting Cleve Backster’s laboratory and wrote to Puthoff. Puthoff invited him to SRI for a week in June.

That story comes from Puthoff decades later. It is participant testimony, not a stenographic record of 1972. But its broad sequence aligns with a declassified CIA internal history that reconstructs what happened next.

At SRI, the strange thing first arrived through people.

Accounts differ in color and sequence, and the memoir literature has accumulated decades of embellishment. The core is less disputed. In 1972 Puthoff began working with Swann. Russell Targ, another physicist with a background in lasers, soon became central to the program. The two physicists would become the names most associated with the SRI remote-viewing work.

One of the first famous episodes did not involve a remote geographical site. It involved a magnetometer.

A later SRI report and the CIA’s own internal reconstruction describe an experiment in which Swann was asked to affect a superconducting magnetometer. The apparatus was described as heavily shielded. Puthoff and colleagues said the recorded output changed while Swann directed attention toward it. This became part of the early case that something anomalous might be occurring.

The honest way to read such a report is with two minds at once.

The first asks what the investigators actually observed. A shielded instrument produced a deviation during a session with a subject claiming psychokinetic ability. The report exists. The names, apparatus, and claimed observation are not inventions of later UFO culture.

The second asks what would be required to establish causation. Was the apparatus fully characterized? Were environmental disturbances excluded? Was the event replicated under conditions designed after the anomaly appeared? Were controls and analysis procedures specified in advance? Could an independent laboratory reproduce the effect? A strange trace is not yet a new force of nature.

This is where Puthoff’s earlier engineering background becomes fascinating. An engineer normally distrusts single events. Devices have transients. Instruments drift. Shielding is imperfect. Humans bump things. Cables couple. Electronics saturate. The first anomaly is the beginning of troubleshooting, not the end of physics.

What happened next was not a Nobel Prize announcement.

It was a letter.

The CIA internal history says Puthoff wrote Kenneth Green in the Agency’s Office of Scientific Intelligence on June 27, 1972, describing the apparent magnetometer anomaly. Green was an acquaintance from Puthoff’s earlier national-security world. The connection is worth noticing because it shows how research crosses institutional boundaries in real life. No anonymous gatekeeper searched the world for the best psychic laboratory. A technically credible person told another technically credible person about something odd.

The next step cost less than a used car.

According to the CIA history, the Office of Technical Service arranged an August demonstration at SRI under a work order for $874.

Eight hundred seventy-four dollars.

That is one of the best numbers in the entire story because it restores scale.

The first CIA commitment was not a vast classified program secretly convinced that human beings could see across continents. It was the bureaucratic equivalent of buying a look. If the claim was nonsense, the loss was trivial. If it survived even crude testing, the agency could spend more.

The demonstration impressed the responsible officers enough that the OTS director authorized another $2,500 for further investigation.

Then, in October, the money changed category. The CIA internal history says OTS funded an expanded effort of roughly $50,000 under Contract 8473, dated October 1, 1972.

The numbers make a staircase.

$874.

$2,500.

$50,000.

The remote-viewing program became serious one administrative step at a time.

Puthoff later remembered the work as having “started as a lark” and recalled the first substantial CIA funding as about $50,000. That is broadly compatible with the archival chronology, but memory compresses the small steps. Nobody tells the legend with the $874 check.

They should.

The small first check tells us what the government knew at the time. Not much.

That is a crucial correction to hindsight. Once a program survives for decades, its beginning looks preordained. Later classifications, code names, congressional interest, and declassified binders throw a shadow backward. In 1972, the future did not exist yet. A CIA technical officer was not deciding whether to launch Stargate. He was deciding whether an odd claim deserved another modest experiment.

The program’s later longevity cannot be used as evidence that the first officers already possessed the conclusion.

This is a general rule of institutional history: do not let the size of the archive at the end inflate the certainty at the beginning.

The label “remote viewing” was itself an act of engineering.

“Clairvoyance” arrived carrying centuries of spiritualism, séances, fraudulent mediums, psychical research, folklore, and metaphysics. “Remote viewing” sounded like a task. A person here attempts to describe a target there. The term did not solve the epistemology, but it narrowed the experiment. It replaced a grand claim about psychic powers with an operational question about information transfer.

That move was smart.

It was also rhetorically powerful enough to become dangerous. Technical language can make a phenomenon easier to test, but it can also make a phenomenon feel more established than it is. “Remote viewing” sounds like a capability. In the earliest work, capability was exactly what had to be demonstrated.

The same is true of the phrase “psychic spying.” It is excellent television and poor chronology.

Before there was spying, there were demonstrations.

Before there were trained military viewers, there were civilians whose reported abilities looked interesting enough to test.

Before operational tasking, there was a question about whether any information at all could cross the experimental boundary without known sensory means.

The order matters because each stage asks a harder question.

Can an individual produce an anomalous response under laboratory conditions?

Can the effect replicate?

Can it be trained?

Can it provide accurate information about a target selected by someone else?

Can an intelligence consumer tell when the information is correct before feedback?

Can it improve a decision?

Can another laboratory do the same thing without the original team?

An experiment can pass one stage and fail the next. The history of remote viewing is full of arguments caused by pretending all stages were one.

Psychical research introduces another nasty complication: advocates often claim the phenomenon is variable, person-dependent, context-sensitive, or inhibited by excessive control. That may be true. It may also make the hypothesis harder to kill.

A good scientific claim needs to lose cleanly.

If an effect vanishes under better controls, one possibility is that the controls suppress a real effect. Another is that the earlier effect depended on an uncontrolled pathway. The experiment alone must help distinguish those explanations. When the object of study is alleged human cognition with no agreed mechanism, the number of ways to rescue failure becomes almost unlimited.

Puthoff and Targ understood at least part of this problem. Their later papers emphasized blinding, randomized targets, independent judging, and statistical evaluation. They were not simply asking a psychic to perform and applauding a resemblance afterward. The SRI program tried to turn an elusive claim into a protocol.

That protocol would become the real invention.

The remote-viewing procedure, in its simplest form, separated the subject from a target and asked for descriptions or drawings. In outbound experiments, a person might travel to a randomly selected location while the subject remained elsewhere. The subject’s transcript could later be compared with the site. In other experiments, coordinates or other abstract cues were used. Over time the program developed judging methods intended to quantify matches rather than rely entirely on astonishment.

The moment you quantify a psychic claim, the experiment changes character.

A drawing that vaguely resembles a bridge can be persuasive when shown beside a photograph of a bridge. It becomes less impressive when the judge must compare the drawing against several possible locations, each containing lines, water, buildings, roads, open space, or vertical structures. The target pool matters. The transcript matters. The order matters. Whether the judge can obtain cues from the materials matters. The scoring method matters. The existence of multiple unreported trials matters.

Remote viewing was therefore not one question. It was a stack of questions disguised as one.

Can a subject produce information that blind judges match to a remote target above chance? If yes, does the effect replicate? If yes, is the effect caused by anomalous cognition rather than cueing, statistical flexibility, sensory leakage, or selective reporting? If anomalous cognition remains, is it stable enough for intelligence? If it is useful, does it reveal a mechanism? If a mechanism is proposed, does it survive physics?

Puthoff’s public reputation often leaps from the first rung to the last.

The SRI program itself spent years climbing and slipping between them.

One reason the story survived is that some sessions were difficult to dismiss emotionally. Human beings are pattern-recognition machines, and some descriptions looked uncannily specific. Intelligence officers who watched a subject sketch features later associated with a target did not experience the event as a p-value. They experienced it as a security problem.

That difference between statistical evidence and witnessed astonishment runs through classified anomaly programs.

A sponsor who sees one remarkable demonstration may authorize a year of research. A scientist looking at the full dataset may see regression toward chance. A critic may discover a cueing route in the judging materials. A believer may point to a later operational success that does not fit the critic’s mechanism. Everybody can be arguing from real pieces of the record and still be talking past one another.

The SRI setting amplified the effect because the investigators were physicists rather than professional parapsychologists. That fact cut both ways. It gave the program legitimacy in technical and intelligence circles. It also meant the researchers were entering a field with a long history of methodological traps that experimental psychologists and magicians knew intimately: sensory leakage, subtle cueing, optional stopping, target-set artifacts, post hoc matching, experimenter expectancy, and the human tendency to remember hits.

Physics credentials do not immunize a laboratory against psychology.

At the same time, critics sometimes treated the move into parapsychology as if it erased Puthoff and Targ’s technical competence everywhere else. That is equally lazy. A person can be a capable laser physicist and overinterpret a psychic experiment. He can also be right about an anomaly for reasons his critics initially miss. Biography becomes useful only when competence is kept local to the claim.

By 1972, Puthoff was becoming something rarer than a physicist willing to investigate psychic phenomena. He was becoming a translator between two bureaucratic languages.

To a subject like Swann, the experience could be discussed in terms of impressions, images, intention, or consciousness. To a sponsor, Puthoff could talk about information channels, signal-to-noise, protocols, shielding, coordinates, and operational utility. To a journal, he could present methods and statistics. To SRI management, he could present a funded research program.

The contract history proves the translation had practical value.

A claim that would have sounded absurd in an intelligence requirements meeting became a scoped work order.

Then another.

Then a contract.

Translation is not proof.

It is, however, a form of institutional power.

The scientist who can make an embarrassing question legible to a serious organization changes what the organization is allowed to investigate.

That may be Puthoff’s most durable talent.
