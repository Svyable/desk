# Chapter 13 — Build the Impossible

A laboratory on the edge of accepted science has one advantage over a university department: nobody mistakes its mission.

EarthTech was built to look at things other institutions did not want to spend faculty time explaining away.

That does not mean everything brought through the door was equally plausible. Quite the opposite. An organization that invites unconventional energy, propulsion, gravity, and anomalous-physics claims inherits the full distribution: serious theoretical questions, ingenious mistakes, sincere inventors who have fooled themselves, entrepreneurs who have mistaken prototypes for products, and people selling miracles.

The work is less glamorous than the mythology.

Before a new law of physics is needed, somebody has to check the power meter.

For once, the public record lets us see that work directly.

In 2006 Scott R. Little of EarthTech presented an AIAA paper with one of the most useful titles in the entire Puthoff archive: “Null Tests of Breakthrough Energy Claims.”

Null tests.

Not breakthroughs.

Not suppressed technologies.

Failures.

The abstract says EarthTech had evaluated a number of claimed new-energy devices with a “singular lack of success.” Little presented the case histories not to mock inventors but to teach other investigators how easily energy experiments fool people.

This paper is more important to Puthoff’s biography than ten speculative interviews because it reveals what the Austin laboratory actually did when a miracle entered the room.

Measure input.

Measure output.

Find the hidden term.

Try again.

The opening sounds almost painfully conventional. If a device is claimed to extract energy from an unusual source, compare all output energy with all input energy. Electrical measurements become difficult when current and voltage vary rapidly or are out of phase. Mechanical input requires torque and angular velocity. Thermal output requires calorimetry. Small excesses are especially treacherous because measurement error can be larger than the alleged effect.

Little notes that reaching one-percent calorimetric accuracy is already serious work. Pushing toward one-tenth of a percent requires far more effort.

That sentence should be taped above every over-unity video on the internet.

A machine that claims three hundred percent excess power does not need a national metrology institute to reveal itself. A machine claiming a two-percent anomaly may require months of calibration before anybody knows whether there is an anomaly at all.

EarthTech’s case history with Ken Shoulders’ so-called charge clusters is a useful example because it lived close to Puthoff’s own zero-point interests. Shoulders claimed that microscopic electron clusters could produce extraordinary energy effects and held patents describing them. EarthTech pursued the claim for years.

That is the important part.

Puthoff’s laboratory did not reject the idea because the words sounded impossible.

It tried to reproduce it.

According to Little, EarthTech experimented with multiple setups looking first for direct electrical-energy output and later using calorimetry. The experiments were difficult: low energies, fast discharges, electrically noisy conditions, hard input-power measurements. After enough work to obtain what the lab regarded as reasonable calorimetric accuracy, the results were disappointingly negative.

A mythology-minded writer would treat this as a tragedy of almost-discovered vacuum energy.

The more useful interpretation is methodological.

The laboratory’s curiosity survived long enough to make failure expensive.

That gives the null more evidentiary weight than a five-minute dismissal.

The next cases get better.

EarthTech tested devices in which vigorous cavitation of water was claimed to create more heat than the mechanical input could explain. One was associated with Yuri Potapov, whose apparatus forced water through a swirl chamber and produced vortexing and cavitation. Claims for the device reached multiples of input energy.

EarthTech obtained a genuine device and built calorimetry around it.

No excess heat appeared.

Another cavitation device drove a rotor with a thirty-horsepower electric motor and generated enough steam to make the experiment, in Little’s understated language, exciting and sometimes dangerous. The developer claimed up to fifty percent more heat output than mechanical input.

EarthTech measured the mechanical input directly.

The result was not fifty percent excess.

The paper reports a near-perfect energy balance, typically about 99 percent with roughly one-percent uncertainty.

More revealingly, when the developer visited the lab, EarthTech discovered the likely source of much of his anomalous reading: incorrect use of the electrical power analyzer.

That is edge physics in its least cinematic and most important form.

The universe did not change.

The meter was being used wrong.

A researcher specializing in miracle claims will encounter this repeatedly. Instruments do not merely report reality. They implement models of reality. A power analyzer assumes something about waveforms, sampling, phase, bandwidth, calibration, and load. If the input is a high-frequency low-power-factor signal, an instrument that performs beautifully on household AC can lie by double digits.

Little gives an example from EarthTech’s sonofusion work. The laboratory built apparatus using an ultrasonic transducer in heavy water with a palladium target, following claims that acoustic cavitation could produce anomalous heat. The input was a high-voltage, roughly twenty-kilohertz, low-power-factor electrical drive.

A digital oscilloscope multiplying voltage and current in real time appeared sophisticated.

EarthTech found it could be wrong by ten percent or more.

A medium-quality power analyzer specified around two-percent accuracy was reportedly off by about twenty percent under those conditions.

Only after finding a more suitable instrument did the group trust its input-power measurement at the necessary level.

Forty-eight runs with EarthTech’s own apparatus produced no sign of excess heat.

Then the better experiment happened.

Roger Stringham, the claimant, allowed EarthTech to bring a portable calorimeter and its power analyzer to test the claim on his own apparatus with his cooperation.

That is almost the ideal configuration for an extraordinary-device test. The inventor cannot reasonably object that an outsider built the wrong machine because his own machine is being measured. The skeptical laboratory cannot be accused of merely failing to reproduce secret construction details. Both sides stand around the same hardware.

EarthTech’s result was brutal.

The apparent excess heat on the test day was traced to erroneous input-power measurement that understated the energy delivered to the system.

No new energy source was required.

The paper does not gloat.

It says Stringham continued working with redesigned apparatus and higher claims, and that EarthTech looked forward to examining them again.

That sentence may tell us more about Puthoff’s research culture than almost anything else.

Failure did not automatically turn the claimant into an enemy.

The laboratory left the door open to another test.

This attitude is scientifically generous and institutionally risky. It can preserve genuine anomalies long enough to mature. It can also spend years chasing moving targets whose failure conditions change every time the apparatus fails.

Little names the problem explicitly. A single null test almost never kills a breakthrough-energy claim because the claimant can argue that the wrong materials were used, assembly was imperfect, environmental conditions were wrong, or some subtle parameter was missed. Strictly speaking, the paper notes, repeated null tests can pile up indefinitely while one robust positive result would change everything.

That asymmetry is real.

It is also why preregistered defeat conditions matter.

Before the test, the claimant should specify the apparatus, operating range, expected output, acceptable measurement method, and conditions under which a null will count. Otherwise the miracle can retreat forever into the one parameter the laboratory did not preserve.

The EarthTech paper becomes even more important when it reaches a test derived from Puthoff’s own zero-point ideas.

The logic began with hydrogen.

If the hydrogen ground state is partly maintained by zero-point fluctuations, and if a Casimir cavity suppresses some electromagnetic modes, perhaps hydrogen introduced into an appropriate cavity would lose some ground-state energy. Circulate hydrogen into and out of such a region and maybe an extractable cycle could exist.

This is exactly the kind of bridge from theory to apparatus the previous chapters have been demanding.

EarthTech built experiments to look.

The paper says they were unsuccessful.

One approach produced apparent heat in an apparatus involving hydrogen and finely divided metal. Investigation traced the transient to ordinary chemistry: oxidation catalyzed by the metal after the apparatus had acquired enough oxygen. The heat burst could reappear after the system sat overnight or was opened because fresh oxygen had entered.

The vacuum did not recharge.

The chemistry did.

Then EarthTech tried a cleaner spectroscopic question. If molecules inside a Casimir cavity experienced a depressed ground-state energy, the corresponding dissociation energy might shift. The group took an apparatus to the Synchrotron Radiation Center at the University of Wisconsin–Madison and used extreme-ultraviolet radiation to probe molecular hydrogen under the relevant cavity conditions.

The experiment was technically difficult. Equipment problems forced rebuilding at the facility.

The predicted ground-state shift was not found.

This is an extraordinary paragraph in the biography because the hypothesis came close to Puthoff’s own theoretical interests and the public result was still null.

The correct inference is not that every zero-point proposal died at Wisconsin.

The correct inference is that an experimentally accessible consequence was sought and not observed under those conditions.

That is what failure is supposed to look like.

It also corrects a caricature of Puthoff as somebody for whom every unusual experiment confirms the unusual hypothesis. EarthTech’s published record contains the opposite. Devices failed. Input meters were wrong. Cavitation balanced. Hydrogen did not show the hoped-for shift.

This does not establish that every EarthTech program was equally rigorous. It does not settle questions about unpublished proprietary work. It does not tell us whether Puthoff weighted positive and negative results symmetrically in every later public argument.

It does change the prior.

A laboratory willing to publish its nulls is behaving better than a miracle factory.

That is the engineering side of Puthoff that tends to disappear behind remote viewing and UFOs. His Austin organizations—EarthTech International and the Institute for Advanced Studies at Austin—gave him a setting in which exploratory theory and laboratory evaluation could sit near each other. Corporate biographies filed with the SEC describe him as leading those organizations from 1985 onward. The organizations’ own materials emphasize advanced energy and propulsion questions, along with technical evaluation.

Self-description is not independent validation. The 2006 null-test paper is stronger evidence because it shows the institution failing claims in public.

EarthTech was not merely an edge incubator.

It was also an edge filter.

The phrase matters because there are two bad ways to handle extraordinary technology claims.

One is to accept the inventor’s explanation too quickly.

The other is to refuse to measure because the explanation sounds impossible.

A good test begins by separating observation from theory. Does the device produce the claimed thrust? Does output energy exceed all measured inputs? Does a sample exhibit the claimed electromagnetic response? Does the anomaly persist when equipment is replaced, orientation is changed, shielding is added, thermal equilibrium is reached, or an independent team runs the protocol?

Only after the anomaly survives should new physics enter the room.

This order is a form of intellectual hygiene.

It is also emotionally difficult. Inventors tend to arrive attached to mechanisms. The machine is not simply producing excess heat; it is tapping the zero-point field. The pendulum is not merely behaving unexpectedly; it is modifying inertia. The capacitor is not merely leaking current; it is coupling to gravity. The explanation often arrives before the measurement has earned it.

Puthoff’s own theoretical interests complicate his role as evaluator because he is not metaphysically neutral about the vacuum. He has published proposals in which zero-point fields do physical explanatory work. That may make him better able to understand an unconventional claimant’s mechanism. It may also make him more willing to grant the mechanism prior plausibility.

The solution is not personality analysis.

It is controls.

An evaluator’s priors matter less when the apparatus can humiliate everyone.

This is why the EarthTech null paper is so valuable. It gives us evidence about laboratory norms rather than asking us to infer them from Puthoff’s personality.

The group wanted breakthrough energy badly enough to spend years on it.

It reported failure anyway.

That does not abolish confirmation bias.

It gives confirmation bias an adversary.

The public record is still uneven. Some EarthTech work appears in papers and conference proceedings. Some testing has been described in talks or on the organization’s site. Proprietary work for clients is, by definition, harder to audit. Claims that a private lab tested a device should therefore be treated as evidence of testing, not as evidence that the device passed.

This sounds pedantic until one watches how breakthrough stories travel.

EarthTech evaluated the technology becomes EarthTech validated the technology.

NASA was briefed becomes NASA confirmed it.

A defense customer asked for a report becomes the Pentagon has it.

An inventor received a patent becomes the invention works.

Each upgrade can happen without anyone deliberately lying. Language inflates as it moves away from the source.

Puthoff’s career is unusually vulnerable to this inflation because serious institutions really do appear around him. Stanford. SRI. CIA. DIA. Peer-reviewed physics journals. Bigelow. SEC-filed companies. The nouns are impressive enough that readers can stop checking the verbs.

The verbs are where truth lives.

Funded.

Published.

Observed.

Tested.

Failed.

Replicated.

Demonstrated.

Deployed.

Those are different stages.

EarthTech also sits inside a longer American tradition of private patrons financing research conventional institutions avoid. Wealthy industrialists funded psychical research in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Aviation pioneers backed strange aerodynamic ideas. Foundations supported consciousness research. Venture capital later normalized the idea that private money should tolerate technical risk public institutions cannot.

Private funding changes the epistemic environment.

It can protect a question from fashion. A donor can pay for ten years of work while grant panels remain hostile. That is sometimes exactly what a discovery needs.

It can also protect a question from correction. If the patron is committed to the idea and the research group depends on the patron, negative results may struggle to become terminal.

The distinction is governance.

A good patron buys freedom to fail.

A bad patron buys insulation from failure.

EarthTech’s published nulls show that private edge research can, at least sometimes, purchase failure honestly.

Robert Bigelow would become the most important patron in Puthoff’s later public story.

The relationship makes sense before any UFO enters the room.

Bigelow was wealthy enough to create private institutions at the edge of conventional science. Puthoff was technically trained, accustomed to sensitive sponsors, tolerant of anomalous claims, and experienced at translating them into research language. The partnership solved a mutual problem. Bigelow could fund questions he considered important. Puthoff could place those questions inside a technical frame.

In 1995 Bigelow created the National Institute for Discovery Science, usually known as NIDS. Its subjects would include aerial phenomena, unusual animal deaths, and other anomalies. Puthoff joined its scientific network.

By then he had learned something from Austin that is easy to miss if the biography is told only through extraordinary claims.

Sometimes the edge yields a new question.

Sometimes it yields a bad power meter.
