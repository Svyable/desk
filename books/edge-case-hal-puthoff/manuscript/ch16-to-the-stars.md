# Chapter 16 — To the Stars

In 2017, Hal Puthoff became part of a company that put the edge on a cap table.

To The Stars Academy of Arts & Science was incorporated as a Delaware public benefit corporation and presented itself as a hybrid of entertainment, science, aerospace, and public disclosure. Its founders and early principals included Tom DeLonge, the musician who had developed relationships with government and aerospace figures interested in UAP; Jim Semivan, a retired CIA operations officer; and Puthoff, the physicist whose biography already crossed several generations of classified-adjacent anomaly research.

This was not a secret organization.

It filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

That distinction makes TTSA one of the best-documented institutional episodes in Puthoff’s later life. Corporate filings name him as co-founder, Vice President Science and Technology, and director. They describe his EarthTech and IASA leadership, prior work at SRI and other institutions, and technical interests. Other filings record ownership and governance changes. In March 2021, a Form 1-U reported that TTSA was scaling back science and technology commercialization, emphasizing entertainment, and moving Puthoff from the board to its scientific advisory board.

The paper trail is almost aggressively ordinary.

This is useful because public discussion around TTSA was not.

At launch, the organization surrounded itself with claims of advanced aerospace science, government insiders, UAP evidence, materials, and technology commercialization. It entered a culture already primed by years of rumors about hidden UFO programs. The combination of a rock musician, former intelligence officers, aerospace specialists, and a physicist from the Stargate lineage was irresistible.

The SEC filings let us replace some of that atmosphere with numbers.

In May 2017 the company issued 2.5 million shares of Class A common stock to Harold E. Puthoff. The same financial note records five million shares issued to a consulting company owned by Jim Semivan and says the company recognized two hundred thousand dollars of stock-based compensation connected with those founder issuances. This does not tell us what Puthoff believed. It tells us that he was not a decorative scientist placed on a website after the fact. He was economically and organizationally inside the venture.

That matters because the public image of TTSA often treated its scientific personnel as a council of witnesses standing outside the company’s commercial machinery. The filing is more useful. It shows a startup.

A strange startup, certainly.

But a startup.

The company had intellectual property, media assets, employees, loans, equity issuances, related-party transactions, auditors, operating expenses, and a need for cash. Those facts do not make its scientific ambitions false. They make the incentives legible.

A scientist evaluating a sample inside a public-benefit corporation is operating inside more than one system at once. He may want the truth. The company may want a patent. Investors may want appreciation. Media operations may want a story. Executives may want another financing round. None of those interests requires dishonesty. They do require disclosure because the same ambiguous result can have different value to different parts of the institution.

Then came the Navy videos.

TTSA played a role in bringing public attention to videos associated with U.S. military encounters, including footage later acknowledged by the Department of Defense as genuine Navy video. That acknowledgment is one of the most instructive evidence lessons in the entire biography.

Authentic video does not mean authenticated interpretation.

A government can confirm that footage came from a military system without confirming that the object shown is extraterrestrial, transmedium, antigravity, or even aerodynamically extraordinary. Provenance answers one question.

Cause answers another.

This should have been obvious after remote viewing.

A real CIA report does not prove ESP.

A real DIA contract does not prove warp drive.

A real Navy video does not prove non-human technology.

But authentic provenance matters. It removes one mundane alternative. The video is no longer a random internet fabrication. The problem becomes narrower and more interesting.

Puthoff’s role in TTSA was broader than video analysis. The company described a science and technology division pursuing advanced aerospace and materials questions. It announced efforts involving material samples said to have unusual provenance or properties. It discussed an ambitious technology roadmap. The public could buy shares under Regulation A offerings, which meant extraordinary-sounding aspirations now lived beside risk disclosures, balance sheets, executive compensation, and securities law.

The materials story is a perfect case.

TTSA called its effort A.D.A.M., for Acquisition and Data Analysis of Materials. Its filings described samples reported to have come from advanced vehicles of unknown origin. That sentence is evidence of what the company said it had been told about provenance. It is not chain of custody.

In July 2019 the company entered into an asset-purchase agreement with DeLonge for a set of metamaterials. The audited financial statements later put the purchase price at thirty-five thousand dollars.

Thirty-five thousand dollars is an unexpectedly clarifying number.

It is large enough to show the acquisition was a real corporate transaction. It is small enough to puncture the atmosphere surrounding phrases like recovered metamaterial. The accounting system did not book an alien artifact. It booked an asset purchase.

What would upgrade that asset scientifically?

Not the purchase agreement.

Not the seller’s belief.

Not a story about where a prior owner obtained it.

The sample would need preserved chain of custody, contamination controls, documented preparation history, high-resolution compositional and structural measurements, comparison materials, independent laboratories, blind or at least adversarial replication, and a result that discriminates between exotic manufacture and unusual but terrestrial industrial processes.

The word metamaterial is especially vulnerable to inflation because metamaterials are already a real field of engineering. The term refers broadly to deliberately structured materials whose properties depend importantly on architecture as well as bulk chemistry. A sample can therefore be a metamaterial without being extraterrestrial, revolutionary, or inexplicable.

Anomalous layering is not origin.

Unusual isotopic composition is not origin until measurement artifacts, fractionation, contamination, feedstocks, and known industrial routes are addressed.

A material claim becomes extraordinary when the ordinary manufacturing tree is exhausted by reproducible analysis.

TTSA never publicly delivered that kind of conclusion for non-human manufacture.

That absence belongs beside the excitement, not after it.

The company’s own financial statements offer another kind of scale correction. As of December 31, 2020, the audited balance sheet listed forty-seven thousand three hundred thirty-three dollars in cash and total assets of roughly five hundred seventy thousand dollars. Total liabilities were about 1.45 million dollars. The company also disclosed short-term merchant loans carrying high average annual interest rates—26.7 percent in 2020 and 22.9 percent in 2019—and a line of credit associated with DeLonge.

These are not the finances of a Lockheed-scale black engineering program.

They are the finances of a small, capital-constrained media and research company.

Again, that does not prove the scientific questions were empty. It tells us what kind of institution was actually available to answer them.

The distinction matters when launch presentations are remembered years later. TTSA spoke about ambitious aerospace possibilities, beamed-energy concepts, spacetime metric engineering, materials analysis, and other difficult work. But a company with tens of thousands of dollars of cash, modest tangible assets, and substantial liabilities has a different experimental frontier from a national laboratory.

Some projects fail because nature says no.

Others fail because the instrument budget says no first.

Corporate ambition is therefore not a clean proxy for technical progress.

This collision of anomaly culture and corporate paperwork is one of the most revealing documents in the story.

Marketing wants possibility.

Securities regulation wants risks.

The result is a more grounded record than conference lore.

TTSA’s filings describe an organization trying to commercialize intellectual property across entertainment, science, and technology. They also disclose uncertainty, capital needs, and evolving strategy. By 2021 the company explicitly said it was scaling back science and tech commercialization and placing greater emphasis on entertainment. Puthoff moved from director to scientific adviser.

The wording is unusually direct.

The March 2021 current report says TTSA had taken steps to refocus its operations, scale back science and technology commercialization, and place greater emphasis on entertainment. As a result, it said, Puthoff transitioned from director to the scientific advisory board. The company was seeking a replacement director with entertainment and fundraising experience.

That is not an outsider’s skeptical reconstruction.

It is the issuer’s own filing.

If one wanted to write the event as a suppressed breakthrough, the corporate record is inconvenient.

If one wanted to write TTSA as pure theater, the genuine backgrounds of the people involved and the real public impact on UAP discourse are inconvenient.

Again the biography refuses the easy category.

TTSA mattered.

It helped move UAP discussion from internet subculture toward national press, congressional interest, and official acknowledgment. It assembled people with real government histories. It publicized authentic military footage. It created a corporate home for technical claims that mainstream aerospace organizations did not want to own publicly.

It did not publicly demonstrate a revolutionary propulsion technology.

It did not publicly establish that unusual materials were manufactured by non-human intelligence.

It did not convert the physics roadmaps into flight hardware.

By the company’s own filing, science and technology commercialization was later scaled back.

That outcome belongs in any honest account of Puthoff’s institutional method.

The edge institution can be historically successful without being technically successful in the way its most ambitious launch rhetoric implies.

TTSA changed a conversation.

That is not the same as changing propulsion.

Puthoff’s participation also made the continuity of his life newly visible to a younger audience. People encountering him through UAP media discovered the remote-viewing history and interpreted it according to their priors.

For believers, the continuity became evidence of long government knowledge. First psychic spying, then NIDS, then BAASS, then TTSA: a hidden chain.

For skeptics, it became evidence of a closed social network recycling extraordinary beliefs. Same people, new acronym.

The network itself cannot decide between those interpretations.

Networks are evidence about relationships.

Claims still need independent support.

This sounds like a minor methodological point until one realizes how much modern UAP argument is social epistemology. People decide what to believe by deciding whom to trust. A retired official says classified material is compelling. A pilot says the object behaved impossibly. A physicist says the performance may imply metric engineering. A journalist says multiple sources corroborate a program. A skeptic says sensor artifacts and narrative contagion explain the case.

Very few readers can inspect the raw sensor data, classified records, original material samples, or complete chain of custody.

Trust fills the missing bytes.

Puthoff has unusually high trust in some communities and unusually low trust in others because the same biography can be read as either courageous persistence or repeated credulity.

The right response is not to average the reputations.

It is to reduce dependence on reputation.

Release calibrated data where security permits.

Publish methods.

Provide chain of custody.

Let independent laboratories test material blindly.

State what result would defeat the favored hypothesis.

Separate witness testimony from physical measurement.

Make extraordinary performance numerical.

This is not hostile to UAP research.

It is how UAP research would stop depending on personalities.

A mature field would make Hal Puthoff less important.

That may be the best test of whether the edge has become science.
