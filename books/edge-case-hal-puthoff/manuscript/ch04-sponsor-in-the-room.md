# Chapter 4 — Sponsor in the Room

The Central Intelligence Agency did not need to believe in psychic powers to be frightened by them.

That is the cleanest way to understand why the SRI work escaped the laboratory.

In the summer of 1972, according to the CIA’s own later public history, the Agency began investigating remote viewing with outside researchers. The motive lived inside the Cold War. U.S. intelligence had collected information about Soviet and Eastern European interest in parapsychology. If an adversary was spending money on a capability that sounded absurd, the United States had several choices: ignore it, mock it, imitate it, or find out whether anything was there.

Intelligence organizations are professionally bad at ignoring low-probability threats.

They are also professionally vulnerable to them.

An analyst who dismisses an exotic program and is later wrong can become the person who missed a strategic surprise. An analyst who investigates and finds nothing has spent comparatively little. This incentive structure creates a habitat for fringe research that ordinary science does not share.

The habitat is easy to misread from outside. Classification makes a project look important because secrets feel important. In reality, programs are classified for reasons that may have nothing to do with the truth of the central hypothesis. The target may be sensitive. The sponsor’s interest may be sensitive. The identity of an operational unit may be sensitive. The possibility that an adversary is being assessed may be sensitive. A program can be classified while investigating a phenomenon that ultimately proves useless.

Remote viewing entered that habitat.

The SRI researchers called one early sponsor-controlled effort SCANATE, a name associated with “scanning by coordinates.” Government personnel selected or protected target information. Subjects attempted to describe sites. The design addressed the objection that investigators could consciously or unconsciously cue targets they already knew. This was an important improvement, and it produced the kind of results that kept people returning.

One should resist the urge to make those results cleaner than they were.

Declassified files include summaries of striking sessions and ordinary failures. A 1978 Army remote-viewing report, for example, explicitly says a particular viewer’s impressions showed “absolutely no target correlation.” That sentence is more valuable than ten legends because it proves the archive is not merely a victory scrapbook. Remote viewing sessions could miss badly.

The existence of misses does not disprove an effect. The proportion, scoring, and distribution matter. But operational intelligence has a harsher problem than experimental statistics. A spy cannot tell the commander which session is the hit until reality is known.

Suppose a viewer describes a bridge, industrial structures, water, and a tall crane. If the target later contains those features, the result may be impressive. If the commander must act before confirmation, the same description is a bundle of possibilities. Which element is reliable? How much is symbolic? Which details are imagination? What confidence should attach to each statement? Intelligence is not scored by resemblance after the answer key arrives.

Puthoff’s career sits between those two evaluation cultures.

The laboratory wants to know whether information appears above chance. The operator wants to know whether this report should change a decision.

Those questions are related and not equivalent.

The CIA’s own retrospective account, published decades after declassification, makes the distinction unusually clear. The Agency says it stopped its direct research in 1977, after roughly five years, and the broader program continued elsewhere in the Defense Department. When the CIA revisited the program in the 1990s, it commissioned an independent evaluation. The conclusion it emphasizes is almost paradoxical: there were enough apparently accurate experiences to keep the statistical argument alive, but the phenomenon was too unreliable and inconsistent for intelligence use.

That is a much more interesting outcome than either side’s slogan.

If every result had been fraud or error, the program would be a bureaucratic comedy. If remote viewing had become a dependable sensor, it would be one of the most important discoveries in the history of intelligence. Instead, the government spent years in the middle: interested enough to continue, disappointed enough not to operationalize it as a dependable collection system, and divided enough that later scientific reviewers would still disagree about what the laboratory data meant.

Puthoff helped create that middle.

One of the reasons was the way he framed the subject. Intelligence officials did not need to accept occult vocabulary. They could think in terms of information transfer. The mind became a noisy channel. The target became a source. The transcript became output. Statistical judging estimated whether output contained target-related information.

Information theory is agnostic about mechanism. You can measure a channel’s performance before you know what carries the signal.

That was conceptually elegant.

It also created a trap. Once a human narrative is treated as a communications channel, it can become easy to forget how much interpretation occurs between raw statement and score. Bits do not judge themselves. A human judge compares material. Target pools have structure. Language is flexible. Drawings contain reusable geometry. The channel metaphor can clarify the question while hiding the psychology of evaluation.

The government record shows that sponsors worried about methodology. They did not simply surrender to wonder. Internal assessments and later critiques raised issues around controls, replication, alternative explanations, and operational value. A declassified CIA review of parapsychology emphasized that confidence depended on the skill and integrity of experimenters. That sounds obvious until one notices how different it is from mature physics. Nobody evaluating a voltmeter should have to trust the operator’s metaphysical honesty to know whether a voltage exists.

Extraordinary human-subject research places much more weight on protocol.

This is why magicians have sometimes been so useful to parapsychology. A physicist knows how an instrument can fail. A magician knows how a demonstration can deceive. A psychologist knows how expectations alter judgments. The ideal laboratory needs all three forms of suspicion.

SRI had strong technical people. Whether it had enough adversarial suspicion at each stage is one of the central disputes of the next decade.

For intelligence sponsors, however, the bar for another contract could be lower than the bar for scientific consensus. One anomalous trial under sponsor control could justify more work. Soviet reports could justify more work. A gifted subject could justify more work. The cost remained modest.

This funding ecology explains how programs survive without ever reaching closure.

Each year produces enough anomaly to justify another year but not enough reliability to settle the argument. Skeptics say the failure to converge is evidence against the phenomenon. Advocates say the phenomenon is difficult, human, and not yet understood. Sponsors continue because the potential payoff remains high. The program accumulates history, and history begins to look like validation.

Longevity is not evidence in the way people think it is.

A twenty-year research program proves that people funded twenty years of research. It does not by itself prove the researched effect.

Still, longevity changes people.

Puthoff spent years interacting with subjects, sponsors, technical monitors, intelligence officials, and data he considered anomalous. By the time an outsider encountered the topic through a skeptical article, Puthoff had a private archive of experience. This creates an asymmetry in argument. The researcher feels the critic has seen five percent of the case. The critic feels the researcher has become captured by years of ambiguous material.

Both can be right.

Long immersion produces expertise and motivated reasoning at the same time.

The sponsor’s presence intensified this. When an intelligence professional says a description matches a target the researcher could not have known, the validation carries authority. But intelligence confirmation can be hard to audit. The target information may remain classified. The degree of match may be summarized rather than exposed. The researcher can know more than the public, but the public cannot independently inspect the decisive material.

This is the beginning of what I think of as classified epistemology.

Classified epistemology is not secret truth. It is a condition in which claims, evidence, and validation are distributed across compartments so that no ordinary reader can reconstruct the full chain. In that environment, reputation becomes a substitute for missing access. “The sponsor confirmed it” takes the place of the confirmation.

Puthoff would spend much of his later life in versions of this structure.

At SRI, the hidden element might be a target or intelligence evaluation. In advanced aerospace work, it might be a sponsor’s threat question. In modern UAP debates, it might be sensor data, materials access, or testimony said to exist behind classification. The public is repeatedly asked to decide how much weight to place on serious people pointing toward rooms they cannot enter.

The only stable defense is evidence bookkeeping.

What is public? What is classified but attested by a document? What is reported by a participant? What has independent replication? What is inferred? What is simply claimed?

Remote viewing is a perfect training ground because every category appears.

The CIA’s interest is verified.

SRI’s government-funded experiments are verified.

Some apparently impressive sessions are documented.

Criticisms of controls are documented.

Failed or poor sessions are documented.

The existence of a reliable psychic information channel is disputed.

The mechanism, if any, is unknown.

Operational usefulness was ultimately judged inadequate by the review that closed the government program.

None of those statements cancels the others.

This may sound like excessive caution. It is actually a way to preserve the strange parts without inflating them.

A government agency investigating remote viewing during the Cold War is already astonishing. It does not need the additional claim that the CIA secretly proved psychic powers. The archive becomes more interesting when allowed to remain unresolved.

Puthoff’s role was not to make the CIA psychic.

It was to make psychic claims testable enough that the CIA could become a sponsor without first becoming a believer.
