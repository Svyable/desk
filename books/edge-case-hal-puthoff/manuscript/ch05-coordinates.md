# Chapter 5 — Coordinates

A coordinate is a peculiar thing to give a psychic.

It contains enormous information if you have a map and almost none if you do not. Forty degrees north, a hundred degrees west: the numbers specify a place only through a system learned in advance. They do not smell like the prairie, contain the geometry of a building, or whisper whether a river bends nearby.

That made coordinates attractive to the SRI team.

If a subject could describe a site after receiving only a coordinate pair, the cue appeared informationally sterile. No descriptive word had been supplied. No photograph had leaked. The target could be thousands of miles away. The experiment seemed to isolate the extraordinary claim cleanly.

It did not isolate the evaluation problem.

The history of coordinate remote viewing became dominated by examples because examples are how human beings understand free-response data. A subject draws a shape. The target contains a similar shape. A subject mentions water. The target is near water. A subject describes a gantry, crane, sphere, building, road, mountain, or antenna. The sponsor recognizes something.

Then the story begins editing itself.

Misses become context. Hits become nouns.

This is not necessarily fraud. It is ordinary cognition. A hundred pages of transcript can collapse in memory into the three details that made everybody in the room stop talking.

Puthoff and Targ’s challenge was to build a scoring system stronger than memory.

Their laboratory work used target pools and blind judges. The judge might visit possible target sites or compare transcripts against target materials, ranking which transcript best matched which site. If correct pairings occurred more often than chance, the researchers argued that some information had crossed the sensory barrier.

The statistical logic is legitimate in principle. The devil lives in the materials.

Free-response experiments are vulnerable to what later critics called cueing or leakage in the judging process. Suppose the transcript itself contains information unrelated to psychic perception but correlated with the target’s position in the experiment: references to weather, travel time, experimenter remarks, paper handling, dates, or comments made after a session. A judge may unconsciously use those cues. Even a subtle difference in transcript length can matter if sessions systematically differed.

The target set can create another problem. If one target is a swimming pool and every other target is dry, the word “water” is highly diagnostic. If half the targets contain water, it is not. Statistical significance depends not only on the viewer’s output but on how discriminable the targets are.

These problems became central to the later Marks and Kammann critique. But before the critique, sponsors had already been shown operational cases that were harder to reduce to a clean laboratory score.

The Semipalatinsk example is the most durable.

A declassified government summary describes a Soviet R&D site, known within the intelligence community by a code name, characterized by a large rail-mounted crane and large metallic structures. The viewer’s reported impressions included a large crane and the assembly of large spherical forms. For proponents, this looked like a direct hit on details no subject could plausibly infer.

The first discipline is to notice what the same document says about cueing. The viewer was given coordinates and was told the site was an R&D facility in the Soviet Union.

That is not a trivial cue.

It does not tell you “large rail-mounted crane” or “metallic gores forming a sphere.” Those remain specific. But it changes the space of plausible imagery. Industrial structures, fences, machinery, rail lines, laboratories, towers, military geometry, and construction become more likely responses than beaches or cathedrals. The proper question is not whether the cue explains everything. It is how much evidentiary weight remains after the cue is accounted for.

This way of thinking is less satisfying than deciding whether the session was psychic.

It is also more scientific.

Evidence should move probability in proportion to how surprising it is under competing explanations. A correct generic feature moves it slightly. A highly specific, independently verified feature that was not cued moves it more. A feature selected from a long transcript after target reveal moves it less than the same feature specified in advance as diagnostic. Multiple independent hits matter more than one celebrated session.

The early SRI literature often mixed these levels because the program had two goals at once. It was trying to establish a phenomenon scientifically and explore whether the phenomenon could be useful operationally.

Operational people do not always care about elegant statistics. If a source gives one unique detail that leads to a verification, the source may be useful even if much of the rest is noise. Human intelligence works like this all the time. Sources are partial, biased, deceptive, mistaken, or uncertain. Analysts fuse them with other collection.

Puthoff himself later emphasized that remote-viewing information should supplement normal information rather than replace it. That is a more modest claim than “psychic spying works,” and it creates a different test.

If a viewer produces twenty statements and one prompts an analyst to check an overlooked possibility, the session might be operationally valuable even if blind scoring is poor. But this introduces a search problem. Intelligence analysts are excellent pattern finders. Give them enough vague material and they may discover a match after the fact.

The gold standard would be prospective usefulness.

Before verification, did the remote-viewing output cause a decision, collection task, search area, or analytic hypothesis that later proved correct at a rate worth the cost? Could users distinguish useful sessions from useless ones before feedback? Did the information add value beyond existing intelligence?

Those questions are much harder than asking whether a sketch resembles a target.

The government’s eventual judgment turned heavily on this distinction.

Remote-viewing advocates could point to laboratory statistics and celebrated operational examples. Intelligence users evaluating the full program had to ask a harsher question: can we depend on it?

Dependability is where anomalous capabilities usually die.

The military does not require every sensor to be perfect. Radar produces clutter. Sonar produces false contacts. Human sources lie. Satellites miss windows. But mature sensors have characterized failure modes. An operator knows something about sensitivity, specificity, resolution, conditions, and confidence. The system can be calibrated.

Remote viewing resisted calibration.

The same viewer could appear extraordinary in one session and useless in another. Some targets seemed easier than others. Feedback and judging methods changed. Training methods evolved. The alleged signal did not behave like a stable channel whose performance could be specified to a commander.

This instability became fuel for both sides.

Advocates argued that human cognition is variable and that ordinary intelligence sources are variable too. Skeptics argued that uncontrolled interpretation can always produce occasional spectacular matches in variable free-response data. The argument became recursive because variability itself could be treated as either a property of the phenomenon or evidence that there was no phenomenon.

Puthoff’s engineering instinct appears in the attempt to improve protocol rather than abandon the subject.

Coordinate remote viewing became structured. Procedures were documented. Training emerged. Later Army units would adapt methods associated with SRI and Ingo Swann. The program acquired stages, vocabulary, and an operator culture.

This is another point where existence and engineering blur.

If training improves scores, that supports the idea of a trainable skill. It can also improve a subject’s ability to produce descriptive material that judges find matchable. Without rigorous controls, both hypotheses predict improvement.

The deepest methodological problem is that free-response human data are semantically rich.

Numbers are brutal. If I predict 7 and the answer is 4, I missed. Language is generous. “Tall, vertical, metallic, movement, open space, water nearby” can attach itself to ports, bridges, dams, industrial plants, ships, towers, construction sites, and dozens of other targets. Drawings add another layer of flexibility. A curve can become a dome, road, shoreline, pipe, hill, or dish.

This does not make meaningful matches impossible.

It makes precommitment valuable.

Modern anomaly research should specify in advance which target features count, how many statements will be scored, what the comparison set is, who judges, what information the judge receives, and how failed sessions remain in the dataset. The more extraordinary the inference, the less room there should be for interpretive mercy.

SRI was working toward versions of this discipline before contemporary open-science vocabulary existed. That should be credited. The program also left enough openings that critics found serious leverage. That should be credited too.

The biography becomes especially interesting when Puthoff’s response to criticism is considered.

He did not recant. He defended the work, published responses, continued government-supported research, and appears to have remained convinced that the phenomenon was real. This was not a youthful detour later abandoned. Remote viewing became part of his model of the world.

That matters because later Puthoff is sometimes presented as if he moved independently from conventional physics to vacuum-energy theory to advanced propulsion to UAP. The remote-viewing years taught a prior lesson that may have been more influential than any technical result: institutions can reject a phenomenon socially while portions of the government investigate it privately.

That lesson is factually true in at least one case.

Parapsychology was marginal in mainstream science. The U.S. government really did fund it.

From that fact, however, two very different philosophies can grow.

One says: therefore ridiculed claims deserve occasional controlled investigation because institutions can be too conservative.

The other says: therefore ridicule itself is evidence that secret insiders may know the claim is real.

The first is defensible.

The second is epistemic dynamite.

Puthoff has spent much of his career close enough to both that readers need to keep them separate for him.

Coordinates were supposed to remove information from the cue.

The larger history shows how hard it is to remove information from a human system.
