# Chapter 6 — Nature Says Yes, Sort Of

In October 1974, *Nature* published a paper by Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff titled “Information transmission under conditions of sensory shielding.”

That sentence has done enormous work for half a century.

For believers, publication in one of the world’s most prestigious scientific journals is evidence that remote viewing passed the gate of serious science. For skeptics, the paper is a cautionary tale about what peer review can and cannot establish. For the biographer, it is something more specific: the moment Puthoff’s strange research became impossible to dismiss as a private government curiosity.

The paper exists. It was reviewed, revised, and published. Its abstract says the authors were presenting experimental results suggesting one or more perceptual modalities by which individuals obtained information not presented to any known sense. The authors argued that such abilities could be studied under laboratory conditions.

Notice the verbs.

“Suggesting” is not “proving.” “Can be studied” is not “has been explained.” The paper was bold, but the strongest public mythology around it is bolder.

Peer review is frequently misunderstood because people want it to function like certification. A published paper feels stamped TRUE. That is not what journals do. Editors and reviewers ask whether work is sufficiently interesting, methodologically serious, and legible to enter the scientific conversation. A high-profile journal may publish a controversial result precisely because exposing it to scrutiny is valuable.

The remote-viewing paper entered that scrutiny almost immediately.

The historical importance is undeniable. Parapsychology had long suffered from isolation. Here were two physicists at a major research institute publishing claims about anomalous information transfer in *Nature*. The authors were not hiding from scientific attack. They provided enough detail for other researchers to attempt replication and critique.

That is a real virtue.

It is not the same as being right.

The paper combined several kinds of experiments and subjects, including well-known individuals. This created a rhetorical problem that has never gone away. Spectacular anecdotes and statistical laboratory data occupied the same conceptual package. A reader could be impressed by a famous subject’s apparent success and carry that impression into evaluation of experiments that required a different standard.

Scientific papers usually try to reduce narrative heat. Paranormal research generates it automatically.

A target description that seems uncannily accurate produces a visceral response. The reader wants to know how the subject could possibly have known. A p-value produces a cooler response. It asks whether the aggregate result is surprising under a statistical null. The two forms of evidence can support each other, but they can also distract from each other’s weaknesses.

Puthoff and Targ framed remote viewing as an information-transfer problem partly to escape this trap. Their experiments attempted to quantify performance. But free-response data remained semantically complicated, and critics focused on exactly that point.

David Marks and Richard Kammann later published a failed replication and critique in *Nature*. They argued that they could not verify the SRI conclusions. Their later work emphasized cues in transcripts and judging procedures, claiming that supposedly blind judges could match records using ordinary information embedded in the materials.

Puthoff, Targ, and allies disputed the force of these criticisms. The argument became technical and personal in the way long scientific disputes often do.

The important fact is not that critics existed. Every consequential claim attracts critics. The important fact is that the criticism addressed a plausible ordinary mechanism that could generate apparent success.

A strong extraordinary claim has to do more than produce an anomaly. It has to survive the best mundane mechanism available.

If a judge can match transcripts to targets because of cues, then above-chance matching no longer requires psychic information transfer. The burden shifts back to the experimenter to remove those cues and demonstrate the effect under cleaner conditions.

This is how science should work.

The problem is that public belief rarely updates as cleanly as protocols do. Once a dramatic result has entered culture, later methodological corrections become footnotes. “Published in *Nature*” is short. “Published in *Nature*, challenged by a failed replication and a transcript-cueing critique, followed by responses and later experiments with changed methods” is not.

Puthoff’s biography is full of truths too long for slogans.

He and Targ also published a substantial 1976 article in the *Proceedings of the IEEE*, another venue whose prestige became part of the legitimacy story. A declassified Defense Intelligence Agency document later pointed to publication in major journals as evidence that the field had achieved a higher scientific standard. The government itself was influenced by peer-review signals.

Again, that influence was rational and incomplete.

An intelligence manager is not a specialist in experimental psychology. If a respected journal publishes a controversial technical paper, that lowers the reputational cost of further investigation. It does not eliminate the need for replication.

The story becomes stranger when one reads government documents from the period because some internal evaluators were simultaneously impressed and skeptical. One CIA-linked assessment of parapsychology noted that confidence in reported results depended heavily on confidence in the experimenters’ skill and integrity. Other documents summarized striking target correspondences. Still others recorded null or poor sessions.

This is not how a secret discovery looks.

It is how a contested research program looks.

That difference matters because later retellings often use classification as a narrative editing tool. Contradictory documents become evidence of compartmentation. Negative findings become cover. Program termination becomes proof that the work went deeper underground. Once that style of reasoning takes hold, no possible archival outcome can defeat the hypothesis.

Puthoff’s own public posture did not require such an escape. He continued to argue for anomalous effects in the open literature. That exposed him to criticism and preserved a record we can evaluate.

The best way to respect that choice is to take the criticism seriously.

Marks and Kammann’s replication failure did not prove every SRI result was cueing. A failed replication can fail for many reasons, especially in human-subject work. Their cueing argument, however, identified a concrete pathway by which at least some judging success could be contaminated. That is more powerful than a general skeptical attitude because it generates a fix: strip transcripts of cues, randomize properly, separate experimenters, predefine scoring, and repeat.

Remote-viewing research did evolve toward stronger controls in later years. The government program continued through different organizations and managers. Edwin May, who later led much of the research, emphasized statistical work and altered protocols. By the time the CIA commissioned its 1995 review, the debate had moved well beyond the 1974 paper.

The paper remained symbolic because it represented a crossing.

Puthoff had crossed from conventional quantum electronics into a scientific borderland and persuaded a premier journal to let the borderland onto its pages.

This says something about him that neither side captures well.

He was not afraid of reputational contamination.

That can be intellectual courage.

It can also be a vulnerability.

Scientists use reputation as a crude error-control system. If every implausible claim received equal attention, research would drown. Fields develop norms about what is worth time. Those norms can become dogma, but they also protect finite attention from perpetual-motion machines and miracle cures.

A scientist willing to ignore those norms gains access to neglected possibilities and to an ocean of noise.

The question becomes selection.

How does he decide which impossible claim deserves an experiment?

Puthoff’s later career suggests a consistent answer: look for a claim with either an unusually consequential implication, a technically interesting mechanism, credible witnesses or sponsors, or some piece of data that seems resistant to easy dismissal. Then build a small institution around the test.

That strategy can produce valuable negative knowledge. It can also create decades-long ambiguity if the tests never achieve decisive resolution.

The 1974 *Nature* paper is therefore not a verdict.

It is a document showing that the question survived one gate.

The next gate was replication.

That gate would be much less polite.
