# Chapter 7 — Cueing

David Marks did something more damaging to remote viewing than call it nonsense.

He proposed a mechanism.

That is how a serious criticism should work. Skepticism is cheap when it consists of an adjective. Fraudulent. Impossible. Pseudoscientific. Credulous. None of those words explains a result. If an experiment appears to show that subjects describe remote targets above chance, the useful skeptic has to identify a pathway by which ordinary information could masquerade as extraordinary information.

Marks and Richard Kammann went after the pathway.

Their 1978 paper in *Nature* reported an attempted replication of the kind of remote-viewing procedure associated with SRI. They did not reproduce the claimed effect. Later, Marks and collaborators argued that transcripts used in judging could contain cues allowing targets to be matched without paranormal perception. Dates, references to sequence, experimenter remarks, and other apparently irrelevant material could become identifiers. A judge did not have to know consciously that a clue was a clue. It was enough that the records were not informationally sterile.

This is the kind of criticism that should make an experimenter grateful and furious at the same time.

Grateful because a concrete failure mode can be fixed. Furious because if the failure mode survives, an impressive result can collapse without any fraud at all.

Remote viewing was especially vulnerable because its data were not simple numbers. A transcript might contain pages of words and drawings. A judge then compared that semantic cloud against possible targets. If the judge had any nonpsychic clue about which transcript belonged to which trial, the scoring process could become contaminated even though the viewer had remained perfectly blind.

This is a subtle distinction. The leak does not have to reach the subject.

The viewer can know nothing about the target and still appear successful if the judge knows something about the transcript.

That possibility is an important correction to the popular image of sensory shielding. Discussions of paranormal experiments often focus on whether the subject could peek through a door, hear a conversation, glimpse a photograph, or infer a target from body language. But the entire evidence chain matters. A target can be protected perfectly at the viewing stage and then leak during analysis.

Modern clinical trials learned the same lesson through different failures. Blinding the patient is not enough if the physician knows treatment assignment and changes care. Blinding the physician is not enough if the statistician receives revealing labels. Randomization is not enough if investigators exclude inconvenient outcomes after seeing them. Scientific evidence is produced by a pipeline, and information can enter at every junction.

Puthoff and Targ disputed the critics’ account and defended the SRI work. Later experiments changed procedures. The debate became a dispute over which datasets were contaminated, whether cueing explained the strongest results, whether replications under cleaner conditions still showed an effect, and whether statistical findings from later laboratories inherited the weaknesses of the early work.

This is where biographies usually choose a team.

A believer can say Marks exposed one procedural vulnerability without explaining the entire program. A skeptic can say a demonstrated cueing pathway is exactly what one expects when an extraordinary effect shrinks under tighter control. Both positions can be defended. The archive does not permit a clean sentence in which one paper destroys or proves remote viewing.

What it does permit is a change in burden.

Once a mundane pathway has been demonstrated, extraordinary interpretation becomes more expensive. The next experiment should be designed specifically to make that pathway impossible. Transcripts can be standardized and stripped of identifying information. Judges can receive only the material necessary to score. Target pools can be constructed prospectively. Sessions can be registered before outcome. All trials can remain in the analysis. Independent groups can receive the protocol before seeing the result.

If the effect survives, confidence rises.

If it disappears, the disappearance becomes evidence too.

Puthoff’s public career is important because he did not respond to criticism by leaving the field. He continued to work within the government-supported program, and the program itself evolved. That fact is sometimes offered as evidence that the critics failed. It is not. Research programs continue for institutional reasons as well as scientific ones. Sponsors can remain interested while investigators disagree about mechanism. A disputed effect can justify additional testing precisely because the dispute is unresolved.

The better question is whether the research became progressively more capable of defeating its own preferred explanation.

That is a harder historical reconstruction.

By the early 1980s, remote-viewing work had accumulated multiple protocols, subjects, sponsors, and locations. SRI documents in the declassified archive include revised evaluation procedures and discussions of methodological development. Edwin May eventually became a central scientific figure in the program and later argued that the aggregate laboratory database supported an anomalous effect. Others disagreed.

The multiplication of data solved one problem and created another.

Large programs are harder to summarize honestly.

A single trial can be read from beginning to end. A twenty-year literature requires choices about inclusion, statistical model, protocol quality, independence, and publication bias. When different eras used different methods, the phrase “the remote-viewing data” becomes misleading. There is no single experiment called Stargate.

There are experiments, operational tasks, training sessions, reports, failures, striking anecdotes, statistical analyses, critiques, and retrospective judgments.

Puthoff’s SRI period occupies only part of that history, but it shaped the vocabulary later reviewers inherited.

Cueing also exposes a psychological asymmetry that followed him into later subjects.

When an extraordinary claim is criticized, proponents often ask whether the critic can explain every anomalous case. That sounds fair until one notices the burden shift. Science ordinarily does not require a mundane explanation for every unexplained observation before rejecting a new force of nature. It requires the new explanation to outperform alternatives under controlled tests.

A hospital can contain ten unexplained infections without proving a new pathogen. A radar can contain unidentified tracks without proving exotic aircraft. A material can display an unusual isotope ratio without proving extraterrestrial manufacture. An instrument can produce an anomalous transient without proving psychokinesis.

“Unexplained” is a state of knowledge, not an ontology.

Puthoff has spent much of his career near cases where that distinction is under pressure.

The strongest defense of his approach is straightforward: somebody has to investigate the residue. If every anomaly is dismissed because ordinary explanations usually win, rare discoveries can be lost. Science advances partly by refusing to discard stubborn observations too quickly.

The strongest criticism is equally straightforward: people who specialize in residues can become selected for exactly the cases where ordinary explanation is hardest to reconstruct. Missing metadata, classified targets, degraded samples, ambiguous sensor records, and participant memories create an evidentiary environment in which the anomaly always survives because decisive information is absent.

Cueing is valuable because it is the opposite of that environment.

It is specific.

It says: here is a route by which information may have traveled.

That is what makes it scientifically productive even if it does not explain every SRI result.

There is another reason this episode matters to Puthoff’s biography. His later public arguments often emphasize that critics should examine data rather than dismiss subjects by reputation. On this point he is right. But the same rule applies to criticism. A critique that identifies an information leak cannot be answered by saying the overall phenomenon remains impressive. The mechanism has to be closed.

This is the symmetry an edge researcher needs to preserve.

Treat the extraordinary claim as if it could be true.

Treat the ordinary explanation as if it could be true too.

Then build the experiment so one of them suffers.

Remote viewing rarely provided that clean cruelty.

It provided probability distributions, debates over effect size, arguments about judging, rare gifted subjects, sponsor impressions, and operational anecdotes. Every improvement in control seemed to produce another argument about whether the new procedure still measured the same thing. That is not unique to parapsychology. Psychology, medicine, and social science all struggle when measurement changes the behavior being measured.

But paranormal claims pay a higher price for ambiguity because their prior plausibility is lower and their implications are larger.

A tiny methodological flaw that would be irritating in an ordinary perception experiment can become decisive when the conclusion is that minds obtain information without a known sensory channel.

This is why “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence” is less useful than it sounds. The word extraordinary does not specify a method.

Better evidence means fewer alternative pathways.

Cueing was an alternative pathway.

The SRI researchers had to close it.

The history that followed never produced enough consensus to make everybody agree that they had.
