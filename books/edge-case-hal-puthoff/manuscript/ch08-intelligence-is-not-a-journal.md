# Chapter 8 — Intelligence Is Not a Journal

A scientific paper can survive while being useless to a commander.

This seems obvious until a research program is funded by an intelligence agency. Then the two standards begin contaminating each other.

Remote viewing could, in principle, pass a laboratory test without becoming good intelligence. It could also, in principle, produce an occasional operational success without satisfying a clean laboratory model. The U.S. government spent years discovering how much trouble lives between those possibilities.

The laboratory asks whether an effect is distinguishable from chance under specified conditions.

Intelligence asks what to do on Tuesday.

An analyst staring at a possible missile site does not need a philosophical result about anomalous cognition. She needs information that changes a collection plan, warning estimate, target package, or decision. The information has to arrive before the answer is known. It has to contain enough specificity that a user can separate signal from imagination. Ideally, the system has characterized reliability.

Remote viewing never became that kind of instrument.

This conclusion should not be smuggled backward into every experiment. The later government review did not establish that every laboratory result was chance. Jessica Utts, a statistician asked to review the program in 1995, argued that the accumulated statistical evidence supported the existence of an anomalous information-transfer effect. Ray Hyman, the psychologist and longtime critic of parapsychology, disagreed that the evidence justified that conclusion and emphasized methodological and replication concerns. Their disagreement is one reason simplistic summaries of the review are misleading.

They were closer on the operational problem.

The American Institutes for Research evaluation, commissioned when CIA inherited the remaining program in the 1990s, examined both research and application. The practical verdict was poor. Even where viewers produced material that could later be judged as matching a target, users could not reliably separate useful information from incorrect material before verification. The output was inconsistent. CIA chose not to restore the program.

A later CIA public retrospective states the paradox bluntly: the review found enough accurate experiences to challenge a simple randomness story, yet the phenomenon was too unreliable, inconsistent, and sporadic to be useful for intelligence.

One can disagree with the first clause and still understand the second.

A sensor with unknown calibration is a liability.

The government had spent roughly two decades learning that anomaly and capability are different categories.

Puthoff was present near the beginning of that education. By the time the program passed through Army and DIA organizations, other investigators and managers had taken larger roles, including Edwin May. Puthoff had left SRI in the mid-1980s. It would be wrong to attribute the entire later Stargate history to him.

But the standards developed during the SRI years followed the program.

One of the strangest features of intelligence research is that failure can remain classified.

A normal academic field publishes some null results and loses many more in file drawers. Classified programs create a larger drawer. Outsiders may hear only the stories participants choose to tell. A spectacular hit becomes conference lore. Ten ambiguous tasks remain in an archive. This selection effect can make a terminated program look more successful after declassification than it looked to the people who had to use it.

The reverse can happen too.

A program may contain genuinely interesting data that disappear into bureaucracy because the operational sponsor loses patience. Termination does not prove scientific falsity. Budgets end for political, strategic, managerial, and practical reasons.

This is why the 1995 review should be read as a bounded judgment.

It asked whether the program’s research established anomalous cognition and whether remote viewing had intelligence utility. The reviewers split on the first question. The sponsoring institution decided against continuation on the second.

That is enough.

There is no need to turn closure into either a debunking ritual or a secret victory.

Puthoff’s later career suggests he did not internalize the closure as proof that the underlying phenomenon was false. This is understandable if he believed the early laboratory work and specific sessions contained effects too strong to dismiss. Scientists do not automatically abandon a hypothesis because an agency stops buying it.

But institutional failure should still count as evidence.

The idea that remote viewing could become an intelligence tool was tested for years under unusually motivated sponsorship. It did not become a dependable collection discipline. Any modern claim that the technique is operationally mature has to explain that history rather than leap over it.

The same rule applies to Puthoff’s later engineering interests.

A physical effect can exist without producing a useful device.

A vacuum fluctuation can be real without yielding extractable power.

A metric solution can be mathematically valid without providing a buildable propulsion system.

An unidentified object can be genuinely unidentified without being non-human technology.

Puthoff’s biography is a long education in the ladder between existence and use.

Remote viewing reached a peculiar intermediate rung: enough formal research, sponsorship, publication, and anomaly to remain historically serious; not enough reliability to become what its most enthusiastic sponsors wanted.

That intermediate rung is difficult to communicate because public debate prefers binaries.

Did it work?

The answer depends on what “work” means.

Did the United States actually fund remote-viewing research? Yes.

Did trained subjects sometimes produce descriptions that investigators and sponsors considered striking? Yes.

Did statistical analyses produce results some qualified reviewers regarded as evidence for anomalous cognition? Yes.

Did other qualified reviewers identify methodological weaknesses and reject that conclusion? Yes.

Did the program become a reliable intelligence collection capability? The official decision was no.

Was a physical mechanism established? No.

This structure is almost a template for Puthoff’s later public life.

He would repeatedly operate where several lower rungs were real and the top rung remained unearned.

There was a real contract.

There were real scientists.

There was a real paper.

There was a real observation.

There was a real witness.

There was a real sample.

Then, somewhere between those facts and the extraordinary conclusion, uncertainty widened.

An edge researcher can respond to that widening in two ways. He can retreat until only consensus questions remain. Or he can treat the gap itself as a career.

Puthoff chose the gap.

That decision had consequences for how different communities saw him.

Within conventional physics, the remote-viewing years became reputational baggage. A scientist associated with psychic research starts future conversations with a credibility tax. Skeptics can reasonably ask whether the same judgment that accepted one controversial interpretation is operating in the next.

Within anomaly-research communities, the same history becomes a credential. Puthoff had been inside a program later declassified and confirmed as real. He had worked with intelligence sponsors when the public treated psychic spying as science fiction. That history made him an archetype of the scientist who knows that ridicule and secret government interest can coexist.

Both reputations are based on true facts.

Neither is a substitute for evaluating the next claim.

This is one of the most important habits a reader can carry through the rest of the book: reputation should alter how carefully we check, not decide the result in advance.

The government’s willingness to investigate remote viewing proves that the government was willing to investigate remote viewing.

It does not prove ESP.

The government’s decision to stop using it proves that the capability failed the sponsor’s practical threshold.

It does not prove every anomalous laboratory result was ordinary.

This sounds almost painfully modest.

It is also the only posture that keeps the biography from turning into propaganda for either side.

Puthoff left SRI before the program’s final judgment. He carried something else with him: a willingness to take fundamental physical questions seriously when practical payoff was distant and reputational risk was high.

The next problem did not involve a person describing a target.

It involved the vacuum.
