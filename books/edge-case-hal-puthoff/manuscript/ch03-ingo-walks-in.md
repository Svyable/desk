# Chapter 3 — Ingo Walks In

Ingo Swann is the kind of person who ruins a clean history.

If the early SRI program had recruited anonymous subjects from a psychology pool, remote viewing might have become a narrow argument about experimental design. Swann arrived with a personality, a history in psychical research, artistic talent, unusual claims, and enough confidence to make the laboratory itself part of the story. He was not a passive human detector waiting to be characterized by physicists. He helped shape what the researchers thought the phenomenon was.

This is important because remote viewing later acquired the aura of a discovered technology, as if Puthoff and Targ had found an unknown sensory organ and measured it. The historical record is more interactive. Subjects, investigators, sponsors, and protocols evolved together.

A later government review of the SRI program says this almost too plainly. Research in remote viewing, it notes, followed earlier SRI work in psychokinesis and more conventional clairvoyance. The major impetus came when Swann suggested that the experiments could be made more interesting because, as the review quotes him, he could “look anywhere in the world” if given latitude and longitude.

The first coordinate experiments were run, the review says, largely to placate him.

Then they appeared to work well enough that the laboratory built a more controlled protocol around the idea.

That sequence changes the intellectual ownership of the method.

The coordinate was not merely an experimental variable selected by Puthoff to eliminate descriptive cueing. It began as part of the subject’s own model of his alleged ability.

That can be productive.

A subject may understand the phenomenology of a rare skill better than an outside investigator. Elite athletes help design tests of movement. Expert meditators help researchers distinguish experiences that naïve questionnaires blur together. Radar operators know what a transient looks like on their own screens. Skilled observers can tell scientists which manipulations destroy the task before the scientists understand why.

But co-design changes the epistemology.

If the person whose ability is under test also helps define the method that elicits the ability, then a successful protocol may be measuring a trainable interaction between person, expectations, experimenter language, and judging procedure rather than a fixed human faculty waiting to be discovered.

Swann had already participated in experiments before arriving at SRI. He claimed forms of perception and influence that exceeded ordinary senses. Puthoff was open enough to test him. The famous early magnetometer episode followed. Whatever one thinks of the interpretation, it established a working relationship in which Swann was not merely asked whether he could perform a fixed laboratory task. He was treated as somebody whose subjective reports might help design the task.

The magnetometer episode itself became part of the program’s founding myth, but the surviving technical account is worth reading because it shows how quickly a single anomaly can acquire theoretical weight.

Puthoff and Targ later described the instrument as a superconducting quantum interference device magnetometer located in a well under a building, protected by multiple layers of shielding including superconducting niobium. A decaying field inside the instrument produced a calibration signal on a recorder. The researchers reported that when Swann placed his attention on the interior, the output changed.

That is the observation as reported.

The interpretation is another matter.

The apparatus was sensitive by design. A sensitive instrument is valuable because it records small perturbations. It is dangerous because every small perturbation now demands explanation. A signal can be physical without being paranormal. A shield can be excellent without being absolute. An experiment can be honest without being diagnostic.

The first task after an anomaly is not to name the new force.

It is to inventory every ordinary route into the instrument.

Temperature.

Mechanical vibration.

Electrical coupling.

Magnetic contamination.

Power fluctuations.

Recorder behavior.

Human movement.

Timing coincidence.

Instrument recovery from its own prior state.

The stronger the shielding, the more interesting a surviving disturbance becomes. But strong shielding does not abolish the need for replication under a protocol designed after the disturbance has been seen.

The government’s later program review is surprisingly candid about the next conceptual leap. Once Swann’s coordinate suggestion produced results SRI regarded as successful, the researchers enlarged the work, used additional subjects, varied target distances, investigated predictors of success, and eventually built training around the method.

That is no longer merely a test.

It is technology development.

Swann’s role in coordinate remote viewing illustrates the ambiguity.

The idea was strange even by parapsychology standards. Give a person geographic coordinates and ask for information about the location. Latitude and longitude do not physically resemble a site. If the subject succeeds under proper blinding, coordinates should function only as labels, not as information-bearing descriptions. This makes them attractive experimentally. It also makes any success seem more dramatic because ordinary inference from the cue is minimized.

The arbitrariness bothered government reviewers too.

A later SRI program review said that if coordinate remote viewing were real, the coordinate cue made the process almost impossible to understand rationally. The numbers’ geographic significance is unknown to a viewer who does not consult a map, yet the method supposedly allows them to produce spatial information. The reviewers noted that the arbitrariness of the approach had not escaped the investigators.

That is a deeper problem than mechanism.

A coordinate might simply be a task label—an arbitrary token around which the viewer organizes attention. If so, perhaps any unique random identifier would work. If only geographic coordinates work, something about the mapping system itself would seem to matter despite its human convention. Those alternatives make different predictions.

This is the sort of basic experiment the mythology tends to skip.

Replace the coordinate with a random number unknown to everyone in the session.

Replace it with a word generated after target selection.

Use deliberately false coordinates mapped by a hidden table to real targets.

Give different viewers different arbitrary identifiers for the same target.

If performance is unchanged, the coordinate is probably not carrying geographic content. It is a ritualized index.

If performance collapses, the claim becomes stranger and more specific.

The government loved the implication either way.

Intelligence work is full of targets that are remote, denied, classified, buried, guarded, or inaccessible. A human ability that could obtain even crude descriptive information without physical penetration would be strategically extraordinary. It would change the meaning of a secure room.

That strategic value is why mediocre evidence could rationally attract attention.

Imagine an intelligence manager in 1973 assigning prior probabilities. The chance that paranormal perception exists may be very low. The payoff if it does exist is enormous. The cost of a small contract at SRI is tiny compared with a satellite program, submarine, or reconnaissance aircraft. You do not need belief to justify the experiment. You need asymmetry.

The archive shows that sponsors did more than read published claims. Government personnel became involved in tests and evaluations. Declassified SRI reports describe sponsor-controlled target selection and attempts to protect target information. The later folklore often compresses this into the CIA proved remote viewing. The actual fact is both stronger and weaker.

Stronger: intelligence personnel really did participate. The program was not invented afterward by memoirists.

Weaker: sponsor participation does not eliminate every source of bias, and an intelligence officer’s amazement is not a replication.

Swann’s most famous operational-style episodes include descriptions associated with distant sites and later training protocols. But the Semipalatinsk case most often cited from the 1970s was Pat Price’s, not Swann’s. Keeping the subjects straight matters because popular retellings frequently pool gifted viewers into one composite capability.

The Soviet target at Semipalatinsk became famous because Price’s drawings were said to correspond to a giant crane and large spherical structures. A declassified evaluator found the crane sketch so impressive that he considered remote viewing or an unlikely information-compromise scenario rather than easy coincidence.

That tells us something important about the environment Swann was helping create.

A few sessions could transform the sponsor’s emotional baseline.

Once a person with access to the target saw what looked like a highly specific hit, the next experiment no longer began in a neutral room. The subject had become someone who might be able to do this. The scientists had become people who might have found something. The sponsor had become the organization that might possess a strategic capability.

Expectancy entered the system even if nobody cheated.

That is either a strength or a methodological hazard.

In conventional instrument development, the sensor does not argue with the engineer. A photodiode does not say that the target definition feels wrong. A human subject does. The subject may have genuine introspective knowledge of a skill. The subject may also build a theory from expectation, feedback, and selective success. When the claimed ability has no agreed external mechanism, the boundary between discovering a protocol and teaching a performance can become hard to see.

This problem intensified when Swann moved from subject toward trainer.

A later government SRI review says the training program developed over several years through Swann’s dedicated participation. It credits him with dissecting the perceptual process into components and says roughly a dozen trainees had completed instruction at various claimed levels of competence by the time of the review.

That is a remarkable role reversal.

The test subject became part of the apparatus.

If training produced better performance under clean blind scoring, that would be powerful evidence for a learnable skill. But training also creates shared vocabulary, expectations, response formats, and ways of handling uncertainty. A trained viewer may become better at producing data that a trained monitor knows how to parse even if no paranormal information channel exists.

The correct test therefore has to cross social systems.

Train viewers one way.

Have an independent laboratory generate targets.

Use judges who do not know the training doctrine.

Score prospectively with criteria fixed before sessions begin.

Then move the whole protocol to a group that has never worked with Swann, Puthoff, or Targ.

A technology becomes a technology when strangers can make it work.

The government record suggests that some sponsors were explicitly interested in that question. Later Army programs trained personnel. Visiting sponsor staff sometimes participated as viewers. SRI reports claimed that unselected people could perform above chance under some conditions.

The evidence never became clean enough to end the argument.

Swann’s presence made the problem more acute because he produced narratives, sketches, and claims that could be interpreted at multiple levels of specificity. A transcript might contain many impressions: shapes, textures, temperatures, motion, structures, colors, terrain. After the target is revealed, some elements will correspond and others will not. The evaluation method determines whether the impressive pieces dominate memory or whether the whole output is scored.

Targ and Puthoff knew this. Their published work used blind judging in an attempt to formalize comparison. But early operational stories continued to circulate because they were better narratives than rank-order statistics.

Pat Price, another central SRI subject, intensified the effect. Price had been a police commissioner and businessman and was described by the researchers as a gifted remote viewer. In one oft-repeated case involving a sensitive U.S. facility, he allegedly provided details that impressed intelligence sponsors deeply. Some later accounts say he produced code words or internal labels. The surviving record contains enough official discussion to establish that sponsors considered some of Price’s output extraordinary. It does not grant us omniscient access to exactly what was known, cued, verified, or remembered at each stage.

This is where biography gets dangerous.

Puthoff had firsthand access to sessions that later readers encounter only through reports, redactions, summaries, memoirs, and arguments. If he believed he had seen performances not explained by ordinary means, that belief is biographically consequential even if the modern reader remains unconvinced. A scientist’s later research choices can be rational relative to experiences that the public cannot fully audit.

That does not mean the public should inherit the conclusion.

Private evidence is allowed to change the witness. It is not allowed to change everybody else without transmission loss.

This principle matters far beyond remote viewing. Decades later, UAP debates would repeatedly feature officials and contractors saying, in effect, I have seen classified information that would change your mind. Perhaps. The listener still cannot inspect it. The correct public posture is neither automatic belief nor automatic accusation of lying. It is to identify the claim as testimony constrained by unavailable evidence.

Puthoff’s life repeatedly lives inside that gap.

Swann also complicates the standard story of scientific hierarchy. He was not credentialed like Puthoff, but he appears to have driven conceptual development. The scientists supplied instrumentation and institutional access; the subject supplied a theory of practice. This reversed the expected flow of expertise.

Science can benefit from such reversals. Skilled operators often know things theoreticians do not. A machinist hears a failure before an engineer sees it on a plot. An athlete knows distinctions in movement that physiology later formalizes. A meditation practitioner can report stable phenomenology worth studying. Expertise does not require a Ph.D.

The danger appears when subjective expertise begins defining its own validation criteria.

If a subject says a miss occurred because the target was poorly chosen, because the interviewer interfered, because the viewer was tired, because the feedback arrived incorrectly, or because skeptical observation suppresses the effect, each explanation may be possible. Together they can make the hypothesis nearly impossible to defeat. Puthoff’s later remote-viewing methods tried to handle some of this with structured procedures, but the underlying tension never disappeared.

The best experimental design for an anomalous claim is not merely one that produces hits. It is one that can embarrass the claim.

This is the lesson hidden in the Swann story.

The early SRI team is often praised for being willing to look. That praise is deserved more than critics sometimes admit. Somebody had to convert anecdotes into trials if the claim was ever going to become scientific. Laughing at a subject is not a control condition.

But willingness to look is only the first scientific virtue. The second is willingness to arrange the test so that the thing you hope to see can fail in a way you cannot reinterpret.

Remote viewing’s history is a long argument about whether SRI ever achieved enough of that second virtue.

Swann’s contribution made the protocol richer, but it also bound the research to gifted-subject models. If spectacular performance depends on rare individuals, replication becomes harder. If ordinary people can be trained, training itself becomes another uncontrolled variable. If a judge scores free-response material, the judge’s expectations matter. If the target is emotionally or visually distinctive, target-set structure matters. If feedback is provided between trials, learning and cueing pathways multiply.

The problem becomes less like testing a photodiode and more like evaluating a conversation between several adaptive systems.

That is not a reason to stop.

It is a reason to demand more from the evidence.

Puthoff did not retreat from the difficulty. He did what would become characteristic: he built a larger research program around it.

Funding followed. Russell Targ joined. Reports accumulated. Government visitors watched demonstrations. The vocabulary stabilized. Remote viewing moved from a phrase inside a laboratory to a capability category inside parts of the national-security bureaucracy.

This transition is the first major event in Puthoff’s biography.

He did not prove that a human mind can see anywhere on Earth.

He helped turn one subject’s extraordinary claim into a protocol, a training system, and a government research problem.

That achievement is historically real even if the underlying faculty remains disputed.
