# Chapter 9 — Twenty-Three Years Later

The remote-viewing program lasted long enough to become several programs.

That is one reason its history is continually mangled.

People use “Stargate” as if it names a single CIA project running from the early 1970s to the mid-1990s under one chain of command. The declassified record is messier. Sponsorship moved among agencies and military organizations. Program names changed. Research and operational activities were not always housed together. Puthoff was central to the early SRI work but was not the scientist running every later phase.

A biography has to resist the gravitational pull of the best-known label.

By the time CIA commissioned the 1995 evaluation, Puthoff had been gone from SRI for roughly a decade. Edwin May had become a central research figure. The government’s final judgment belonged to a mature program with methods and institutional history extending well beyond the famous 1970s experiments.

Why include the ending in a book about Puthoff?

Because endings test origin stories.

A research program can look brilliant at inception and disappointing after scale. A startup can impress ten customers and fail at ten thousand. A medicine can produce encouraging early signals and fail a multicenter trial. A military prototype can perform in demonstrations and become too fragile for the field.

Remote viewing’s long run was a scale test.

The question was no longer whether Ingo Swann or Pat Price could produce a remarkable session. It was whether a trainable, governable, repeatable human capability existed strongly enough to survive personnel changes, procedural changes, targets selected by sponsors, operational uncertainty, and skeptical review.

The answer never hardened into consensus.

That failure to harden is evidence in itself.

Not proof of fraud. Not proof of nonexistence. Evidence about effect size, stability, and reproducibility.

The 1995 review is worth reading slowly because the standard summaries usually erase its design. The CIA did not ask one famous skeptic to walk into a room and announce whether psychic functioning was ridiculous. It contracted with the American Institutes for Research, a large nonprofit research organization, to evaluate both the research program and its operational intelligence applications. AIR then built disagreement into the review panel.

Jessica Utts was a statistician with deep familiarity with parapsychology and the remote-viewing database. Ray Hyman was a psychologist, methodologist, and long-standing critic of paranormal claims. AIR added senior scientists of its own who did not come to the assignment from either camp.

This matters because the final report contains something rarer than a verdict: a controlled collision of standards.

Utts looked at the later experimental database and concluded that the statistical evidence had become too strong to dismiss as chance. Hyman did not simply say the numbers were fake. He accepted that portions of the database contained unusual statistical results. His objection was inferential. A statistical departure from a null model does not tell you what caused the departure. If experimental dependencies, laboratory-specific practices, judging procedures, subject selection, analytical flexibility, or other artifacts remain plausible, the word paranormal has not yet been earned.

The distinction sounds almost pedantic until you replace remote viewing with medicine.

Suppose patients in one clinic recover at a rate unlikely under a simple null model. That is a result. It is not yet a drug mechanism. Maybe the treatment works. Maybe the clinic selected unusual patients. Maybe outcomes were measured differently. Maybe a co-intervention mattered. Maybe the analysis treated dependent observations as independent. The statistical anomaly justifies work. It does not tell you which story won.

Utts and Hyman therefore disagreed at exactly the point where many public arguments pretend there is no disagreement left.

One side says: the odds against chance are enormous, therefore psychic functioning is established.

The other says: if the experiment is not a clean causal discriminator, an impressive p-value can measure the stability of a methodological feature instead of a new human faculty.

Those are not interchangeable complaints. They imply different next experiments.

The AIR report also separated laboratory research from intelligence operations. This is the part of the history most often buried beneath the anecdotes.

An intelligence agency does not ultimately care whether a judge can assign an above-chance score to a session transcript. It cares whether a collection method improves decisions before the answer is known. Can the viewer describe the facility accurately enough that analysts learn something they did not already know? Can the method tell an operator which lead to follow? Can users distinguish a good session from a bad one in real time? Can the output be specific enough to act on without allowing an analyst to match broad imagery after the fact?

A capability can be statistically interesting and operationally useless.

Weather forecasting provides a useful control. A model that beats climatology by a modest amount may be scientifically meaningful. But if it cannot reliably tell an airport whether icing will occur in the next hour, its operational value for that decision is low. Intelligence collection has the same cruelty. “Sometimes striking” is not a mission specification.

AIR’s operational review examined tasking and user experience rather than treating the famous sessions as representative by definition. Its conclusion was bad for the program in a way that no metaphysical argument could repair: the information was not sufficiently reliable or specific for intelligence operations. Users could not know prospectively when the product was correct. The material generally required interpretation and supplementation from ordinary intelligence sources.

That last feature is lethal to the strongest version of the claim.

If remote viewing is valuable only after analysts already possess enough conventional information to recognize the correct pieces, then some of the apparent value can be generated by the analyst rather than the viewer. The output becomes a field of statements from which a knowledgeable reader extracts matches.

This does not mean every impressive session dissolves. It means usefulness needs a denominator.

How many operational tasks were run?

How many produced information later rated useful?

How specific was the information?

How often was it wrong?

How often did a customer act differently because of it?

How often did a “hit” become visible only once the target was revealed?

Anecdotal history is allergic to denominators because a denominator makes the famous case smaller.

The remote-viewing literature has an additional problem. Failure is boring.

A session in which a viewer produces generic forms, colors, water, structures, motion, heat, distance, and texture but misses the target does not become a documentary. Nobody remembers the briefing where the analyst says there is no useful correlation. Yet the declassified archive contains exactly such records. Some are blunt enough to record no target correlation.

That is not embarrassing debris to be swept away from the interesting material. It is the control group history forgot to narrate.

Puthoff was no longer directing the work by the time many of these records accumulated, which makes the ending fairer rather than less relevant. The later program inherited a proposition he helped make institutionally credible. It had years to improve protocols, move sponsors, change managers, train subjects, incorporate criticism, and accumulate experience. If the effect was large and robust, time was its ally.

Instead, the final record became more sophisticated without becoming decisive.

Utts could point to a statistical pattern she regarded as conclusive evidence for anomalous cognition. Hyman could agree that something needed explanation and still reject the paranormal inference. Operational users could remain unimpressed enough that AIR did not recommend carrying remote viewing forward as an intelligence capability.

That is a much stranger ending than “the CIA proved psychic spying” or “the CIA debunked psychic spying.”

A government program can fail one threshold while leaving another unresolved.

Laboratory anomaly: arguable.

Causal interpretation: disputed.

Operational instrument: not established.

Mechanism: absent.

Those four lines are a better epitaph than any slogan.

A robust phenomenon generally becomes easier to isolate as tools improve. The history of electromagnetism is full of confusing early observations, but the signals became cleaner, not more dependent on interpretation. Germ theory survived because organisms could be cultured, stained, transmitted, sequenced, and linked to mechanisms. Continental drift became plate tectonics because seafloor mapping, paleomagnetism, earthquake distribution, and geodesy converged.

Remote viewing did not accumulate that kind of independent convergence.

Its strongest proponents continued to point to statistical datasets, selected operations, and trained viewers. Its critics continued to point to methodological fragility, replication questions, sensory leakage, target dependence, and the gap between laboratory scoring and real-world usefulness. A mechanism did not arrive to organize the evidence.

This distinction matters when remote viewing is compared with once-ridiculed science.

Every fringe field loves Galileo.

Galileo is a poor control group.

Most ridiculed claims do not become heliocentrism. Most anomalies do not become new physics. The lesson of scientific history is not that outsiders are eventually vindicated. It is that some claims survive increasingly hostile tests.

Puthoff’s life becomes more interesting if remote viewing is allowed to remain a partial failure.

He helped create a program that forced government agencies to investigate a taboo claim seriously. That is historically consequential even if the phenomenon never became an instrument.

He also demonstrated an institutional skill that would recur: creating enough technical structure around an implausible question that serious sponsors could fund inquiry without publicly committing to the answer.

This may sound bureaucratic. Bureaucratic achievement is underrated.

Ideas do not get tested merely because they are true or false. They need a room, equipment, personnel, contracts, reporting language, risk tolerance, and somebody able to explain the question in terms a budget owner can defend.

Puthoff was good at building that bridge.

The bridge itself can become misleading. Once a government organization has funded an inquiry, the existence of the contract enters popular culture as evidence for the proposition. Twenty years of expenditure becomes “twenty years of proof.” Declassified documents become talismans.

But agencies investigate adversary capabilities they do not believe exist. Militaries build red teams around unlikely scenarios. Intelligence services pay for forecasts that later fail. DARPA has funded high-risk programs that did not work. NASA has studied concepts that never flew.

Investigation means uncertainty was worth reducing.

That sentence should be printed on the cover of every book about classified fringe research.

The 1995 review is also useful because it exposes a recurring conflict between statistical and mechanistic science.

Suppose a dataset repeatedly produces an effect above chance under controls that appear adequate. A statistician may reasonably say the null hypothesis is inadequate. A physicist may reasonably ask what process could carry the information. A psychologist may ask whether the scoring model captures hidden dependence. An intelligence officer may ask whether any of this helps find the submarine.

These are different questions with different thresholds.

Puthoff’s remote-viewing career was strongest at the first threshold: producing observations and analyses difficult enough to keep research alive.

It was weakest at the last: stable, prospective operational utility.

That pattern may have shaped his transition out of SRI.

In Austin, Puthoff founded the Institute for Advanced Studies at Austin and later EarthTech International. Corporate biographies date his leadership there to the mid-1980s. The new institutional setting was smaller, private, and more directly controlled by him.

He had left a large contract-research institute and built an edge laboratory.

That change mattered.

At SRI, unusual work existed inside an organization with many unrelated programs. In Austin, unusual work became closer to the mission. Puthoff could choose problems whose scientific risk would have made them difficult to host elsewhere.

One of those problems was the electromagnetic vacuum.

Vacuum physics is a dangerous subject for popular imagination because the underlying science is genuinely strange.

Quantum field theory does not describe empty space as classical nothingness. Ground states have fluctuations. The Casimir effect demonstrates measurable consequences associated with boundary conditions and quantum fields. Lamb shifts and spontaneous emission belong to a physical framework in which “vacuum” is not simply absence.

From there, bad reasoning can move very quickly.

If vacuum fluctuations are real, perhaps energy can be extracted without limit.

If the vacuum participates in inertia, perhaps inertia can be engineered.

If gravity and vacuum structure are connected, perhaps spacetime can be manipulated.

Each step requires new evidence. The reality of the first does not grant the next.

Puthoff’s later technical work would live exactly along this chain.

The transition can look like a retreat from psychic research into respectable physics. That description is too simple. Stochastic electrodynamics and zero-point interpretations are themselves minority approaches compared with mainstream quantum theory, though they use real mathematics and engage real phenomena. Advanced propulsion moves farther toward speculative engineering. UAP interpretation later adds another layer of uncertain observation.

The common thread is not subject matter.

It is the edge.

Puthoff repeatedly chooses questions where established physics ends before the hoped-for application begins.

That is intellectually risky territory. It is also where science fiction and engineering proposals can become difficult to distinguish unless the writer is merciless about levels of evidence.

A theory can be mathematically interesting without being correct.

A correct theory can describe an effect too small to exploit.

An exploitable effect can fail economically.

A patent can exist without a working device.

A sponsor can fund all of the above without possessing secret proof.

The remote-viewing program had taught, whether anyone intended it or not, what happens when these levels collapse.

The next phase of Puthoff’s life would repeat the lesson with physics instead of perception.
