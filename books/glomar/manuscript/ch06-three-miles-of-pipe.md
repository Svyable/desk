# Three Miles of Pipe

The operation can be made to sound simple by using the verb lift.

Lower a capture vehicle. Grab the submarine. Lift it.

Almost everything difficult disappears inside that verb.

CIA’s public history describes a system in which sixty-foot sections of steel pipe were added one at a time as the capture vehicle descended. The ship had to maintain position while a mechanical assembly traveled toward a target roughly 16,500 feet below. Then the process had to reverse. Pipe sections came back aboard one after another while the captured load rose through miles of moving water.

The pipe string was not a rope. It was structure, conduit, alignment problem, and accumulated mass. Every connection mattered. Loads changed as the capture vehicle and target moved. The ship above could not simply drift with wind and current. The geometry had to remain controlled enough for a massive device to meet a damaged submarine on a seabed no person could directly supervise.

This is where AZORIAN belongs in the history of engineering rather than only intelligence.

The operation needed precision station keeping at a scale familiar today from offshore drilling but extraordinary in the period. It needed heavy-lift machinery capable of handling repeated pipe operations. It needed a ship designed around a central opening rather than ordinary cargo handling. It needed structural capacity for loads that would change in ways difficult to model perfectly because the target itself had spent years on the ocean floor.

The temptation in retrospective accounts is to turn unprecedented into miraculous. The engineers did not abolish mechanics. They accumulated enough ordinary disciplines—naval architecture, drilling practice, hydraulics, metallurgy, control, fabrication, offshore operations—that the combined system could attempt something no previous salvage project had attempted at that depth.

This matters because secrecy can distort technological history. Classified programs sometimes appear to leap ahead because the public does not see the incremental work that produced them. Later revelation collapses years of design into one dramatic photograph.

AZORIAN’s engineering is more impressive when returned to sequence. Problems were identified, contractors were recruited, alternatives were assessed, designs changed, hardware was built, tests were run, and political authorization continued while the machine slowly became real.

Nothing about three miles of pipe guarantees three miles of control.

That gap—between length and control—is where much of the engineering lives.
