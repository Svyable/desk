# What Was Actually Recovered

The most seductive sentence in any intelligence history begins with supposedly.

Supposedly AZORIAN recovered code books. Supposedly it recovered torpedoes. Supposedly it recovered more than CIA admits. Supposedly the official account understates success to preserve sources and methods.

Some of those propositions may be true. They do not become documented because they fit the incentives of secrecy.

The evidentiary discipline of this book is therefore asymmetrical. CIA's acknowledgment of partial recovery, the six bodies, the existence of the ship and capture system, the planning record, and the White House debates can be treated as documented. Participant recollections and journalistic reconstructions can be cited as testimony or reporting. Specific claims that remain unacknowledged stay in a separate class.

This is not timidity. It is the subject.

AZORIAN created the very problem that later made Glomar famous: how should outsiders reason when the government may possess information but refuses to say whether it does?

The danger runs in both directions. Official silence can tempt believers to enlarge a story indefinitely. It can also tempt skeptics to treat every unacknowledged claim as false. Neither move is justified. Absence of acknowledgment is not proof of absence when acknowledgment itself is controlled.

The practical solution is to build a claim ledger.

Documented: K-129 was located; CIA led a recovery program; Hughes supplied cover; Glomar Explorer was purpose-built; a capture vehicle raised part of the wreck; the target fractured; a portion was recovered; six Soviet sailors were recovered and buried; another mission was planned; media exposure and Soviet attention contributed to cancellation.

Credible but incomplete: participant accounts of specific recovered artifacts and internal assessments not fully released.

Disputed: claims that the operation recovered particular strategic materials in quantities or condition not independently confirmed.

Speculative: theories that public descriptions of failure were themselves a cover for much larger success.

The categories do not settle the mystery. They prevent mystery from becoming evidence.
