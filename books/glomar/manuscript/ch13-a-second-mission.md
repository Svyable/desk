# A Second Mission

Partial failure did not end AZORIAN. It created a second target.

The section that fell back to the seabed still contained whatever intelligence value had justified the first effort. Planning began for another recovery. Later records refer to the proposed follow-on as MATADOR.

This decision is revealing because it supplies an internal vote on value. Organizations often celebrate failed operations publicly while abandoning them privately. AZORIAN produced serious planning for another attempt.

A second mission, however, faced a different environment. The first voyage had benefited from surprise. The ship now had a history. Contractors had accumulated records. Journalists had heard rumors. A burglary involving Hughes-associated offices had generated investigative attention. Soviet curiosity could sharpen.

Secrecy degrades with repetition.

Every additional operation creates more observations that can be compared. A ship returning to the same remote patch of ocean is harder to explain as exploratory mining. A cover that works once may fail statistically: the pattern itself becomes information.

This is where operational security collides with sunk infrastructure. Glomar Explorer existed. The capture technology existed. Skilled crews and procedures existed. Abandoning the second mission would leave extraordinary capability unused. Repeating the mission would increase exposure risk.

The political decision therefore moved away from pure engineering. The question was no longer can the machine work? The first mission had answered that enough to justify another attempt. The question became can the story still work?

For a time Washington believed it could.

Then the cover began to tear from an unexpected direction.
