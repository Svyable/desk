# Neither Confirm Nor Deny

There is a category of information that becomes more informative when you answer a question about whether it exists.

Suppose a requester asks CIA for records of an unacknowledged relationship with a foreign service. If CIA says no records exist when the relationship does not exist, and invokes exemptions when it does, the pattern itself becomes a disclosure mechanism. Over enough requests, denial language can map hidden associations.

The Glomar response solves that inference problem by collapsing existence and nonexistence into the same public answer.

CIA used that posture after the Glomar story broke. The name stuck because the dispute over records of the operation produced litigation. What began as a specific national-security maneuver became a general administrative technique.

Modern Justice Department guidance describes a Glomar response as appropriate when even confirming or denying the existence of records would harm an interest protected by a FOIA exemption. The doctrine now appears far beyond submarines: intelligence relationships, law-enforcement files, privacy interests, investigations, and other sensitive contexts.

This is the second machine AZORIAN built.

Unlike the ship, it is cheap. No steel. No hydraulic system. No moon pool. It requires legal predicates, declarations, judicial deference within limits, and disciplined consistency.

Its elegance is the same as the ocean-mining cover. Observers are not prevented from seeing everything. They are prevented from obtaining one authoritative interpretation.

The tension with transparency is obvious. FOIA begins from access. Glomar creates a circumstance in which ordinary processing itself can disclose protected information. The response therefore withholds not only documents but the fact of whether responsive documents exist.

That is a powerful exception and an easy one to caricature. It is neither unlimited nor imaginary. Courts require agencies to tether Glomar to statutory exemptions, and official acknowledgment can defeat the posture when the government itself has already disclosed the precise fact at issue.

The paradox remains: a transparency law helped formalize a language for refusing to say whether there is anything to be transparent about.
