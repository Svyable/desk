# The Interrogation Machine

## How the Search for Mind Control Built a Science of Breaking People

Sven Hardy Benson

There are two bad ways to write about MKULTRA.

The first is to laugh. The second is to believe everything.

The laugh turns a documented intelligence program into Cold War camp: LSD, hypnotists, spies, prostitutes, poisoned cocktails, a few men in bad suits asking whether a person could be made to forget his own name. The belief turns the same record into proof that somewhere behind the redactions the switch was found, the human mind was solved, and history has been run ever since by technicians who know where to press.

Both versions are convenient because neither requires us to stay with the paperwork.

This book stays with the paperwork.

Its evidence is divided into four classes. **Documented facts** are supported by declassified records, official investigations, contemporaneous memoranda, sworn testimony, or strong primary scientific literature. **Credible but incomplete evidence** includes attributed testimony and surviving records whose context, completeness, or operational result cannot be fully reconstructed. **Disputed allegations** include claims of successful programmable mind control, operational capabilities, hidden continuations, and particular experiments that cannot be independently established from the surviving record. **Speculative synthesis** is mine: the argument that the most consequential product of the mind-control search may have been not control but a practical vocabulary of human breakdown.

That last claim has to earn itself. Similarities between an old research program and later interrogation practice do not establish a secret lineage. A technique can be rediscovered. Institutions can converge on the same ugly idea. A document can be influential without being causal. The book will therefore distinguish documentary transmission from resemblance.

Patents, proposals and research objectives are evidence that somebody wanted a capability. They are not evidence that the capability worked. Testimony is evidence that a person made a claim. It is not proof of the claim. Classification explains why evidence may be missing; it does not transform missing evidence into positive evidence.

There is another problem particular to this history. In 1973, CIA Director Richard Helms ordered MKULTRA files destroyed. Later investigators recovered a surviving cache of financial records and reconstructed portions of the program. The surviving archive is therefore neither nothing nor the whole. That asymmetry matters. It limits what can be closed, but it also creates a temptation to pour anything we want into the burned space.

I will not do that.

The question is narrower and, I think, worse: what did serious people learn while trying to build a machine for the human will?
