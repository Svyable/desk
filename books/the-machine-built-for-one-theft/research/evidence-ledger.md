# Evidence ledger — The Machine Built for One Theft

Checked 2026-09-15. Public, lawfully available sources only.

## Primary / authoritative spine

### CIA — Project AZORIAN museum history
https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/exhibit/project-azorian/

CIA states that K-129 was a Soviet Golf II ballistic-missile submarine; the United States located it roughly 16,500 feet down; CIA led a recovery effort with DoD support; engineers selected a surface heavy-lift approach; Hughes supplied the commercial deep-sea-mining cover; the ship used a derrick, pipe-transfer crane, docking legs, capture vehicle and moon pool; the 1974 lift recovered only a portion after the target broke apart; six Soviet submariners were recovered and buried at sea; press exposure preceded cancellation of a second recovery; CIA characterizes the project as an engineering advance in deep-ocean mining and heavy lift.

**Class:** 1 for program existence, architecture and CIA's stated chronology. CIA's retrospective judgment of intelligence value is an institutional claim, not an independent measure of yield.

### CIA — The Exposing of Project AZORIAN (2020)
https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/the-exposing-of-project-azorian/

Useful for the exposure sequence: June 1974 Hughes-office burglary; stolen documents tying Hughes to CIA/Glomar; FBI and LAPD involvement; press rumors; Los Angeles Times publication February 7, 1975; Jack Anderson broadcast March 18; Soviet guarding of the recovery site by late June; cancellation of further recovery.

**Class:** 1 for CIA's documented account; independent press and archival reconstruction still required for event-level chronology and causal claims.

### CIA — Honoring the Mission of Project AZORIAN (2022)
https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/honoring-the-mission-of-project-azorian/

Interview/context with engineer Sherman Wetmore. Useful as attributed participant recollection, especially contractor/Agency culture and the unresolved mechanical failure.

**Class:** 2 where based on retrospective recollection; do not turn memory into engineering telemetry.

### CIA — Cold War Spy Crane Aids Disaster Recovery Today (2024)
https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/cold-war-spy-crane-aids-disaster-recovery-today/

Documents Sun 800, an 800-ton floating crane built in 1972 to help construct Glomar Explorer, including installation of the roughly 630-ton gimbal platform. CIA identifies the gimbal and heave compensator as key feasibility technologies. The same crane later participated in Francis Scott Key Bridge wreckage removal.

**Class:** 1 for the documented artifact/history; useful concrete bridge between covert engineering and later ordinary infrastructure.

### Naval History and Heritage Command — Navy Non-Combat Submarine Losses
https://www.history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-019/h-019-3.html

NHHC records K-129's 8 March 1968 loss and the 1974 partially successful AZORIAN recovery from roughly 16,000 feet.

**Class:** 1; independent U.S. naval historical corroboration.

## Contradictions and incentive audit

**CIA retrospective prestige.** CIA has an incentive to remember AZORIAN as audacious and successful even though the physical recovery was partial. Treat “greatest intelligence coups” language as institutional self-assessment. The book's interesting unit is not whether the Agency awards itself a trophy but what was built, what came up, what failed, and what became public.

**Press incentives.** Exposure rewarded sensational framing. Jack Anderson reportedly argued that Navy experts considered the target low-value. That claim must be reconstructed from the broadcast/transcript and compared with internal intelligence objectives rather than repeated as either vindication or debunking.

**Soviet/Russian retrospective incentives.** Post-Cold War accounts may carry institutional pride, embarrassment, incomplete archive access, or narrative repair. Separate contemporaneous Soviet observations from memoir.

**Contractor secrecy.** Contractors had both security obligations and later incentives to tell an extraordinary engineering story. Participant testimony is valuable for process but must be checked against drawings, reports, dates and hardware.

**Retrieval mythology.** AZORIAN is unusually tempting in contemporary UAP discourse because it demonstrates an actual secret recovery program, purpose-built infrastructure, private contractors, extreme compartmentation and foreign technology exploitation. The analogy is legitimate only at the level of institutional capability and secrecy mechanics. It is not evidence that any alleged non-human retrieval occurred.

## Chronology

- **8 Mar 1968:** K-129 lost in northern Pacific.
- **1968:** U.S. locates wreck after Soviet search fails, according to official U.S. histories.
- **1970:** CIA says engineering study settles on mechanical capture/heavy lift.
- **1972:** Sun 800 crane built; later used in Glomar construction.
- **1970–74:** Glomar Explorer and recovery system designed/built under Hughes deep-sea-mining cover.
- **Jun 1974:** burglary at Hughes/Summa office begins exposure chain.
- **4 Jul 1974:** Glomar Explorer arrives over recovery site; covert salvage follows.
- **1974 lift:** target breaks; only a portion reaches moon pool.
- **7 Feb 1975:** Los Angeles Times publishes linkage among Hughes, CIA and Glomar.
- **18 Mar 1975:** Jack Anderson broadcasts nationally on operation.
- **Late Jun 1975:** CIA account says Soviets have assigned a vessel to guard recovery site; follow-on recovery canceled.
- **1992:** Robert Gates gives Boris Yeltsin film of burial ceremony.

## Frozen expansion questions

1. What exactly did USS Halibut photograph, and how did acoustic search, navigation uncertainty and image interpretation converge on K-129?
2. How much of the submarine was the lift system designed to carry, and what were actual versus modeled loads at each stage?
3. What failed mechanically? Distinguish claw/tine failure, hull structural failure, dynamic loading and retrospective participant language.
4. How was the cover embedded in corporate paperwork, port activity, crew stories, scientific language, ship appearance and public ocean-mining discourse?
5. Which contractors knew mission purpose, and which saw only bounded engineering requirements?
6. What Soviet vessels observed Glomar, what did they report, and when did Moscow infer salvage rather than mining?
7. What was actually recovered? Build an item ledger with source, classification status, provenance and confidence.
8. What intelligence objectives remained unmet?
9. How did the Hughes burglary become an operational-security failure? Map every handoff from stolen document to police/FBI activity to journalists.
10. What did the Glomar FOIA litigation actually establish, and how did “neither confirm nor deny” migrate into later state secrecy practice?
11. Which commercial deep-ocean technologies genuinely descended from AZORIAN rather than merely resembling it?
12. What can AZORIAN responsibly teach about modern alleged retrieval programs? Build a feature-by-feature comparison with explicit non-equivalence.

## Publication rule

No chapter may use classification as evidence of extraordinary ontology. No recovered object enters the narrative without a source trail. No patent is evidence that a device worked. No testimony is upgraded into proof because the witness held a clearance. No contemporary retrieval allegation inherits credibility merely because AZORIAN happened.
