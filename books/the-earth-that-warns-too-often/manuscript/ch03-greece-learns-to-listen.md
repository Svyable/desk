# Greece Learns to Listen

The VAN story begins with electrodes in the ground and a suspicion that the crust behaves differently before it breaks.

Panayiotis Varotsos and Kessar Alexopoulos were solid-state physicists, not fortune tellers. That biographical fact does not make their earthquake claims correct, but it helps explain the form those claims took. They approached the problem through electrical properties of matter. If stress altered defects, polarization or charge transport in rocks, then a large stressed volume might generate an electrical signature before mechanical failure.

By the early 1980s, the group had begun reporting transient variations in the Earth's electric field that they associated with later earthquakes. They named the phenomena seismic electric signals. In papers published in *Tectonophysics* in 1984, Varotsos and Alexopoulos described electrical variations they believed preceded earthquakes and argued that characteristics of the signals could help infer epicentral region and magnitude.

The claim was not simply that some electrical disturbance happened somewhere before some earthquakes. VAN developed a system.

Stations measured electric potential differences along pairs of buried electrodes. The geometry mattered. A signal observed on more than one dipole at a station could be compared for consistency. The amplitude, waveform and relation among channels were used to distinguish candidate seismic signals from noise. Over time, the group argued that individual stations displayed 'selectivity': each station tended to respond to earthquakes from particular geographic source regions.

Selectivity became both the method's most interesting physical idea and one of its hardest statistical problems.

A naive model says that an electrical precursor should be largest near the future epicenter and decay smoothly with distance. Real crust is not naive. Conductivity varies. Sedimentary basins, faults, coastlines, fluids and mineralized structures can channel or distort current. A distant station might, in principle, be more strongly coupled to a source region than a closer station. If so, a station's map of sensitive source areas could become an empirical fingerprint of crustal conductivity.

But a selectivity map learned from past earthquakes can also absorb coincidence. If a station records many transients and researchers later notice that a subset precede earthquakes in one region, the region becomes part of the station's inferred selectivity. The next apparent success then depends partly on a rule built from the same class of historical data.

This is not fatal. Almost every practical forecasting system is trained on history. The solution is temporal separation: learn the map on one data set, freeze it, and test it on future earthquakes without adjustment. The argument over VAN repeatedly returned to whether the rules had been fixed tightly enough for that kind of test.

The physical signal itself was also contested. The Earth is an electrically busy place. Telluric currents vary naturally. Geomagnetic disturbances induce electric fields in the ground. Lightning and weather alter electrical conditions. Industrial systems switch on and off. Railways and power lines introduce transients. Electrode contacts age. Moisture changes. A pulse in a voltage record is not self-identifying.

VAN researchers developed recognition criteria and argued that SES could be distinguished from man-made noise by their behavior across channels and stations. Independent researchers sometimes found that anomalous transient electric signals resembling SES could be traced to cultural sources. Later VAN-related work used statistical methods such as detrended fluctuation analysis and natural time analysis to argue for differences between SES activity and artificial signals. Each refinement improved one part of the classification problem while making the operational pipeline more elaborate.

There is a general lesson here. A signal that requires expert interpretation can be scientifically legitimate. Seismologists manually pick arrivals. Radiologists interpret images. Astronomers classify artifacts. But expert interpretation creates another variable. If the same people who believe in a precursor also decide which electrical disturbances count as precursors, the experiment needs unusually strong blinding or external scoring.

The telegram system was meant to address part of that concern.

VAN predictions were transmitted before target earthquakes. During the late 1980s, a multi-year set of predictions was documented and later published. Supporters emphasized the prospective character of those records. Critics accepted that messages had been sent but questioned whether the messages specified the target events narrowly enough to score without later judgment.

Suppose a telegram indicates that a sizable earthquake is expected in one of two broad regions over the next several days, with magnitude around a stated value. When an earthquake occurs, how much deviation is allowed? Does a magnitude 5.6 count if the expected magnitude was 6? Does an earthquake just outside the mapped region count? What if two earthquakes occur? What if the event is an aftershock? How long does an electrical 'activity' episode remain predictive? These choices determine the alarm volume in space, time and magnitude.

The phrase 'alarm volume' is useful because it strips away drama. Prediction quality is not the number of correct-looking matches. It is the fraction of the relevant future space-time-magnitude domain placed under alarm compared with the fraction of target earthquakes captured.

A method that keeps half a country under alarm half the time will predict many earthquakes. Its success rate can still be poor.

This is where the VAN debate moved from geophysics into statistical geometry. Critics such as Francesco Mulargia, Paolo Gasperini, David Jackson, Philip Stark and Yan Kagan argued in different ways that the published prediction record did not establish performance beyond chance under sufficiently objective rules. VAN authors disputed assumptions in those analyses, including how alarm regions were constructed, how seismicity was modeled and how aftershocks were treated.

The argument became unusually public in 1996 when *Geophysical Research Letters* devoted a cluster of papers to the method. Reading those papers now is instructive because neither side behaves like the stereotype.

The skeptics do not simply say 'electrical earthquake signals are impossible.' Jackson explicitly says that candidate methods need quantitative observables and predictions, complete alarm histories and plausible physical links. Kagan focuses on the difficulty of classifying successes and the need for general evaluation strategies. Other researchers examine signal recognition and statistical significance. They are trying to make the claim testable.

VAN's defenders do not simply appeal to authority or secrecy. They contest the statistical models, identify what they regard as misread prediction rules, cite prior publications defining tolerances and argue that physical selectivity and signal properties have been demonstrated. They are trying to keep the test from being designed in a way they believe guarantees failure.

This makes the episode more valuable than a simple debunking story.

A mature science needs adversarial evaluation that both sides would accept before the result is known. VAN never quite reached that point. The meaning of the historical record remained entangled with the evaluation rules.

There were other complications. Greek seismicity is clustered. After a mainshock, aftershocks occur in ways that violate a simple homogeneous Poisson model. Earthquake catalogs contain magnitude uncertainty. Networks change. A predictor may target only events above certain thresholds or only those in a station's selectivity region. Statistical significance can swing depending on how those structures are handled.

Those are real issues. They also illustrate why a prediction system cannot be evaluated by a single headline metric.

The cleanest test is operational: freeze everything, then wait.

That is expensive in earthquake science because the events are rare. If the target is magnitude-six earthquakes within selected regions, a prospective test can require years. Researchers age. Funding cycles end. Sensors fail. The temptation to use historical data is enormous because the archive is already full of earthquakes. Historical evaluation is therefore not useless; it is unavoidable. But it needs a firewall between model construction and testing.

Machine learning would later rediscover the same lesson under different vocabulary: training set, validation set, test set. A test set touched during model development is no longer a test set.

VAN's early work predated the current culture of preregistration and reproducible pipelines. That does not excuse ambiguity, but it helps place the fight historically. Earthquake prediction in the 1970s and 1980s was full of optimism. The United States had invested in prediction research. China had publicized the 1975 Haicheng evacuation as a successful forecast. Soviet, Japanese and European researchers studied a wide range of precursors. The idea that short-term prediction might be achievable was not automatically outside respectable geophysics.

The field hardened later.

One reason was the recurrence of failures. Claimed precursors often did not appear before later earthquakes. Famous positive cases weakened under reanalysis. The predicted Parkfield earthquake arrived years outside its expected window. The social consequences of prediction also became impossible to ignore. Haicheng's story looked less clean when examined closely, and the failure to predict the devastating 1976 Tangshan earthquake underscored how dangerous selective success stories could be.

VAN survived this shift longer than many precursor programs because its proponents continued publishing and because Greece remained seismically active enough to provide cases. The method also evolved. Concepts such as SES activity and natural time analysis expanded the framework beyond the earliest pulse-based claims.

Expansion creates a scientific danger: a theory can become harder to kill as it becomes richer.

This does not mean the added concepts are wrong. It means each new degree of freedom needs its own prospective constraint. If a single pulse can predict one kind of event and an extended activity pattern predicts another, the criteria for distinguishing them must be defined before the earthquake. If natural-time measures identify a critical state after SES activity begins, the threshold and analysis window must be fixed before the target event. Otherwise a framework can absorb every outcome.

The deeper physical question remains worth asking. Could stressed crust produce transient electric signals before rupture?

Yes, in the weak sense that several mechanisms make stress-related electrical phenomena plausible. Laboratory rock fracture produces electrical emissions. Fluid motion can generate streaming potentials. Changes in conductivity and polarization are real. Defect activation in minerals may move charge. None of this establishes VAN's field interpretation.

The distance problem is severe. Signals attributed to SES have sometimes been associated with earthquakes hundreds of kilometers away. Any proposed mechanism must explain how a source at depth produces a surface electric field of the reported amplitude at that distance through conductive, heterogeneous crust. Bernard and others explored whether long-distance electrotelluric observations might be physically plausible under conductive structures. Critics argued that the necessary source strength and geometry were implausible or that actual earthquake-related signals should be easier to detect coseismically than precursor signals.

The coseismic comparison is powerful because rupture supplies a natural calibration. If a station records a large 'precursor' from a distant future earthquake but little or nothing when the fault actually slips, why? There can be answers—different mechanisms, frequency content, transient source geometry—but the asymmetry must be explained rather than ignored.

Parkfield later sharpened this point. Electrical signals were recorded during the 2004 rupture close to the fault, with amplitudes falling toward zero over short distances. No comparable precursor appeared. That result does not logically refute every long-distance SES mechanism in Greece, because geology differs. It does raise the price of the claim.

The cultural-noise problem raises it further. Greece is not electrically empty. Industrial switching, rail systems and power infrastructure can produce pulses. Studies by independent groups traced some SES-like transient signals to human activity and questioned whether VAN criteria excluded them reliably. VAN researchers answered with waveform and statistical distinctions. Again, the right next step is not a debate over visual resemblance. It is blind classification on a shared labeled data set containing known cultural sources, geomagnetic events and candidate SES.

That experiment would be almost embarrassingly simple compared with the scale of the claim.

Give multiple groups the same raw multi-station records with times scrambled. Include periods containing known industrial disturbances, lightning, geomagnetic storms and earthquakes. Require each algorithm to identify candidate SES without knowing when earthquakes occurred. Freeze the classification rules. Only then reveal the earthquake catalog and score the associations against matched null catalogs.

A second stage would run the algorithm live. No human revision. Every alarm registered automatically. Every target earthquake scored by rules published in advance.

This is the experiment the telegrams were reaching toward.

The telegram was a social technology for preregistration before preregistration had a name. It established that the researchers had committed to a claim before the outcome. But commitment is only one dimension. The claim itself has to be precise enough that an independent scorer can determine success.

The VAN controversy therefore belongs to a broader history of science learning how to test itself. Astronomy learned blind analysis in some surveys. Clinical medicine learned trial registration. Particle physics adopted fixed significance conventions and blinded signal regions. Psychology, after its replication crisis, embraced preregistration and held-out data more aggressively. Earthquake prediction had encountered the same problem early because rare events and flexible windows make retrospective success so easy.

One can imagine an alternate history in which VAN's signal classifier had been fully automated in 1985, the selectivity maps digitally frozen, every alarm posted to a public registry, and twenty years of raw station data preserved with calibration logs. The debate might have ended decisively.

Perhaps the method would have failed. Perhaps a small conditional association would have survived. Either result would have been more valuable than decades of argument over the interpretation of telegrams.

There is an uncomfortable possibility between success and failure: the signal might exist but be weaker than the prediction framework built around it.

That possibility has become more plausible in the modern era. The 2022 California magnetometer study does not reproduce VAN; it measures magnetic rather than geoelectric behavior and uses a different statistical architecture. But it illustrates a category of outcome that the old debate did not handle gracefully. A modest physical association may survive careful testing while remaining far too weak for individual prediction.

If that is the true shape of some preseismic electromagnetic effect, both historical camps could have been wrong in different ways. Enthusiasts could have inflated a small conditional phenomenon into deterministic warnings. Skeptics could have inferred from the failure of prediction that no antecedent electromagnetic physics existed.

The data we have do not justify that reconciliation. It is a hypothesis about the history, not a verdict.

But it tells us what to look for.

The instrument network should not ask first, 'Can we predict an earthquake?' That question pressures every anomaly toward operational significance. It should ask narrower questions. Does a predeclared feature distribution change before earthquakes? Does the effect replicate in a different network? Does it scale with magnitude or distance? Does it survive geomagnetic and cultural controls? Can a physical mechanism predict its frequency, polarization or spatial coherence?

Only after those questions survive should anyone send the modern equivalent of a telegram.

Greece learned to listen to the ground. The next lesson was harder: deciding what counted as hearing something.
