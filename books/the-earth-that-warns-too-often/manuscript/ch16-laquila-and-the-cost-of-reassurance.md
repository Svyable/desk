# L'Aquila and the Cost of Reassurance

Prediction research eventually reaches a room where nobody is discussing instruments.

On March 31, 2009, experts met in L'Aquila, a medieval city in central Italy that had been shaken for months by a swarm of small earthquakes. Residents were frightened. A local laboratory technician, Giampaolo Giuliani, had attracted attention with claims that radon measurements could anticipate a larger event. Authorities convened a meeting of Italy's National Commission for the Forecast and Prevention of Major Risks.

Six days later, at 3:32 in the morning on April 6, a magnitude 6.3 earthquake struck. Buildings collapsed. Three hundred and nine people died.

The legal case that followed is often summarized inaccurately as 'Italy jailed scientists for failing to predict an earthquake.' Prosecutors themselves said that was not the charge. The case centered on risk evaluation and communication—whether public statements surrounding the commission meeting had been inadequately reassuring and whether some residents changed behavior as a result.

The distinction matters because it changes what the episode teaches.

The scientists did not possess a validated short-term prediction method and refuse to use it. The region was experiencing a swarm. Swarms increase attention, but most do not culminate in a destructive mainshock. The experts had to communicate a low-probability, high-consequence risk in a population already receiving dramatic claims from an unvalidated radon predictor.

This is one of the hardest things institutions do.

Public statements became central. Before and after the meeting, Bernardo De Bernardinis, then a senior civil-protection official, made comments interpreted as reassuring, including the scientifically dubious notion that repeated small tremors represented a favorable release of energy. The commission itself did not issue a clean formal public statement that separated what was known, what was not known and what actions residents should take.

After the earthquake, prosecutors argued that some residents who normally left their homes during tremors had remained inside because of reassurances and died when buildings collapsed. In 2012, six scientists and one official were convicted of manslaughter at the trial level and sentenced to six years. The verdict triggered international outrage. In 2014, an appeals court acquitted the six scientists; De Bernardinis's conviction was reduced rather than erased.

The legal history is important to state accurately because the simplified version distorts the science.

L'Aquila was not primarily a courtroom referendum on whether earthquakes are predictable. It was a disaster-risk communication case wrapped around a science that cannot offer deterministic prediction.

That makes it more relevant, not less.

Every future precursor system will face the L'Aquila problem long before it becomes perfect.

Suppose a magnetic network produces a signal that raises the estimated probability of a damaging earthquake over the next three days from 0.1 percent to 1 percent. Tenfold risk increase. Ninety-nine percent chance the earthquake still does not occur in the window.

What should an official say?

'There is no reason for alarm' is statistically understandable and potentially misleading.

'An earthquake is ten times more likely' is mathematically true and likely to be heard as imminent.

'We cannot predict earthquakes' is institutionally safe and discards information.

'A short-term risk indicator has increased, but the absolute chance remains about one percent' is closer to the truth and requires a public comfortable with probabilities under stress.

The communication problem is not downstream of the science. It should shape the science's outputs from the beginning.

A precursor detector that cannot be translated into calibrated absolute risk is not ready for public use.

This standard protects researchers from the seductive ambiguity of anomaly scores. A model output of 0.83 may look precise but mean nothing unless it is calibrated. If all periods scored near 0.83 contain target earthquakes only two percent of the time, the public message should reflect two percent, not the model's internal number.

L'Aquila also shows why debunking one bad prediction method does not eliminate risk.

Giuliani's radon claims lacked validation sufficient for public prediction. Officials were right not to endorse them as established. But the correct response to an unreliable alarm is not false reassurance. The earthquake hazard remained. The ongoing swarm may have changed short-term probabilities somewhat even if it did not allow deterministic prediction. Buildings remained vulnerable.

'We do not have evidence for this predictor' and 'you are safe' are different statements.

The gap between them killed trust.

This has direct implications for electromagnetic precursor research. Skeptics sometimes respond to weak claims with categorical language: there is no evidence, earthquakes cannot be predicted, reported anomalies are noise. Agencies have good reasons to resist public hype. But if a modest association survives rigorous prospective testing, institutional language will need more precision.

Likewise, precursor advocates cannot use the social cost of missing earthquakes to lower evidentiary standards. 'What if it saves lives?' is not a statistical argument. A detector with too many false alarms can itself create harm, and repeated alarms destroy compliance.

The L'Aquila lesson is symmetry of responsibility.

Do not overstate weak positive evidence.

Do not convert uncertainty into reassurance.

This sounds simple in prose and becomes difficult in a press conference.

The problem is partly linguistic. The word 'prediction' implies certainty to most people. Scientists often use 'forecast' for probabilities and 'early warning' for rapid detection after rupture begins. Public discourse mixes all three.

A mature earthquake information system should separate them explicitly.

Hazard: long-term probability based on faults, seismic history and geology.

Forecast: time-varying probability over a defined region and magnitude range.

Prediction: a much stronger claim about a specific future event.

Early warning: notification after an earthquake has begun but before damaging shaking arrives elsewhere.

A precursor, if validated, would become one input to forecasting. It does not deserve the word prediction by default.

This vocabulary would have clarified much of the historical VAN debate and much of the public misunderstanding around L'Aquila.

The Italian case also demonstrates why communication research belongs inside geophysics. A probability can be mathematically correct and behaviorally disastrous if presented badly. People interpret reassurance through experience. Residents who had begun sleeping outside after repeated tremors may change that behavior after hearing officials describe the situation as favorable. A statement's effect depends on social context, not only literal wording.

Risk messages therefore need recommended actions.

Instead of telling people whether to worry, authorities can say what remains sensible under uncertainty: know safe places, secure heavy furniture, keep emergency supplies, understand building vulnerability, follow official alerts, use early-warning systems where available. These actions have value across a wide probability range.

For a modest precursor signal, low-cost preparedness actions may be rational even when evacuation is not.

This is decision theory in ordinary clothes.

The science should support graded responses rather than one alarm switch.

A future system might have tiers. Baseline hazard. Elevated short-term probability based on seismicity. Further elevation when an independently validated electromagnetic feature appears. Each tier maps to predefined actions for infrastructure operators and public agencies. The thresholds are chosen using expected losses, not scientific drama.

Crucially, the thresholds should be established before the next crisis.

L'Aquila became chaotic partly because scientific assessment, political pressure, media communication and public fear collided in real time. A precursor field that waits until it detects its first apparently strong signal to decide how to communicate has already failed operationally.

This suggests an unusual component for the decisive experiment proposed later in the book: a communication preregistration.

Before prospective testing begins, define what message would be issued at each probability threshold. During the research phase, those messages remain internal or simulated. Human-factors teams test how people interpret them. Infrastructure operators estimate costs of actions. The scientific detector is evaluated not only for statistical skill but for decision value.

Most candidate precursor effects will probably die before reaching this stage.

That is healthy.

Those that survive should arrive in public life with fewer improvisations.

The L'Aquila case also sits uncomfortably beside Haicheng. In one historical story, authorities are praised for acting on imperfect warning and moving people. In the other, officials are condemned for communication perceived as too reassuring before a disaster. It would be easy to infer that the safe political strategy is always to warn.

That would be disastrous.

A world in which every seismic swarm or weak anomaly triggers evacuation would produce constant disruption in active regions. People would stop listening. Economic and health costs would accumulate. Vulnerable populations would bear those costs unevenly.

The purpose of better prediction science is not to maximize warnings. It is to improve decisions.

That may sound like a modest retreat from the dream of knowing the future. It is actually a higher standard.

A claim is not useful because it was right once. It is useful when a system can say how often it is right, how often it is wrong, what the errors cost and what people should do with the number.

L'Aquila is where the abstract distinction between signal and forecast becomes human.

The ground does not owe us certainty.

Officials still owe people clarity.
