# DEMETER

On June 29, 2004, a small French satellite went into orbit carrying one of the more unusual names ever assigned to a geophysics mission: Detection of Electro-Magnetic Emissions Transmitted from Earthquake Regions.

DEMETER was not a euphemism. The hypothesis was written into the spacecraft.

For decades, researchers had reported electromagnetic and ionospheric anomalies near earthquakes. Most evidence came from ground stations, radio-propagation studies or individual events. DEMETER offered a different proposition: put a stable package of plasma and field instruments above the atmosphere, fly it for years, and ask whether seismic regions look statistically different before earthquakes.

The mission deserves attention because it represents a rare institutional wager on a controversial phenomenon. It was a real satellite, built and operated by the French space program, carrying instruments developed by serious laboratories. It collected a large body of public scientific data. None of those facts establishes that earthquakes perturb the ionosphere before rupture. They establish something more modest and historically important: the hypothesis was considered testable enough to justify dedicated hardware.

That distinction—between funding a test and validating the result—should be obvious. In anomalous fields it often is not.

DEMETER orbited at roughly 710 kilometers initially and later around 660 kilometers, on a nearly Sun-synchronous trajectory that sampled approximately fixed local times. Its payload measured electric and magnetic fields, plasma parameters, ions and energetic particles. The stable local-time sampling helped reduce one major source of ionospheric variability while also limiting which parts of the daily cycle the satellite could observe.

The mission ran from 2004 until 2010. Six years is both a long experiment and a short earthquake catalog.

Thousands of earthquakes occurred during that interval. Only a much smaller number met any particular combination of magnitude, depth, latitude, proximity to orbit track and geomagnetic quietness. Every statistical paper therefore had to decide which events counted.

Those decisions are the experiment.

Michel Parrot and collaborators developed automated approaches to search for ion-density anomalies near earthquakes. A 2013 paper by M. Li and Parrot analyzed more than six years of DEMETER ion density and explicitly framed the exercise around tentative earthquake prediction. Later work by Rui Yan, Parrot and Jean-Louis Pinçon used a superposed epoch method. They carefully removed aftershocks and compared ion density near seismic activity with background values under similar conditions. Their 2017 *Journal of Geophysical Research: Space Physics* paper reported nighttime ion-density perturbations associated statistically with earthquakes larger than magnitude five, concentrated particularly around five days before events and within a few hundred kilometers of epicenters.

That is a real published result.

It is also exactly the kind of result that becomes distorted when compressed into public language.

'French satellite detects earthquake precursors' is too strong.

'No one can predict earthquakes, so the result must be noise' is also too strong.

The actual claim is about a distribution measured by a satellite under a particular selection procedure.

To understand its strength, one needs to ask how the background was built. The ionosphere has spatial structure. Comparing an earthquake pass with a global average would be meaningless. It has seasonal structure. Comparing February with July can produce anomalies unrelated to seismicity. It responds to geomagnetic activity. Solar-driven disturbances must be screened. Aftershocks cluster around mainshocks and can contaminate temporal windows. Land and ocean events may couple differently. Deep earthquakes should not necessarily behave like shallow ones.

The DEMETER literature progressively took these issues more seriously.

One can see the field learning in public. Early case studies show striking plots. Later studies automate detection. Later still, researchers build pseudorandom earthquake catalogs, normalize spatial scales and use superposed epochs. This is what maturation looks like when the phenomenon remains uncertain.

But improved statistics do not eliminate the central identification problem.

Suppose ion density is anomalously high five days before earthquakes in a pooled sample. What physical process created the difference?

The lithosphere-atmosphere-ionosphere coupling literature offers several possibilities. Radon or other gases could increase lower-atmosphere ionization and modify conductivity. Stress-activated charge carriers in rocks could alter surface potential and atmospheric electric fields. Aerosol charging might redistribute the electric circuit. Atmospheric waves generated by crustal or near-surface changes could propagate upward. Electromagnetic emissions could interact directly with plasma.

Different pathways should produce different signatures.

A radon-driven mechanism should correlate with geology and atmospheric boundary-layer conditions. A ground-current mechanism should have measurable electric or magnetic counterparts at the surface. A gravity-wave mechanism should show wave structure and propagation. Direct electromagnetic coupling should have frequency and polarization constraints.

If every mechanism is allowed after the anomaly is observed, the satellite has measured correlation, not cause.

This distinction matters because the same ionospheric variable can respond to ordinary nonseismic drivers.

The ionosphere does not know which paper will later call a fluctuation anomalous.

DEMETER's fixed local-time orbit was helpful, but a low-Earth-orbit satellite still samples a moving path. It does not hover over an epicenter. Researchers define a spatial relationship between the satellite measurement and the earthquake. The appropriate radius may scale with magnitude in some theories. The anomaly may be displaced by atmospheric winds or geomagnetic geometry. Conjugate effects may appear in the opposite hemisphere.

Each physically motivated allowance increases the number of ways a match can occur.

A strong study manages this by fixing the allowances before looking at the target data.

One of the most useful ideas in the later DEMETER work was comparison with random or pseudorandom earthquake times and locations. This asks whether the analysis pipeline can create similar apparent precursors when the 'earthquakes' are fake. It is a direct estimate of how surprising the real result is under the pipeline's own complexity.

Synthetic catalogs are not perfect controls because real earthquakes are geographically clustered along plate boundaries and seasonally independent in ways random samples may not preserve. The null catalogs need to retain relevant structures. A random point in the Sahara is not a fair control for an earthquake near Japan if ionospheric climatology differs.

The null has geology too.

This lesson carries into every global precursor analysis. The fake earthquakes should look like real earthquakes in every dimension except the temporal relationship being tested.

DEMETER also generated claims involving energetic particles and VLF disturbances. Some studies reported particle bursts before large earthquakes and considered possible coupling into the radiation belts. These findings are particularly difficult to interpret because energetic particles respond strongly to magnetospheric processes and wave-particle interactions unrelated to seismicity.

The burden of exclusion rises with altitude.

That does not make the studies worthless. It makes independent replication essential.

DEMETER ended in 2010. The experiment therefore contains a built-in opportunity: algorithms developed using its data can be tested on later satellite missions. The instruments will not be identical, but physical predictions should translate at some level. Swarm measures Earth's magnetic field and plasma with exquisite precision. CSES was launched with seismo-electromagnetic science among its explicit goals. GNSS networks provide continuous total electron content. A signal that belongs to the Earth rather than DEMETER should leave descendants.

This has happened in the literature, but not yet with the clarity needed for operational prediction.

Swarm studies have reported pre-earthquake magnetic and plasma signatures in global analyses. CSES papers report event associations and statistical patterns. Reviews by Piergiorgio Picozza, Livio Conti and colleagues catalog a large body of ground- and space-based precursor observations while emphasizing the challenge of separating them from natural and artificial backgrounds.

The existence of this literature creates a peculiar epistemic situation.

There are too many positive papers to dismiss the subject as having no data.

There are too few decisive prospective replications to call the effect established.

This middle state is uncomfortable because scientific culture prefers sorting. The phenomenon is either real or not. But weak signals in noisy natural systems can remain unresolved for a long time without anyone behaving irrationally.

The right response is to ask what information the existing positive literature actually adds.

First, it suggests candidate variables and windows. Nighttime ion density, for example, recurs in DEMETER statistical work. That can be preregistered for future missions.

Second, it suggests conditionality. Larger, shallower earthquakes may be more promising. Land versus ocean differences can be specified.

Third, it identifies nuisance variables: geomagnetic indices, local time, latitude, season and aftershock clustering.

Fourth, it supplies effect-size estimates needed to calculate how much future data a decisive test requires.

This is how exploratory literature becomes a confirmatory experiment.

What it should not do is become a vote count.

Twenty papers finding anomalies are not twenty independent replications if they mine overlapping DEMETER data with related windows and methods. Shared data create genealogies. One influential event, such as the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake, can appear in many papers under different variables. The evidence looks broader than the underlying set of earthquakes.

A source ledger for anomaly research therefore needs a data-lineage column.

Which mission? Which years? Which earthquakes? Which raw channels? Which authors? Which preprocessing? Independence is a property of evidence, not citation count.

This is especially relevant to machine learning. A model can ingest dozens of DEMETER features and discover nonlinear combinations that separate pre-earthquake windows from controls. If the same mission supplies training, model selection and final evaluation, spectacular accuracy can be meaningless. The real test is transfer to later earthquakes and preferably a different sensor system.

DEMETER's greatest scientific value may therefore lie in becoming a training archive rather than the final proof.

Use its six years to generate hypotheses. Freeze them. Test them on Swarm, CSES and future missions without tuning the answer.

The satellite also teaches a political lesson. When a state funds a controversial measurement, the result should become easier to falsify, not harder to question. Mission branding can create institutional momentum around the hypothesis. Teams are formed. Careers develop. Conferences recur. A negative conclusion can threaten the intellectual identity built around the instrument.

This is not misconduct. It is ordinary human attachment.

The antidote is external analysis.

Open data allow skeptical groups to rerun the statistics. Independent researchers can test different null models. A mission designed to search for earthquake effects should welcome a paper showing that a claimed anomaly is solar, seasonal or instrumental. Every killed false positive improves the detector.

Corralitos had to wait almost twenty years for a decisive instrument-history reanalysis. Satellite precursor work should not.

There is one more thing DEMETER clarifies: prediction is not the only scientific prize.

If a reproducible ionospheric change occurs days before some earthquakes but is too weak for warnings, the result would still matter. It would establish coupling between crustal preparation and near-Earth plasma across hundreds of kilometers. That is a remarkable geophysical process.

The effect could fail every public-safety threshold and still rewrite part of Earth-system science.

This possibility is the reason I resist the field's binary vocabulary. Researchers chasing prediction may oversell weak effects because only prediction seems worth the effort. Skeptics may dismiss weak effects because they are useless for prediction. Both positions undervalue physics.

DEMETER put instruments in orbit to see whether the Earth talks upward before it breaks.

The satellite returned enough strange statistics to justify another experiment.

It did not return a clock.
