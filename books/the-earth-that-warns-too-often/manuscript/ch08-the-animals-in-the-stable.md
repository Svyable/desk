# The Animals in the Stable

The earthquake-prediction literature has a room where respectable researchers become visibly uncomfortable.

Animals are in it.

Stories of unusual animal behavior before earthquakes are ancient. Dogs howl. Fish jump. Snakes leave burrows. Birds vanish. Livestock refuse to enter barns. The stories are so persistent across cultures that it is difficult to discuss earthquake precursors without encountering them, and so vulnerable to memory and selection that many geophysicists would rather not.

The discomfort is healthy. Anecdotes about animal behavior have almost every property that makes precursor evidence unreliable. The observations are usually made by people who were not running an experiment. 'Unusual' has no fixed threshold. The relevant time window expands after the earthquake. A dog that barked all night becomes memorable; ten thousand dogs that barked on quiet nights disappear from the record. Reports spread socially after the event. Animals are influenced by weather, predators, estrus, food, humans, noise, illness and one another.

And yet dismissing the entire category because the historical evidence is poor makes a different mistake.

Animals are measurement systems.

They have accelerometers, pressure sensors, chemoreceptors, electroreceptors and magnetosensory abilities in combinations that vary enormously by species. Some detect frequencies humans cannot hear. Some respond to tiny ground vibrations. Some aquatic animals detect electric fields. Birds and other animals use geomagnetic information. If a fault's approach to rupture produces any environmental change large enough to be biologically detected, an animal could in principle respond before a human instrument network recognizes what matters.

The question is not whether animals have a mystical sixth sense.

It is whether continuously measured behavior contains prospective information about future earthquakes after ordinary causes of behavior are controlled.

That experiment has finally begun to exist.

In 2020, Martin Wikelski and colleagues published a study of cows, dogs and sheep in central Italy during the 2016–2017 earthquake sequence. Instead of collecting witness memories, the researchers attached biologging sensors to animals and recorded activity continuously. Their data included thousands of earthquakes, from very small events up through the magnitude 6.6 Norcia earthquake. They reported that collective activity patterns changed before some earthquakes, particularly while animals were in the stable, and that apparent anticipation time varied with hypocentral distance.

The paper was interesting for exactly the right reason: it converted folklore into a time series.

Then the argument became scientific.

In 2021, Gert Zöller, Sebastian Hainzl, Frederik Tilmann, Heiko Woith and Torsten Dahm published a critique. They argued that the statistical analysis did not establish forecasting power and that the claimed anticipatory patterns could not be distinguished from random patterns under more appropriate methods. Wikelski and colleagues responded.

This exchange is more valuable than a hundred stories about nervous dogs.

It demonstrates how an anomalous domain should mature. The first step is not to decide whether the old stories were true. It is to build an instrument that does not remember selectively.

A collar on a cow is not automatically a better precursor detector than a farmer. It produces different problems. The animal's activity has strong daily rhythms. Behavior changes with whether it is indoors or grazing. Animals influence each other. A nearby small earthquake can cause a response that overlaps with the supposed anticipatory window of a later earthquake. During an aftershock sequence, there may be hundreds or thousands of earthquakes, making temporal association extraordinarily easy to obtain if the analysis is flexible.

The animal study therefore lands in the same statistical landscape as VAN and magnetic precursors.

What counts as an anomaly? How long is the precursor window? What earthquakes count as targets? How are aftershocks handled? Is the analysis rule fixed before the target events? How much time is under alarm? What happens when the animals become active and no earthquake follows?

The species changes. The evaluation problem does not.

This is why earthquake science is such a useful training ground for anomalous evidence. It forces very different phenomena through the same narrow gate.

Groundwater belongs in the room too.

Wells respond to earthquakes. That statement is not controversial. Seismic waves and crustal deformation can alter pore pressure, permeability and water levels. Springs change flow. Turbidity changes. Chemistry can shift. The hydrologic response to rupture is a serious part of hydrogeology.

Preseismic groundwater anomalies are harder.

If a fault undergoes slow deformation or microfracturing before dynamic rupture, permeability and pore pressure might change. A well connected to the relevant formation could register water-level or chemical changes. Radon, a radioactive noble gas produced in the uranium decay chain, is especially attractive as a candidate because cracking and fluid movement might change its transport from rock into soil gas or groundwater.

Reports of radon anomalies before earthquakes have accumulated for decades.

The mechanism is plausible enough to survive ridicule. The statistics are not yet strong enough to support reliable prediction.

Radon concentration varies for many reasons: barometric pressure, rainfall, groundwater flow, soil moisture, temperature, ventilation, source-rock uranium content and measurement practice. A spike near an earthquake can be physically interesting while remaining non-specific. The same applies to groundwater level. Pumping can overwhelm tectonic effects. Rainfall can masquerade as them. Tides matter. Seasonal trends matter.

The right design is not one heroic well near one heroic earthquake. It is a network with environmental covariates, long baselines and a locked analysis.

This sounds repetitive because the field's failures are repetitive.

The repetition is the point.

Different precursor communities have repeatedly rediscovered the same methodological trap. The radon researcher sees a spike before an earthquake and asks what crustal process caused it. The magnetometer researcher sees a spectral change and asks the same. The animal-behavior researcher sees agitation. The ionospheric researcher sees plasma-density variation. Each domain has its own mechanism literature, but the causal inference problem is shared.

A future earthquake is a powerful label.

Once the label is known, the time series reorganizes itself around it.

One way to resist that power is to reverse the analysis. Instead of asking, 'What happened before this earthquake?' ask, 'Whenever this candidate precursor happened, what followed?'

This is exactly what a warning system experiences in real time. It has no privilege to ignore false alarms because a more interesting earthquake happened elsewhere.

The animal problem becomes especially revealing here. Suppose a stable of instrumented cows becomes unusually active fifteen times in a month. One episode occurs six hours before a magnitude 4.8 earthquake. If the analysis begins with the earthquake, the episode is a precursor. If the analysis begins with the cows, the detector has fourteen false alarms and one possible hit.

The same arithmetic can make almost any anecdotal precursor collapse.

But there is another possibility. The activity may not be an alarm by itself. It may be a weak feature whose likelihood changes in combination with others. Perhaps animal activity, local ULF magnetic coherence and a hydrologic feature together shift short-term risk more than any channel alone.

This is the seductive promise of multimodal prediction.

It is also statistically dangerous.

With enough channels, some combination will fit historical earthquakes. Machine learning excels at finding combinations. The model may discover genuine structure or simply learn the quirks of a tiny earthquake sample. Held-out testing is therefore even more important for multimodal systems than for single sensors.

The 2020 animal study's strongest contribution may ultimately be not its claimed precursor but its instrumentation model. Animals can be monitored continuously and objectively. The data can be time-stamped. Environmental conditions can be measured. Multiple farms can create spatial baselines. The experiment can be preregistered.

A decisive animal-sensing study would look very different from traditional folklore collection.

Select several seismically active regions and matched control regions. Instrument multiple species and farms for years. Measure activity, location, heart rate where practical, feeding, barn noise, human entry, temperature, pressure and weather. Define candidate behavioral features on an initial training period. Freeze the model. Register every alarm generated during the next several years. Score against a declustered earthquake catalog and against synthetic catalogs preserving seasonal and temporal structure.

Then do the most important analysis: determine whether any behavioral feature adds information beyond conventional seismicity and environmental covariates.

If it does not, the animals may still react to tiny local vibrations or other earthquake-related stimuli without predicting anything. If it does, the spatial dependence may help identify the physical channel.

That channel is where the story becomes interesting again.

If anticipation time scales with hypocentral distance in a reproducible way, as Wikelski and colleagues reported, the slope constrains propagation. A signal moving at seismic-wave speed cannot explain behavior hours in advance if rupture has not occurred. A slowly diffusing chemical or electrical process could, in principle. Atmospheric or groundwater transport has its own timescales. A real distance-time law would be far more informative than the generic claim that animals 'sense earthquakes.'

Mechanism should emerge from the geometry of the behavior.

This is how an odd fact earns twenty pages.

The same logic applies to radon. If radon anomalies are genuine precursors, their timing and amplitude should depend on source depth, rock uranium content, fracture permeability, carrier-gas flow and station distance in ways that can be modeled. A global pile of spikes with inconsistent signs and windows is weak evidence. A reproducible scaling relation would be strong.

Groundwater, animals and electromagnetism may even intersect physically.

Stress changes could alter crack networks and fluid flow. Fluid movement can generate electrokinetic potentials. Gas release changes near-surface chemistry. Electrical changes might influence organisms with sensitive receptors. This coupled picture is sometimes invoked to explain multiple precursor classes.

It is coherent enough to be dangerous.

A theory that explains everything after the fact can become impossible to falsify. If the animals move, it was electrical. If radon rises, it was cracking. If the magnetic field changes, it was current. If nothing happens, the local geology was unfavorable. The only way out is forward prediction of which channels should respond at which sites.

A coupled theory has to predict covariance, not merely allow it.

For a particular earthquake geometry, perhaps it predicts a radon increase at station A, no magnetic change at station B, and animal behavior only within twenty kilometers. That is risky. Good.

The history of Haicheng often bundles different observations into a single aura of forewarning. Modern research should unbundle them and then test whether they recombine.

There is also a human reason animals remain attractive. Machines are narrow. An animal integrates countless environmental variables through evolution and physiology. If there is a weak precursor researchers have not thought to measure, an organism might respond without telling us which channel mattered.

This is both the attraction and the flaw.

An animal is a black-box sensor with hunger.

Machine-learning systems are often described similarly: they can find signals humans do not know how to specify. The lesson from both is that opaque detection does not excuse weak validation. If the black box predicts, it can be scored.

The evidence class for current animal precursor research should therefore be cautious.

Verified: animals can be continuously monitored; the 2020 Italian study recorded measurable collective behavior and claimed anticipatory associations; a published 2021 critique argued those associations lacked forecasting power under alternative analysis.

Credible but incomplete: some animals may detect environmental changes associated with the preparatory phase of some earthquakes.

Disputed: existing behavioral data establish a reliable short-term forecasting signal.

Speculative: animals are responding specifically to stress-activated electrical, chemical or electromagnetic precursors generated by future rupture zones.

The distinction preserves the weirdness without laundering it into fact.

That is what this book should do with every strange channel.

The old stories can remain in the archive. Snakes can still leave holes in winter. Dogs can still bark before earthquakes. But the scientific future of the claim belongs to the stable with accelerometers, the weather station outside, the seismic catalog on a server and an analysis script whose rules were frozen before the ground moved.

If the animals know, they should be able to tell us twice.
