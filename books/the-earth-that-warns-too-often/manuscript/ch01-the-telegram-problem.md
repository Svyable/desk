# The Telegram Problem

In the spring of 1996, a scientific argument about earthquakes had become strange enough to deserve its own issue of *Geophysical Research Letters*. The dispute was not merely over whether rocks can produce electrical effects under stress. It was over something much more dangerous: whether a group of Greek physicists had already found a way to warn people before damaging earthquakes, and whether the rest of seismology was too skeptical to admit it.

The method was known by an acronym that sounded almost administrative: VAN, for Panayiotis Varotsos, Kessar Alexopoulos and Konstantine Nomicos. Their claim was that short-lived changes in the Earth's electric field—what they called seismic electric signals, or SES—could precede earthquakes. In the most ambitious versions of the claim, the signals could be used not merely to say that stress in the crust had changed, but to estimate where a forthcoming earthquake would occur, how large it might be, and roughly when.

That is a different category of proposition from saying that rocks are electrically interesting. It crosses the line from geophysics into public action.

VAN had an answer to the obvious accusation of hindsight. The group sent telegrams. Predictions, they argued, had been transmitted before the earthquakes they were later matched to. This detail matters because earthquake prediction is uniquely vulnerable to retrospective storytelling. A country like Greece has enough seismicity that a vaguely phrased warning can often be made to look impressive after the fact. A telegram establishes that something was written down in advance. It gives the argument a timestamp.

But a timestamp is not the same thing as a test.

David Jackson, a UCLA geophysicist, made that distinction brutally clear in the 1996 debate. For a binary prediction system—alarm on, alarm off—he proposed five basic requirements. The observable that triggers an alarm must be quantitatively defined. The predicted time, place and magnitude ranges must also be quantitative. Every prior alarm must be documented, not only the successful ones. Every earthquake claimed as predicted must be listed. So must every earthquake the method missed. A prediction system becomes scientifically legible only when the denominator is visible.

Jackson's complaint was not that VAN's telegrams had never existed. It was that the telegrams did not, by themselves, contain enough fixed information to reconstruct an unambiguous scorecard. How broad was the alarm region? How much leeway was allowed on magnitude? How long did the prediction window remain open? When a new type of signal appeared, did the permitted rules change? These are dull questions compared with the prospect of predicting earthquakes. They are also the questions on which the prospect lives or dies.

Varotsos and his colleagues answered that the critique misrepresented their published criteria. Most tolerances, they said, had been specified before the 1987–1989 prediction sequence under examination. Only a small number of cases involved a newly recognized phenomenon whose time window had to be characterized during the sequence. They also disputed statistical treatments that assumed an inappropriate Poisson process for earthquake occurrence or mishandled aftershocks. The details became technical because they had to. Once the fight moves from anecdote to evaluation, the argument is no longer about whether some earthquake followed some electrical disturbance. It is about the rules by which events are admitted into the sample.

This is the first important fact about earthquake precursors: the most consequential instrument in the experiment may be the bookkeeping.

The public imagination tends to divide the world into two camps. In one camp are sober seismologists who say earthquakes cannot be predicted. In the other are mavericks who insist the Earth emits warnings that conventional science refuses to hear. The actual history is less clean. Seismologists have repeatedly installed instruments precisely because they wanted the precursor claims to be true. They have drilled boreholes, monitored groundwater, measured strain, recorded electric and magnetic fields, watched radon, launched satellites and built dense networks along faults. They have also repeatedly discovered that a striking anomaly is easier to find after an earthquake than before one.

That asymmetry is the subject of this book.

An earthquake is not mysterious in the sense that its existence is unexplained. Tectonic plates move. Stress accumulates. Faults slip. Elastic strain is released. Seismic waves carry the consequences outward. The problem lies in the final approach to failure. Does a fault that will rupture tomorrow differ observably from one that will not rupture for another decade? If it does, how large is the region in which the difference can be measured? Is the difference mechanical, chemical, electrical, hydrological, atmospheric, ionospheric, biological—or some combination? And even if some physical antecedent exists, does it contain enough information to support a useful prediction?

Those questions are often collapsed into one. They should not be.

A precursor can be real without being predictive. Suppose a measurable magnetic feature becomes slightly more common in the two days before moderate earthquakes. That would be scientifically important. It might reveal previously unappreciated stress-sensitive physics in the crust. But if the same feature is common on ordinary days, if many earthquakes occur without it, or if it appears over too broad a region, it may have almost no practical forecasting value. The effect could survive as physics and fail as a warning system.

The reverse is also possible in principle. A statistical risk marker can improve forecasts without having a satisfying mechanism. Medicine works with such markers all the time. The problem in earthquake science is that the base rate is punishing. Large earthquakes are rare at any given place and time. A warning system with what sounds like good sensitivity can still drown society in false alarms.

This is why the VAN telegrams are more than a historical curiosity. They force the evaluation problem into view before we even ask what produces SES.

Imagine that a station in central Greece records an unusual electrical transient. The researchers identify it as seismic rather than industrial. Based on previous calibration, they assign it to a geographic region and a magnitude band. A warning is sent. Three days later an earthquake occurs inside the region and magnitude band. Success seems obvious.

Now widen the frame. How many electrical transients occurred that year? How many were rejected by the researchers before being called SES? How many alarms did those accepted signals generate? How much geographic area was under alarm, and for how many cumulative days? How many earthquakes occurred outside the alarm windows? How many aftershocks were counted as independent targets? Were the classification rules fixed before the data were seen? Did the station sensitivity remain stable? Were there nearby industrial electrical sources? Could a remote reference station distinguish a local crustal event from a regional geomagnetic disturbance?

The answers determine whether we have witnessed prediction or selection.

The Greek debate became unusually intense because VAN did something many fringe prediction systems do not: it engaged with the scientific literature at length, built an instrumentation network, issued documented claims and invited mathematical argument. The method therefore sat in an uncomfortable zone. It was not a newspaper astrologer's prophecy. It was also not a mature forecasting system whose inputs and scoring rules could be handed to an independent center and run blind.

The distinction matters for another reason. Once an earthquake prediction becomes public, its scientific quality is no longer the only variable. People may sleep outside. Schools may close. Hospitals may change staffing. Officials may be blamed for acting or not acting. Insurance, transportation and markets can respond. A false alarm is not free. Neither is silence before a real event.

Earthquake prediction therefore has a threshold problem that laboratory science often postpones. When does an interesting signal become actionable information?

The safest institutional answer has been to keep the bar high. The U.S. Geological Survey states plainly that neither it nor any other scientists can predict a major earthquake in the required sense of specifying time, location and magnitude. That position is supported by a long record of failed or unreplicated precursor claims. It also protects the public from the recurrent commerce of earthquake prophecy.

Yet the sentence can be misunderstood. 'We cannot predict earthquakes' does not logically mean 'nothing physically changes before rupture.' Those are different propositions. The second is stronger, and much harder to establish.

This book lives in that gap.

The gap widened in 2022, when researchers from Google and QuakeFinder published an unusually careful case-control study in *Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth*. QuakeFinder had spent years operating a dense array of ground-based magnetic stations near California faults. The investigators started from more than 330,000 site-days of magnetometer measurements and selected earthquakes using rules tied to the USGS catalog. They divided the data by time into training and test sets. The later test data were kept out of the model-development process. They used pairs of nearby stations and cross-spectral power to suppress purely local noise. Their analysis found a modest difference in magnetic behavior during a window 24 to 72 hours before 19 earthquakes of magnitude 4.5 or greater.

The result was not a prediction system. The authors said so. The effect was too small. The mechanism was unknown. Nineteen earthquakes is a larger sample than many classic precursor papers, but it is still nineteen earthquakes. The analysis did not vindicate VAN, Loma Prieta, ionospheric anomalies, radon, animal behavior or any particular theory. It did something more interesting: after decades in which many electromagnetic precursor claims had failed hard tests, a modern analysis designed specifically to reduce p-hacking and local noise still found a small antecedent association worth investigating.

That is not a triumph. It is an irritation.

Good science needs irritations. A field can become distorted by weak positive evidence, but it can also become lazy around a strong negative consensus. The right response to a modest, carefully obtained anomaly is not to promote it into a warning system. Nor is it to dismiss it because earlier claims were worse. It is to ask what experiment would make the anomaly either harden into physics or disappear.

The history offers unusually good controls.

Parkfield, California, was one. For decades the central San Andreas fault near the small town produced moderate earthquakes with an apparent regularity that tempted researchers to expect another event. Instruments accumulated there. When the magnitude 6.0 earthquake finally arrived on September 28, 2004—later than the simplest recurrence model had suggested—it occurred inside one of the most intensively monitored patches of crust on Earth. The magnetometers saw coseismic changes. Electrical instruments saw effects associated with the rupture. But careful analyses found no convincing preseismic electric or magnetic signal above background. If the Earth had whispered electromagnetically before Parkfield, a lot of equipment was listening.

Loma Prieta offers the opposite kind of lesson. In 1990, Stanford researchers reported unusual ultra-low-frequency magnetic activity recorded near Corralitos before the October 1989 magnitude 7.1 earthquake. The observation became one of the canonical electromagnetic precursor cases. Nearly two decades later, Jeremy Thomas, Jeffrey Love and Malcolm Johnston reexamined twenty-one months of Corralitos data, the operating log and comparison magnetometer records. They concluded that the famous anomaly was most consistent with sensor-system malfunction. The precursor did not merely fail to replicate. Its evidentiary status changed because someone widened the time series.

A short window can make an anomaly look singular. A long window can reveal its relatives.

This is one reason the earthquake precursor field is so useful beyond seismology. It is a natural laboratory for studying how humans reason about rare disasters. We are exquisitely sensitive to patterns immediately before catastrophe. After an event, every oddity acquires narrative gravity: a strange animal, a flickering instrument, a change in well water, a radio disturbance, a headache, a light in the sky. Most of those observations were not collected under a protocol designed to compare them against ordinary time. The catastrophe chooses the window for us.

Retrospective selection is not fraud. Often it is how discovery begins. The first unusual observation tells you what instrument to build next. The mistake is to let discovery logic masquerade as validation logic.

The same tension follows us from Greek electrodes to satellites. France's DEMETER mission, launched in 2004, was explicitly designed in part to investigate electromagnetic disturbances associated with earthquakes. Statistical studies of its ion-density measurements have reported anomalies several days before some earthquakes. Other researchers using Swarm data have reported global pre-earthquake magnetic or plasma patterns. Critics answer that the ionosphere is noisy, driven by solar and geomagnetic activity, season, latitude, local time and human radio systems. A satellite observes an enormous number of possible variables around an enormous number of earthquakes. Without locked hypotheses and matched controls, the search space itself can manufacture significance.

None of that proves the reported satellite effects are false. It explains why the decisive experiment is harder than drawing an anomaly around an epicenter after the ground moves.

The structure of the problem repeats. A proposed signal begins as a case. The case becomes a mechanism. The mechanism becomes an algorithm. The algorithm becomes a forecast. At every transition, information is lost and assumptions are added.

VAN jumped unusually far along that chain. The Greek group did not merely claim to have observed electrical transients. It claimed an operational relationship between those transients and future earthquakes. That ambition is what made the method famous and what made its evaluation so unforgiving.

There is something admirable in that. A prediction that can be scored is scientifically braver than a post hoc explanation that cannot lose. The trouble was that the scoring rules themselves became part of the dispute.

The telegram, then, is the right place to begin. It is physical, dated, bureaucratic and human. Somewhere a signal moved through copper wire or a telephone exchange because researchers believed another signal in the ground meant something. The first signal was an observation. The second was a decision.

Between them lies almost the entire science of prediction.
