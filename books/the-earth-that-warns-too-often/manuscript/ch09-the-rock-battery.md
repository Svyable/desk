# The Rock Battery

Friedemann Freund's earthquake theory starts with an object so small it sounds incapable of carrying a public warning: an oxygen bond inside a mineral.

The claim is not that quartz crystals forecast earthquakes or that rocks possess some occult electrical memory. It is a solid-state physics proposal. In many igneous and high-grade metamorphic minerals, Freund and collaborators argue, oxygen can occur in peroxy bonds—pairs of oxygen atoms in a defect configuration that is electrically quiet under ordinary conditions. When the surrounding rock is stressed, those bonds can break in a way that activates mobile electronic charge carriers. Freund calls them positive holes.

The phrase is easy to misunderstand. A positive hole is not a tiny cavity moving through rock. It is the absence of an electron in an oxygen sublattice, a defect state that can propagate through the material as neighboring electrons rearrange. In semiconductor physics, holes are ordinary objects of calculation. The unusual claim is that crustal rocks can activate and transport them over distances relevant to pre-earthquake phenomena.

In laboratory experiments, stressed igneous rocks have produced measurable electrical currents and surface potentials. Akihiro Takeuchi and other collaborators reported currents consistent with positive-hole transport. Freund's group developed the idea across papers on charge generation, conductivity changes, air ionization and infrared emission. A 2011 *Journal of Geophysical Research* model by Chieh-Hung Kuo and colleagues took the proposal upward into the atmosphere, exploring how rock currents and surface charge might perturb ionospheric plasma.

This is the most ambitious kind of earthquake precursor theory because it tries to unify phenomena that otherwise look unrelated.

Stress activates charge in the crust. Charge flows out of stressed rock. At the surface it changes electric potential and may ionize air. Currents produce magnetic fields and low-frequency electromagnetic emissions. Surface charge could alter the atmospheric electric circuit. Recombination processes might affect thermal infrared emission. Atmospheric electrical changes might couple into the ionosphere. Animals might respond to some part of the electrical or chemical environment. What looks like a zoo of precursors becomes one battery with many terminals.

A unifying mechanism is scientifically attractive for the same reason it is epistemically dangerous.

If one process explains every anomaly, every anomaly starts to look like independent support for the process. But the observations may not be independent, and some may not be real precursors at all.

The rock-battery theory therefore deserves two separate evaluations.

First: does the solid-state mechanism operate in rocks under laboratory and field-relevant conditions?

Second: does that mechanism cause measurable, prospective signals before natural earthquakes?

The first question has a substantial experimental literature. The second remains open.

This separation matters because mechanism work can be good even when the forecasting interpretation is wrong.

Take the laboratory result. If nonuniform stress applied to igneous rock produces electrical current through activation of defect states, that is a physical property worth understanding. It may matter for rock physics, fracture, triboelectric phenomena or other geophysical settings. The experiment does not need an earthquake to be interesting.

Scaling is where the earthquake enters.

A laboratory slab has boundaries the experimenter controls. A crustal source is kilometers underground. Stress is distributed through heterogeneous rock. Temperature and pressure differ. Fluids occupy cracks. Conductive minerals and pore electrolytes provide alternate current paths. The rock mass is already fractured. If positive holes are activated, their lifetime and transport distance under these conditions determine whether a surface sensor sees anything.

The theory needs a source volume.

How much rock is sufficiently stressed, for how long, before a large earthquake? If the final rupture nucleates in a small patch, is the stressed volume large enough to generate a detectable current? If the stress change is distributed over tens or hundreds of cubic kilometers, why do conventional strain and geodetic measurements not always show a corresponding short-term preparation? The answer could be that activation depends on deviatoric stress or microstructural changes too subtle for bulk deformation. That answer has to be quantified.

The theory also needs a circuit.

An electric current does not simply flow because charges exist. There must be a path and a return. Freund's work uses the battery analogy deliberately. The stressed volume acts as a source; positive holes can flow into surrounding unstressed rock; electrons follow different pathways or remain associated with the source. Surface charge can accumulate if the geometry permits.

At crustal scale, conductivity architecture becomes decisive. Wet sediment, saline fluids, graphite films, mineralized faults and temperature all change the circuit. The same geology that might channel a signal could short it out.

This is where the theory could become productively specific.

Given a conductivity model and a stress source, calculate current density at the surface. Predict the magnetic field at specified distances and frequencies. Predict where surface potential should peak. Predict whether the signal should appear first near topographic highs or conductive boundaries. Predict how rainfall changes it. Then install instruments before earthquakes and see whether the spatial pattern occurs.

That is much harder than explaining an old anomaly after the fact.

The Loma Prieta case shows why the distinction matters. Positive-hole theory has sometimes been discussed alongside ULF magnetic anomalies, but the famous Corralitos precursor is now strongly challenged as sensor malfunction. A mechanism cannot use a weakened observation as its strongest field validation merely because the waveform resembles what the model could produce.

Parkfield is a stronger constraint. If stress-activated crustal currents produce detectable pre-earthquake magnetic or electric effects for magnitude-six events under common California geology, the dense Parkfield network should have had a chance to see them. It did not. A rock-battery explanation can survive if it predicts that Parkfield's rock type, source volume, conductivity or magnitude should suppress the effect. Those conditions need independent support.

This is the recurring price of survival: less universality, more prediction.

The atmospheric part of the theory faces an even larger scaling problem.

The fair-weather atmosphere carries a global electric circuit. Thunderstorms charge the ionosphere relative to the ground. Atmospheric conductivity varies with altitude, aerosols, cosmic rays, radon and weather. Local electric fields near the surface can be strongly distorted by terrain, vegetation and meteorological conditions. To perturb ionospheric plasma hundreds of kilometers above an impending earthquake, a crustal process must alter this already active electrical system by enough, over enough area and time, to be distinguished from ordinary variability.

Models show possible pathways. Possibility is the beginning of the work.

DEMETER and Swarm provide a field test in principle. If the rock battery affects the ionosphere, satellite anomalies should display predictable dependence on earthquake magnitude, depth, crustal composition, local time, geomagnetic conditions and distance from the source region. Perhaps the sign of the plasma-density change should depend on background electric fields. Perhaps conjugate effects should appear along magnetic field lines. A mechanism capable of making those predictions is more valuable than a list of anomalies.

The positive-hole framework sometimes reaches beyond what the evidence can comfortably hold. Freund and collaborators have proposed links to radon release, air ionization, infrared anomalies, electromagnetic emissions and animal behavior. A 2021 review by Freund, Guy Ouillon, John Scoville and Didier Sornette explicitly presented peroxy defects as a candidate unifying process for a broad class of pre-earthquake phenomena while also calling for systematic statistical testing.

That final demand is the important one.

Unification should be earned prospectively.

Suppose positive holes really are activated over a large crustal volume before some earthquakes. A decisive campaign could measure several outputs simultaneously: borehole and surface electric fields, vector magnetic fields, air-ion concentrations, radon, atmospheric electric field, infrared radiance and ionospheric plasma. The theory should specify the sequence.

Does ground current rise first? Does air ionization follow? Does the ionosphere respond only when meteorological conductivity permits? How do amplitudes scale with distance? Which channels should remain quiet?

Without such ordering, multimodal data become a buffet.

With ordering, the mechanism becomes vulnerable.

There is a second type of vulnerability: material specificity. Peroxy defects are associated particularly with igneous and high-grade metamorphic rocks. Earthquakes occur in varied lithologies, including sedimentary sequences and subduction settings. If the mechanism depends on peroxy-rich rocks, precursor occurrence should correlate with crustal composition. A global database could test that.

This would be a beautiful result even if prediction remained poor.

Imagine that magnetic precursor effects turn out to be reproducible only in fault systems cutting certain crystalline lithologies. That would explain part of the historical inconsistency. It would transform 'some earthquakes have electrical precursors' into a geochemical statement. Conversely, if claimed signals appear equally in geological settings where the proposed charge carriers should be scarce or disconnected, the theory loses explanatory power.

The same test applies to depth. Deep earthquakes in subduction zones occur under pressure-temperature conditions very different from shallow crustal events. Can the charge transport mechanism operate there? Can a surface signature survive the path? If not, the model should exclude those events before analysis.

Exclusions are scientifically valuable when they come first.

There is an uncomfortable cultural aspect to the rock-battery theory. Because Freund worked at NASA Ames and the SETI Institute and because the proposal touches earthquake lights, ionosphere anomalies and animal behavior, the theory circulates widely in popular and anomalist communities. Institutional affiliation can be used as a substitute for validation: NASA scientist says rocks produce earthquake electricity.

That is not how evidence works.

NASA affiliation establishes where some work was performed, not that NASA has endorsed earthquake prediction. Publication establishes that papers entered scientific scrutiny, not that the mechanism has become consensus. Laboratory current establishes current. It does not establish a forecast.

The distinction is especially important in fields adjacent to anomalous claims. Readers often look for authority tokens because the evidence is difficult to evaluate. A government lab, a patent, a classification stamp or a prestigious journal can become a social proof multiplier. This book treats those as provenance, not verdicts.

The strongest case for positive holes does not need authority tokens. It is experimental: stress certain rocks and electrical effects occur. The strongest case against promoting the theory to an earthquake warning system is equally empirical: there is not yet a broadly replicated prospective field record showing that those effects provide reliable short-term predictive information.

Both can be true.

The theory also intersects with earthquake lights, luminous phenomena reported around some earthquakes. Electrical discharge, rock fracture, atmospheric ionization and piezoelectric effects have all been proposed. Some earthquake-associated lights are well documented photographically; others are confused with power-line arcs, transformers, sky glow or retrospective reports. Positive-hole surface charge offers one candidate mechanism.

Again, the strongest experiment would not begin with a light.

Instrument an active region with all-sky cameras, electric-field mills, spectrometers, lightning mapping and power-grid telemetry. Define luminous event classes before earthquakes. Determine whether any class increases before or during seismic events after ordinary electrical failures are removed. Then ask whether positive-hole theory predicts the observed spectra and fields.

The sequence—observation first, mechanism second—is easy to say and hard to maintain when the mechanism is elegant.

Elegance exerts pressure backward. Once you know a rock battery could produce air ionization, an old story about strange animal behavior begins to look electrical. Once a model can perturb the ionosphere, a TEC anomaly becomes confirmation. The theory starts collecting phenomena.

A better habit is to make it spend predictions.

Every time the theory explains a new class, ask what it excludes. If it explains radon, does it predict the sign and timing? If it explains magnetic fields, does it predict polarization? If it explains animal behavior, which sensory systems and species should respond? If it explains infrared emission, which wavelengths and surface geology?

A theory that grows only by explanation becomes folklore with equations.

A theory that grows by failed predictions can become physics.

The rock battery is worth a chapter because it is one of the few proposed bridges from microscopic mineral defects to macroscopic precursor observations. It gives the field something more specific than 'stress does something electromagnetic.' It can be taken seriously enough to test hard.

It also sharpens the book's central distinction.

Even if positive-hole activation is real in the crust before some earthquakes, the effect might be small and conditional. It might change magnetic noise distributions without producing a clear alarm. It might alter atmospheric electricity only under particular weather. It might be scientifically real and operationally disappointing.

That outcome is not failure.

It would mean the Earth does change before some ruptures, but the change does not carry enough information for prophecy.

The history of earthquake prediction has spent too long treating those as the same result.
