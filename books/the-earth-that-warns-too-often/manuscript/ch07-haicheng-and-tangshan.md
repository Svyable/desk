# Haicheng and Tangshan

Earthquake prediction acquired one of its most durable legends in northeastern China in February 1975.

On February 4, a magnitude 7.3 earthquake struck near Haicheng in Liaoning Province. Chinese authorities had issued warnings and evacuations before the mainshock. Buildings collapsed, but many people were outside. The event entered the global literature as the rare example of a damaging earthquake successfully predicted in time to save lives.

Eighteen months later, on July 28, 1976, a much larger earthquake devastated Tangshan. No comparable successful short-term warning preceded it. Hundreds of thousands of people died by official and later historical estimates that vary substantially with source and counting method.

Haicheng and Tangshan sit beside each other in the history of prediction like a controlled experiment designed by a cruel statistician.

They were not controlled. That is the point.

The Haicheng warning emerged from a complicated mixture of long-term concern, foreshock activity, official monitoring and reports of unusual phenomena. The region had been recognized as seismically concerning before February 1975. In the days and hours before the mainshock, an intense foreshock sequence occurred. Local authorities took actions that included moving people out of vulnerable buildings.

This is important because foreshocks are seismology, not anomaly folklore.

A foreshock is an earthquake that happens before a larger earthquake. It becomes a foreshock only in retrospect; if no larger event follows, it was simply an earthquake. A strong swarm or escalating sequence can raise short-term concern, but most small earthquakes are not followed by large ones. The operational challenge is the same base-rate problem that appears with electromagnetic signals.

Haicheng's apparent success therefore does not prove that unusual animal behavior, groundwater changes or electromagnetic effects predicted the mainshock. It does show that, under some circumstances, a conspicuous seismic sequence combined with preexisting concern can trigger a useful public response.

The distinction was often lost as the story traveled.

Accounts of Haicheng accumulated reports of snakes emerging from winter hibernation, livestock acting strangely, well-water changes and other anomalies. Some may have been genuinely reported before the earthquake. Others are difficult to reconstruct prospectively from the surviving record. Political conditions in 1970s China complicate source evaluation. Local campaigns encouraged mass observation of earthquake precursors. That produced enormous observational coverage and an enormous multiple-comparisons problem.

If millions of people are instructed to notice unusual natural phenomena, unusual natural phenomena will be noticed every day.

The Earth is full of animals behaving strangely, wells changing level, lights appearing, radios crackling and people feeling uneasy. To establish predictive value, one needs the denominator: how often were these observations reported without a damaging earthquake?

Tangshan supplied the harsher denominator. Whatever combination had worked at Haicheng did not produce an effective warning there.

This does not make Haicheng meaningless. It changes the question from 'Can earthquakes be predicted?' to 'What information existed at Haicheng that was absent, weaker or misinterpreted at Tangshan?'

Foreshocks are the first candidate.

Some large earthquakes are preceded by obvious foreshock sequences. Many are not. Even when foreshocks occur, distinguishing them prospectively from ordinary clustered seismicity is difficult. The information is probabilistic. A significant nearby earthquake or swarm can raise the conditional probability of a larger event, but usually not enough to justify deterministic language.

This is a recurring pattern in the book. Nature may provide risk information without providing prophecy.

Haicheng also highlights the role of institutional readiness. An imperfect signal can save lives if the cost of precaution is low and vulnerability is high. Telling people to leave brittle buildings for a winter evening has costs. Those costs may be acceptable if authorities already believe short-term risk is elevated. Evacuating a modern megacity for every ambiguous electromagnetic anomaly is a different decision.

The value of a precursor cannot be separated from the action it triggers.

This makes the simple true-positive/false-positive framework incomplete. Suppose a warning system produces frequent alarms. If the recommended response is to inspect emergency communications, the false-alarm cost is low. If the response is to evacuate hospitals, stop trains and shut factories, the cost is enormous. The same likelihood ratio can support one action and not the other.

Prediction researchers sometimes resist this framing because it seems to move the goalposts from physics to policy. But the public meaning of 'prediction' is already policy-laden. A signal becomes socially consequential only through decisions.

Tangshan adds another institutional lesson: successful cases can create overconfidence in a method whose conditionality is not understood.

After a success, people naturally extract the features that appear to have mattered. The danger is survivorship bias. If Haicheng had been followed by ten equally well-instrumented warnings that failed, it would be interpreted differently. Tangshan arrived too soon to allow that illusion to become permanent, but the myth of a uniquely successful prediction survived because successful interventions are psychologically sticky.

The same happens in medicine and intelligence. A technique is credited for the one crisis it anticipated and quietly excused for the crises it missed. The stronger the narrative, the harder the denominator has to work.

Chinese earthquake science did not stop after Tangshan. China built extensive monitoring programs and later invested in space-based seismo-electromagnetic research, including the China Seismo-Electromagnetic Satellite program. The continuity matters. A state can learn from prediction failure by changing instruments rather than abandoning the question.

But the political environment surrounding prediction can shape the science.

Mass campaigns can generate observations unavailable to a small laboratory. They can also generate confirmation pressure. If officials expect precursors, local observers may overreport. If failure has political consequences, negative data may be underemphasized. These are not uniquely Chinese problems. Every institution has incentives. Commercial firms want their detector to work. Academic groups want publishable anomalies. Government agencies want to avoid both panic and blame.

An evidence ledger should therefore include incentive structure alongside instrument specifications.

Haicheng also complicates the relationship between traditional observations and modern sensors. Reports of animal behavior are often dismissed because they are anecdotal. Yet animals possess sensory systems humans do not. Fish detect pressure changes. Many species sense low-frequency vibration. Some animals detect electric or magnetic fields. A real animal response to a preseismic environmental change is biologically conceivable.

Conceivable is cheap.

To turn animal behavior into evidence, one needs continuous objective monitoring before many earthquakes and during long control periods. Video, accelerometers, feeding logs, movement sensors and environmental measurements can replace memories of dogs barking or snakes appearing. The question is not whether an animal could sense something. It is whether measurable behavior changes prospectively, consistently and specifically before earthquakes.

Modern livestock and wildlife telemetry makes that experiment possible in a way it was not in 1975.

The same is true for wells. Groundwater levels and chemistry can change in response to strain, pressure and permeability. Coseismic hydrological effects are well established. Preseismic changes have been reported. But rainfall, pumping, tides, barometric pressure and seasonal hydrology also move well levels. A single well behaving strangely before a major earthquake is a discovery case. A network with hydrologic controls can become science.

Haicheng's historical mixture of foreshocks, wells, animals and social observation is therefore not embarrassing clutter. It is a prototype of a multi-sensor problem.

The mistake is to count every channel as an independent vote.

If one physical process changes groundwater, gas emission and animal behavior, those channels are correlated. If one weather event changes groundwater and animal behavior, they are also correlated. A system that says 'three anomalies occurred' without modeling dependence exaggerates evidence.

The modern analogue is combining magnetometers, radon detectors, GPS, thermal infrared and ionospheric data. Multimodal convergence sounds powerful, but only if the channels fail independently under the null.

Haicheng also invites a counterfactual that is impossible to settle: how many lives were saved specifically because of prediction rather than because people were already reacting to foreshocks?

This distinction matters for evaluating the transferability of the case. If the evacuation depended mainly on an obvious local foreshock sequence, Haicheng supports aggressive response to certain seismic swarms, not a general precursor system. If nonseismic observations materially improved the decision, their prospective record matters. Historical accounts differ in emphasis.

The safest classification is layered.

Documented: a damaging earthquake occurred on February 4, 1975; warnings and evacuations took place before it; substantial foreshock activity occurred; casualties were lower than they might have been under full building occupancy.

Credible but incomplete: a wider set of monitored anomalies and local reports contributed to the warning process.

Disputed or hard to validate retrospectively: the predictive value of specific animal, electrical, groundwater or other claimed anomalies.

Speculative synthesis: Haicheng demonstrated a general short-term earthquake-prediction capability.

Tangshan makes that last claim especially difficult.

This four-class structure may feel overly legalistic for a dramatic event. It is necessary because 'Haicheng was predicted' compresses several propositions into one sentence.

The history of earthquake prediction is full of such compressed sentences.

'Loma Prieta had a magnetic precursor.' The instrument recorded an anomaly; later reanalysis attributed it to malfunction.

'VAN predicted Greek earthquakes.' Telegrams were sent and claimed matches exist; scoring and signal classification remained contested.

'DEMETER detected ionospheric precursors.' Statistical studies report pre-earthquake perturbations; causal attribution and operational prediction remain unsettled.

'Google found earthquake signals.' A 2022 case-control study found a modest magnetic association in held-out data; the authors explicitly said it was not useful for prediction.

Decompression is epistemology.

Haicheng and Tangshan also show why governments are often more conservative about prediction than individual researchers. An agency does not only evaluate whether a signal is interesting. It inherits the consequences of being believed.

After the 2009 L'Aquila earthquake in Italy, public controversy and criminal proceedings focused not on a successful or failed electromagnetic precursor but on risk communication around a seismic swarm and statements made after a meeting of experts. The case was legally complex, and most convictions of scientists were later overturned, but it became a global warning about the social terrain around earthquake advice.

Scientists do not control how probabilistic statements are heard.

A precursor field that wants operational relevance therefore needs communication rules before it needs dramatic press releases. What probability threshold changes advice? Who issues the message? How is uncertainty stated? What action is recommended? How are false alarms explained? What happens when channels disagree?

Haicheng's authoritarian political context allowed rapid coordinated action that is difficult to reproduce in pluralistic societies. That does not make the science better or worse. It changes the decision system surrounding the science.

One can imagine a future electromagnetic precursor with modest but real predictive value. The most dangerous mistake would be to announce, 'An earthquake will strike.' The more honest output might be, 'A predeclared multi-station feature associated in prior data with elevated short-term earthquake probability has been detected; current estimated probability of an M6+ event within this region over 72 hours is X, compared with baseline Y.'

That sentence is less exciting. It is also usable.

The Haicheng legend survived partly because it offers the opposite kind of sentence: they knew, they warned, people lived.

Real systems are rarely so merciful.

Tangshan is the correction written into the same national history. It says that one successful warning does not establish a transferable predictor. It says that earthquakes with different preparatory behavior may occur under the same institutional program. It says that the cost of assuming universality can be catastrophic.

But Tangshan should not be used to prove the impossibility of all precursors either. A method can have sensitivity below one hundred percent. Some fault ruptures may genuinely have foreshock sequences while others do not. The scientific task is to discover conditionality without using conditionality as an excuse.

That requires larger samples than history likes to provide.

This is where global data become essential. Instead of arguing endlessly over Haicheng, assemble thousands of earthquake sequences and matched controls. Quantify foreshock rates by tectonic setting, magnitude and depth. Do the same for hydrology, radon, magnetic data and ionospheric parameters where continuous records exist. Freeze the analysis. Test future events.

The romantic era of the singular prediction needs to give way to population science.

Haicheng remains important because it shows the upper bound of what people hope for: a warning early enough to matter.

Tangshan remains important because it shows the cost of believing a warning system exists before its boundaries are known.

Between them is the entire problem.
