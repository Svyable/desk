# Corralitos

At 5:04 in the afternoon on October 17, 1989, the Santa Cruz Mountains moved.

The Loma Prieta earthquake ruptured a segment of the San Andreas fault system beneath northern California. It killed people, collapsed part of the Cypress Street Viaduct in Oakland, damaged the Bay Bridge and interrupted a World Series whose television audience made the shaking instantly national. In the years that followed, one of the earthquake's quieter details acquired a life of its own: a magnetometer seven kilometers from the epicenter had behaved strangely before the rupture.

The instrument site was near Corralitos. A Stanford group led by Antony Fraser-Smith had been measuring low-frequency magnetic noise as part of work that was not originally built around the expectation of catching this particular earthquake. Their system covered narrow frequency bands across an enormous range, from ultra-low-frequency measurements below a hertz up through much higher ELF and VLF bands. The proximity of the Corralitos ULF instrument to the eventual epicenter made the record irresistible after the fact.

In a 1990 *Geophysical Research Letters* paper, Fraser-Smith and colleagues described several features they considered anomalous. A narrow-band signal around 0.05 to 0.2 hertz appeared roughly a month before the earthquake. Beginning on October 5, the background noise increased over much of the ULF range. About a day before the earthquake, part of the spectrum dipped. Then, approximately three hours before rupture, activity between roughly 0.01 and 0.5 hertz rose to an exceptionally high level.

The researchers were cautious in the formal language. The features 'may have been magnetic precursors.' They looked for obvious alternatives. Upper-atmospheric magnetic activity did not appear to explain the largest increase. There had been no significant local foreshocks that would simply shake the instrument. The higher-frequency Stanford system, located much farther from the epicenter, did not show a corresponding precursor.

For anyone interested in electromagnetic earthquake prediction, Corralitos looked like the case you would choose if you could design history.

The station was close. The earthquake was large. The anomaly evolved over multiple timescales. The largest burst came only hours before the rupture. The data were recorded before the earthquake, so the waveform itself was not invented afterward. And the observation appeared in a mainstream geophysical journal.

This is how a canonical case is born.

The trouble with canonical cases is that the story tends to freeze at the moment of publication.

A scientific observation has at least two lives. In the first, it is the surprising feature that survives the researchers' initial checks. In the second, other people ask what the instrument did during all the boring times when nothing happened.

That second life is where Corralitos changed.

The original claim involved an anomaly defined relative to the researchers' understanding of the background. But a rare event can make ordinary variation look extraordinary if the baseline window is too short or poorly characterized. To know whether the pre-Loma Prieta signal was unusual in the way that matters, one needs a long record before and after the earthquake, instrument logs, comparison stations and some way to distinguish local sensor problems from geophysical sources.

Jeremy Thomas, Jeffrey Love and Malcolm Johnston undertook that reanalysis years later. Their 2009 paper in *Physics of the Earth and Planetary Interiors* examined twenty-one months of Corralitos data from January 1989 through October 1990, along with the station logbook. They also compared the record with magnetic data from Japan, the Fresno observatory and the global Kp geomagnetic index.

Their conclusion was not gentle. They argued that the famous anomaly was an artifact of sensor-system malfunction.

The evidence came from context rather than one dramatic counterexample. Noise-level changes occurred not only during the claimed precursor period but at other times long before and after the earthquake. The station record contained operational problems. Features in the Corralitos data did not behave like a stable natural magnetic signal when compared with independent magnetic records.

In other words, the anomaly became less singular when the window widened.

This is one of the most important methodological moves in all of precursor research. A prospective-looking measurement can still produce a retrospective illusion because the event determines which segment of the measurement record receives attention.

The instrument really recorded what it recorded. The earthquake really occurred nearby. The error, if Thomas and colleagues are right, lay in the causal bridge.

This distinction is worth slowing down for because public debates about anomalies often flatten everything into 'real' or 'fake.' Corralitos was not a fabricated graph. The original researchers did not need to invent the magnetic activity. An instrument can honestly produce an unusual trace for reasons that have nothing to do with the phenomenon the scientists hope to study.

That is why calibration history is evidence.

A malfunctioning sensor is not a boring footnote to an exotic claim. It is a competing physical hypothesis. The sensor has electronics, feedback loops, power supplies, temperature response and mechanical installation. Those components live in the same universe as the fault.

The strongest anomaly research treats the apparatus as another natural system that can surprise you.

Corralitos also demonstrates the importance of negative channels. The Stanford ELF/VLF system did not show an identifiable precursor. This was reported in the original paper. Negative channels are easy to demote because every instrument has different sensitivity and distance. But they help constrain mechanism. If the source produced broad electromagnetic emissions, why did one band see dramatic activity and another not? A plausible answer may exist. The theory has to give it.

The same demand applies spatially. A local crustal source should generally have some distance dependence. If only one station sees the event, proximity supports a local source, but it also makes a local instrumental or cultural source harder to exclude. A network turns the ambiguity into geometry.

This is why modern designs use multiple nearby stations, remote references, GPS timing and cross-spectral methods. The lesson did not require knowing in advance that Corralitos was wrong. It came from taking the positive case seriously enough to make future positive cases harder to fool.

The original Loma Prieta paper also teaches another lesson: timescale proliferation.

The reported features appeared on timescales from weeks to hours. This can be physically meaningful. Fault preparation, if it exists, may unfold through stages. But each additional timescale enlarges the search space. An investigator examining thirty days of multiband data before an earthquake can find rising power, falling power, bursts, quiet intervals, spectral shifts and polarization changes. If the relevant feature is not declared beforehand, almost any large earthquake can acquire a distinctive electromagnetic prehistory.

The problem becomes severe when the anomaly has no fixed sign. Some studies report increases. Others report decreases. Some report horizontal components. Others use vertical-to-horizontal ratios. Some use absolute power. Others use polarization, entropy or spectral slope. Flexibility can be justified by different mechanisms. It can also protect the hypothesis from failure.

A mature precursor theory should eventually narrow the possibilities.

Corralitos became a template in another way. Later papers cited the Loma Prieta result as precedent for ULF precursors around earthquakes elsewhere. The original observation acquired authority through repetition. By the time the 2009 reanalysis appeared, the case had already seeded a literature.

This is normal science. Citation chains are how fields remember. But anomaly research is unusually vulnerable to what I think of as inherited uncertainty. A new paper says its result is 'consistent with previous ULF precursor observations' and cites Loma Prieta. The reader sees multiple cases and feels convergence. If the foundational case later weakens, the conceptual weight of the later papers does not automatically update.

Scientific memory has no garbage collector.

One purpose of this book's evidence ledger is to perform that update manually. Corralitos belongs in the documented-fact category as a recorded magnetic anomaly before Loma Prieta. The original interpretation that it represented a crustal precursor was credible enough to motivate research, but it is now strongly challenged by a detailed reanalysis attributing the behavior to sensor-system problems. It should not be counted today as clean independent evidence for electromagnetic earthquake precursors.

That classification is more informative than calling the episode 'debunked.'

The case remains scientifically valuable because it shows what a plausible false positive looks like.

The false positive had several qualities we often use to trust anomalies. It occurred before the event. It was recorded instrumentally. It was spatially close. It was reported by serious researchers. It had apparent temporal structure. Obvious solar and seismic alternatives were considered. None of those safeguards was sufficient.

The missing safeguard was a fuller model of the instrument over time.

Imagine applying the same standard to modern satellite precursor claims. A plasma-density excursion occurs five days before a magnitude-seven earthquake. The satellite data are impeccable. The anomaly is real. The researchers control for Kp geomagnetic activity. What is the equivalent of the Corralitos logbook?

It includes the satellite's instrument health, orbit, local time, seasonal ionospheric climatology, solar flux, storms, substorms, lightning, tides, lower-atmosphere waves, radio transmitters and the frequency with which similar excursions occur without earthquakes. The object being calibrated is no longer one sensor. It is the entire near-Earth environment.

This makes satellite work harder, not easier.

The Corralitos reanalysis also raises a delicate question about how skeptics should behave. If one famous positive case collapses, it is tempting to generalize: electromagnetic precursors are artifacts. USGS today says there is no convincing evidence of electromagnetic precursors despite decades of study, a position supported by multiple negative or critical analyses. But Corralitos can only directly settle Corralitos.

The proper update is narrower. A class of ULF evidence once considered strong lost weight. Instrumental explanations deserve more prior probability in future single-station cases. Long baselines and independent stations become mandatory. Claims that cite Loma Prieta as established precedent should be revised. None of that proves that no fault ever produces a preseismic electromagnetic effect.

That last possibility is what keeps the subject alive.

The 2022 Google/QuakeFinder analysis is interesting partly because it was constructed as an answer to the single-station problem. Its key feature used cross-power between pairs of nearby magnetometers. A local malfunction at one station should not produce coherent spectral power at another station kilometers away. The study also used a train/test split by time and a case-control design. It did not rely on identifying a Loma Prieta-like hero trace.

This is what scientific progress can look like when a famous result fails: the next experiment is built around the failure mode.

There is still plenty to criticize in the later study. The earthquake sample is small. The effect is modest. The frequency features are not tied to a settled mechanism. Site pairs and event-selection rules create complexity. A statistical difference between windows does not tell us what caused it. But those are different weaknesses from the Corralitos weakness.

The apparatus has learned something.

The emotional shape of the Loma Prieta story is familiar in anomaly research. A strong case appears. The case gains cultural significance. Years later, a more exhaustive analysis erodes it. Believers see moving goalposts; skeptics see inevitable correction. Both reactions can miss the useful part.

The useful part is the anatomy of the error.

There was a particular sensor system at a particular place with a particular operational history. The anomaly did not vanish because a committee voted against it. It changed status because additional data provided a better competing explanation.

That is how controversial evidence should become less controversial.

A parallel can be drawn to medicine. If a biomarker spikes before a disease diagnosis, the association may be real but noncausal. If the assay drifts, the signal may be instrumental. If the marker also spikes frequently in healthy controls, it may be nonspecific. The correct response is not to ridicule biomarkers. It is to run controls.

Earthquake science has unusually difficult controls because one cannot randomize faults. But time supplies controls in abundance. Every day without a large earthquake is a control day. Every distant station is a control station. Every frequency band not predicted to respond can be a control channel. Every earthquake outside the proposed sensitivity region can be a control event.

A robust precursor should survive being surrounded by controls.

Corralitos did not.

The reanalysis also makes the phrase 'anomalous' look different. An anomaly is not an ontological category. It means deviation from an expected distribution. Change the distribution and the anomaly can disappear.

This is a subtle source of conflict between discoverers and auditors. The discoverer remembers the shock of seeing the trace before a historic earthquake. The auditor sees twenty-one months of traces and asks how often comparable shocks occur. Both are looking at the same data from different temporal scales.

Discovery needs the first view. Validation needs the second.

The best systems institutionalize both. A monitoring network can maintain an automated anomaly detector that does not know when earthquakes occur. Every anomaly is logged. Separately, an evaluation pipeline tests whether anomaly rates change before earthquakes compared with matched control periods. Researchers can still inspect individual cases for mechanism, but those cases no longer determine whether the association exists.

That architecture removes some of the emotional investment from any one trace.

It also prevents a future Loma Prieta from becoming too important.

This is not hypothetical. Large earthquakes are attention machines. When one occurs near a sensor network, analysts immediately search every channel: magnetic field, electric field, groundwater, radon, GPS, thermal infrared, ionosphere, weather, animal behavior. The search is scientifically reasonable. It is also statistically guaranteed to find something unusual because natural systems are full of unusual things.

The discipline is to treat the found anomaly as a candidate feature for the next earthquakes, not as proof about the one that just happened.

Fraser-Smith's group did not have another Loma Prieta waiting on command. No earthquake researcher does. That scarcity is part of the tragedy of the field. Every large event is precious data, and the desire to extract meaning from it is overwhelming.

But rarity does not relax the rules. It makes them more important.

There is a final irony in the Corralitos story. The original anomaly has been weakened so substantially that it no longer serves well as evidence for prediction. Yet the episode did more for rigorous electromagnetic monitoring than a clean positive might have done. It taught later researchers to worry about long baselines, station health, remote references and local noise. It supplied a concrete failure mode.

A false precursor can be a real scientific instrument.

The station at Corralitos did not warn California. It warned the field about itself.
