# The Bomb in the Salt Dome

The road into one of the strangest nuclear experiments in the United States did not lead to Nevada.

It led into the pine country of southern Mississippi.

Near Hattiesburg, beneath a 1,470-acre tract later known as the Salmon site, sits the Tatum Salt Dome. In the 1960s the Atomic Energy Commission and Department of Defense selected it for Project Dribble, part of Vela Uniform. The purpose was not to test a new battlefield weapon. It was to test a possibility that frightened treaty designers: an underground explosion might be deliberately muffled.

The idea of seismic decoupling is almost offensively simple.

A tightly coupled explosion detonated in rock drives strong stress into the surrounding medium. Put the same explosion inside a sufficiently large underground cavity and the gas can expand into empty space before loading the walls. Less energy couples into seismic waves. To a distant network, the explosion can appear smaller than its actual yield.

If that reduction is large enough, a treaty threshold can be cheated.

Project Dribble turned the evasion hypothesis into a physical experiment.

On October 22, 1964, the United States detonated Salmon, a 5.3-kiloton nuclear device 2,710 feet below ground, roughly 1,200 feet below the top of the salt dome. The shot created an underground cavity. On December 3, 1966, Sterling, with a yield of about 380 tons, was detonated inside the cavity Salmon had made. The comparison was designed to help determine how much a cavity could reduce the seismic signature of a later explosion.

The comparison was not merely qualitative. A later technical review of the U.S. decoupling program reports a maximum decoupling factor of about 70, with an uncertainty of plus or minus 20, for Sterling. In this usage the factor compares seismic amplitude from a normally coupled explosion with the amplitude when the yield is fired in a cavity. A factor near seventy is not a subtle nuisance in a detector. It is the reason the problem was taken seriously.

But Sterling also supplied the counterargument to the cleanest version of the evasion story. The Salmon cavity had a radius of about 17.4 metres, and investigators already knew it was too small for complete decoupling of the Sterling shot; the later review says full decoupling would have required roughly sixty percent more cavity volume. The famous experiment was therefore not a demonstration of some frictionless maximum. It was an engineered, imperfect case whose geometry mattered.

The choice of salt was not arbitrary. Salt is mechanically unusual, relatively homogeneous compared with many rock masses, and capable of forming large domal structures. It had already attracted interest for underground nuclear work. For Vela Uniform, the dome offered a place where source-medium interactions could be measured with fewer geological complications than a fractured mountain range.

The experiment also produced a lesson no treaty negotiator could enjoy: concealment has engineering requirements.

A cavity large enough to decouple an explosion has to exist before the clandestine shot. It must remain stable. The device has to be emplaced. The cavity's dimensions have to fit the yield. Construction or precursor explosions can create their own signatures. Geology constrains where the trick is possible. The evader may reduce one observable and create others.

That does not make decoupling irrelevant. It turns it from magic into a costed scenario.

Verification becomes more mature when evasion is treated this way. The useful question is not “can a test be hidden?” in the abstract. Almost any detection system can be defeated under some contrived condition. The useful question is what an adversary must build, where it can be built, what yield can be concealed, what preparations are observable, what residual channels remain, and how much confidence the evader has that the whole sequence will work.

Project Dribble supplied real data to that calculation.

The 1967 U.S. Geological Survey report is revealing in its plainness. Sterling was compared not only with Salmon but with a small high-explosive shot in a nearby drill hole. USGS, the Coast and Geodetic Survey, and the Air Force Technical Applications Center made surface seismic measurements under Lawrence Radiation Laboratory coordination. The problem was being attacked as a calibration problem: sources, spectra, amplitudes, geometry.

After Sterling, the cavity was used again for two large methane-oxygen chemical explosions, Diode Tube in 1969 and Humid Water in 1970, each with a yield of roughly 315 tons. These experiments extended the calibration work without additional nuclear detonations. The site became a laboratory for the physics of source coupling, not merely a scar from one test.

The DOE's current fact sheets are almost bureaucratically calm about this history. They record depths, yields, cleanup, residual contamination, and stewardship responsibilities. That tone is useful. It returns a subject often inflated into secrecy lore to measurable facts.

Salmon happened. Sterling happened. Their purposes were tied to Vela Uniform and seismic verification. The cavity-decoupling problem was serious enough to justify nuclear experimentation. Those facts do not prove that any foreign state successfully used large-cavity decoupling to hide a clandestine nuclear test.

This distinction is important because verification research often gets cited backwards.

A government studies an evasion method; therefore somebody must have used it.

A laboratory models a low-yield clandestine test; therefore there is evidence one occurred.

A sensor program is funded; therefore the target threat exists at the implied scale.

None of those inferences is valid automatically. Defense and verification organizations study possibilities precisely because uncertainty is dangerous. A program can be evidence that experts considered a scenario plausible enough to spend money on. It is not evidence that the scenario occurred operationally.

Project Dribble is stronger as an experimental control than as a conspiracy ingredient.

It shows what serious work on hiding a nuclear test looks like. There are yields, cavity dimensions, geologies, shot dates, instrument deployments, seismic records, post-shot drilling, environmental consequences, and later technical reports. The problem leaves an administrative and physical tail.

That is useful when evaluating modern allegations of perfectly hidden nuclear activities. Secrecy can hide intent and data. It has more difficulty erasing civil engineering.

The salt dome also reveals the asymmetry between the detector and the evader. A monitoring network must work everywhere. An evader needs one favorable site. This asymmetry drives much of the technical anxiety in arms control.

Imagine a global seismic threshold that reliably detects normally coupled explosions down to a certain yield. If decoupling suppresses seismic amplitude by a large factor under ideal conditions, the evader may move a materially larger test below the nominal threshold. The monitor cannot dismiss the possibility just because most geology is unfavorable. It must understand the edge case.

At the same time, the evader faces its own asymmetry. The concealment scheme has to succeed on the first politically consequential attempt. The monitoring community can improve after every calibration shot, earthquake, mine blast, and suspected event. Knowledge accumulates on both sides, but the monitor's archive is global.

This is why the history of nuclear verification has a strange experimental richness. The system learns not only from nuclear tests but from chemical explosions, natural earthquakes, mining events, cavity collapses, and controlled source experiments. NNSA's modern Source Physics Experiment program continues that tradition. It uses nonnuclear explosions and dense instrumentation to improve models of how source conditions map into observable signals.

The goal is not simply to lower a detection threshold. It is to make the inverse problem less fragile.

If analysts know how emplacement depth, geology, cavity geometry, and source type alter waveforms, they can assign more realistic likelihoods to ambiguous events. That makes claims harder to manipulate politically because the uncertainty has a physical basis.

Project Dribble also complicates a popular story about test-ban verification: that once underground explosions were detectable, a comprehensive ban became technically easy and politics alone stood in the way.

The technical problem never disappeared. It moved.

Large, normally coupled tests became easy to detect. The frontier shifted to smaller yields, unusual geologies, evasive configurations, chemical-explosion confusion, and attribution. Better instruments reduced one uncertainty and exposed another.

That pattern recurs throughout this book. Technology does not deliver “verification” as a binary state. It reshapes the region of ambiguity.

The Tatum Salt Dome is a literal monument to that fact.

A bomb was detonated to make a cavity. Another bomb was detonated inside the cavity to see how much quieter it could become. Scientists listened from far away. Then they compared what the ground had said.

The experiment was not about destruction.

It was about the size of a lie physics might permit.
