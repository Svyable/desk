# Below Hearing

The atmosphere carries a second weather that humans cannot hear.

Pressure waves below about twenty hertz move through air too slowly in frequency for ordinary hearing, though sufficiently strong infrasound can be felt or can interact with structures. Large explosions produce it. So do volcanoes, meteors, ocean waves, storms, avalanches, earthquakes, rockets, aircraft, and some auroral processes. The atmosphere is full of events writing pressure into frequencies below conversation.

Nuclear-test monitoring learned to listen there.

The International Monitoring System is designed to include sixty infrasound stations. Each station typically uses an array of microbarometers spread across a site rather than one pressure sensor. The geometry allows analysts to estimate the direction and apparent velocity of an arriving wave and reject some local noise. Pipes or porous-hose systems can average wind fluctuations that would otherwise overwhelm the faint coherent pressure signal.

The instrument is sensitive enough that weather becomes part of the telescope.

Infrasound does not travel through a static atmosphere. Wind and temperature vary with altitude, season, latitude, and time. Those profiles bend acoustic rays. The stratosphere can create ducts that carry sound over continental distances. Other conditions create shadow zones. A station may hear one explosion clearly in winter and struggle with a similar source in summer because high-altitude winds reversed.

That dependence is not a defect to be calibrated away once. It is the medium.

Atmospheric models therefore sit inside event interpretation. Analysts ask not only when a wave arrived but whether the atmosphere could have carried it from the proposed source to the station. Multiple arrays create intersections of back-azimuths. Travel times constrain distance. Frequency content and signal duration add information about source physics.

An atmospheric nuclear explosion would be an obvious infrasound source. Underground explosions can also couple energy into the atmosphere through ground motion, venting, or surface displacement, though far less efficiently and in ways sensitive to depth and local geology.

That weak coupling became scientifically useful in comparisons of North Korean tests. CTBTO-affiliated research on the 2013 and January 2016 tests examined differences in observed infrasound and atmospheric propagation. One hypothesis was that the 2016 test occurred deeper, leading to less coupling through the lithosphere-atmosphere boundary. The inference was tentative, but the method is interesting: combine a solid-Earth sensor with an atmospheric sensor to learn about source depth that either system alone constrains poorly.

The boundary between media becomes an instrument.

This is a recurring theme in verification. The strongest evidence often appears where an event is forced to cross a physical interface. A buried explosion shakes the surface. A maritime blast launches acoustic energy and may perturb the ionosphere. A seismic wave reaches an island and converts into a T phase. A radioactive noble gas escapes rock and becomes an atmospheric tracer.

Interfaces leak information.

The infrasound network's most famous non-nuclear detection arrived on February 15, 2013, when a meteoroid entered the atmosphere over Russia near Chelyabinsk. The blast shattered windows and injured people. Far away, CTBTO stations registered low-frequency pressure waves circling the globe. The event was detected by twenty infrasound stations in the network then operating. One of the farthest detections came from Antarctica roughly fifteen thousand kilometers away.

At the time, it was the strongest event recorded by the IMS infrasound network.

A system built to detect atomic explosions had become one of the best instruments on Earth for measuring a natural atmospheric explosion from space.

This was not merely a publicity opportunity. Infrasound provided independent data for estimating the event's energy and trajectory. Meteoroid science gained a distributed sensor whose stations had known responses, precise timing, and global geometry. The treaty mission gained a spectacular calibration event in a source regime relevant to false alarms.

Again, nuisance and science were the same data viewed from different questions.

The 2013 meteor also demonstrates why source classification requires more than amplitude. A very large natural explosion in the atmosphere can exceed many human explosive events. If a monitoring system equated size with military origin, Chelyabinsk would be catastrophic evidence of a war nobody fought.

Direction, timing, source trajectory, waveform structure, satellite observations, seismic coupling, and eyewitness evidence all matter.

The world supplies multimodal context for free if analysts are willing to integrate it.

Infrasound has an unusual place in public imagination because it occupies frequencies people do not perceive directly. Invisible or inaudible signals attract mythology. Mysterious hums, physiological claims, secret weapons, atmospheric anomalies, and unexplained booms all get pulled toward the term.

The verification community provides a useful antidote to both dismissal and fantasy. Infrasound is real, measurable, propagates globally under favorable conditions, and can be generated by extraordinary events. Its propagation is also modelable, noisy, directional, and constrained by atmosphere. A pressure signal is not made more interesting by calling it mysterious. It becomes interesting when arrays agree on its direction, frequency structure, travel path, and source time.

This is the evidentiary standard anomalous acoustics often lacks.

A person reports a boom. Another person hundreds of kilometers away reports a boom. Social media connects them into one event. That may be correct, but without synchronized sensors it is difficult to know whether the experiences were physically related. An infrasound array converts narrative coincidence into measurable wavefront coherence.

The technology therefore sits at the boundary between folklore and geophysics.

Large meteors are one example. Volcanic eruptions are another. Explosive eruptions produce infrasound that can be detected remotely even when clouds hide visual observations. Rocket launches create distinctive signals. Large chemical explosions appear clearly. The Beirut port explosion on August 4, 2020, was recorded by the IMS. Each event improves the catalog of what non-nuclear large explosions look like.

That catalog matters for verification because the target event is rare.

A global nuclear test ban, when largely respected, produces a statistical paradox. The network succeeds and therefore sees fewer true positives. Maintaining discrimination skill then depends on surrogate sources, historical tests, chemical explosions, and natural events. A successful security regime can starve its sensors of the very data class they were built to recognize.

Calibration has to become creative.

This is one reason national programs continue high-explosive experiments and why the CTBTO organizes science-and-technology work around natural and anthropogenic sources. Meteors, volcanoes, earthquakes, and industrial explosions are not replacements for nuclear tests, but they exercise pieces of the sensing chain: propagation, timing, association, array performance, model error, and analyst response.

Infrasound also illustrates the importance of network growth. Chelyabinsk was detected by twenty stations out of the smaller network available in 2013. Later fireballs have been observed by larger fractions of a more complete system. As the number of stations rises, the same event gains more crossing lines of evidence.

Network completeness changes what can be known retrospectively.

If the Vela event had produced strong atmospheric infrasound in 1979, there was no mature global international network waiting to archive it. The absence of such data in the historical record is not evidence the event produced none. The instrument did not yet exist in the needed form.

This matters whenever historians apply modern expectations to old anomalies.

We should ask what sensors were available, where they were, what they recorded, what was saved, and what remains classified. An event cannot fail a test that history had not built.

The modern network changes the future baseline. A sufficiently large atmospheric explosion today is likely to leave pressure records in multiple countries. Those records are time-stamped and geographically distributed. Even if politics becomes confused, the air has witnesses.

Not ears in the human sense.

Better than ears.

Ears forget.
