# The Small Test Problem

Large explosions are generous witnesses.

They shake many stations, move air, sometimes couple into oceans, and create stronger chances for radioactive material to escape. Monitoring a large underground nuclear test at a known site is no longer the frontier problem it was in the 1960s.

The frontier moved downward.

Low-yield testing matters because a small experiment may still produce weapons-relevant information while presenting weaker signals to distant sensors. The event population around it is also more crowded. Small earthquakes are common. Mining and construction blasts are common. Military conventional explosions occur. Industrial accidents occur. At low enough magnitude, the planet's background stops being sparse.

Detection becomes a sorting problem.

The U.S. government says this explicitly in public budget documents. NNSA's nuclear detonation detection program funds work to improve geophysical models, seismic analysis, radionuclide detection, and capabilities for low-yield or evasively conducted underground nuclear explosions. Source Physics Experiment III is described as improving discrimination between underground explosions and natural earthquakes. Other Nevada test-bed work uses high explosives and dense sensors to understand low-yield signatures.

This is Class 1 evidence that the technical problem is active.

It is not evidence that Russia, China, the United States, or any other state is secretly conducting prohibited low-yield nuclear explosions.

That boundary matters because arms-control politics frequently cites research requirements as though they were intelligence findings. An agency budget says it needs better capability against evasive tests. A commentator rewrites that as “the government knows evasive tests are happening.” The inference is illegitimate unless separate evidence exists.

The small-test problem deserves seriousness without that inflation.

One reason is the scaling of observables. Seismic magnitude does not decrease linearly with yield in the intuitive way a layperson might expect, and coupling varies with geology. A small, well-coupled explosion near a station can be easier to detect than a larger, poorly coupled one farther away. Regional networks can see events global networks barely notice. Template matching against previous shots at the same site can detect signals below ordinary event-building thresholds.

Modern monitoring therefore uses historical memory as a sensor.

If a test site has produced earlier events, their waveforms reveal propagation paths through the same crust. A new weak signal can be cross-correlated with the template. The method can extract similarity from noise even when the event is too small for comfortable standalone classification.

The price is dependence on precedent. A first test at a new site lacks the template advantage.

This creates a strategic incentive to diversify sites and a monitoring incentive to improve regional coverage, satellite context, and other channels.

Radionuclide monitoring faces its own low-yield challenge. Less fission can mean less material available to escape. Deep burial can contain it. Yet noble gases remain attractive because even tiny quantities can be diagnostically powerful if they are detected above background and traced convincingly.

The background problem gets worse as sensitivity improves.

Medical-isotope plants and reactors release radioxenon. A sensor capable of finding minuscule test emissions must also understand those legitimate sources in exquisite detail. Better sensitivity without better source attribution can produce more alarms and less confidence.

This is the general law of mature sensing: after hardware improves, the bottleneck moves into context.

Infrasound behaves similarly. Lower-yield underground events couple weakly into the atmosphere and may be detectable only under favorable wind conditions. Hydroacoustic signals may arise indirectly through Earth-ocean conversion. Space-based systems may see nothing if the shot remains underground. No single channel rescues the monitor at every yield.

That is why current field experiments use multiple sensor types around controlled non-nuclear explosions.

NNSA's October 2023 P-tunnel experiment included accelerometers, seismometers, infrasound sensors, electromagnetic sensors, radiotracers, chemical sampling, and meteorological measurements. The purpose was to validate models and detection algorithms, not to imitate every aspect of a nuclear shot perfectly. The design recognizes that low-yield verification is increasingly a joint-inference problem.

The electromagnetic sensors are particularly interesting because they show how research widens when standard channels approach thresholds. Underground explosions can produce various electromagnetic and electrokinetic effects through rock fracturing, charge separation, cables, instrumentation, and other mechanisms. Whether such signals become operationally useful for global nuclear verification is an empirical question. The field experiment measures first and promotes later.

That order matters in frontier sensing.

A weak or exotic channel should not be presented as mature capability merely because it is instrumented. Research programs deliberately measure candidate observables that may prove useless. The existence of a sensor in an experiment is evidence of a question, not an answer.

The same caution applies to claims about perfect evasion.

Low yield does not mean invisible. Regional seismic arrays are extraordinarily sensitive. Intelligence satellites can observe construction or tunnel activity. Communication patterns, logistics, drilling, and site preparation may provide context. A state seeking weapons knowledge has to decide whether a clandestine small test produces enough value to justify the cumulative risk across these channels.

The monitor's task is to make that calculation unattractive.

This is why detection thresholds should be thought of strategically rather than only technically. An adversary cares not merely whether an event is detected at ninety percent probability but whether the resulting evidence will trigger scrutiny, sanctions, inspections, or other consequences.

An ambiguous detection can still be deterrent if it initiates a broader intelligence response.

Conversely, a highly sensitive sensor can be strategically weak if political institutions routinely dismiss its alerts as inconclusive.

Verification is a chain whose weakest link can move.

At high yield, the weak link may be attribution. At low yield, it may be detection. In a region with strong templates, it may be proving nuclear nature. In a heavily instrumented environment, it may be political willingness to act.

The small-test problem therefore resists one-number solutions.

There is no universal yield below which the world becomes deaf.

There are probability surfaces shaped by medium, depth, geography, background, network status, source history, weather, and adversary behavior.

The work continues because the boundary of observability is not a line.

It is terrain.
