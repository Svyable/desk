# The Ground Keeps Score

An underground explosion does not disappear. It changes the bookkeeping.

Instead of throwing most of its energy into light, blast, and radioactive debris above the surface, it couples energy into rock. The rock compresses, fractures, melts, shifts, and launches waves through the planet. A sufficiently distant observer does not see the cavity. The observer sees a seismogram.

That fact made seismology the central technology of underground test verification.

The first temptation is to imagine a nuclear explosion as an earthquake with a different cause. Both shake the ground. Both radiate body waves and surface waves. Both can be located from arrival times at separated stations. Both are described with magnitudes that compress a huge dynamic range into convenient numbers. But the source physics differ enough to create discriminants.

An ideal explosion begins as a rapid volumetric expansion. An ideal tectonic earthquake begins as shear slip on a fault. The explosion pushes outward in all directions more symmetrically than an earthquake does. That tends to favor compressional energy. Earthquake shear produces stronger S-wave and surface-wave components. Analysts learned to compare body-wave magnitude with surface-wave magnitude, to inspect P-to-S ratios, spectral content, depth, onset character, regional phases, and other features.

The word learned matters. The discriminants were not handed down by elastic theory fully formed. They were built through known earthquakes and known explosions.

Vela Uniform helped create that calibration set.

The program sponsored underground nuclear explosions in geologies selected partly because geology was the problem. Project Shoal was conducted in granite in a seismically active area near Fallon, Nevada, in 1963. Project Long Shot was detonated on Amchitka Island in Alaska in 1965. Project Dribble used the Tatum Salt Dome in Mississippi. Each event was an experiment in how a source writes itself into a particular Earth.

Geology is not passive paper.

Hard rock couples energy differently from salt. Layering refracts waves. Attenuation removes high frequencies. Faults scatter energy. Depth changes the balance between body and surface waves. Near-source damage modifies spectra. Propagation paths cross crust, mantle, and core structures whose details matter if the question is not merely where an event occurred but what kind of source produced it.

This is why a global verification system needs regional knowledge.

A threshold based on one test site cannot be transplanted cleanly to another. The same explosive yield can produce different seismic magnitudes in different media. A country accused of exceeding a yield limit can argue that the conversion model is biased. Suddenly a crustal velocity model becomes part of diplomacy.

The problem became especially sharp under the Threshold Test Ban Treaty. Signed by the United States and Soviet Union in 1974, the agreement prohibited underground nuclear weapon tests above 150 kilotons. Detection alone was no longer enough. Verification required yield estimation.

Yield estimation from seismic measurements is an inverse problem with geological nuisance parameters.

Suppose two events have the same body-wave magnitude. One occurs in hard rock that couples energy efficiently. The other occurs in a geology that couples less efficiently. Their yields need not match. If treaty compliance turns on the difference between 140 and 170 kilotons, uncertainty in coupling is not a footnote. It is the case.

This history explains the enormous effort spent on calibration explosions, site characterization, and eventually cooperative measures that could reduce dependence on pure inference.

It also explains why public arguments that begin with “the seismic magnitude was X, therefore the yield was Y” should trigger caution. The conversion is model-dependent.

Modern seismic monitoring is far better than the early Cold War system, but the old problem survives at lower yields. NNSA's contemporary research still includes Source Physics Experiments designed to improve discrimination between explosions and earthquakes and field experiments at the Nevada National Security Site that use chemical explosives, radiotracers, accelerometers, seismometers, infrasound sensors, electromagnetic sensors, and meteorological measurements. In 2023 NNSA publicly described a subsurface chemical explosion in P tunnel intended to improve models and detection algorithms for low-yield underground nuclear explosions.

Sixty years after Vela Uniform, the United States was still deliberately making explosions to understand explosions.

That persistence should cure any impression that verification is solved once a network becomes sensitive.

Sensitivity and discrimination trade problems. As detection thresholds improve, the network sees more small earthquakes, mine blasts, construction shots, collapses, and local industrial activity. The event population thickens. The difficult cases move downward in size.

The Earth is not quiet enough to let a small bomb stand alone.

Modern analysis therefore uses more than one discriminant. Location can matter: an explosion at a known test site has a different prior probability than a shallow event on an active fault. Waveform correlation can compare a new signal with previous known tests from nearly the same source region. Relative methods can estimate location and magnitude differences with exquisite precision even when absolute Earth models are imperfect. Regional P/S ratios can add source information. Depth helps. Aftershock patterns help. Infrasound or hydroacoustic coupling can add independent constraints.

The 2017 North Korean test is a good example. The International Monitoring System recorded an event of revised magnitude 6.1 at the known test site. More than one hundred facilities contributed. The Reviewed Event Bulletin characterized the source as consistent with a man-made explosion. Eight and a half minutes later, a second smaller event occurred near the same location. It was not classified as an explosion. Later evidence supported interpretation of the second signal as a cavity collapse.

One site produced two different source types minutes apart.

That is a useful reminder that context does not replace waveform physics. If analysts simply labeled every event near a test site “test,” the second event would be misclassified. A verification system has to remain capable of surprise even when the strategic story seems obvious.

Seismic arrays also demonstrate the value of geometry. A single seismometer can record a signal. A network can locate it. Arrival-time differences turn the planet into a triangulation surface. The International Monitoring System's 50 primary and 120 auxiliary seismic stations are distributed globally for precisely this reason. Primary stations transmit continuously. Auxiliary stations provide data on request to refine events.

The distinction between detection and refinement is embedded in the hardware.

This architecture produces an epistemic hierarchy. First, automated systems notice phases that may belong together. Then association algorithms propose events. Then analysts review waveforms and event parameters. Then national data centers can apply their own methods. The system distributes both raw monitoring data and processed products to Member States, which retain the authority to decide what an event means politically.

A seismogram is therefore never merely a squiggle. It sits inside a chain of transformations.

Sensor response converts motion to voltage. Station processing conditions the signal. Telecommunications move it. algorithms pick arrivals. Association logic links phases. Earth models estimate location. Magnitude formulas compress amplitude. Discriminants compare source classes. Analysts decide whether automatic picks are credible. Policy officials decide whether the evidence supports an accusation.

At each stage, uncertainty can be reduced, hidden, or accidentally invented.

The best verification cultures make the chain inspectable.

This is why international access matters. A state that receives monitoring data can reproduce event locations, challenge phase associations, apply different models, and compare conclusions. Disagreement does not vanish, but it can attach to specific technical steps instead of floating as geopolitical assertion.

Seismology also sets a standard for anomalous evidence that other fields rarely meet. A claimed event is not strengthened simply because multiple people saw the same plot. Independent stations with known locations and calibrated timing contribute new geometric information. Repeated observations from the same processing pipeline may not be independent. Correlated errors can create false confidence.

The verification community learned this because adversaries have incentives to exploit every shortcut.

A treaty cannot assume the signal will arrive in the convenient part of parameter space. It has to ask how the method fails near thresholds, in unusual geology, under deliberate concealment, and during coincident natural activity.

That pressure makes the science harsher and, often, better.

The ground keeps score, but it does not write the score in plain language.

Humans had to learn how to read it.
