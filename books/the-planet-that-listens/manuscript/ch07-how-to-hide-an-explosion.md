# How to Hide an Explosion

The best way to understand a detection system is to try to cheat it on paper.

Treaty verification has always had a shadow discipline: evasion analysis. Engineers and physicists ask how an adversary might reduce, disguise, relocate, or reinterpret the signatures a monitoring system depends on. The work can sound alarmist because it is designed to live in edge cases. A responsible analyst does not ask what a cooperative tester would do. The analyst asks what a technically competent adversary would do after reading the treaty and knowing the monitor's physics.

That distinction has produced some of the most persistent arguments around nuclear test bans.

For underground explosions, the obvious levers include yield, depth, geology, cavity decoupling, timing, masking by other seismic events, and containment. Each lever changes a different part of the observability problem.

Lower the yield and every signal becomes weaker. Increase burial depth and venting may be reduced, decreasing the chance of radionuclide release and atmospheric coupling. Choose geology that transmits less seismic energy and the same yield may look smaller. Use a pre-existing cavity and coupling can be reduced further. Conduct a shot near mining activity or seismic unrest and the event may enter a busier background.

None of these tricks makes a nuclear explosion physically silent.

They move it toward the region where classification errors become more likely.

That region matters because political verification rarely requires perfect invisibility. An evader may only need plausible deniability. If sensors produce an ambiguous event that governments disagree about, the strategic objective may be achieved even if technical experts privately suspect a test.

The 1979 Vela incident shows how powerful ambiguity can be. Even if one assumes for the sake of argument that the event was nuclear, the absence of an uncontested public conclusion meant the suspected actors did not face the consequences a fully verified violation might have triggered. The detector did not have to be defeated. Confidence had to be fractured.

This makes the social architecture of monitoring part of the attack surface.

A sophisticated evasion strategy can target thresholds, but it can also target institutions. Conduct an event in a region where propagation is poorly modeled. Exploit a civilian background source. Time activity so that attribution becomes politically awkward. Create enough uncertainty that agencies with different mandates emphasize different evidence.

The physics and the bureaucracy can be manipulated together.

This does not mean every ambiguous event is deliberate deception. That is the paranoid failure mode. The point is narrower: a verification system that assumes ambiguity is always accidental is incomplete.

The history of arms control contains repeated efforts to reduce this space by improving calibration and adding channels.

Cavity decoupling is one case. Containment is another. If an underground nuclear explosion is fully contained, radionuclide monitoring may receive no direct fission signature. The 2017 North Korean test produced a large, clearly explosion-like seismic event at the known test site, but the CTBTO reported no radioactivity attributable to the event or its aftershocks. The absence did not make the explosion natural. It demonstrated that radionuclide confirmation depends on release.

This is why the four monitoring technologies should not be imagined as four witnesses guaranteed to show up.

A deeply buried test may be primarily seismic. An underwater explosion may be hydroacoustically loud. An atmospheric event may be obvious to infrasound and space-based optical systems. A vented underground event can create the powerful combination of seismic location and radionuclide identity. The monitoring problem is conditional on environment.

Evasion analysis therefore asks which channel is hardest to suppress for a given test configuration.

Seismic energy is difficult to eliminate completely for an underground shot because the source is embedded in the ground. But coupling can be reduced. Radionuclides are chemically distinctive but can be contained. Infrasound can reveal surface coupling or venting but depends heavily on atmospheric propagation. Satellite observations can cover broad areas but may not see through rock. Hydroacoustics is extraordinarily efficient in the ocean but geometry matters.

A test designer seeking secrecy would optimize across this matrix.

The monitor does the same thing from the other side.

Modern U.S. budget documents make clear that low-yield and evasively conducted underground nuclear explosions remain a research priority. NNSA's nuclear detonation detection work includes improvements to geophysical models, seismic analyses, radionuclide technologies, test beds for low-yield monitoring, and Source Physics Experiment campaigns. This is documented program activity. It does not establish that a particular adversary is conducting such tests. The distinction should be kept visible because strategic research is routinely misread as secret confirmation of the scenario it studies.

One of the most interesting modern approaches is to combine dense local experiments with global monitoring objectives.

At the Nevada National Security Site, NNSA has run underground chemical explosions with instrumentation extending beyond seismometers. Accelerometers, infrasound sensors, electromagnetic sensors, radiotracers, chemical samplers, and meteorological measurements allow the source and propagation environment to be characterized together. The purpose is to improve predictive models and detection algorithms.

This is verification science becoming deliberately multimodal at the source.

The more channels are measured during a known event, the better analysts can understand which signatures survive at distance and which are artifacts of local geometry. Calibration is not glamorous. It is what makes later confidence possible.

Evasion also raises a problem of scale that public debate handles poorly.

Can a one-kiloton test be hidden? Can a hundred-ton test be hidden? Can a ten-ton experiment be distinguished from a large chemical blast? These are not variations of one question. The physical effects, strategic utility, and monitoring thresholds change by orders of magnitude.

A state might gain useful weapons information from experiments that do not resemble full-scale Cold War tests. That possibility motivates low-yield monitoring. But the lower the yield, the greater the event population of ordinary human and natural sources competing for attention.

At some point, verification becomes less about finding rare large events and more about statistical triage among thousands of small ones.

The monitor's advantage is history. Known test sites develop waveform templates. Repeating sources can be cross-correlated. Mine blasts have schedules and spatial clusters. Earthquakes follow tectonic structures. Regional networks add sensitivity. Satellite imagery can reveal excavation or tunnel activity. Intelligence from outside the treaty system can change priors.

The evader's advantage is that only one successful ambiguous event may matter.

This adversarial balance makes claims about absolute detectability suspect. Statements such as “no nuclear test can be hidden” and “small tests are impossible to detect” are both too coarse. Detection is a probability distribution over yield, geology, depth, network geometry, noise, source design, and analyst method.

A more honest statement is that concealment becomes increasingly expensive as independent observables accumulate.

That cost can be physical. Build a cavity. Drill deeper. Select an inconvenient site. Improve containment. It can be operational. Move equipment and people without observation. It can be political. Accept the risk that ambiguous signals will trigger inspections, sanctions, or intelligence focus even if public proof remains incomplete.

The monitor does not need perfect knowledge to impose cost.

This point is often lost in debates that treat verification as courtroom proof. Arms control works in a strategic environment where suspicion itself can alter behavior. A state that knows low-yield activity will attract national technical means, treaty-network scrutiny, atmospheric sampling, and intelligence attention may decide the marginal value of the test is not worth the risk.

Detection capability therefore has deterrent value even when public attribution remains imperfect.

But ambiguity also creates opportunities for propaganda. A state can accuse a rival using classified evidence outsiders cannot inspect. The rival can call the accusation fabricated. Supporters on each side can select the sensor channel that fits their politics. Technical uncertainty becomes geopolitical raw material.

The best defense is an evidentiary system in which as much as possible can be independently reconstructed.

That is why the International Monitoring System matters beyond its raw sensitivity. A seismic event seen by stations hosted across multiple countries is harder to dismiss as one government's invention. Shared data do not force shared interpretation, but they constrain the range of plausible argument.

The system is not enough by itself. National technical means remain important. On-site inspection, if available under treaty conditions, can search for local evidence. Intelligence can reveal preparations. Environmental sampling can identify isotopes. Satellite imagery can show surface change.

A robust verification case looks less like one perfect sensor than a prosecution assembled from physics that did not coordinate with the suspect.

That phrase matters: did not coordinate.

Independent evidence is valuable because the same deception has to survive different laws of nature.

A cavity may suppress seismic coupling but not satellite-visible excavation. Deep burial may contain radionuclides but require more drilling. Timing an explosion with an earthquake may complicate association but not necessarily waveform analysis. A low-yield shot may evade distant stations while appearing clearly on regional arrays.

Concealment moves cost from one channel to another.

The history of test detection is the history of making those moves expensive enough that secrecy becomes an engineering project rather than a switch.

That is the real answer to the question of how to hide an explosion.

You do not hide it from physics.

You try to make physics argue with itself long enough for politics to lose interest.
