# Four Ways to Know

The International Monitoring System divides the planet into four technologies because the treaty needs an organization chart.

The event does not care.

A nuclear explosion couples into rock, water, air, radiation, and sometimes space in proportions set by where and how it occurs. The monitoring system's seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound, and radionuclide branches are human categories laid over one physical event. Their real power appears when those categories disagree usefully.

This is the difference between redundancy and complementarity.

Two seismometers at similar distances from a source can provide redundancy. If one fails, the other may still detect the event. A seismometer and a radionuclide station provide complementarity. One measures energy propagating through the Earth. The other measures material carried through the atmosphere. Their errors share fewer causes.

Verification improves fastest when evidence streams fail differently.

A seismic event can be located precisely yet remain non-nuclear. Xenon can establish fission yet be difficult to attribute geographically. Infrasound can reveal source-to-atmosphere coupling while being strongly dependent on winds. Hydroacoustics can propagate across entire ocean basins while missing sources in unfavorable geometries. No channel deserves the final word by design.

This is why the CTBTO describes its technologies as complementary rather than as four votes.

Voting would be a mistake. A three-to-one majority of weak or correlated sensors is not automatically stronger than one highly diagnostic observation. A detected fission product tied by transport modeling to an explosion-like event can outweigh the absence of infrasound that was unlikely to propagate to a station anyway. Conversely, a strong seismic event without radionuclides is not exonerated if containment makes a release unlikely.

The evidentiary weight is conditional.

That sentence sounds elementary and is violated constantly in public discussion of anomalies.

People count sources rather than dependencies. Three newspapers become three confirmations even if all quote one unnamed official. Multiple social-media videos become multiple sensors even if they show the same compression artifact. Several instruments aboard one platform can fail from one power anomaly. Independent physical channels are valuable because their common-mode errors are narrower.

Nuclear verification has been forced to confront this for decades.

Consider an underground explosion. Seismic stations are the primary detection mechanism. A shallow event may also generate infrasound. If the test vents, radionuclides can appear. Hydroacoustic detections may occur indirectly if seismic energy couples into oceans. Satellite systems outside the IMS may contribute optical or other signatures. Intelligence can add context about site activity. The case grows not by counting observations but by connecting each observation to a forward model of the same source.

This is the most important methodological idea in the book: evidence should converge through prediction, not narrative.

Start with a source hypothesis. A buried explosion of approximate yield Y at location X and depth D in geology G should produce a certain family of seismic waveforms. It may produce an atmospheric signal with some coupling efficiency. If containment fails, radionuclides should enter air on some timeline. Weather models predict where the plume can travel. Surface deformation may be visible to satellites. If later evidence lands where those predictions said it should, confidence increases.

This is stronger than collecting anomalies after the fact and connecting them because they are temporally close.

The distinction became painfully clear in the Vela case. Once the double flash was known, investigators searched for corroboration. An unusual Arecibo ionospheric signal, sheep thyroid radioactivity, hydroacoustic interpretations, intelligence reporting, and regional nuclear ambitions all entered the story. Some may be genuinely related. But post-event collection creates a large hypothesis space. A planet that produces constant anomalies will always offer something nearby in time.

A modern verification regime reduces this freedom by having standing sensors, fixed procedures, and archived backgrounds.

The station was there before the accusation.

That is a form of preregistration.

The International Data Centre also separates stages of inference. Raw data arrive from more than three hundred facilities. Automatic systems detect phases and generate event products. Analysts review them. Member States receive both data and analysis. National data centers can conduct independent work. The final political determination belongs to states.

This separation prevents one technical office from silently converting a signal into an accusation.

The architecture contains another subtle safeguard: access is distributed. Member States do not have to trust a press statement describing an event. They can inspect data available through the verification system. States with stronger technical capacity can run their own pipelines. States with less capacity can use CTBTO tools and training.

The system therefore separates equality of evidence from equality of interpretation.

That distinction is democratic in a peculiar technical sense. Everybody may not have the same supercomputer, national intelligence assets, or laboratory expertise. But the treaty system tries to create a common evidentiary substrate.

National technical means still matter. The United States maintains space-based nuclear detonation detection, AFTAC capabilities, national laboratory expertise, and other sensors not fully public. Other nuclear powers operate their own systems. Classified data can strengthen or challenge an international assessment.

The existence of secret sensors does not make the international network redundant. It makes the international network a reference frame.

If a government claims a treaty violation based on intelligence it cannot reveal, shared IMS data may show whether the public physical environment is at least consistent with the accusation. If the shared network sees nothing where a large event should have appeared, the secret claim carries a larger explanatory burden.

This is one way transparency and secrecy can coexist without pretending they are equivalent.

The four-technology system also teaches a lesson about negative evidence. Suppose seismic stations detect an explosion-like event but radionuclide stations do not detect fission products. That is not “one sensor says yes, one says no.” The radionuclide channel may have had low probability of detection because the shot was contained. The correct combination requires modeling the expected miss rate.

Absence is data only when detection opportunity existed.

The same rule applies to eyewitness testimony, satellite imagery, and intelligence. A satellite that was not overhead cannot fail to see an event. A clouded optical sensor contributes less negative evidence. A hydroacoustic station in a shadow zone is not a silent witness.

The geometry of opportunity is part of the evidence.

This point becomes crucial in historical cases. The modern reader looks back at 1979 and asks why there was no global infrasound confirmation, no dense international radionuclide network, no modern atmospheric transport reconstruction. The answer is not that those systems saw nothing. Many of them did not exist in mature form.

Historical uncertainty should not be filled with imaginary modern sensors.

Nor should modern confidence be projected backward.

The International Monitoring System was designed after decades of learning from ambiguous events, calibration tests, treaty disputes, and scientific advances. Its architecture is an answer to specific failures of earlier regimes.

Seismic stations because underground tests matter.

Hydroacoustic stations because oceans hide geography but carry sound.

Infrasound because the atmosphere remembers explosions below hearing.

Radionuclide stations because an explosion can look man-made without being nuclear.

Four technologies because no one channel knows enough.

The network is therefore a physical argument against monoculture.

That argument reaches beyond arms control. Any high-consequence anomaly should be pursued with sensors whose errors are not copies of one another. A radar return and an infrared detection are more valuable together if their processing pipelines and physical mechanisms differ. A biological effect and a chemical assay should be linked by a mechanism, not merely by timing. A historical document and a later memoir should not be counted as independent if the memoir derives from the document.

The first question after “what was observed?” should be “what could make all these observations wrong together?”

Nuclear verification has had to ask that question because getting it wrong can move states toward confrontation.

The result is not certainty.

It is a disciplined way to earn it.
