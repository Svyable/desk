# Beirut, Volcanoes, Whales, and Tsunamis

A global monitoring system becomes interesting when the list of things it was not built for gets longer than the original procurement memo.

The International Monitoring System was built to detect nuclear explosions. Its public record now includes earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, meteors, non-nuclear industrial explosions, ocean sounds, and biological signals. Seismic and hydroacoustic data are shared with tsunami warning centers. Infrasound stations have recorded major volcanic explosions and the 2020 Beirut port blast. Hydrophones record whales. The same infrastructure supports arms control, disaster science, atmospheric research, ocean acoustics, and planetary-defense work.

This is often described as a set of civil and scientific spin-offs.

The phrase undersells the structure.

The network does not perform these secondary missions by bolting unrelated instruments onto nuclear-monitoring stations. The secondary uses emerge from the same physics. A tsunami begins with an earthquake or other displacement that seismic and hydroacoustic systems can observe. A volcanic eruption is an atmospheric explosion. A meteor is another atmospheric explosion with a trajectory. A whale is an acoustic source in the same ocean that carries blast energy.

The world is unified underneath the bureaucracy.

The 2020 Beirut explosion is a stark example. The ammonium nitrate detonation devastated the port and surrounding city. It was chemical, not nuclear. Monitoring systems still recorded its seismic and infrasound signatures. For treaty verification, such an event belongs to the background population of large human explosions that must not be mislabeled. For disaster science, it is a source whose energy, timing, and propagation can be studied. For forensic engineering, remote signals can provide independent constraints on what happened.

One event enters several disciplines without changing itself.

Volcanoes create a similar overlap. Explosive eruptions can generate infrasound detectable hundreds or thousands of kilometers away. Clouds that obstruct optical systems do not stop low-frequency pressure waves. Remote infrasound can therefore help characterize eruptions in regions where conventional observations are sparse.

Again, the security network benefits from learning the source. A volcano is a false-alarm class for atmospheric explosion monitoring. Volcanology benefits because the array already exists.

Tsunami warning is a more formal civil application. CTBTO says seismic and hydroacoustic data from 164 relevant stations are transmitted in near real time to national tsunami warning centers under agreements with participating states. By 2026, twenty-four warning centers in twenty-three countries had signed such agreements.

That is a striking institutional inversion.

A system justified by fear of clandestine nuclear explosions supplies data for saving people from natural disasters.

The inversion should not be romanticized. The network would not exist in its present form without nuclear danger. But the conversion of sunk security infrastructure into public-safety capability is worth understanding as a policy pattern.

Security systems tend to be funded for rare catastrophic cases. Rare catastrophic cases require high uptime. High uptime produces continuous environmental data. Continuous environmental data often has scientific value. The question is whether institutions design access pathways before the value is lost behind mission boundaries.

The CTBTO has done this more deliberately than many national-security systems.

Its scientific conferences invite work on non-nuclear sources. Its civil-applications program supports tsunami warning and other uses. Researchers can study IMS-related phenomena under defined arrangements. The organization's legitimacy benefits too: stations hosted by many countries provide value even in long periods with no nuclear tests.

A monitoring network that never produces local benefit can look like infrastructure for somebody else's security problem.

A station that contributes to earthquake, tsunami, meteor, or volcanic science has a broader constituency.

The whale songs are perhaps the best symbol because they are so far from nuclear strategy emotionally and so close physically.

A hydrophone suspended in the deep ocean records pressure. It does not know whether the source is a weapon, an earthquake, a ship, ice, or an animal. Classification happens later. The raw instrument is radically indifferent.

That indifference is why archives are valuable.

If operators kept only detections classified as treaty-relevant, the scientific world would disappear from the data. Preserving broader streams allows future questions nobody anticipated when the station was installed.

This is the same reason astronomical sky surveys become valuable beyond their original target lists. A persistent sensor accumulates options for later knowledge.

There are limits. Bandwidth, storage, privacy, security, and treaty rules constrain what can be released. Some national systems are much more sensitive than public networks and cannot expose raw capability without strategic cost. Scientific access must not become a backdoor map of blind spots.

The point is not open everything.

The point is to recognize accidental observatories while designing them.

A good architecture can separate operational latency from later scientific release, redact sensitive metadata, aggregate some products, or create trusted research enclaves. Data that are dangerous today may be harmless decades later. The Vela gamma-ray record is historical proof that security data can change scientific fields once the barrier becomes permeable.

Civil applications also provide independent performance checks.

When the network correctly localizes a known volcanic eruption or meteor, it validates timing and propagation models. When a tsunami-generating earthquake appears across stations, the system exercises data transport under real urgency. When a chemical explosion is recorded, analysts gain a non-nuclear anthropogenic source for discrimination studies.

The side use can improve the primary use.

This feedback loop is one reason the “mission creep” frame can be too crude. Some secondary applications consume resources and distract. Others deepen understanding of the background classes the mission already needs.

The right test is whether the additional science improves or degrades the verification function while producing public value.

Whale acoustics may seem far from that test, but even there the archive of biological sound helps characterize the ocean noise field. Every source understood is one less category of unexplained signal.

This is the hidden scientific tax of reliable accusation.

Before saying bomb, the system has to learn Beirut.

Before saying bomb, it has to learn volcano.

Before saying bomb, it has to learn meteor, earthquake, mining blast, ship, ice, reactor, isotope plant, and whale.

The prohibited event is defined against the whole world.

That makes verification one of the strangest general-purpose sciences humans have built.
