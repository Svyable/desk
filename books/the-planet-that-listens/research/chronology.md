# Chronology

## 1945–1962 — the detection problem forms

- 1945: nuclear weapons become an operational reality; later global monitoring disciplines emerge from the need to detect foreign nuclear development and tests.
- Late 1940s onward: U.S. organizations that later feed into AFTAC missions develop long-range nuclear monitoring capabilities.
- 1950s: test-ban diplomacy repeatedly confronts the verification problem, especially underground tests and inspection requirements.
- 1958–1961: U.S., UK and USSR observe a testing moratorium before resumed testing demonstrates fragility of unverifiable political restraint.

## 1963–1973 — Vela becomes an observatory

- 5 August 1963: Limited Test Ban Treaty signed by United States, United Kingdom and Soviet Union, banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, underwater and in outer space.
- October 1963: first Vela satellites launched for space-based nuclear-test monitoring.
- 26 October 1963: Project Shoal underground nuclear test in Nevada under Vela Uniform.
- 22 October 1964: Salmon, 5.3 kt, detonated 2,710 feet underground in Tatum Salt Dome, Mississippi, Project Dribble/Vela Uniform.
- 29 October 1965: Long Shot nuclear test on Amchitka Island, Alaska, Vela Uniform.
- 3 December 1966: Sterling, approximately 380 tons, detonated in Salmon cavity to study decoupling/source coupling.
- 1969–1970: chemical methane-oxygen explosions in Salmon cavity extend calibration work.
- 1 June 1973: Klebesadel, Strong and Olson publish gamma-ray bursts discovered with Vela data.

## 1974–1996 — thresholds, Vela ambiguity and comprehensive-ban architecture

- 1974: United States and Soviet Union sign Threshold Test Ban Treaty, setting 150 kt ceiling for underground nuclear weapon tests and intensifying yield-verification questions.
- 22 September 1979: Vela 6911 records disputed double flash over South Atlantic/southern Indian Ocean region.
- October–December 1979: U.S. agencies pursue debris searches, Arecibo data, intelligence hypotheses and competing technical assessments.
- November 1979: White House Ruina panel reports preliminary skepticism of nuclear interpretation; Arecibo disturbance treated as important but uncertain corroborating lead.
- 1980: declassified State Department and intelligence records show continuing disagreement, including later hydroacoustic arguments.
- 1992: United States conducts its last nuclear explosive test and begins continuing moratorium era supported by Stockpile Stewardship.
- 24 September 1996: Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty opens for signature.

## 1996–2017 — the international network becomes operational

- Late 1990s–2000s: CTBTO Preparatory Commission progressively installs and certifies IMS facilities and builds International Data Centre operations.
- 9 October 2006: DPRK announces first nuclear test; IMS detects event at 22 stations; later Xe-133 detection reported at Yellowknife.
- 25 May 2009: DPRK announces second nuclear test; event detected by more than 60 IMS seismic stations; no attributable radionuclide detection follows.
- 12 February 2013: DPRK event detected before public announcement; ultimately registered by 94 seismic and two infrasound stations.
- 15 February 2013: Chelyabinsk meteor blast becomes strongest infrasound event then recorded by IMS; detected by 20 infrasound stations.
- 8–14 April 2013: radioxenon detections at Takasaki and Ussuriysk consistent with a fission event timed to the February DPRK test.
- 6 January 2016: DPRK announces nuclear test; CTBTO automatic detection begins with 27 primary seismic stations.
- 9 September 2016: DPRK announces fifth nuclear test; IMS detects event.
- 2017: completion/certification of IMS hydroacoustic component reported.
- 3 September 2017: sixth DPRK announced test; revised magnitude 6.1; more than 100 facilities contribute; later nearby smaller event interpreted as likely cavity collapse; no attributable IMS radionuclide detection.

## 2018–2026 — persistent detection and scientific spillover

- 2018: NNSA publicly describes more than 50 years of space-based nuclear-detonation detection across Vela, DSP, GPS and other hosts.
- December 2018: Bering Sea bolide becomes another major infrasound event in IMS record.
- 4 August 2020: Beirut chemical explosion recorded across monitoring systems, adding major non-nuclear anthropogenic calibration case.
- 2021: CTBTO scientific work reports hydroacoustic detections related to 2017 DPRK test, illustrating cross-media propagation.
- 7 February 2022: large South Atlantic fireball later characterized using IMS infrasound records.
- 2023: NNSA conducts P-tunnel subsurface chemical explosion at Nevada National Security Site using seismic, infrasound, electromagnetic, radiotracer, chemical and meteorological measurements for low-yield monitoring research.
- 2023: U.S. officials describe continuing Source Physics Experiments, Xenon International, IDC re-engineering, transparent notification of large NNSS high-explosive tests, and support for 36 U.S. IMS stations.
- 23 July 2026: NNSA announces delivery of fifth next-generation Global Burst Detector payload for a Space Force GPS satellite, continuing space-based nuclear-detection lineage.
- 2026: IMS remains around 90% complete; CTBTO states it continuously records natural and human phenomena beyond nuclear tests.
- August 2026: Mexico becomes most recent signatory in CTBTO’s listed tsunami-warning data-sharing agreements, bringing total to 24 warning centers in 23 countries.
