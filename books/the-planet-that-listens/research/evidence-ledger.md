# Evidence Ledger

This ledger separates claim status rather than prestige of source. A senior official’s statement can remain Class 2 or 3. A mundane instrumentation fact can be Class 1.

## Class 1 — verified / declassified / documented

- Project Vela existed as a U.S. nuclear-detonation-detection program; Vela satellites began launching in 1963 to monitor treaty compliance.
- Vela satellite data led to the discovery of cosmic gamma-ray bursts; the 1973 Klebesadel–Strong–Olson publication established the astronomical event class.
- The United States conducted Vela Uniform nuclear experiments including Shoal, Salmon, Sterling and Long Shot to improve detection and identification of underground nuclear explosions.
- Salmon was a 5.3-kiloton underground shot in the Tatum Salt Dome on 22 October 1964; Sterling was about 380 tons on 3 December 1966 and was detonated in the Salmon cavity as part of decoupling-related work.
- The 1979 Vela 6911 satellite recorded a double-flash optical signal on 22 September 1979 that U.S. agencies investigated as a possible nuclear explosion.
- Declassified records establish substantial disagreement inside the U.S. government over the Vela event.
- Declassified material records a CIA assessment summarized as greater than 90% probability of a nuclear test; this is Class 1 evidence of the assessment, not of the event itself.
- The Ruina/White House scientific panel investigated non-nuclear and malfunction explanations and became skeptical of a nuclear interpretation.
- Arecibo recorded an unusual ionospheric disturbance on the date of the Vela event; U.S. officials considered it possible corroborating evidence while acknowledging mechanism/timescale uncertainty.
- Declassified memoranda record DIA/NRL-related hydroacoustic interpretations supportive of a nuclear event; the public record does not supply a complete reproducible raw-data chain.
- The CTBT verification architecture specifies 50 primary and 120 auxiliary seismic stations, 11 hydroacoustic stations, 60 infrasound stations, 80 radionuclide stations and 16 radionuclide laboratories; around 90% of 337 facilities were operating by 2026.
- The IMS detected all six DPRK declared nuclear tests between 2006 and 2017.
- The 2013 DPRK event was recorded by 94 seismic stations and two infrasound stations; later Xe-131m and Xe-133 detections in Japan/Russia were consistent with a fission event on the relevant timescale.
- The 2017 DPRK event had a revised magnitude of 6.1; its Reviewed Event Bulletin used 41 primary and 90 auxiliary seismic stations; hydroacoustic/infrasound associations existed; no IMS radioactivity attributable to the test or subsequent aftershocks was detected.
- CTBTO infrasound stations recorded the 2013 Chelyabinsk fireball at 20 stations, including a detection about 15,000 km away in Antarctica.
- The IMS records non-nuclear events including earthquakes, volcanoes, meteors, large chemical explosions and biological/ocean acoustic sources.
- CTBTO data are supplied to participating tsunami warning centers in near real time under civil-use agreements.
- NNSA publicly continues low-yield/evasive nuclear-explosion monitoring research, Source Physics Experiments, Global Burst Detector work and ground-based seismic/infrasound/hydroacoustic/radionuclide capability.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete / model-dependent

- The September 22, 1979 Vela signal was caused by a nuclear explosion. This interpretation is supported by some declassified intelligence/scientific assessments and later technical critiques, but the accessible record remains incomplete and contested.
- Hydroacoustic signals discussed in 1980 were genuinely generated by the same source as the Vela optical event and were diagnostic of a nuclear maritime explosion. Official testimony supports this interpretation, but full raw data/calibration are not available in the present research pass.
- The Arecibo disturbance was causally related to the Vela event. Temporal coincidence and historical analogies make it relevant; source coupling remains insufficiently reconstructed here.
- Elevated iodine-131 reported in Australian sheep after the Vela event was related to a nuclear detonation. The record is suggestive but source attribution and transport are not closed.
- Modern multi-phenomenology monitoring would probably narrow a Vela-like ambiguity substantially. This is a technically grounded counterfactual, not a directly tested result.
- Low-yield tests can yield weapons-relevant information while sitting near monitoring ambiguity thresholds. Plausible and central to monitoring research, but strategic utility varies strongly by program and objective.
- Persistent monitoring archives materially improve rare-event science because they create long baselines and repeated natural calibration events. Strongly supported as a synthesis, but effect size varies by field.

## Class 3 — disputed allegations / claims without independent confirmation

- The 22 September 1979 event was definitively an Israeli nuclear test.
- The 22 September 1979 event was definitively a South African nuclear test.
- The event was definitively a joint Israeli–South African nuclear test.
- The Ruina panel knowingly falsified its technical conclusion under political orders.
- The meteoroid/spacecraft explanation was definitively correct.
- Hydroacoustic evidence publicly available today proves a nuclear test beyond reasonable technical dispute.
- Any specific contemporary state is currently conducting prohibited low-yield nuclear tests unless supported by an explicit separate evidence file.
- The existence of NNSA low-yield/evasion research proves adversaries have successfully used the modeled evasion methods.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis / open conjecture

- Arms-control verification is best understood as an accidental planetary observatory created by adversarial epistemology.
- False-positive research is structurally responsible for a significant fraction of the network’s scientific spillovers because reliable accusation requires rich models of non-target phenomena.
- Security systems that preserve anomalous raw data, provenance and alternative hypotheses are more likely to generate scientific discoveries than systems optimized only for target classification.
- Declassification policy can function as a delayed scientific instrument by reopening old sensor archives to methods unavailable when the data were collected.
- The best general anomaly program is not one optimized for proving the anomaly but one optimized for classifying the complete source population occupying the same physical measurement space.
- Verification strength is better modeled as the cost imposed on sustaining ambiguity than as a binary property of detectability.

## Claim-control questions

For every strong statement in later revisions ask:

- Does the source establish the event, or only establish that somebody believed the event?
- Are multiple reports independent, or do they descend from one inaccessible source?
- What physical mechanism connects the source hypothesis to each sensor channel?
- Was the sensor actually capable and geometrically positioned to observe the predicted signal?
- Is absence of a signal genuinely unlikely under the favored hypothesis?
- Does an alternative hypothesis state predictions that could defeat it?
- Are political incentives being used as evidence of the event rather than as evidence about process quality?
