# The Spy War with Spain

In the spring of 1898, the United States knew it might soon fight Spain.

Knowing where Spain's fleet was proved harder.

That difference is the intelligence story of the decade.

The country had spent the 1880s creating something new: a permanent Office of Naval Intelligence, a system for gathering information before a war forced the question. The approaching conflict with Spain gave the office its first large test. The test did not produce a clean triumph. It produced something more useful for understanding what intelligence had become.

The United States now possessed an organization whose job was to know.

That made ignorance less acceptable.

For most of American history, the absence of information had been ordinary. A commander sent a scout because he lacked a map. A diplomat wrote home because Washington could not see what he saw. A wartime network was improvised because nobody had expected to preserve the expertise between wars.

By 1898, the Navy Department had officers collecting foreign naval information as a profession. Naval attachés watched fleets and shipyards from European capitals. Technical reports accumulated. War planning increasingly depended on estimates of what an adversary could do, not merely what it had done.

Then the USS Maine exploded in Havana harbor on February 15.

The cause of the explosion became one of the most politically explosive questions in American history. The loss of the ship accelerated an already dangerous confrontation over Cuba. Newspapers, politicians, diplomats, naval officers, Cuban revolutionaries, Spanish officials, and the public all participated in an atmosphere where information and agitation were difficult to separate.

The intelligence problem was not only secret collection.

It was distinguishing evidence from noise while events were moving faster than institutions were accustomed to moving.

When war came in April, the Navy needed answers that sounded simple.

Where were the Spanish ships?

What condition were they in?

What ports could support them?

What coal was available?

What did Spain intend to do with the squadron commanded by Rear Admiral Pascual Cervera?

The answers required a network extending far beyond Washington.

American naval attachés in Europe watched Spanish preparations. Officers in London, Paris, Berlin, Madrid, and elsewhere gathered reports, purchased material, cultivated contacts, read newspapers, observed ports, and passed information home. The Navy also used agents whose work moved beyond ordinary diplomatic reporting. In early 1898, intelligence activity accelerated under the pressure of possible war. Attachés were told to obtain information on ships, movements, armament, supplies, and Spanish intentions. Some were also drawn into procurement, trying to buy vessels and materiel before Spain or another buyer could do the same.

This mixing of missions was characteristic of a young service.

The same officer might be collector, buyer, observer, liaison, and improviser.

Permanent bureaucracy did not mean mature specialization.

It meant there was finally a desk in Washington expecting the reports.

The Office of Naval Intelligence had been founded in 1882 partly because American naval officers understood how much they did not know about rapidly changing foreign technology. Steel hulls, new guns, armor, torpedoes, engines, mines, and communications were remaking naval war. A fleet could become obsolete while still looking impressive in a harbor.

Information about hardware therefore carried strategic value.

A ship's name told little by itself.

What mattered was speed, endurance, armament, armor, machinery, maintenance, crew quality, ammunition, coal, and the ability to reach a theater of operations in fighting condition.

Intelligence was becoming technical.

It was also becoming logistical.

A navy powered by coal had an appetite that intelligence officers had to understand. A fleet without access to fuel was a fleet whose strategic choices narrowed. Coaling stations, commercial supplies, neutral ports, and shipping routes therefore became intelligence subjects. The map of naval power was also a map of places where a ship could replenish what made movement possible.

This is one reason the Spanish-American War belongs between the founding of ONI and the twentieth century.

The United States was learning that a modern power had to study systems, not just units.

The Spanish fleet became the most immediate system to understand.

Cervera's squadron left Spain and moved into the Atlantic under conditions that generated uncertainty in Washington. Reports arrived from different sources. Some were timely. Some were wrong. Some were technically accurate but strategically unhelpful. A ship seen in one port was no longer there when the report reached the decision maker.

That is a basic intelligence problem that technology has never eliminated.

Information has a half-life.

A perfect report about a moving target becomes wrong through the passage of time.

The faster the target moves and the slower the communication, the more analysis becomes a problem of predicting what happened after the source stopped watching.

In late April, Secretary of the Navy John D. Long cabled American naval attachés in London and Paris to make every effort to obtain reliable information about the movements of the Spanish fleet. The instruction itself is revealing. The United States had created permanent collectors overseas, and now the Secretary could task them directly against a wartime problem.

The machinery existed.

The uncertainty remained.

At one point American naval intelligence still appeared to believe Cervera's force might be at Cadiz after it had already moved to the Cape Verde Islands. That is not evidence that the intelligence system was useless.

It is evidence that a system can be institutionalized and still be wrong.

This distinction matters because histories of intelligence often drift toward two lazy conclusions.

The first is that a successful operation proves the service was brilliant.

The second is that a mistaken estimate proves the service was incompetent.

Real intelligence organizations live between those verdicts. They process incomplete information about adversaries who know they are being watched and who are trying to preserve freedom of action. A useful service does not abolish uncertainty. It reduces it enough for somebody else to decide.

The Spanish fleet eventually entered the Caribbean and reached Santiago de Cuba.

The strategic puzzle then changed.

Once the fleet's location became clearer, the United States could concentrate naval power and maintain a blockade. The final destruction of Cervera's squadron off Santiago on July 3 was a battle, not an intelligence operation. Yet the ability to bring American naval force against the Spanish squadron depended on the larger process of locating, tracking, interpreting, and communicating.

Collection created the possibility of concentration.

The difference between knowing that Spain had a fleet and knowing where to meet it was the difference between inventory and intelligence.

But the 1898 story becomes more interesting when the lens widens beyond the fleet.

American naval intelligence activity included clandestine missions directed into Spain and other locations. Officers and agents sought information about naval plans, ships, defenses, mines, and preparations. Some work resembled traditional espionage. Other work looked like aggressive observation under diplomatic cover. Much came from public sources and commercial contacts.

The border between open-source intelligence and espionage was already porous.

A newspaper shipping notice might answer a question that a spy had been tasked to answer.

A shipyard worker might know something no newspaper could print.

A naval attaché could openly inspect what a host government allowed him to see and secretly seek what it did not.

Modern intelligence communities preserve similar distinctions in organization charts. Reality keeps mixing them.

The key is not whether information was glamorous to obtain.

The key is whether it reduced uncertainty.

This was especially true in 1898 because the press itself had become an intelligence environment.

American newspapers competed ferociously for stories about Cuba, Spain, naval movements, battles, atrocities, diplomacy, and rumor. Some reporting was excellent. Some was sensational. Some correspondents possessed access government officials envied. Some published claims that governments had to investigate simply because public reaction made them consequential.

An intelligence officer could not ignore the newspaper.

He also could not simply believe it.

That tension has only intensified.

By the twenty-first century, governments would monitor social media, commercial satellite imagery, online ship trackers, leaked databases, and open-source investigators alongside classified collection. The 1890s version used telegraphy, newspapers, port observers, commercial networks, and attachés.

The technology changed.

The analytical problem did not.

What is true?

How old is it?

Who benefits if I believe it?

What does it mean for the decision that must be made now?

The war also demonstrated the advantages and dangers of bureaucratic memory.

ONI had been collecting information on foreign navies for years. That meant wartime officers did not have to begin with a blank page. Reports on Spanish ships, weapons, ports, organization, and capabilities already existed.

The archive could be mobilized.

But archives contain yesterday.

War happens today.

A naval intelligence file describing the theoretical speed of a cruiser could mislead if the ship's machinery had deteriorated. A technical specification could overstate what an exhausted crew could achieve. A chart could omit a newly placed mine. An attaché's impression of an officer corps could be colored by social access and personal prejudice.

Permanent collection therefore created a second-order problem.

The United States now had to know how much confidence to place in what it knew.

That is the beginning of professional analysis.

The distinction between facts, estimates, assumptions, and confidence levels did not arrive fully formed in 1898. Neither did modern intelligence doctrine. But the institutional necessity was becoming visible. Once enough information accumulates, the challenge is no longer obtaining facts alone.

It is managing contradiction.

The Spanish-American War is often remembered as short, decisive, and lopsided. That retrospective clarity can make the intelligence environment look easier than it was.

People inside the spring of 1898 did not know how quickly the conflict would end.

They did not know exactly where the enemy fleet would appear.

They did not know whether European powers would complicate the war.

They did not know whether Spanish forces would strike the American coast.

Public anxiety about possible raids was real enough to affect naval dispositions and political pressure. Coastal communities imagined enemy warships appearing offshore. The American Navy had to consider possibilities that later history rendered unnecessary.

This is another rule of intelligence history.

Do not use the outcome to erase the branches that were still alive beforehand.

A decision can look foolish after the feared event fails to happen.

The fairer question is whether the fear was reasonable with the information available at the time.

The war also exposed a broader transformation in American power.

An intelligence office built to study foreign navies was now supporting a war fought across the Caribbean and Pacific. The United States defeated Spain in Cuba and Puerto Rico and destroyed Spanish naval forces in Manila Bay and Santiago. The settlement transferred Puerto Rico and Guam to American control, ended Spanish sovereignty in Cuba, and placed the Philippines under the United States after Spain ceded the islands.

American intelligence therefore moved with American expansion.

That fact should make any celebration of professionalization less comfortable.

Better intelligence did not merely defend the continental republic.

It helped a state operate overseas.

The permanent intelligence apparatus was growing because the strategic perimeter was growing.

There is no neutral institutional story here.

An intelligence capability is a tool of the power that funds it.

As the United States acquired overseas possessions and fought beyond North America, the questions handed to intelligence officers changed. They needed information about foreign ports, political movements, colonial societies, insurgent networks, languages, terrain, communications, and local actors who might become partners or enemies.

The geography of secrecy expanded with the geography of rule.

That expansion led directly into the next decade.

After Spain's defeat, Filipinos who had fought Spanish colonial rule did not simply accept American sovereignty. The Philippine-American War turned the United States from supporter of anti-Spanish revolutionaries into an occupying power fighting a Filipino independence movement.

The intelligence problem changed with the political relationship.

A local ally could become the target.

A scout could become an informer.

Knowledge acquired in cooperation could be repurposed for counterinsurgency.

The most dramatic intelligence operation of that war would culminate in the capture of Emilio Aguinaldo in 1901 through deception and the use of Filipino scouts.

The moral distance between the chapters is shorter than Americans often prefer.

In 1898, the language of liberation helped justify war against Spain.

By 1901, American forces were using deception to capture the leader of a Filipino republic resisting American rule.

Intelligence follows policy through that turn without asking whether the policy is consistent.

That is why institutions matter more than adventure.

A spy network improvised for one emergency may disappear when the cause changes.

A permanent intelligence office survives.

It receives the next question.

The Spanish-American War therefore earns the 1890s not because ONI knew everything or because a single secret won the war.

It earns the decade because the office created in 1882 finally entered the condition it had been designed to anticipate.

Peace became war.

Files became tasking.

Attachés became collectors against an enemy.

Technical study became operational urgency.

The Navy discovered both the advantage of having spent years looking outward and the limits of believing that an office could make the ocean transparent.

The lesson was more mature than triumph.

Permanent intelligence can reduce surprise.

It cannot eliminate fog.

The fog merely becomes somebody's job.
