# The Wire Inside the Wire

The most important espionage story of the American 2020s is not finished.

That is the first fact the chapter has to preserve.

As of September 2026, the decade still has more than three years to run. Intelligence operations now underway may not become visible until 2028, 2035, or after the people involved are dead. A penetration exposed tomorrow may make this selection look provincial. Declassification may later change the scale or meaning of cases we think we understand today.

So the choice is provisional.

The story, so far, is Salt Typhoon.

Beginning publicly in 2024, U.S. officials disclosed that actors affiliated with the People's Republic of China had compromised multiple telecommunications providers in a broad cyber-espionage campaign. The FBI said the activity resulted in theft of customer call-data logs, compromise of private communications involving a limited number of identified victims, and copying of certain sensitive information related to court-ordered U.S. law-enforcement requests.

By 2025, U.S. and allied agencies were describing a wider pattern of Chinese state-sponsored access to telecommunications and other networks around the world. A joint advisory said the actors focused in part on large backbone routers and provider-edge infrastructure and sometimes modified routers to preserve persistent, long-term access.

The technical details matter.

The strategic idea matters more.

The target was not only a person using the network.

The target was the network.

That makes Salt Typhoon the cleanest endpoint for a 250-year story that began with invisible ink.

Washington's spies had to insert themselves into the path a message traveled.

A courier carried intelligence through occupied territory.

British cryptanalysts exploited the cable system carrying the Zimmermann Telegram.

Yardley's Black Chamber read diplomatic traffic copied from commercial communications infrastructure.

Walker compromised the cryptographic material protecting Navy traffic.

Hanssen used authorized access to search electronic FBI systems.

The OPM intruders targeted the archive.

Salt Typhoon went after the layer beneath all of them.

The carrier.

Telecommunications networks occupy an unusual place in modern life because almost everyone depends on them while almost nobody experiences the network directly. The user sees a phone, contact name, signal bars, message window, or call button. Behind the interface sits a sprawling system of towers, fiber, switches, routers, authentication systems, operational support platforms, cloud services, lawful-intercept functions, billing records, signaling protocols, vendors, administrators, and interconnections.

The convenience comes from hiding the complexity.

Espionage lives in the complexity.

A service that gains durable access to a telecommunications provider may be able to learn not only what one target says but how targets connect, when they communicate, what infrastructure carries the traffic, and which administrative systems can be used to identify or reach them.

The difference between content and metadata becomes crucial.

A phone conversation says something.

A call record says that two numbers communicated at a particular time for a particular duration or through a particular service context.

The content may be encrypted or inaccessible while the relationship remains visible elsewhere in the system.

Intelligence services have always valued both.

A letter tells you what was written.

The pattern of couriers tells you who is connected.

A decrypted cable reveals a diplomatic instruction.

Traffic analysis can reveal which headquarters suddenly became busy.

A modern telecommunications network produces relationship data as a condition of operating.

That data can be intelligence even when the words remain unread.

In public descriptions of Salt Typhoon, the FBI said customer call-record data was stolen at significant scale. Later testimony described records relating to millions of customers, while private communications were compromised for a much more limited number of identified victims.

That asymmetry is revealing.

The largest intelligence value does not always come from reading everybody's conversations.

Sometimes the map is enough.

Who talks to whom?

Which numbers repeatedly contact a government office?

Which device moves with which other device?

Which official suddenly begins communicating with a person outside the normal pattern?

Which contacts cluster around an event?

Which foreign intelligence officer communicates with a previously unknown American number?

A network graph can generate questions before any human source is recruited.

It can also help validate information obtained elsewhere.

This is the fusion principle from the OPM breach one layer deeper.

Personnel records supply biography.

Telecommunications records supply behavior.

Commercial records may supply purchases.

Travel systems supply movement.

Public sources supply profession and relationships.

A foreign intelligence service does not need one omniscient database if it can combine partial ones.

Modern espionage therefore increasingly resembles data integration.

The spy service becomes a company whose product is other people's hidden structure.

Salt Typhoon was especially disturbing because telecommunications providers also support lawful-intercept functions used by American law enforcement under court authorization. Public U.S. statements said the actors copied select information related to court-ordered law-enforcement requests.

The irony is severe.

A system built so the United States could conduct legally authorized surveillance became an intelligence target for another government.

The back door for one authority can become attack surface for another.

This does not mean lawful interception caused the entire compromise or that every intercepted communication became readable to the intruders. Public reporting and official statements have drawn narrower boundaries.

The important architectural lesson is more general.

Exceptional access remains access.

Any mechanism powerful enough to expose protected communications under authorized conditions must itself be secured against unauthorized use.

That security problem cannot be wished away through policy vocabulary.

The foreign service sees capability, not legitimacy.

The American legal system distinguishes a court-authorized wiretap from espionage.

A compromised router does not.

This is where the title of the chapter comes from.

The wire inside the wire is the hidden administrative and technical layer that makes the public communications system governable.

Operators need management access.

Networks need routing tables.

Providers need troubleshooting tools.

Law requires certain lawful capabilities.

Equipment needs updates.

Vendors need support paths.

Trusted relationships connect one network to another.

Every one of those functions exists because a communications system too locked down to administer would stop functioning.

The attacker looks for the administrative path that users never see.

In August 2025, U.S. and allied cybersecurity agencies released a joint advisory describing Chinese state-sponsored actors targeting networks globally, including telecommunications, government, transportation, lodging, and military infrastructure. The advisory emphasized exploitation of backbone and edge routers, use of trusted connections to move between networks, and techniques designed to maintain persistent access.

The agencies noted that the activity overlapped only partially with the commercial label Salt Typhoon and other industry tracking names.

That qualification is important because threat-group names create a false sense of neatness.

Cybersecurity companies observe clusters of infrastructure, tools, behavior, victims, and timing, then assign labels for analytical convenience. Government agencies may organize the same activity differently based on classified intelligence. One company's Salt Typhoon may not map perfectly to another company's cluster or to an intelligence service's understanding of the people behind it.

The name is useful.

It is not the adversary's passport.

This is a new version of the old cover-name problem.

VENONA analysts had ALES and other codenames that had to be connected cautiously to real people.

Cyber analysts have campaign names that must be connected cautiously to actors, organizations, and state direction.

The vocabulary changes.

Attribution remains inference built from evidence.

By 2025, the FBI publicly described the relevant activity as PRC-affiliated and said U.S. government partners had previously attributed compromises at U.S. telecommunications providers to PRC-affiliated actors. The joint advisory went further in describing Chinese state-sponsored activity and linked aspects of it to China-based companies providing cyber products and services to the Ministry of State Security and People's Liberation Army.

Those official statements justify stronger language than was appropriate for OPM in 2015.

They still do not justify pretending every intrusion grouped under a commercial nickname has one operator, one sponsor, or one purpose.

The archive is still forming.

That is what writing intelligence history before the decade ends looks like.

Salt Typhoon also demonstrates the strategic value of persistence.

A smash-and-grab intrusion steals what exists during the breach.

Persistent access creates a collection position.

The difference resembles stealing one diplomatic pouch versus secretly obtaining a copy of every pouch that passes through the route for months.

Intelligence services prefer the stream when they can keep it.

Persistence produces future value.

Today's target may not be important yet.

Tomorrow's crisis may make the same access invaluable.

This is why defenders worry about adversaries living inside infrastructure rather than merely visiting it.

A preexisting collection position can shorten the distance between political intent and intelligence access when a crisis begins.

The service does not have to break in after the world changes.

It is already there.

That logic explains why 2020s cyber campaigns are often discussed alongside geopolitical contingency planning. A state preparing for possible confrontation benefits from understanding the communications, logistics, leadership networks, military infrastructure, and decision systems of the other side before the confrontation arrives.

Espionage is contingency preparation.

It has always been.

Washington wanted information before the British moved.

ONI collected foreign naval data before war.

The Black Chamber read negotiating instructions before diplomats settled.

Technical reconnaissance watched missile sites before launch.

A persistent network intrusion is the digital form of the same appetite.

Know before you need to know.

The 2020s add another complication: the infrastructure is largely private.

The United States government does not own every telecommunications provider whose systems carry official and civilian traffic. Private firms build, operate, upgrade, secure, and troubleshoot the networks. Equipment vendors create hardware and software. Contractors integrate systems. Global supply chains produce components.

National security therefore depends on corporate security practices over which the government has influence but not complete operational control.

This is not unprecedented.

The Black Chamber depended on commercial cable access.

Project AZORIAN depended on private engineering and industrial cover.

Railroads hired private detectives before the federal government built permanent domestic intelligence institutions.

The public-private boundary has always shaped American intelligence.

The scale is different now because the private infrastructure is itself the medium through which government operates.

A cabinet secretary's call can travel through systems serving millions of ordinary customers.

The intelligence target and the civilian platform are the same object.

That creates defense problems traditional military secrecy was not designed to solve.

You can guard a base perimeter.

You cannot place a Marine beside every router in a national telecommunications system.

The defense has to become distributed technical practice: hardened configurations, multifactor authentication, network segmentation, logging, anomaly detection, secure management interfaces, rapid patching, vendor discipline, architecture that assumes compromise, and cooperation between companies and government.

The 2025 joint guidance focused heavily on visibility because defenders cannot remove an intruder they cannot see.

That sounds elementary.

It is one of modern cybersecurity's deepest problems.

Complex networks generate enormous normal activity. The attacker succeeds partly by making malicious administration resemble legitimate administration. A command sent by a stolen credential may look like the authorized user's command. A modified router may continue forwarding traffic normally while also preserving hidden access. A trusted interconnection can carry hostile movement because the network treats trust as a property inherited from architecture.

The old espionage principle returns.

The best cover is belonging.

Van Lew's network used ordinary Richmond life as concealment.

McParland used social identity.

Ames used professional access.

Hanssen used credentials.

A network intruder uses the identity of a device or administrator the system already trusts.

The machine is being deceived in a human way.

This is why “zero trust” became such an influential security phrase. The ideal is not literal universal suspicion. Networks cannot function if every packet triggers an existential inquiry.

The idea is to stop assuming that location, credential, device, or prior authentication permanently establishes legitimacy.

Verify again.

Limit privilege.

Assume an internal path may be compromised.

Segment what one identity can reach.

Watch behavior after access is granted.

It is continuous evaluation for computers.

The Hanssen and Ames lessons have become architecture.

Still, the architecture cannot eliminate the person.

Network engineers configure the systems.

Executives decide how much to spend on security.

Government officials choose disclosure rules.

Regulators set requirements.

Users accept or reject updates.

Vendors patch vulnerabilities.

Analysts decide whether an anomaly is worth escalating.

A cyber-espionage campaign can involve machines at every stage and remain a chain of human decisions.

That continuity is the reason the book can begin in 1778 and end here without changing subjects.

The word “cyber” describes the environment.

Espionage describes the purpose.

Salt Typhoon also forces a distinction between espionage and disruption that becomes increasingly important in state cyber operations.

The publicly described Salt Typhoon activity is fundamentally espionage: access for collection. Other Chinese state-sponsored campaigns have been described by U.S. officials as pre-positioning in critical infrastructure with potential disruptive use during a crisis.

Those missions can overlap technically while differing strategically.

The same network access might support observation today and effects tomorrow.

That ambiguity affects deterrence.

States have long tolerated espionage as an unpleasant but normal feature of international competition. They respond differently to sabotage or armed attack.

Cyber access blurs the threshold because a foothold can have multiple possible futures.

Is the adversary listening?

Preparing to break something?

Both?

The defender may not know until intent changes.

This is why access itself becomes strategic.

The capability can matter before it is used.

The Salt Typhoon campaign also changes the democratic dimension of espionage. Telecommunications systems are civilian infrastructure. Collection directed at political figures, government officials, or national-security targets can incidentally expose ordinary people connected to them. Call records at scale contain people who never imagined themselves as intelligence subjects.

Modern surveillance expands through association.

You do not need to be the target to appear in the target's network.

That was true in OPM, where spouses and other connected people appeared in background-investigation data.

It is even more natural in communications data.

A social graph is made mostly of edges between the important and the ordinary.

Espionage at network scale therefore touches civilian privacy even when the intelligence requirement is foreign and strategic.

The target boundary becomes harder to see from the outside.

This creates political pressure for governments to secure infrastructure without explaining every reason a particular victim may matter. A provider may learn that a system is compromised without being told which intelligence target led investigators there. A victim may be notified without receiving the classified attribution. Citizens may know an adversary stole call records without knowing what inferences were drawn.

We return to the VENONA problem.

The state may know more than it can read aloud.

The difference is that the affected population can now number in the millions.

As of September 2026, remediation and broader defense efforts continue. The public record has expanded since the first disclosures, but it remains incomplete. The 2025 multinational advisory showed that the activity was not merely a handful of American telecom breaches. It described a global campaign against important network infrastructure and warned defenders about long-term access techniques.

That larger frame strengthens Salt Typhoon's claim to the decade so far.

The operation is not important because hackers reached famous people's phones.

It is important because communications infrastructure became intelligence infrastructure for the adversary.

The network designed to connect the country could be used to map the country.

That is a conceptual change worthy of the final chapter.

It is not necessarily the final selection.

The book should be honest enough to leave the door open.

A different espionage case may emerge before 2030 with greater consequence: an artificial-intelligence compromise, a space-system penetration, a supply-chain operation, an insider case, a biotechnology theft, a new signals-intelligence disclosure, or something not yet visible enough to name.

The most important operation of an unfinished decade may already have happened without the public knowing it.

That uncertainty is not a weakness in the chapter.

It is the proper ending for an intelligence history.

People living inside espionage events do not possess the final archive.

Washington did not know what every Culper report would mean to later historians.

American officials in 1945 did not know which atomic spies would be exposed through cryptanalysis and confession.

CIA officers watching Soviet sources disappear did not know Ames was responsible.

OPM employees did not know which stolen records an adversary would value most.

The historian arrives later and mistakes hindsight for visibility.

The 2020s chapter denies us that comfort.

We are still in the operation's time.

We know enough to see the shape.

We do not know enough to see the edge.

America's first 250 years of espionage therefore end where they began: with a communications problem.

George Washington needed to move information through a network he did not control.

The solution was trusted people, aliases, invisible ink, couriers, dead drops, and routes designed to survive interception.

Two and a half centuries later, the country depends on communications networks so vast that no single person can understand the whole path a message takes.

The protection is encryption, authentication, hardened infrastructure, software, monitoring, policy, and the assumption that trusted systems deserve continuous verification.

The attacker still wants the same thing.

Get inside the path.

Stay there.

Learn who is speaking.

Learn what matters.

Leave before the target understands how much you heard.

The wire changed.

The desire did not.
