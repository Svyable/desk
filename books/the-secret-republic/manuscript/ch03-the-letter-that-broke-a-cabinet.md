# The Letter That Broke a Cabinet

In the summer of 1795, the British government gave George Washington a piece of intelligence about one of his own cabinet officers.

That sentence contains almost every difficulty intelligence would create for American presidents during the next two centuries.

The information had been obtained by a foreign power. It concerned an American official. It arrived inside a diplomatic struggle. Its language was open to interpretation. It was politically explosive. And once the president knew it, he could not return to the condition of not knowing.

The document was a dispatch written by Joseph Fauchet, the French minister to the United States. British forces had intercepted French diplomatic correspondence at sea. Among the captured material was a message that discussed conversations with Edmund Randolph, Washington's secretary of state.

Randolph was not a peripheral figure. He had served as governor of Virginia, attorney general of the United States, and then secretary of state after Thomas Jefferson. He sat inside an administration already strained by the great foreign-policy question of the 1790s: how should the fragile American republic navigate a European war in which Britain and revolutionary France were fighting each other while Americans themselves divided over the two powers?

The United States had won independence with French help. It traded extensively with Britain. The French Revolution had created ideological excitement and horror. Britain was seizing American ships and impressing sailors. American political factions were hardening into something close to parties even while many of the country's founders insisted parties were dangerous.

Then came the Jay Treaty.

Washington's administration had sent Chief Justice John Jay to negotiate with Britain. The resulting treaty sought to settle outstanding disputes left from the Revolution and prevent another war. To Federalists, it was a practical accommodation with the strongest maritime power in the Atlantic. To many Republicans, it looked like a humiliating tilt toward Britain and a betrayal of France.

Washington needed cabinet advice on whether to proceed.

The British had an intercept.

This is where the espionage story becomes more interesting than the scandal.

An intercepted message is not the same thing as truth.

It is a document produced by a person, for a recipient, under circumstances, using language that may flatter the writer, impress the recipient, conceal uncertainty, exaggerate influence, or misunderstand another person's meaning. Diplomatic reporting is intelligence, but diplomats can be wrong about the people they report on.

Fauchet's dispatch suggested that Randolph had discussed political unrest and money in a way that could be read as corrupt or compromising. The British made the captured material available to the Washington administration. The president confronted a problem no constitutional clause could solve for him: how much weight should he give hostile intelligence about his own secretary of state?

Randolph resigned in August 1795 after Washington confronted him with the dispatch.

He denied wrongdoing and later published a defense.

The episode has often been summarized as a scandal in which a French dispatch exposed Randolph's improper dealings. The State Department's own later historical account is more cautious and notes that Randolph was innocent of the charge that cost him office. That does not make the dispatch irrelevant. It makes the dispatch more instructive.

Badly interpreted intelligence can be politically consequential even when the underlying accusation is weak.

This is why the Fauchet dispatch earns the 1790s.

The decade produced no American intelligence bureaucracy comparable to what would follow. The federal government was still inventing itself. Yet the episode reveals an intelligence environment already international, intercepted, partisan, and vulnerable to selective disclosure. A foreign government collected a foreign diplomat's message and placed it into the American decision system at exactly the moment when British-American relations were under fierce dispute.

The British did not have to forge a letter.

They only had to decide which real letter Washington should see.

That is a form of intelligence power often overlooked in stories focused on stealing secrets. Collection creates selection. Whoever possesses a large archive of intercepted communications can choose what to reveal, to whom, and when. The information can be authentic while the act of disclosure remains strategic.

Modern intelligence professionals distinguish collection from analysis and covert action. History is less tidy. A true intercepted document can function as intelligence for one government and influence for another. A leak can simultaneously inform, embarrass, warn, and manipulate.

Washington had no way to treat the British handoff as politically neutral.

He also had no way to ignore it simply because Britain had motives.

That is the president's dilemma in miniature.

Sources have motives. Adversaries sometimes tell the truth. Allies sometimes omit it. A person offering intelligence may be trying to change your behavior, but that does not mean the information is false. Refusing to believe anything said by an interested party is not skepticism. It is another kind of gullibility.

The harder discipline is to separate the provenance of information from its content without forgetting either one.

Who obtained this?

How?

Why are they showing it to us?

What exactly does the document establish?

What else would need to be true for the most damaging interpretation to hold?

What evidence points the other way?

Those questions sound procedural. In 1795, they were career-ending.

Randolph's problem was timing. Once Washington had read Fauchet's words, the secretary had to defend himself against an adversary's description of a private conversation. Context had already lost the race. The accusation arrived as a captured document, and captured documents possess a physical authority that rumor does not. There it is, in writing. Someone wrote it before the scandal. It feels harder than recollection.

But a document is still testimony.

It records what the writer believed, wanted his reader to believe, or found useful to say.

American intelligence would relearn this distinction repeatedly. A stolen enemy plan can be genuine and obsolete. A diplomatic cable can accurately record the diplomat's misunderstanding. An intercepted message can use deception. A source can provide real documents while lying about their meaning. A dataset can be authentic while the analyst draws the wrong inference from it.

The evidence is not the conclusion.

Washington's administration was operating without the institutional habits later built around such problems. There was no national intelligence estimate with confidence levels, no inspector general reviewing handling, no congressional intelligence committees, no formal analytic tradecraft standards. The Cabinet itself was part of the analytical machine, and the person implicated by the intelligence sat inside that machine until the accusation exploded.

Early American government was intimate in ways a later bureaucracy would find dangerous. A small number of people held enormous ranges of responsibility. Personal relationships, reputation, correspondence, faction, and policy were tightly braided. An intelligence allegation against a cabinet officer was not simply a security matter. It was a political fact the moment it became known to the president.

The Jay Treaty sharpened everything.

Randolph had been navigating a cabinet divided over relations with Britain and France. Alexander Hamilton had left formal office but remained influential. Federalist and Republican newspapers turned foreign policy into domestic combat. The administration's choice about the treaty would help define American neutrality and the country's economic relationship with Britain.

In that atmosphere, a French minister's suggestion that Randolph had discussed money or political maneuvering could not remain a narrow question of personal conduct.

It became evidence about faction itself.

This is one of espionage's most dangerous political transformations. A secret begins as information about an individual and becomes a story about a group. One suspected contact becomes proof that a party is disloyal. One foreign relationship becomes evidence that an entire movement serves a foreign power. The logic is emotionally efficient and analytically hazardous.

The young United States was especially vulnerable because political parties were not yet accepted as normal democratic infrastructure. Opposition could be described as faction, and faction could be imagined as disunion. Foreign governments did court American political allies. European wars did spill into American domestic politics. The fear of foreign influence therefore grew from something real.

Real vulnerabilities are the best fuel for exaggeration.

The same pattern appears throughout American history. Soviet espionage in the 1930s and 1940s was real; that reality did not make every anti-communist accusation true. Foreign cyber operations are real; that does not make every political opponent a foreign asset. Intelligence becomes dangerous to democratic politics when evidence of actual penetration is treated as permission to abandon distinctions.

Randolph's case sits near the beginning of that history.

His resignation did not destroy Washington's administration, and the United States did not build a counterintelligence service in response. The Jay Treaty proceeded. Randolph worked to clear his name. The political system moved on.

Yet the event established a pattern presidents would never escape.

Foreign intelligence can enter domestic politics without asking permission.

The government that receives it then has choices about secrecy and disclosure. Keep it secret and risk concealing misconduct. Reveal it and risk amplifying a foreign power's objective. Investigate it and risk becoming accused of politicizing intelligence. Decline to investigate it and risk ignoring a real compromise.

There is no procedure that makes the dilemma disappear.

Only better procedures for surviving it.

The British interception itself also points toward the future. Sea power had always included information power. Captured mails, diplomatic dispatches, ship papers, and correspondence moved along the same routes navies controlled. Britain possessed a long tradition of opening, reading, copying, and exploiting foreign communications. The United States, still commercially and militarily weaker, operated in an international system where other powers could see parts of its diplomatic environment that it could not see itself.

That asymmetry matters.

Weak states often imagine intelligence as something they do to stronger states in order to compensate for material inferiority. Strong states use intelligence too, and their logistical reach can make collection routine. The British did not need to send a daring operative into Randolph's office to produce the crisis. Maritime interception put Fauchet's message into British hands.

Infrastructure became intelligence infrastructure.

That sentence will recur.

Ships carrying mail. Telegraph cables. Radio spectrum. Telephone exchanges. Fiber-optic networks. Cloud infrastructure. Routers.

Every system built to move information creates positions from which information can be observed.

The 1795 episode is therefore not as distant from modern cyberespionage as the paper makes it look. Britain exploited control over the physical pathways through which diplomatic communications traveled. In the 2020s, foreign operators would seek persistent access to telecommunications networks for the same strategic reason: the network knows who is talking, sometimes what is being said, and often enough context to identify whose conversations matter.

The medium changes the scale.

It does not change the appetite.

There is also a more personal continuity. Randolph faced the cruel structure of compromised-context cases. If the interpretation was wrong, how could he prove a negative after the relevant conversation had passed? Secret relationships and ambiguous language make exoneration difficult because innocence rarely creates records designed for future litigation.

Spies benefit from ambiguity.

So do false accusations of spying.

A mature counterintelligence system must be able to live with both possibilities at once. It needs enough suspicion to detect betrayal and enough evidentiary discipline not to manufacture it.

That balance is harder in political crises because leaders do not receive intelligence as disinterested judges. They have policies underway. They have rivals. They have prior beliefs about the accused person. They have deadlines. They have reputations to protect. The information arrives inside all of that.

Washington is often presented in American memory as a figure above faction. In 1795, he was still a president making decisions under pressure, surrounded by advisers who disagreed deeply about the country's direction. The British intercept did not descend into an empty room.

No intelligence ever does.

Analysts sometimes describe information as if it moves through a pipeline: collection, processing, analysis, dissemination, decision. The diagram is useful because it separates functions. Human organizations are less linear. Decision makers influence collection priorities. Political concerns shape which questions feel urgent. Analysts know what leaders are worried about. A dramatic report changes the attention paid to later reports. Once a suspicion enters the system, new facts can be interpreted through it.

The Fauchet dispatch was a piece of paper entering a field of forces.

Its power came from both.

The lesson is not that Washington should have ignored it. That would be too easy. A secretary of state potentially compromised by a foreign minister is exactly the kind of matter a president cannot wave away.

The lesson is that intelligence creates obligations of method.

Authenticity must be separated from interpretation. Provenance must be separated from motive. Allegation must be separated from proof. Political consequence must not be mistaken for evidentiary strength.

The United States in the 1790s had not yet built institutions capable of formalizing those distinctions.

It had built a cabinet.

One intercepted dispatch helped break it.

That is why this decade's defining espionage story does not feature a secret agent slipping through a dark street. It features a document moving from one government to another and changing what a president believed about a man already sitting beside him.

Sometimes the most consequential act of espionage is not stealing the secret.

It is choosing who gets to read it.
