# The Minister Who Would Not Stay a Minister

Joel Roberts Poinsett arrived in Mexico City in 1825 as the first American minister to the newly independent Mexican republic.

He did not behave like a man content to observe.

That is why this chapter begins with a definitional problem.

Was Poinsett a spy?

If espionage means only a clandestine agent stealing protected documents from a foreign government, the answer is too broad. Poinsett was an accredited diplomat. His identity was public. He attended official meetings, wrote formal dispatches, represented the president, and performed the ordinary work of an American envoy.

He also sent encrypted communications, cultivated political networks, involved himself deeply in Mexican factional struggles, promoted American objectives, and became such a symbol of foreign interference that the Mexican government formally requested his recall in 1829.

The 1820s therefore belong to a borderland of intelligence.

That borderland matters because the United States would spend much of its history operating there.

Diplomats collect information. They are supposed to. They meet officials, businessmen, journalists, military officers, opposition figures, intellectuals, clergy, and foreigners; they report what they learn to their own government. Much of that collection is overt. Some conversations are private. Some sources expect confidentiality. Diplomatic posts also provide cover and infrastructure for intelligence officers in later eras.

The line between diplomatic reporting and espionage is not whether a government wants information.

All governments want information.

The line becomes harder when the diplomat moves from understanding politics to shaping it secretly.

Poinsett did not invent American intervention in Latin America. Nor was Mexico a passive stage on which an American manipulated events at will. Mexican politics after independence were complex, contested, and driven by Mexican actors with their own ideologies, regional interests, institutions, ambitions, and conflicts. A chapter that credits Poinsett with controlling them would reproduce the very arrogance that made his presence inflammatory.

His importance lies in something subtler.

He represented an American republic discovering that diplomatic access could be used as political leverage in a neighboring republic whose territory Americans coveted.

The United States had recognized Mexican independence in 1822. By the middle of the decade, relations involved commerce, boundaries, Indigenous nations crossing frontiers, U.S. migration into Texas, Spanish threats of reconquest, and American desire to purchase additional territory. President John Quincy Adams and Secretary of State Henry Clay saw an opportunity and a problem. Mexico was independent, but its political order was unsettled. The United States wanted influence without war.

Poinsett was sent into that environment.

He had experience abroad, including earlier travel and semi-official missions in Latin America. He was energetic, confident, politically opinionated, and unusually willing to move beyond the narrow social role of ceremonial envoy. In Mexico he associated himself with York Rite Masonic networks that became intertwined with political faction. The lodges were not simply an American intelligence front; Mexican participants had their own purposes. But Poinsett's involvement made American influence visible enough that his surname became a political accusation.

“Poinsettismo” came to signify meddling.

When the host country turns the name of a diplomat into a word for foreign interference, the mission has ceased to be invisible.

This is the paradox of covert influence conducted by an overt representative.

The diplomat possesses access precisely because everyone knows whom he represents. That access becomes more powerful when some of what he does can be denied, obscured, or described as private association. The cover is not a false identity. It is the elasticity of diplomacy itself.

Poinsett's dispatches add another layer.

American diplomatic correspondence had used codes and ciphers since the founding era. By the mid-1820s, encrypted traffic to and from Mexico became especially important because the post was politically sensitive and the information valuable. Secret writing did not make Poinsett an intelligence officer in the modern sense. It shows that the American state understood a basic fact: diplomacy creates secrets even when diplomats are not spies.

Negotiating positions are secrets.

Assessments of foreign leaders are secrets.

Instructions from Washington are secrets.

Private reports on factional politics can become secrets because public disclosure would damage relationships or reveal intent.

A state that expands its diplomacy expands the amount of information it must protect.

The 1820s are therefore the decade in which American espionage history cannot be separated cleanly from the professionalization of foreign relations.

The War of 1812 had demonstrated the cost of weak preparation and uncertain information. The Monroe Doctrine of 1823 announced that further European colonization or intervention in the Americas would be regarded with hostility by the United States, while also presenting the Western Hemisphere as a special strategic sphere. The doctrine was grander than the military power available to enforce it. Britain, with its navy, had its own reasons to oppose renewed European colonial adventures.

American ambition was ahead of American capability.

Information helped close the gap.

A minister in Mexico could tell Washington what factions were rising, what officials believed, how Spain's possible return was being discussed, what boundary proposals might be acceptable, and how Mexican leaders viewed American settlement in Texas. He could also advocate, cultivate, and pressure.

This is intelligence as the companion of asymmetrical power.

The United States was stronger than some of its neighbors in population and economic potential but still weak compared with Britain. It lacked a worldwide clandestine service. Diplomats became part of the information network because they were already there.

Poinsett also became part of the political network.

That was more dangerous.

A foreign diplomat who reports on factions may irritate politicians when his dispatches leak years later. A diplomat perceived to be supporting one faction changes the incentives of the factions while he is still in the country. Local actors may seek his backing. Rivals may use his involvement to discredit opponents. Ordinary political competition becomes entangled with sovereignty.

The collector starts affecting the thing collected.

That is one of the oldest methodological problems in intelligence and social science alike.

Observation is rarely perfectly passive.

A case officer recruiting a source changes the source's risk. A covert subsidy changes the political organization receiving it. An information operation changes the media environment being measured. A cyber intrusion can prompt defensive actions that alter the network. Poinsett's presence affected Mexican politics partly because Mexicans believed it did.

Perception became operational reality.

The United States also wanted territory.

American efforts to acquire Texas from Mexico predated the Texas Revolution and annexation. Poinsett carried instructions and participated in discussions related to boundaries and possible purchase. He did not simply arrive with a secret plan to steal Texas, and the later loss of the territory cannot be reduced to his actions. But American territorial interest made every political involvement more suspect from the Mexican point of view.

Intelligence cannot be separated from the intentions of the power collecting it.

A country observing a neighbor it has no designs on is different from a country observing territory it hopes to acquire.

The same act of meeting an opposition figure changes meaning when attached to expansion.

This is one reason Poinsett's story should be told from both sides of the border.

American accounts can celebrate his energy and familiarity with Latin American politics. Mexican accounts have reason to remember intervention. Both perspectives describe the same structural fact: the U.S. envoy did not maintain the distance expected of a foreign representative.

By 1829, Mexican pressure for his removal had become impossible for Washington to ignore. The Mexican government formally requested his recall in October. Poinsett left the post in early 1830.

A diplomat had become a liability to diplomacy.

That failure is what makes the case valuable.

Espionage history often selects successes because success leaves a satisfying narrative: secret obtained, battle won, spy caught, plot stopped. Failed or overreaching influence operations reveal limits more clearly. Poinsett demonstrated that covert or semi-covert political work performed under diplomatic cover can consume the credibility of the diplomat's overt role.

Cover has a cost.

If an embassy becomes known primarily as a platform for manipulation, ordinary diplomacy becomes harder. Officials avoid meetings. Opposition figures become suspect for attending them. Accurate reporting can deteriorate because sources censor themselves. The government gains short-term access and loses long-term trust.

Modern intelligence services solve this imperfectly through organizational separation, liaison rules, legal authorities, diplomatic conventions, and the use of officers whose intelligence roles are not publicly acknowledged. The embassy still contains overlapping functions because geography makes overlap efficient.

The problem does not disappear.

The host country knows it too.

Foreign security services watch embassies precisely because embassies are natural collection hubs. Diplomatic immunity protects representatives while making them attractive platforms for activities that would expose an ordinary visitor to arrest. Governments tolerate a certain amount of reciprocal intelligence activity because closing every channel would damage diplomacy more than it improved secrecy.

Poinsett operated before these conventions matured into the twentieth-century intelligence environment, but the logic was already present.

His case also warns against treating encryption as evidence of espionage.

Governments encrypt normal diplomacy because confidentiality is necessary to negotiate honestly and report candidly. A cipher protects the channel; it does not define the morality of the content. This seems elementary until a leaked encrypted message becomes public and the secrecy around it is offered as proof that something improper must have occurred.

Secrecy is sometimes evidence of wrongdoing.

Sometimes it is evidence of a government doing ordinary government work.

The analytical task is to distinguish them.

Poinsett's encrypted dispatches contained political reporting and instructions that American leaders did not want foreign readers to possess. Some of his political activity in Mexico went beyond what the host government regarded as acceptable. Those facts can coexist without turning every coded telegram into a covert operation.

The distinction becomes especially important later in this book when signals intelligence enters the story. Codebreakers read diplomatic communications because diplomacy contains secrets. The messages they exploit may concern routine negotiation, espionage, war planning, trade, or gossip. The codebreaker cannot assume classification equals significance.

Neither should the historian.

What makes Poinsett significant is not simply that he used secret writing.

It is that his mission shows the American state experimenting with influence through diplomatic presence in a region where it had growing strategic ambition but limited formal intelligence capability.

The United States of the 1820s was learning to project power without admitting it was projecting power.

That is a recurring phase in the life of rising states.

Military intervention is expensive and obvious. Diplomatic persuasion is cheaper and legitimate. Covert influence occupies the space between. It can promise results without the political cost of open coercion. It can also create resentment precisely because the target experiences interference without the interfering power accepting public responsibility.

Poinsett did not operate a modern covert-action program from a nineteenth-century embassy. The analogy should not be stretched that far.

But he reveals the appetite that later institutions would professionalize.

Know the factions.

Know the leaders.

Know what they fear and what they want.

Build relationships before a crisis.

Protect the reporting.

Use influence when influence appears cheaper than force.

And try not to become the story.

Poinsett became the story.

His recall is therefore part of the intelligence lesson rather than an embarrassment to be edited out.

An operation can produce information and still damage the larger objective. A diplomat can acquire access and lose legitimacy. A country can gain leverage and deepen suspicion that shapes relations for decades.

Intelligence effectiveness is not measured by collection alone.

It is measured against policy.

That sounds obvious, but bureaucracies repeatedly confuse activity with result. More sources, more reports, more intercepts, more access, more meetings, more covert relationships—these can all look like success inside the organization producing them. The state cares about something larger: did the activity improve the country's position without imposing greater costs elsewhere?

Mexico's request that Poinsett leave suggests the answer was at least mixed.

The episode also foreshadows a century of American relations with Latin America in which intelligence, diplomacy, private business, military power, and political influence would repeatedly blur. Later interventions would be more organized and sometimes far more coercive. Poinsett belongs near the beginning because the institutional machinery was still small enough for one man's role to be visible.

He was a minister.

He reported.

He encrypted.

He cultivated.

He interfered.

Mexico noticed.

That sequence is why the 1820s chapter cannot offer the reader a comfortable verdict about whether he was a spy.

The uncertainty is the point.

Espionage lives next door to diplomacy, and the door between them has never stayed completely shut.
