# Chapter Briefs

## 1 — 1770s — The Invisible Ink Republic

**Selection:** The Culper Ring, organized under Benjamin Tallmadge beginning in 1778 for George Washington.

**Why it earns the decade:** It demonstrates that the American state learned clandestine tradecraft before it fully became a state: aliases, codes, dead drops, invisible ink, cutouts, maritime couriers, and disciplined compartmentation. Its larger importance lies less in any single cinematic secret than in the creation of a repeatable intelligence network serving strategic command.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that another Revolutionary operation had a clearer direct effect on independence, or that later retellings have substantially exaggerated Culper reporting and consequences.

## 2 — 1780s — Treason at West Point

**Selection:** Benedict Arnold's attempt to surrender West Point to the British and the capture of Major John André in September 1780.

**Why it earns the decade:** It is America's foundational insider-threat story. The danger was not a stranger penetrating a perimeter but a celebrated American commander already inside it.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the plot posed less strategic danger than historians have generally assessed, or that another 1780s counterintelligence episode produced a more durable institutional legacy.

## 3 — 1790s — The Letter That Broke a Cabinet

**Selection:** British interception and use of French minister Joseph Fauchet's dispatch, which implicated Secretary of State Edmund Randolph in 1795 and helped force his resignation.

**Why it earns the decade:** A foreign intelligence intercept entered the highest level of American domestic politics. The episode shows that intelligence can be consequential even when its interpretation is contested.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that Randolph's fall was substantially independent of the intercepted dispatch or that the document's political effect has been overstated.

## 4 — 1800s — Agent 13

**Selection:** General James Wilkinson's continuing secret relationship with Spain while holding senior American military authority.

**Why it earns the decade:** Wilkinson turns the insider problem into something almost unbelievable: one of the country's senior military officers was also a paid Spanish agent. His survival exposes the weakness of early American counterintelligence and the protection conferred by rank, ambiguity, and political usefulness.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that Wilkinson delivered little intelligence of practical value during this decade or that his Spanish relationship no longer materially affected his American conduct.

## 5 — 1810s — The Spy Papers That Helped Start a War

**Selection:** The John Henry affair. Britain had sent Henry into New England in 1809 to report on Federalist disaffection; President Madison purchased and released Henry's papers in 1812.

**Why it earns the decade:** The papers became political evidence in the run-up to war and show the combustible boundary between foreign collection, influence, domestic faction, and public persuasion.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the papers had negligible influence on congressional or public opinion and were mostly a political sideshow to maritime conflict.

## 6 — 1820s — The Minister Who Would Not Stay a Minister

**Selection:** Joel Roberts Poinsett's encrypted diplomacy, political networking, and intervention in Mexican politics while serving as the first U.S. minister to Mexico, 1825–1829.

**Why it earns the decade:** This is deliberately a border case. Poinsett was a diplomat, not a formally acknowledged intelligence officer. Yet his secret dispatches and active role in Mexican political networks reveal how an expanding republic practiced covert influence before it had a professional foreign intelligence service.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If “espionage” is defined narrowly as clandestine acquisition of protected information rather than covert political action, another case should replace it. The chapter must make that definitional problem part of the story rather than hiding it.

## 7 — 1830s — The Deaf Spy of Texas

**Selection:** Erastus “Deaf” Smith's scouting, courier interception, and intelligence work for Sam Houston during the Texas Revolution.

**Why it earns the decade:** The case shows intelligence operating in the unstable space between rebellion, nation-making, and American continental expansion. Smith's value came from movement, local knowledge, and the ability to see a battlefield before commanders could.

**What would make this selection wrong?** The Texas Republic was not yet the United States. If the book's scope is limited strictly to federal U.S. operations, this case belongs outside it. The manuscript instead treats it as part of the American expansion story and says so explicitly.

## 8 — 1840s — The Mexican Spy Company

**Selection:** Winfield Scott's campaign intelligence network in Mexico, associated with Ethan Allen Hitchcock and Manuel Domínguez in 1847.

**Why it earns the decade:** It made human intelligence a practical part of an invading army's movement through unfamiliar territory. Mexican agents and couriers gathered information while risking punishment from their own countrymen.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the network's importance was inflated after the campaign or that another collection effort more directly enabled Scott's victories.

## 9 — 1850s — The Private Intelligence State

**Selection:** The rise of Allan Pinkerton's detective agency as a railroad, mail, security, and intelligence organization.

**Why it earns the decade:** Before the federal government possessed a durable domestic intelligence service, private firms built information networks for corporations and clients. Pinkerton's later wartime role grows out of this private infrastructure.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If the chapter cannot distinguish detective work, security, and intelligence clearly enough, it should narrow to documented clandestine collection operations rather than treating all private investigation as espionage.

## 10 — 1860s — The Spy Mistress of Richmond

**Selection:** Elizabeth Van Lew's Union intelligence network in Confederate Richmond.

**Why it earns the decade:** Van Lew's network joined prisoners, couriers, government clerks, Black Unionists, and civilians inside the Confederate capital. It also offers a corrective to spy history that centers only uniformed men or famous operatives.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If specific claims about the network rely on later legend rather than contemporaneous evidence. The chapter must preserve the National Park Service's corrections to the mythology around Mary Jane Richards.

## 11 — 1870s — The Company Spy

**Selection:** Pinkerton operative James McParland's infiltration of the Molly Maguires amid Pennsylvania's coal-labor conflict.

**Why it earns the decade:** It shows espionage turned inward by private capital. Intelligence was not only a national-security instrument; it could be hired in an industrial struggle and then enter criminal prosecutions.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If “espionage” is limited to state secrets and foreign enemies. The chapter's argument depends on a broader history of clandestine intelligence and should make the labor-power controversy central, not incidental.

## 12 — 1880s — America Invents a Permanent Spy Office

**Selection:** Creation of the Office of Naval Intelligence by General Order No. 292 on March 23, 1882.

**Why it earns the decade:** The important story is an institution rather than a single spy. Intelligence became permanent, bureaucratic, global, and increasingly professional through attachés, technical collection, and systematic record keeping.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that earlier offices have a stronger claim to continuous national intelligence status or that ONI's early work was too limited to justify the institutional turning-point argument.

## 13 — 1890s — The Spy War with Spain

**Selection:** ONI's attaché and agent networks tracking Spain and the Spanish fleet before and during the Spanish-American War.

**Why it earns the decade:** The young permanent intelligence apparatus was tested in an overseas war. Collection, procurement, observation, and clandestine networks connected Washington to European ports and enemy naval movements.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that wartime decisions depended much more heavily on ordinary diplomatic and press reporting than on ONI's clandestine work.

## 14 — 1900s — The Ruse at Palanan

**Selection:** Frederick Funston's 1901 operation, using Macabebe Scouts and deception to capture Philippine revolutionary leader Emilio Aguinaldo.

**Why it earns the decade:** It is a case study in intelligence fused with disguise, captured communications, local intermediaries, and counterinsurgency. It also forces the book to confront espionage from the perspective of a people resisting American rule.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If the capture's strategic effect on the Philippine-American War is overstated or if the chapter romanticizes a coercive colonial campaign.

## 15 — 1910s — The Telegram That Changed the War

**Selection:** British Room 40's interception and decryption of the Zimmermann Telegram and the decision to share it with the United States in 1917.

**Why it earns the decade:** Foreign intelligence changed American political conditions. The message made Germany's proposed alliance with Mexico visible to the U.S. public at a critical moment.

**What would make this selection wrong?** The United States entered World War I for multiple reasons, especially unrestricted submarine warfare. The chapter fails if it turns one intercept into a monocausal explanation.

## 16 — 1920s — The Black Chamber

**Selection:** Herbert Yardley's Cipher Bureau and its cryptanalysis of Japanese diplomatic communications around the Washington Naval Conference.

**Why it earns the decade:** Peacetime codebreaking became a national instrument of diplomacy. Secret reading could shape negotiations without the public ever knowing the negotiators had a second copy of the conversation.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the decrypts were less timely or influential than later institutional histories suggest.

## 17 — 1930s — The Soviet Harvest

**Selection:** Soviet penetration associated with the Ware Group, Whittaker Chambers, and Alger Hiss.

**Why it earns the decade:** The case became a defining argument about ideology, loyalty, evidence, and state penetration. It must be told backward from later evidence without pretending investigators in the 1930s possessed what later archives revealed.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If the chapter collapses legal, archival, and political questions into a single certainty. Hiss's 1950 conviction was for perjury, not espionage, and that distinction remains mandatory.

## 18 — 1940s — How Moscow Stole the Bomb

**Selection:** Soviet atomic espionage involving Klaus Fuchs, Theodore Hall, Julius Rosenberg's network, Harry Gold, David Greenglass, and others.

**Why it earns the decade:** Few secrets were more consequential than the design and progress of the atomic bomb. The case joins ideology, human recruitment, scientific openness, wartime alliance, and the emergence of nuclear superpower rivalry.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If the manuscript implies espionage independently created the Soviet bomb rather than accelerating a capable indigenous program, or repeats disputed claims about individual participants beyond the evidence.

## 19 — 1950s — The Messages Nobody Could Read Aloud

**Selection:** VENONA's secret exploitation of Soviet communications and its role in understanding espionage cases while the program remained too sensitive for ordinary courtroom use.

**Why it earns the decade:** The government could know something through intelligence that it could not safely explain in public. The chapter is about the democratic and prosecutorial problem created when proof itself is secret.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If VENONA is portrayed as complete, contemporaneously available omniscience rather than a slow cryptanalytic achievement with gaps, aliases, and interpretive limits.

## 20 — 1960s — Thirteen Days Seen from 70,000 Feet

**Selection:** U-2 photography and imagery analysis revealing Soviet offensive missile deployments in Cuba in October 1962.

**Why it earns the decade:** Technical reconnaissance supplied evidence at the center of a nuclear confrontation. The photographs mattered because analysts could interpret them and policymakers could decide what degree of certainty they justified.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If the chapter treats imagery as self-explanatory or ignores human reporting, signals intelligence, diplomacy, and the political judgments surrounding the crisis.

## 21 — 1970s — The Ship That Pretended to Mine the Ocean

**Selection:** CIA's Project AZORIAN and the Hughes Glomar Explorer's 1974 attempt to recover part of Soviet submarine K-129 from the Pacific floor.

**Why it earns the decade:** Espionage became a feat of secret industrial engineering on an almost absurd scale. The cover story had to hide not a courier or camera but an enormous purpose-built ship.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If the intelligence recovered was too limited to justify the operation's historical importance. The chapter should separate technical audacity from intelligence yield.

## 22 — 1980s — The Family That Sold the Navy

**Selection:** John Walker's Soviet espionage ring, exposed in 1985 after years of compromising U.S. Navy cryptographic material.

**Why it earns the decade:** Walker demonstrates how a single insider with access to keys and procedures can turn an adversary's intercepted traffic from noise into readable intelligence—and how one spy can recruit a family and friend into an enduring system.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Aldrich Ames began spying in 1985 and arguably caused more direct human loss. The decade selection favors Walker because the ring's exposure in 1985 revealed a long-running compromise of naval communications and because Ames receives the 1990s chapter at the moment his betrayal became fully visible.

## 23 — 1990s — The Mole Who Killed the Sources

**Selection:** Aldrich Ames, arrested in 1994 after nearly a decade of spying for Moscow.

**Why it earns the decade:** Ames compromised U.S. human sources in the Soviet system, some of whom were executed, and forced painful questions about why obvious warning signs failed to trigger action sooner.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If casualty or operational-loss figures are repeated without careful sourcing, or if the chapter reduces a systemic counterintelligence failure to one man's greed.

## 24 — 2000s — The Spy Inside the FBI

**Selection:** Robert Hanssen, arrested in 2001 after espionage for the Soviet Union and Russia while serving inside the FBI.

**Why it earns the decade:** The institution responsible for catching spies had one inside its own counterintelligence machinery. His case produced major internal-security reforms and remains a study in how organizational trust can become an attack surface.

**What would make this selection wrong?** If later scholarship shows another 2000s espionage operation had greater strategic consequence, particularly in the emerging cyber domain.

## 25 — 2010s — The Database That Knew Everyone

**Selection:** The 2015 disclosure of breaches at the U.S. Office of Personnel Management, including background-investigation records on roughly 21.5 million people.

**Why it earns the decade:** A foreign intelligence service no longer needed to recruit a clerk to copy selected personnel files. A successful intrusion could remove an archive at population scale, including the intimate material used to judge who could be trusted with national secrets.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Attribution must be handled carefully. The theft's intelligence value is evident from the data, but the manuscript should distinguish official public statements, later assessments, and press attribution rather than state as fact what an agency did not formally assert at the time.

## 26 — 2020s — The Wire Inside the Wire

**Selection:** The PRC-affiliated campaign publicly tracked as Salt Typhoon, disclosed beginning in 2024 and still under investigation and remediation.

**Why it earns the decade so far:** The campaign compromised multiple telecommunications providers, stole call-data records, reached a limited number of private communications, and copied certain information related to court-ordered U.S. law-enforcement requests. It turns the communications infrastructure itself into collection terrain.

**What would make this selection wrong?** Nearly half the decade remains. This is explicitly a provisional selection as of September 2026. Another operation may prove more consequential before 2030, and the understood scope of Salt Typhoon may itself change.
