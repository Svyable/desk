# Show It What Good Looks Like

“Make it better” is one of the most expensive sentences in management.

Everybody understands it until two people disagree about what better means.

A writer thinks the paragraph needs more force. The editor thinks it needs less. A sales manager wants the proposal shorter. The product manager wants another page of evidence. A founder asks for a “premium” design and is disappointed when the designer produces something expensive-looking rather than quiet. A parent says the family schedule should be simpler and discovers that one person's simplicity is another person's missing information.

Humans survive these instructions because they learn taste socially.

They see examples. They watch what gets accepted. They hear why a senior person rejects one option and chooses another. They notice which mistakes draw attention and which pass without comment. Over time, “good” stops being a sentence and becomes a pattern.

Agents need the pattern too.

This is where many users make their second great mistake. The first is giving a vague job. The second is trying to repair vagueness with adjectives.

Make it concise.

Make it strategic.

Make it executive-ready.

Make it thoughtful.

Make it on-brand.

Make it rigorous.

These words can help. They can also become decorative labels attached to standards nobody has actually defined.

The agent economy will make examples more valuable because examples compress judgment.

Suppose you want an agent to write customer renewal emails. You can spend a page explaining tone: warm but not gushy, direct but not cold, confident without pressure, specific about value, brief enough for a busy reader, never defensive about price.

Or you can provide five emails that worked and three that made you wince, with short notes about why.

The second set carries information the adjectives do not.

It shows length.

It shows rhythm.

It shows how much context is normal.

It shows whether the company uses first names, contractions, bullets, links, or formal closings.

It shows how directly price is discussed.

It may also reveal that your verbal description of the style was wrong.

You said “warm.” The actual examples are restrained.

You said “brief.” Half the successful notes are longer when the customer has a complicated history.

You said “never defensive.” One of the best examples explains a price increase candidly because the context demanded it.

Examples discipline the principal as much as the agent.

They force taste to touch evidence.

This matters because capable models are very good at satisfying the surface form of an instruction. Ask for a strategic memo and you may receive the visible grammar of strategy: headings, tradeoffs, crisp language, perhaps an executive summary. The artifact can look like work you have seen before without containing the judgment that made the original work valuable.

A strong exemplar helps reveal the difference.

The principal can ask: what did this good piece *do*?

Did it eliminate irrelevant alternatives?

Did it expose the assumption carrying the decision?

Did it use primary sources?

Did it state what would make the recommendation wrong?

Did it connect the analysis to a real decision rather than merely surveying the topic?

Now “good” becomes partly testable.

The goal is not to turn every qualitative job into a scorecard. Some judgment resists clean metrics, and pretending otherwise creates false precision. The goal is to make the parts of quality that can be observed visible enough that improvement has direction.

Anthropic's work on agent evaluations makes a useful distinction in practice: an evaluation needs tasks and some way to grade whether the behavior succeeded. That sounds obvious. It becomes surprisingly difficult once the work is open-ended.

Did the research agent succeed?

If the answer is “the memo sounded smart,” the evaluation is weak.

Did it use the right sources?

Did it miss a material counterexample?

Did it answer the actual decision question?

Did it distinguish facts from forecasts?

Did it preserve uncertainty where the evidence was uncertain?

Did it invent a quotation?

Those questions are more useful because they can catch a fluent failure.

The same principle applies outside writing.

A calendar agent can be evaluated on whether it created conflicts, respected protected time, and correctly identified commitments.

An expense agent can be evaluated on reconciliation and exception handling.

A coding agent can be evaluated on tests, behavior, and unintended changes.

A purchasing agent can be evaluated on delivered result, budget, and compliance with constraints rather than how persuasive its shopping rationale sounded.

Good training moves from aesthetic approval toward observable consequence wherever possible.

The phrase *wherever possible* matters.

The agent economy will produce a lot of fake rigor. People will invent numeric scores for judgment because numbers feel automatable. “Executive readiness: 8.7.” “Brand alignment: 93 percent.” “Strategic quality: high.” The score can become a costume for the original subjective opinion.

If a human cannot explain what changes between a seven and a nine, the decimal point has not solved the problem.

A better method is comparative.

Which of these two outputs is better for the job, and why?

What specific property makes the rejected one risky or weak?

What would an excellent practitioner notice that the agent missed?

Can that property become a rule, an example, or a test?

This is how taste begins to externalize without pretending to become mathematics.

A collection of examples should contain variation.

If every exemplar is a perfect ordinary case, the agent may learn the center and remain helpless at the edges. Include good work under different conditions. Include near misses. Include cases where the right answer was to escalate. Include cases where the ordinary template was deliberately broken.

Bad examples are especially useful when they are annotated narrowly.

“This draft is too long” teaches less than “the first three paragraphs repeat information the customer already knows, so the actual decision does not appear until paragraph four.”

“This analysis is wrong” teaches less than “the conclusion depends on 2024 pricing even though the contract renewal uses the 2026 schedule.”

“This response is not empathetic enough” teaches less than “the customer reported a service interruption and the draft begins with policy instead of acknowledging impact.”

Specific correction is expensive the first time.

Vague correction is expensive forever.

There is a managerial habit worth developing here: whenever you change an agent's work, name the reason before you make the change.

You do not need to write an essay. One sentence is enough.

Without the sentence, your edit remains tacit. With it, the difference can become training data for the role.

This is harder than it sounds because experts often edit faster than they can explain. The eye sees the problem. The hand fixes it. Stopping to articulate the reason feels slower.

That friction is the price of turning personal skill into reusable leverage.

It also reveals which parts of your skill are not yet teachable.

Sometimes you will stare at two versions and know one is better without being able to say why. Do not invent a framework to hide the uncertainty. Keep both examples. Mark the preference. Return later.

Patterns may emerge after twenty cases that were invisible after two.

This is how an agent's apprenticeship becomes a research project into the principal's own taste.

The important asset is not the pile of examples by itself. It is the relationship among examples, criteria, and outcomes.

An email may feel good and fail commercially.

A sales script may violate the founder's taste and convert better.

A code change may be elegant and create operational fragility.

A report may impress executives and systematically miss the early signal it was created to detect.

Training on preference alone can preserve style while destroying purpose.

The role needs contact with consequence.

Where possible, connect examples to what happened next.

Which customer renewed?

Which explanation reduced support follow-up?

Which bug fix survived production?

Which forecast was calibrated?

Which meeting structure led to a decision?

Which household reminder actually reduced missed commitments instead of creating more notifications everybody ignored?

Outcome data is messy. It contains confounding factors. A customer may renew because of product value rather than the email. A project can succeed despite a poor memo. Do not turn correlation into certainty.

Still, consequences are a useful counterweight to taste.

The principal is training an agent to serve reality, not merely to imitate the principal's favorite sentences.

This becomes especially important when the principal is wrong.

An agent can make bad judgment more scalable. If the examples contain bias, obsolete practice, weak reasoning, or historical accidents, the trained role may reproduce them with admirable consistency.

Human apprentices can inherit the same problem. A bad institution can train people well in bad habits.

The solution is not to avoid examples. It is to make examples contestable.

Why is this one good?

According to whom?

Under what conditions?

Is the success attributable to the property we think it is?

What counterexample would change the rule?

When was the example produced, and is the environment still the same?

These questions keep training from becoming fossilization.

There is another distinction worth preserving: teaching examples and qualification cases should not be the same set forever.

If you show the agent ten examples and then congratulate it for handling those same ten examples, you have measured familiarity more than generalization.

A programmer would recognize this as testing on the training set.

A nontechnical operator can understand the problem just as easily. If you show a new assistant exactly how five past customers were handled, then evaluate the assistant only on those five customers, you still do not know what happens with customer six.

Keep some cases back.

Teach with representative examples.

Qualify with different cases that require the same underlying judgment.

For a coding agent, teach the repository's pattern for authentication on one feature, then see whether it applies the same constraints correctly in another part of the codebase.

For a household travel role, teach that very short connections are unacceptable, then test a different trip where the cheapest itinerary contains a technically legal but miserable connection.

For a research agent, show what a properly sourced claim looks like, then give it a new topic where the tempting source is a secondary summary rather than the primary record.

The point is not to make the exam tricky.

It is to find out whether the role learned the standard or memorized the answer.

A mature example set needs maintenance.

Some examples expire. A regulatory change makes an old workflow invalid. A company's strategy changes. A model improves and no longer needs a workaround. A customer's needs change. A style evolves. A security practice becomes unsafe.

If old examples remain forever, the role develops institutional scar tissue.

The agent behaves strangely because it is obeying lessons whose original incidents everyone forgot.

Human organizations accumulate this too. Procedures often contain rules created after a particular failure, long after the context disappears. Nobody remembers why the form has a field nobody uses.

Agent systems can accumulate policy debt faster because adding text is easy.

Every correction tempts the principal to add another sentence.

Soon the instructions resemble a legal code written entirely in response to edge cases.

This is why tests are often better than prose for known failures.

If the agent once omitted the tax treatment on a specific transaction type, a regression case can verify that future versions handle it. You do not necessarily need to burden every future prompt with a warning about that one mistake.

If the agent once sent a message when it should have escalated, the evaluation suite can include similar cases.

The job remains readable while the apprenticeship remains remembered.

Software engineers will recognize this instinct immediately. A bug becomes a test so that the same class of failure does not return unnoticed.

The agent economy spreads that habit beyond software.

An editor can maintain examples that catch fabricated quotations.

A recruiter can test whether the system applies stated criteria consistently across differently worded résumés.

A finance team can keep cases involving duplicate invoices, partial refunds, and unusual currencies.

A household agent can be tested on school holidays, split custody, travel days, and two people with the same first name.

The test does not need to be automated to be useful.

A folder of ten cases you rerun after a major change is better than trusting memory.

This brings us to model upgrades.

People naturally assume a smarter model makes the role better. Often it will. Sometimes it will behave differently in ways that matter locally.

The new model may be more verbose.

It may interpret a standing instruction differently.

It may use tools more aggressively.

It may become better at one edge case and worse at another.

It may stop responding to a workaround you built for the old version.

Without examples and evaluations, you experience the upgrade as a mood change.

With them, you can test the role.

This distinction is crucial for ownership. If your agent's quality exists only as a feeling produced by repeated interaction, you are dependent on continuity you cannot measure. If the quality is represented partly in examples and tests, you have an artifact that can travel.

You can compare models.

You can compare vendors.

You can train a replacement.

You can give the role to a human.

You can discover that the cheaper model is sufficient for ordinary cases while the expensive model belongs only at the difficult edge.

Examples create optionality.

They also reduce the cult of personality around models. Instead of asking which model is “best,” you can ask which system performs this job under these conditions.

That is a much more economic question.

The answer may vary by role.

A model that excels at coding may not be your best customer-communication agent. A system that produces beautiful prose may be poor at strict extraction. A cheap model may perform perfectly on a bounded classification task while a frontier model is justified for ambiguous research.

The job decides.

This is the deeper meaning of showing the agent what good looks like.

You are not trying to make the machine appreciate your taste as a human admirer would.

You are building a reference surface against which a changing system can be judged.

The surface contains examples, but also constraints, consequences, and known failures.

Over time, it becomes a kind of institutional memory.

This can feel almost trivial when the examples are small. A folder of approved emails. A handful of reconciliations. Ten contract cases. Twelve good research memos. A set of code changes and the tests that mattered.

Do not mistake small artifacts for small value.

The factory is often hidden in the fixtures.

Manufacturing quality depends not only on the raw machine but on jigs, gauges, tolerances, workholding, inspection, and process knowledge built around it. A general-purpose model is an extraordinary machine. The examples and evaluations around a role are the fixtures that make repeated output dependable.

Competitors can rent the same intelligence.

They may not have your fixtures.

That advantage is not permanent. Good practices spread. Models improve. Employees leave. Vendors standardize common workflows. Some of what you painfully teach today will become a default capability tomorrow.

This is not an argument for hoarding prompts as secret intellectual property.

It is an argument for learning how to turn judgment into operating assets faster than the environment changes.

That is a renewable skill.

When the base model gets better, the examples do not necessarily become worthless. They can let you see where the improvement matters. They can free you to raise the standard. They can shift the agent from ordinary execution into harder cases.

The principal keeps moving the frontier.

The best training relationship is therefore not imitation.

It is calibration.

This is what we mean by good.

This is how we know.

This is where the example stops applying.

This is the evidence that would change our mind.

This is the case where you call me.

Those sentences turn taste into something sturdier than a vibe without pretending judgment can be reduced to a number.

A prompt can ask for excellence.

An apprenticeship has to demonstrate it.
