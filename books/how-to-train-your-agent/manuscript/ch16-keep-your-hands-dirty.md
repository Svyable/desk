# Keep Your Hands Dirty

The best time to discover you can no longer do the work is not when the agent fails.

Automation has carried this irony for a long time. The machine handles ordinary conditions because ordinary conditions are predictable enough to automate. The human is left responsible for the abnormal case precisely when practice matters most.

Lisanne Bainbridge described this tension in 1983 in a paper whose title has outlived many of the systems it discussed: “Ironies of Automation.” The more the automated system handles, the more difficult it can become for the human operator to maintain the knowledge and situation awareness needed to intervene when automation reaches its edge.

Later human-factors research found related out-of-the-loop problems in automated control environments. Mica Endsley and Esin Kiris, for example, studied automation and situation awareness in a navigation task and found that operators could be slower and less prepared to recover when automation failed.

Those systems are not modern AI agents. A cockpit, process-control system, or 1990s expert system is not a language model using software tools. The analogy has a boundary.

The management problem does not.

If a machine takes the repetitions, what happens to the person who once learned through the repetitions?

This question becomes personal in the agent economy because the automation can arrive before the institution has redesigned the training path.

A junior analyst once learned by building models from messy source data. A coding apprentice learned by tracing bugs. A young lawyer read the documents. A new editor made thousands of sentence-level choices. A customer-support worker learned which apparently simple cases were not simple because customers brought them one after another.

Some of that work was drudgery.

Some of it was tuition.

Agents make the distinction urgent.

The lazy response is to preserve all manual work because hardship builds character. That wastes human life.

The equally lazy response is to automate everything the machine can do and assume judgment will somehow remain.

The principal needs to know which contact with the work produces the judgment required to supervise it.

This is why experienced people often feel strangely safe with agents at first. They already have the internal model. They can let the machine draft because they know what a good draft looks like. They can approve a generated code change because they have spent years debugging. They can direct research because they know which sources are suspicious.

The danger appears later.

Who trains the replacement principal?

A profession can enjoy several years of extraordinary leverage by giving agents to senior people while quietly consuming its apprenticeship ladder underneath them.

The junior no longer performs the routine work because the agent is cheaper and faster. The senior continues supervising because the senior learned before the automation arrived. The organization looks productive.

Then the senior leaves.

The junior has five years of experience approving machine work and one year of experience doing enough of the underlying work to know when the machine is strange.

The résumé says experienced.

The internal model may be thin.

This is the hollow expertise problem.

It does not mean every domain requires the old path forever. Training can be redesigned. In fact, it may improve dramatically once we stop confusing cheap labor with education.

A new analyst can work on historical cases without the commercial pressure to produce the answer. A coding learner can debug a problem before seeing the agent's solution. A lawyer can analyze a smaller but carefully chosen set of matters with direct feedback rather than spend months on document volume. A doctor can use simulation to encounter rare cases repeatedly, while real supervised practice remains where consequence matters.

Agents can generate endless variation and immediate comparison.

That is an educational gift.

The key is to use the agent as a training partner rather than a substitute for every difficult cognitive act.

One simple practice is the independent first pass.

Before asking the agent to analyze the unfamiliar contract, write down your own concerns.

Before seeing the generated diagnosis of a software bug, predict where the failure lives.

Before asking the research agent for a conclusion, state what evidence would change your mind.

The first pass may take five minutes. It prevents the machine's answer from becoming the frame through which you see the entire problem.

This matters because fluent output creates anchoring. Once the agent presents a coherent explanation, alternatives become harder to generate. The user shifts from thinking to checking.

A 2025 CHI study of knowledge workers by Hao-Ping Lee and colleagues examined self-reported critical-thinking behavior around generative AI. The researchers found that higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less reported critical-thinking effort, while the work itself shifted toward verification, response integration, and task stewardship. The study is not direct proof that AI causes long-term cognitive decline. It is survey research. But the shift it describes is recognizable: when generation becomes cheap, cognition moves toward oversight.

Oversight is a real skill.

It should not become the only skill.

A person who sees only completed machine reasoning can become excellent at approving familiar-looking output and poor at generating an independent model of the situation.

Keeping your hands dirty means preserving enough direct practice to maintain surprise.

Surprise is a professional asset.

The experienced accountant sees the number and thinks it is too clean.

The mechanic hears the engine and notices a rhythm the diagnostic code did not explain.

The editor reads a beautifully sourced paragraph and feels the argument has somehow become weaker.

The engineer looks at a passing test and asks what the test never covered.

These reactions come from internal expectations built through cases.

The agent's output is dangerous when it falls outside reality but inside the principal's tolerance for plausibility.

Direct practice narrows that tolerance.

This does not require doing half the work manually forever.

Sampling can preserve contact.

A manager whose agent autonomously processes routine cases can review a random sample, not only escalations. A programmer can periodically solve a contained problem without generation. A writer can draft some pages from a blank screen. A financial analyst can rebuild one model from raw data instead of always editing the agent's workbook.

The purpose is not purity.

It is calibration.

Does your sense of difficulty still match the real task?

Can you still recognize a good solution before the agent tells you it is good?

Can you recover when the system is unavailable?

Do you understand which parts of the workflow have changed since you stopped touching them?

A second practice is the takeover drill.

Automation researchers worry about out-of-the-loop performance because the human may need to resume control under unusual conditions. Agent users should occasionally rehearse the same transition.

What happens if the agent's memory is unavailable?

If the vendor goes down?

If the tool loses access halfway through a task?

If the role suddenly begins producing inconsistent results after an update?

Can the human or team continue the essential function at reduced capacity?

Not every task needs a disaster plan. Losing the dinner-planning agent is inconvenient. Losing the only functioning understanding of payroll before a deadline is different.

The importance of manual competence should scale with consequence.

This is where business continuity meets skill continuity.

A company may have backups of its data and no backup of the judgment needed to operate without the agent.

A household may know the passwords and not know which obligations the agent has been quietly coordinating.

A one-person business may have automated client intake so completely that the owner no longer remembers the sequence of checks the role performs.

Receipts and role documentation help.

Practice helps more.

A third practice is deliberate disagreement.

Ask the agent for a recommendation, then build the strongest case against it without asking the agent to do that for you first.

Or reverse the order: form your view, then ask the agent to attack it.

The point is not to manufacture conflict. It is to preserve a cognitive path that does not begin inside the model's framing.

This becomes important as agents become better than the principal at generating arguments. If the machine can produce both sides more fluently than the human, the human may feel there is no reason to struggle with either.

But the struggle is partly how preferences and values become clear.

Which risk bothers you?

Which uncertainty can you live with?

Which tradeoff matters?

Those are not always questions with an objective answer the agent can derive.

A fourth practice is teaching.

Explain the decision to another human.

Nothing exposes borrowed understanding faster than the need to answer a question without the agent in the room. A person who can repeat the machine's conclusion may discover that she cannot explain why the conclusion holds, which assumption is essential, or how the result would change under a different condition.

Teaching is expensive. That is partly why it works.

The agent economy will encourage us to remove friction. Some friction is the gym.

This is the central distinction between drudgery and formative difficulty.

Drudgery consumes effort without improving the internal model much. Formative difficulty forces discrimination.

Manually moving the same data between systems for the thousandth time is probably drudgery.

Reconciling a strange mismatch may be formative.

Formatting references may be drudgery.

Deciding which source is authoritative is formative.

Typing boilerplate code may be drudgery.

Tracing why the system fails only under concurrency may be formative.

Writing the fiftieth routine scheduling email may be drudgery.

Handling the conflict among three commitments may be formative.

Agents should remove the former aggressively and expose the latter intentionally.

This is an educational design problem hiding inside a productivity tool.

Employers have incentives to get it wrong. The company benefits immediately when agents reduce labor hours. The cost of weakened skill formation arrives years later, perhaps after the employee has left. The worker benefits immediately too; nobody enjoys pointless repetition. The temptation is aligned toward maximum delegation.

Institutions may need to create training work that the production process no longer economically requires.

That sentence sounds absurd until you recognize that simulators already do exactly this. Airlines do not wait for real emergencies to train pilots. Militaries rehearse scenarios nobody wants to occur. Medical education uses simulation because rare conditions cannot be scheduled conveniently. Cybersecurity teams conduct exercises.

The agent economy may turn more knowledge work into something that needs rehearsal.

A profession can build a case library. Trainees can make decisions before seeing the historical outcome. Agents can vary facts to test whether the learner understands the principle rather than memorizing the case. Senior experts can annotate why a plausible answer fails.

The machine becomes part of the apprenticeship infrastructure.

This is a better response than protecting obsolete junior work merely because it once produced seniors.

There is also a class question inside skill preservation.

People with enough time and institutional support can afford deliberate practice. Workers under pressure to maximize output may be told simply to use the agent and move faster. The wealthy student can learn both the craft and the automation. The precarious worker may be rewarded for throughput until the underlying skill becomes inaccessible.

If direct practice becomes a luxury, expertise can stratify.

The agent economy could democratize capability at the surface while concentrating deep judgment among people who retained access to formative experience.

That possibility deserves attention because access to output and access to learning are not the same thing.

A person can use a system that writes excellent code without being given the time to become an engineer.

A person can produce a polished analysis without learning statistics.

A person can navigate a bureaucracy with an agent without understanding the rights and obligations underneath it.

These are valuable capabilities. They are not equivalent to expertise.

The distinction should not be used to gatekeep ordinary users. Many old professional gates protected rents as well as safety. Agents will rightly let people do more for themselves.

The mature response is to know which kind of competence the situation requires.

For reversible exploration, borrowed capability may be enough.

For consequential judgment, somebody needs a deeper model of the domain.

That somebody might eventually be another agent system with superior evaluation. The human may cease to be the best technical supervisor. The next chapter deals with that problem.

Even then, keeping hands dirty can matter for ownership.

A principal who understands none of the work becomes dependent on whoever or whatever does. If the agent says the job requires another thousand dollars of compute, can the principal judge? If it says a policy makes a request impossible, can the principal challenge the policy? If it says a vendor is uniquely suitable, can the principal recognize lock-in?

You do not need to outperform your workers to govern them.

You need enough contact with the domain to know which questions preserve your agency.

This is why good executives often insist on direct customer contact even when they have research teams. Why good editors still read. Why technical leaders sometimes write code. Why restaurant owners taste the food.

They are not trying to prove they can do every employee's job.

They are keeping the reality channel open.

Agent owners need a reality channel too.

The form will vary. Sample the work. Do periodic manual passes. Teach the reasoning. Rehearse failure. Read the source material. Stay close to the people affected by the agent's decisions.

The worst future is not one in which machines do more work than humans.

It is one in which humans retain responsibility for systems they no longer understand well enough to question.

Keeping your hands dirty is not nostalgia for labor.

It is maintenance for judgment.
