# The Last Job Title Is Owner

Every book about a fast technology is in danger of freezing the present and calling it the future.

This one has the same problem.

The agents described in these pages will improve. Some current limitations will look quaint. Interfaces will change. Memory will improve. Tool use will become more reliable. Evals will become easier to generate. Systems will learn preferences with less explicit instruction. Multi-agent orchestration may disappear inside products instead of remaining something users think about. Tasks that currently require careful human decomposition may become routine.

Any useful conclusion has to survive that improvement.

So forget the current boundary between human work and agent work.

It is moving.

METR's measurements of frontier systems on software-oriented tasks have shown that the length of tasks agents can complete at specified reliability levels has been increasing. That evidence should not be turned into a countdown clock for every profession. Software tasks are not the economy. A benchmark is not a labor market. Reliability at one threshold is not autonomy everywhere.

The narrower lesson is enough.

Do not build your career around a fixed list of things the machine cannot do.

That list has an expiration date.

Build around the things that remain necessary when the list changes.

Somebody has to decide what the system is for.

Somebody has to decide which outcomes count.

Somebody has to decide what authority is acceptable.

Somebody has to decide what evidence is enough.

Somebody has to own the consequences when several plausible objectives conflict.

For now, that somebody is usually a person or an institution made of people.

That is what *owner* means in this chapter.

Not necessarily shareholder.

Not necessarily founder.

Not necessarily boss.

Owner means the principal who cannot escape the question by saying the agent did it.

A nurse can own a judgment without owning the hospital.

A developer can own a production change without owning the software company.

A parent can own a family decision without owning the family.

An employee can own a result while still working inside a hierarchy.

Ownership is the place where delegation stops.

This is why the word matters in an agent economy.

When action becomes cheap to delegate, responsibility becomes easier to blur.

The system found the source.

The agent wrote the code.

The model recommended the candidate.

The automation moved the meeting.

The assistant booked the flight.

The workflow rejected the claim.

Every sentence can be factually true and institutionally evasive.

A tool can explain how an action occurred.

It cannot decide who was entitled to make the action consequential.

That is a governance question.

At small scale, it is also a personal one.

The owner of an agent role decides what the role is allowed to become.

That decision begins before autonomy.

It begins with the job.

This book started by arguing that prompting is too small a frame. A prompt is an instruction in a moment. A role persists. The principal defines the objective, the sources, the examples, the tests, the tools, the boundaries, the escalation rules, and the evidence that allows authority to expand.

The agent goes through an apprenticeship.

Capability is treated as a candidate for permission rather than permission itself.

Corrections become operating knowledge instead of disappearing into yesterday's conversation.

Memory is separated from experience.

Probation creates evidence.

Promotion changes authority.

Receipts make action inspectable.

Human practice is preserved where it produces judgment.

The operating layer becomes an asset.

None of these ideas requires today's models to remain imperfect forever.

In fact, improvement makes several of them more important.

The more capable the machine, the more consequential a vague objective becomes.

The more autonomous the system, the more important authority boundaries become.

The more work an agent can perform, the more expensive it becomes to inspect everything manually.

The more persuasive the output, the more valuable independent evidence becomes.

The easier it is to create agents, the more important it becomes to avoid creating unnecessary bureaucracy.

Capability growth does not eliminate management.

It moves management upward.

A programmer once managed memory addresses directly and later relied on higher-level languages, compilers, operating systems, runtimes, frameworks, and cloud services. The work did not disappear. The layer of attention changed.

A business owner once reconciled every line by hand and later relied on accounting software. The need to know whether the business was solvent did not disappear.

A traveler once called airlines one by one and later used search engines. The need to decide where to go did not disappear.

Agents are a larger abstraction jump because they can operate inside the sequence rather than merely provide a better instrument.

But abstraction does not remove the need for a principal.

It concentrates the principal's attention on fewer, higher-leverage decisions.

That is the opportunity and the trap.

The opportunity is obvious.

One person can attempt work that previously required more coordination, more specialization, more money, or more time. Small firms can gain capabilities once reserved for larger ones. Experts can extend their reach. Generalists can cross boundaries far enough to discover which specialists they actually need. Households can reduce administrative friction. Public institutions can potentially serve people with lower transaction cost. New products can be tested before a traditional organization is built around them.

The trap is that leverage magnifies poor ownership too.

A confused objective executed slowly is annoying.

A confused objective executed by ten agents around the clock is infrastructure.

A bad metric pursued by one employee creates distorted behavior.

A bad metric embedded in an autonomous system creates distorted behavior at machine speed.

A weak manager can exhaust a team.

A weak principal can now exhaust a digital workforce that never gets tired enough to push back.

The agent economy will not merely reward people who can get more done.

It will punish people who cannot decide what should be done.

That sounds like a comforting victory for human judgment.

It is not.

Agents will increasingly participate in deciding too.

They will propose goals, decompose problems, generate evaluations, detect anomalies, compare strategies, and supervise other agents. The principal may sometimes be unable to outperform the system at any of those local tasks.

Ownership does not require being the smartest entity in the loop.

Boards hire chief executives with skills individual directors do not possess. Patients rely on physicians whose medical knowledge exceeds their own. Clients hire attorneys precisely because the attorney knows more about the law. Citizens delegate technical administration to institutions they cannot individually reproduce.

Principals have always delegated to expertise.

The enduring skill is not superior execution.

It is legitimate direction under uncertainty.

Why are we doing this?

Who bears the downside?

Whose preferences count?

What are we unwilling to trade away?

What evidence would make us stop?

Which decisions cannot be inferred from performance data because they are decisions about values rather than performance?

These questions do not become obsolete when machines answer the easier questions better than we do.

They become more exposed.

This is especially important because the word *owner* can encourage the wrong fantasy.

The agent economy will produce a great deal of one-person-company mythology.

One human, one laptop, one billion-dollar business, infinite workers, no meetings, no payroll, no friction.

Some extraordinary companies may become much smaller than historical equivalents. Cheap machine labor can reduce staffing needs in particular functions. A founder may reach meaningful scale before hiring roles that once arrived much earlier.

But institutions exist for reasons beyond producing text and code.

They pool capital.

They carry liability.

They create continuity beyond one person.

They provide specialized judgment.

They negotiate conflict.

They absorb shocks.

They satisfy legal and regulatory obligations.

They create trust through reputations and repeated relationships.

They do physical work.

They care for people.

They create communities whose value cannot be represented as a swarm of tasks.

The agent economy will change the boundary of the firm.

It will not make every boundary disappear.

Nor should everyone become an entrepreneur.

Ownership in this chapter is not a motivational command to quit your job.

Many people prefer the risk sharing, mission, colleagues, benefits, resources, and scope of working inside an organization. Many economically valuable projects require institutions larger than any one person's agent bench. Many people should not have to become tiny corporations to earn a good living.

The point is about posture.

Inside whatever institution you inhabit, understand the delegated system around your work well enough to remain a principal rather than merely its operator.

Do not become the person who moves outputs between agents without understanding the objective.

Do not become the human approval button at the end of a process you can no longer interrogate.

Do not measure your value by the quantity of artifacts the system can create.

Do not surrender the boring evidence because the answer sounds good.

Do not preserve obsolete manual work merely because it once gave you status.

Do preserve enough contact with reality to know when the abstraction has drifted.

That is survival.

The word may sound melodramatic. Most technological transitions do not arrive as a single day when the old economy ends and the new economy begins. People continue using old tools beside new ones. Job titles persist after the work beneath them changes. Institutions adapt unevenly. Some companies move too fast. Others too slowly. Regulation arrives after some failures and before others. The economic gains distribute unevenly.

Survival is therefore less about preparing for one dramatic replacement event than about avoiding a slow loss of agency.

You can lose agency while remaining employed.

You can lose it while becoming more productive.

You can lose it while the dashboard says everything is improving.

The loss occurs when the system performs more of your work and you understand less of what makes the work good.

When you can no longer define the objective without asking the tool.

When you cannot tell whether the evidence is strong.

When you approve what you cannot challenge.

When your personal method lives entirely inside a vendor you cannot leave.

When every correction disappears into a conversation instead of improving the next case.

When your leverage belongs entirely to somebody else's platform or somebody else's organization and you have no transferable capability to show for it.

When the agent becomes the principal and you become the peripheral device.

The alternative is not heroic human control over every detail.

That would waste the technology.

The alternative is designed dependence.

You depend on the model for broad capability.

You may depend on vendors for compute, search, payments, storage, and communication.

You may depend on your employer for data, permissions, institutional identity, and customers.

You depend on other humans for expertise, trust, care, and legitimacy.

Modern life is made of dependencies.

Ownership means knowing which dependencies exist, which you can replace, which you can audit, which you accept, and which are too important to leave invisible.

This is why portability matters.

You may not own the model.

Own the job definition where you are entitled to.

Own your method.

Own the evaluation habits.

Own the skill of turning a failure into a durable improvement.

Own enough of the operating layer that you can rebuild it under a different system.

Own the judgment about when the machine should stop.

Own your share of the outcome.

That last line is economic, not merely psychological.

Productivity gains are not automatically paid to the person who creates them.

If your agent system allows you to produce twice as much, the extra value can flow to customers through lower prices, to an employer through higher expectations, to a platform through fees, to owners through profit, or to you through wages, equity, free time, bargaining power, or a business of your own.

The technology expands the pie and changes the bargaining terrain.

It does not decide the split.

The worker of the agent economy has to learn capture as well as capability.

Sometimes the right move is to become more valuable inside the firm.

Sometimes it is to negotiate scope rather than hours.

Sometimes it is to keep the saved time.

Sometimes it is to start something.

Sometimes it is to refuse a system that turns productivity into surveillance and permanent acceleration.

Sometimes the benefit is not income at all.

The household agent that gives a parent forty minutes back may create value that never appears in GDP.

The research agent that lets a scientist test one more idea may create value years later.

The accessibility agent that lowers the coordination burden around a disability may create dignity before it creates money.

Ownership includes the right to choose what the dividend is for.

That may be the most human economic question in the book.

What do you want the leverage *for*?

More output is not an answer.

It is a capacity.

The industrial economy became extraordinarily good at producing things and then had to keep answering what should be produced. The information economy became extraordinarily good at producing and moving information and then had to answer what deserved attention.

The agent economy will become extraordinarily good at producing action.

Then we will discover that action was not the scarce thing we thought it was.

Direction was.

This is why the last job title is owner.

Not because everyone will own a company.

Because the person who survives cheap intelligence is the person who can still own a purpose.

The practical beginning is modest.

Choose one recurring piece of work.

Write down what the job is before asking the agent to do it.

Show it real examples.

Let it work in shadow mode.

Notice where it fails.

Turn the important failures into something persistent.

Test the role again.

Give it only the authority it has qualified to use.

Require evidence for consequential actions.

Stay close to enough of the work that your judgment does not become ceremonial.

When the role becomes reliable, preserve what made it reliable somewhere you can understand and, where you have the right, carry forward.

Then repeat.

That sequence sounds less exciting than summoning an army of digital employees.

Good.

The future will contain enough summoning.

What it will need are principals.

The agent does not need you to be faster than it.

It does not need you to type more.

It does not need you to know every answer first.

It needs you to know what the answer is for.

That is the job that remains after the jobs change.
