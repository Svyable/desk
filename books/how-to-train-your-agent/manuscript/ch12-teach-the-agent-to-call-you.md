# Teach the Agent to Call You

The person who never asks for help is rarely the person you trust with the hardest work.

At first this seems backward. Independence is valuable. A senior employee is expected to solve more problems without supervision. The whole promise of an agent is that it can carry work without turning every step into another request for human attention.

But maturity is not the elimination of escalation.

It is the improvement of escalation.

A new employee asks too often because she does not yet know which decisions are ordinary. An overconfident employee asks too rarely because she mistakes completion for competence. The experienced professional learns the edge of the role. She handles the center quickly and interrupts somebody else when the case crosses into territory where another decision, permission, or expertise is required.

Agents need the edge made visible.

This is harder than telling the system, “Ask if you are unsure.”

Uncertainty is not a reliable internal light that turns red at the correct moment. A model can be fluent when wrong and cautious when right. It can have enough context to produce an answer without having enough authority to act. It can be confident about the technical task and unaware of a social consequence outside its representation.

The principal should therefore train escalation around situations, not moods.

A legal threat arrives.

Two authoritative sources conflict.

The customer's identity cannot be resolved.

The action cannot be reversed.

A transaction exceeds the role's limit.

The requested work falls outside the stated job.

A tool reports partial success and the real state is uncertain.

The agent would have to invent a missing fact to continue.

A person asks it to override standing policy.

A request involves a category the principal has decided deserves licensed or specialist judgment.

These are observable conditions.

The agent does not need to experience anxiety. It needs a rule for handing the problem back.

Current engineering and security guidance repeatedly makes room for this. OpenAI's agent guidance describes human intervention as appropriate after exceeded failure thresholds and before high-risk actions. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre has advised organizations to decide in advance when agents should stop and seek human approval. The important word is *advance*.

Escalation should not be invented after the system is already in trouble.

A principal who trains only successful completion is teaching the wrong job.

This can happen unintentionally. Every time the agent asks a question, the user experiences friction. The whole point was to save time. So the user says, “Use your best judgment.” The phrase may be appropriate. Repeated often enough, it becomes a culture in which interruption is treated as failure.

Soon the system is optimized toward finishing.

The principal may even celebrate low escalation rates as evidence of autonomy.

Then one day the agent completes a task that should have stopped.

This is the management version of training people not to bring bad news.

Organizations do it constantly. A manager says the door is always open and visibly punishes interruptions. A company says safety comes first and rewards schedule above everything. A leader says “tell me early” and then attacks the person who surfaces uncertainty before a deadline.

The formal instruction loses to the lived incentive.

Agents do not absorb culture exactly as humans do, but the surrounding system can reproduce the same effect. If evaluations reward completion and penalize escalation, if users override every pause, if the interface makes escalation cumbersome, the role will behave differently than a policy sentence suggests.

Good training includes examples where escalation is the correct answer.

Not merely cases where the agent fails.

Cases where the agent demonstrates competence by refusing to proceed.

A purchasing agent receives an invoice whose bank details changed unexpectedly. Stop.

A scheduling agent sees two people with the same name and cannot determine which one the principal meant. Ask.

A coding agent discovers that the requested fix requires changing an authentication boundary outside its authorized repository. Escalate.

A research agent finds that the primary source contradicts the number in the executive brief. Surface the conflict instead of silently choosing the more convenient figure.

The evaluation should mark those outcomes as success.

This changes the principal's own psychology. The interruption becomes evidence that the role is protecting the boundary rather than failing to automate.

There is an obvious cost.

An agent that escalates too often is a machine for manufacturing questions.

The principal has simply converted execution work into supervisory work.

This is why escalation needs calibration.

Early in childhood, over-escalation can be acceptable. A rookie asks. The principal observes which questions recur. As patterns become understood, routine cases move inside the role. The escalation rate should fall for the center of the job while remaining sensitive at the edges.

If the same question arrives every week, one of three things is probably true.

The role needs a new rule.

The agent lacks a reliable source.

Or the task genuinely requires human judgment and should be acknowledged as part of the job rather than treated as a temporary exception.

That classification is valuable. It tells the principal whether training is progressing or merely accumulating interruptions.

Escalation also needs a destination.

“Ask a human” is not a complete design.

Which human?

How quickly?

With what evidence?

Can the agent continue other work while waiting?

What happens if nobody responds?

Does the request expire?

Can a lower-risk fallback be used?

An agent that discovers a customer identity conflict and posts a vague question in a busy channel has technically escalated and operationally failed.

A mature escalation should make the decision easy to receive.

“Two active accounts match the customer's name. The email domain matches Account B, but the invoice number matches Account A. I have not issued the refund. Please choose A or B.”

That is different from “I'm not sure what to do.”

The agent has reduced the problem to the human judgment that remains.

This may become one of the most valuable skills in agent design: compressing exceptions.

If agents handle the routine work, humans inherit the irregular remainder. A poor system dumps the entire context on the reviewer. A good one explains why the normal path failed, preserves the evidence, and asks the smallest necessary question.

The difference determines whether one person can supervise three agents or thirty.

Human attention is not only limited in volume. It is expensive to reload.

A manager interrupted by a complex exception may need five minutes simply to understand the state. If the agent provides the relevant documents, action history, conflict, and proposed choices, the actual judgment may take thirty seconds.

The machine should do the context assembly before it calls.

This is not an excuse to hide complexity. Compression can become manipulation if the agent omits the evidence that would change the decision. The reviewer needs enough context to challenge the framing.

A good escalation therefore includes both a concise question and a path to the underlying record.

Summary and receipt.

Decision and evidence.

This becomes particularly important when the agent is better at the underlying technical work than the principal. The human may not be able to rederive the whole analysis. The escalation needs to identify what kind of authority is missing rather than ask the human to pretend to be the superior technician.

A coding agent might say the two implementation options both pass tests, but one changes the public API and requires a product decision. The principal does not need to outperform the agent at coding. She needs to decide whether compatibility should be broken.

A research agent might find that the evidence supports two plausible forecasts depending on a disputed assumption. The principal's job is to decide which risk matters, not rerun every source query.

Escalation can therefore move human work upward.

The machine handles execution until it reaches a value conflict, authority boundary, or ambiguity whose cost exceeds its mandate.

That sounds efficient and often is.

It can also create a new form of cognitive exhaustion.

Imagine a day where every routine case disappears and the human sees only conflicts, edge cases, exceptions, and high-stakes decisions. The calendar becomes an emergency room of judgment. There are no easy wins, no repetitive tasks, no gradual warm-up.

The agent has not eliminated work so much as concentrated difficulty.

Organizations need to design for this. A principal supervising many agents may need protected review periods rather than constant notifications. Escalations can be queued by urgency. Some can be batched. Some require immediate interruption. Some can be routed to specialists rather than the owner of the agent.

The agent should know not only when to call but how loudly.

A typo in a draft does not deserve the same channel as a suspected fraudulent payment.

This sounds like ordinary incident management because it is.

Agent systems turn personal workflows into operations.

The individual who once handled tasks sequentially may now have multiple processes running in parallel, each capable of producing a request for judgment. The principal begins to need queue design, severity, routing, and escalation paths that once belonged only to organizations.

This is the agent economy arriving in the nervous system.

A small business owner with five active agents may discover that her real job is not issuing instructions. It is deciding which exceptions deserve her finite attention.

This makes silence meaningful.

If an agent can proceed without escalation, the principal is implicitly trusting all ordinary cases that fit the role. Silence becomes delegated authority.

That is why monitoring matters. The absence of questions is reassuring only if the agent is still observing the triggers correctly.

A change in model behavior could make the system less likely to escalate.

A tool might stop exposing a warning field.

A policy update might create a new category of exception the role does not recognize.

The principal should occasionally test the alarm.

Give the role a known escalation case after an upgrade.

Review a sample of autonomous decisions to see whether hidden exceptions are slipping through.

Track whether the nature of escalations changes suddenly.

The fire alarm is not trusted because it remained quiet all year.

It is trusted because we know it can still ring.

There is a second form of escalation that matters: escalation to expertise.

Agents make it easier for people to enter domains they do not know. This is one of their greatest benefits. A small business owner can explore legal questions before calling counsel. A patient can organize questions before an appointment. A homeowner can understand possible causes before hiring a professional. A programmer can examine a financial model. A marketer can query a dataset.

The system can extend curiosity across boundaries.

It can also create the illusion that the boundary disappeared.

A mature agent should help the principal recognize when the work has entered a region where qualified human expertise matters.

This is not merely a disclaimer pasted at the bottom of every response. Blanket disclaimers become wallpaper. The useful behavior is contextual referral.

“This clause appears to change indemnification in a way that could materially shift legal exposure. I can summarize the difference, but this is a reasonable point for counsel to review.”

“This electrical symptom could involve a condition where remote troubleshooting is not appropriate. Stop work and use a qualified electrician.”

The role needs criteria for recognizing the edge.

This protects the principal from a dangerous combination: abundant capability and borrowed confidence.

The third form is escalation to the future self.

Not every decision deserves an immediate human response. An agent can surface unresolved questions for later review rather than interrupting the present task.

“Three subscriptions have usage below the cancellation threshold, but each renewal date is more than sixty days away. Add to Friday review.”

This is a small design choice with large effects on attention.

Humans are poor at treating every notification as a neutral queue item. An interruption creates urgency simply by arriving. Agents can flood the principal with low-grade importance until the human disables the alerts or stops reading them.

Good escalation protects credibility by being selective.

An alerting system that cries wolf trains the principal too.

It teaches the human to ignore the machine.

Then the rare critical escalation arrives in a channel whose authority has been spent.

The relationship between agent and principal is therefore reciprocal. The agent learns which cases require attention. The principal learns whether the agent's requests deserve it.

Trust is damaged by unnecessary escalation as well as missed escalation.

This is why thresholds should be reviewed using outcomes.

Which escalations did the human resolve immediately?

Which were predictable enough to turn into policy?

Which should have been routed elsewhere?

Which did the human ignore without consequence?

Which autonomous cases should have escalated but did not?

The correction ledger should contain both false alarms and missed alarms.

A role matures when that boundary sharpens.

At some point, the best agent may appear less intelligent than the flashy one because it stops more often.

The flashy system always has a plan. It can answer any question, produce another option, and continue through uncertainty. It feels capable because motion never stops.

The trained system has learned that some forms of continuation are expensive ignorance.

It pauses.

That pause is one of the hardest behaviors to sell in a product demo.

It may be one of the most valuable behaviors in real work.

The agent economy will not be survived by making machines that never need us.

It will be survived by building machines that know which parts of our judgment are worth interrupting.
