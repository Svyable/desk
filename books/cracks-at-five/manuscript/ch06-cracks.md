# Chapter 6 — Cracks

A crack is information without interpretation.

That sentence is the center of the book.

By 1995, signs of distress at Sampoong were not purely retrospective discoveries in rubble. Korean historical accounts describe cracks and deformation before the final day. On June 29 the indications became severe enough that upper areas were closed and technical discussions were held.

Why did that not produce a full evacuation?

One answer is money. Closing a major department store during business hours imposes an immediate, visible cost. Evacuating and finding nothing catastrophic later would have looked like an expensive overreaction. Continuing to operate offered revenue now and placed the risk in an uncertain future.

But incentives work through interpretation. Managers do not usually say, `I choose death over sales.` They say the evidence is incomplete, repair can begin, the problem is local, someone else has not ordered closure, or the expert did not use the word collapse. Ambiguity becomes an asset.

Safety systems therefore fail when they demand certainty at the moment certainty is least available. The correct threshold for evacuation is not proof of imminent collapse. It is a sufficiently high combination of consequence and credible warning.

The asymmetry is brutal. A false evacuation costs hours and money. A false decision to remain can cost hundreds of lives.

Sampoong's cracks were not merely defects in concrete. They were tests of whether the organization could act under uncertainty. It failed the test before the slab failed.
