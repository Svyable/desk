# Chapter 5 — Machines on the Roof

Machines are rarely treated as part of a building's biography after installation. They should be.

Rooftop air-conditioning equipment became one of the most memorable elements of the Sampoong story. Later reconstructions describe heavy units being relocated across the roof rather than lifted cleanly away and reset. Their weight, movement and subsequent vibration are treated as contributors to progressive damage.

The important point is not that air conditioners are dangerous. It is that maintenance can become structural work without anyone calling it structural work.

Facilities departments routinely move equipment, cut openings, add tanks, install signs, route pipes and place temporary loads. Each operation can look local. The structure experiences the sum.

This is why the direct-cause hunt can mislead. If one asks, `What caused Sampoong?`, the answer can sound like a competition between bad design, weak construction, extra load, moved machinery, corruption and the decision not to evacuate. These causes operated at different layers. Some reduced capacity. Some increased demand. Some generated damage. Some prevented correction. One determined how many people remained exposed when physical failure arrived.

The roof machinery is therefore most useful as a boundary-crossing fact. A noise complaint, an equipment relocation and a structural safety margin belonged to different organizational categories. Physics did not respect those categories.

The machine moved. The load path had to absorb the decision.
