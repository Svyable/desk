# Chapter 2 — A Building Changes Its Mind

Buildings have biographies even when their drawings pretend otherwise.

Sampoong began with one program and became another. The distinction matters because structural design is not an abstract reserve of strength waiting to absorb whatever an owner later imagines. Occupancy determines loads, penetrations, mechanical systems, circulation and fire behavior. Change the use and you change the problem the structure is being asked to solve.

The later forensic investigation did not identify one magic defect. A 2011 forensic-science paper summarizing the investigation describes overlapping errors in design, construction and management. Investigators surveyed the collapse and ground conditions, tested recovered concrete and steel, examined design and construction records, and performed structural analysis. That breadth itself is evidence against the folk version in which one rooftop machine simply crushed an otherwise healthy store.

A useful way to read Sampoong is as a sequence of edits. A floor is added. Columns and openings are altered. Restaurants place heavier demands on the upper level. Heated floors and equipment add dead load. Mechanical equipment sits on the roof. Maintenance decisions create new stresses. Each edit inherits all previous edits.

Software engineers would call this technical debt. Structural engineers do not need the analogy. Concrete remembers directly.

The crucial distinction is between a change that is merely undocumented and a change that has not been recalculated. Paperwork failure is dangerous because it can hide physical failure, but the absence of paper is not itself what breaks a slab. The load path breaks when reality no longer matches the assumptions under which the building was made safe.

Sampoong's biography became dangerous because the institution that kept changing the building did not maintain a trustworthy model of what the building had become.
