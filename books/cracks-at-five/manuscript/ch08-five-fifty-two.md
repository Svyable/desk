# Chapter 8 — Five Fifty-Two

Time becomes unstable in catastrophe records.

Some Korean sources place the beginning of collapse at approximately 5:52 p.m.; others report around 5:57. That five-minute difference is trivial to the structural mechanism and important to historical reconstruction. It is exactly the kind of discrepancy a careful book should preserve rather than silently harmonize.

Clocks may have differed. Witnesses reconstructed time afterward. News organizations rounded. Official logs may use dispatch time rather than first movement. The right response is not to choose the most cinematic minute. It is to build a source-indexed chronology.

When failure began, the south wing did not provide a graceful final warning. Progressive collapse converted local structural failure into cascading loss of support. Floors fell onto floors. The event took seconds.

That speed changes how we assign responsibility. The meaningful evacuation window was not during the collapse. It was during the hours when ambiguity still existed.

This is a recurring pattern in high-consequence systems. Physical systems often move from recoverable to unrecoverable faster than institutions can deliberate. Flood walls overtop, thermal runaway accelerates, fires flash over, financial markets gap, aircraft lose altitude. If decision architecture waits for unmistakable confirmation, confirmation arrives as the event.

At Sampoong, the final seconds were engineering. The lost afternoon was governance.
