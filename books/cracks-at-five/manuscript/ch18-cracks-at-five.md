# Chapter 18 — Cracks at Five

The ruins of Sampoong made the danger obvious. The achievement would have been making it obvious while the building still stood.

That requires a different conception of safety.

Safety is not the absence of visible failure. It is the capacity to convert weak, distributed signals into action before certainty arrives. It requires a trustworthy model of what has been built, disciplined control of alterations, inspection independent enough to be inconvenient, and emergency authority that can tolerate a false alarm.

The four evidence classes used in this book are themselves a response to Sampoong's lesson. Verified facts belong apart from credible testimony. Disputed claims should not borrow emotional force from the death toll. Synthesis should be labeled as synthesis. Information becomes safer when its status travels with it.

Sampoong's most important warning may therefore be smaller than the collapse. It is the ordinary organizational sentence: `We know there is a problem, but we do not yet know enough to stop.`

Sometimes that sentence is disciplined caution. Sometimes it is how a system purchases one more hour of normality.

At five o'clock on June 29, the building had not yet fallen. That is the moment worth studying.

Because after the walls came down, everyone agreed there had been a problem.
