# Chapter 14 — Criminal Negligence

After mass death, law is asked to translate a system failure into individual culpability.

That is difficult work.

Public anger after Sampoong included demands for severe punishment. Yet criminal law distinguishes bad judgment, negligence, recklessness and intentional killing. The existence of warning does not automatically establish an intent to kill. The fact that managers benefited from continued business does not erase the evidentiary burden attached to a homicide theory.

The later sentences therefore became part of the public argument about whether ordinary negligence law could express the scale of the harm. Korean labor and legal commentary has continued to use Sampoong when discussing corporate disaster responsibility.

A careful history must do two things at once. It must not soften decisions that kept the public inside a visibly distressed building. It must also refuse to retrofit mental states from outrage alone.

This distinction matters because accountability systems learn from categories. If every catastrophic outcome is described as murder, institutions may miss the more common danger: ordinary organizational incentives repeatedly producing intolerable risk without anyone needing to desire the result.

The challenge is to punish culpable conduct without pretending that punishment itself repairs the information architecture that made the conduct possible.
