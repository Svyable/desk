# Chapter 1 — Pink Walls

Before Sampoong became rubble, it was a promise.

The department store belonged to the Seoul of fast money, new apartments, branded goods and confidence that construction could keep pace with desire. Tens of thousands of people could pass through on a busy day. Its pink exterior did not advertise structural ambition. It advertised normality: shopping, restaurants, escalators, sales, appointments, work.

That normality is where the story has to begin, because disaster histories are distorted by hindsight. Once we know a building will collapse, every alteration looks like a clue and every crack like a countdown. People inside the building did not possess that ending. They lived with a structure that had stood for years and with a city where change itself was routine.

Korean accounts place Sampoong inside a much wider sequence of rapid urbanization and public failures. The Seongsu Bridge collapse in October 1994 had already killed thirty-two people. In 1995 a gas explosion at a Daegu subway construction site killed more than one hundred. Sampoong arrived inside a society already arguing about the cost of compressed growth.

Yet phrases such as `compressed growth` and `ppalli-ppalli` can become alibis of their own. A culture does not reduce a column diameter. A national tempo does not move an air-conditioning unit. People, firms and public offices make specific decisions. The job of this book is to keep the social explanation connected to the engineering chain.

The Korean Academy of Korean Studies describes the store as originally designed as a commercial complex and later changed into a department store without adequate structural diagnosis, followed by further expansion. That account matters because Sampoong was not a single mistake frozen in concrete. It was a building whose intended use, loading and physical arrangement kept changing.

The pink walls made those changes invisible to most of the people who depended on them.
