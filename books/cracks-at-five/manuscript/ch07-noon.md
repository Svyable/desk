# Chapter 7 — Noon

Disasters have a strange middle period in which everyone later wants a clean decision point.

June 29 had several.

Reports from the upper floor intensified. Managers inspected. Experts were consulted. Air conditioning was shut down. Parts of the fifth floor were closed. The building below continued to function as a department store.

The moral outrage attached to those hours is justified, but outrage can flatten the mechanism. We need to know who said what, when, with which authority, and what evidence was physically available at that moment. The definitive Korean prosecutorial white paper remains essential because later summaries often blend testimony, inference and reconstructed timeline.

This draft therefore keeps some famous details in Class 2 until the underlying records are recovered. Did every executive understand collapse as imminent? Which technical advisers recommended what? Exactly when did particular managers leave? Which employees attempted informal evacuation? These are answerable historical questions, not spaces for dramatic invention.

What is already solid is enough. Serious warning existed. Business continued. The result was a building full of workers and customers near the evening shopping period.

The hours before Sampoong fell expose a recurring weakness in organizations: emergency authority is often less clearly designed than ordinary authority. Everyone knows who can open the store. Far fewer systems state who can close it immediately, on incomplete evidence, and be protected afterward for being wrong.

A safety culture is not what people say after an accident. It is the authority structure for an inconvenient afternoon.
