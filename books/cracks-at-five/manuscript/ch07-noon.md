# Chapter 7 — Noon

By the morning of June 29, the question was no longer whether Sampoong had defects. Serious signs of failure were visible on the fifth floor. Management responded, but not by emptying the building: parts of the upper floor were closed, inspections were made, and business continued below. The Academy of Korean Studies' account is careful on the point that matters most. It says severe signs of collapse appeared that morning and management decided to continue operating while repairs were attempted.

That sentence is more useful than the tidier versions of the story that have accumulated since. Later retellings often supply a cinematic chain of exact times, expert warnings, executive conversations and motives. Some of those details may prove correct. Until the 1995 Seoul District Prosecutors' Office white paper and the underlying testimony are in hand, they should not be made to carry the chapter. The surviving high-confidence record already gives us the harder fact: people with authority recognized a serious structural problem and chose a response compatible with keeping most of the store open.

There is a temptation to make the decision incomprehensible. It is more disturbing if it was not. Closing a large department store on incomplete evidence imposed an immediate, visible cost. Continuing to trade preserved revenue and avoided the embarrassment of a false alarm. The danger, meanwhile, was probabilistic until it suddenly was not. An evacuation order had to be justified before the building supplied the proof.

That is the strongest defense available to the decision-makers, and it deserves to stay in the story. Warning signs are not the same thing as a countdown clock. Cracks can be alarming without establishing that collapse is minutes away; experts can disagree; managers can reasonably fear disrupting thousands of people unnecessarily. Hindsight turns every clue into an arrow pointing toward 5:52 p.m. The people inside the day did not have hindsight.

But uncertainty cuts both ways. If the consequence of being wrong is hundreds of people under a failing concrete structure, the relevant institutional question is not whether collapse has been proved. It is who has the authority to stop ordinary operations while the uncertainty is resolved, what evidence that person needs, and what happens to them if the building turns out to be safe.

Sampoong had abundant machinery for opening the doors each morning. On June 29 it proved much less capable of closing them early.
