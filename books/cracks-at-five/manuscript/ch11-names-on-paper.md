# Chapter 11 — Names on Paper

A mass casualty event produces a second emergency made of names.

Who was inside? Who was injured but unidentified? Who had gone home without reporting? Which missing-person report was duplicated? Which body had not yet been identified? Which worker counted as an employee of the store, a tenant, a contractor or an informal worker?

Contemporary Korean records show the missing-person number moving substantially as authorities reconciled deaths, rescues, duplicate reports and people later found alive or elsewhere. On July 17, for example, the accident headquarters reported a provisional 274 missing after subtracting identified dead, a rescued person, returns and duplicate reports while adding new reports. By late August, Seoul used a review process to provisionally recognize many missing people as dead based on defined evidentiary criteria.

This administrative work can look peripheral beside collapsed concrete. It is not. Families cannot receive certainty, compensation, funerals or legal status without it. Workers' compensation authorities faced the unusual problem of people presumed dead before ordinary missing-person periods had elapsed.

The Seoul Archives preserves casualty and missing-person records, some restricted for privacy and some open. Together they reveal a disaster as a problem of identity resolution.

The lesson is severe: institutions need to know who is exposed before disaster, not only after it.
