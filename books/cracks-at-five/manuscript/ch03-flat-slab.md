# Chapter 3 — Flat Slab

A flat-slab building can look wonderfully simple. Floors meet columns without deep beams running everywhere beneath them. The architectural space is cleaner. Services have room. Ceilings can be flatter. But visual simplicity does not mean structural simplicity.

Around a column, load from the slab must find a way into a comparatively small support. One critical failure mode is punching shear: the column and slab connection can lose its ability to carry the concentrated forces, allowing the column to behave, in simplified terms, as if it were punching through the slab.

Sampoong's collapse investigation centered heavily on this mechanism. The later forensic summary identifies the store as a reinforced-concrete flat-slab structure and stresses that many design, construction and management errors overlapped. Popular retellings often compress the mechanism into one image—undersized columns or an overloaded roof. The real lesson is harsher. Structural reserve can be consumed incrementally long before the public sees anything dramatic.

That creates an epistemic problem. A crack is not automatically a collapse prediction. Buildings crack for many reasons. Engineers distinguish location, orientation, width, rate of change, associated deformation and the structure around it. The same visible line can be cosmetic in one context and terrifying in another.

The owner of a public building therefore needs more than inspection. It needs escalation logic. What observation forces what action? Who has the power to close a floor? What combination of deformation, vibration and crack growth triggers evacuation without waiting for certainty?

At Sampoong, the engineering problem and governance problem converged. The closer the structure came to failure, the more valuable time became. The institution had no equivalent of a circuit breaker.
