# Chapter 4 — The Fifth Floor

The fifth floor matters because use became load.

Restaurants are not just rooms with tables. Commercial kitchens bring equipment, storage, water, mechanical services and concentrations of mass. Korean historical accounts describe the upper-level changes as part of the cumulative structural problem. Later engineering retellings also emphasize heated restaurant floors and other additions that increased loading.

It is tempting to narrate every added kilogram as criminal recklessness. That is too easy. Buildings are altered constantly. The safety question is whether alteration is reviewed against the structure that actually exists.

Sampoong demonstrates the danger of what might be called administrative invisibility. A restaurant operator can see a floor as leasable space. A manager can see it as revenue. A designer can see it as a loading category. A maintenance worker can see pipes and vibration. A structural analyst sees a system of slabs, columns and transfer forces. None of these views is false. Failure begins when one becomes authoritative while the others disappear.

The fifth floor also became an early-warning surface. By June 29, serious deformation there was visible. Korean accounts describe reports from restaurant areas, sagging and cracking, and management meetings held while business continued below.

The question is no longer whether the building had a defect. The question becomes who was entitled to transform observation into an order.

A strong safety institution gives bad news a short path to power. Sampoong gave it a meeting.
