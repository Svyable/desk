# Cracks at Five

## Sampoong and the Price of Looking Away

Sven Hardy Benson

A department store does not look like a machine for deciding which warnings matter. That is one reason Sampoong remains difficult to understand.

By the morning of June 29, 1995, the danger was no longer hidden inside equations. Floors had deformed. Cracks were widening. People could hear and feel the building behaving differently. Managers summoned technical advice. Parts of the fifth floor were closed. The air-conditioning system was shut down. Yet the store below remained open.

The easy moral is greed. It is not wrong. It is insufficient.

A building like Sampoong contains many different knowledge systems. Designers know load paths. Contractors know what was actually built. maintenance workers know what changed. tenants know which ceilings leak and which doors stick. managers know revenue. inspectors know what paperwork says. shoppers know almost nothing except that the doors are open. Each can possess a true fragment while the institution as a whole remains incapable of saying the one sentence that matters: leave now.

This book follows that fragmentation from design to collapse, then follows the reverse process after collapse, when investigators, rescuers, prosecutors, archivists and families had to reconstruct one event from rubble, records, testimony and names.

The purpose is not to turn Sampoong into a metaphor for everything. It is to understand exactly how warning can become ordinary.
