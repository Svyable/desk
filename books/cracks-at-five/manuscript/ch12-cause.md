# Chapter 12 — Cause

People want one cause because one cause gives the event a shape.

Sampoong resists that comfort.

The forensic record describes design defects, construction problems and management failures acting together. The building's use changed. Loads increased. structural details were inadequate. mechanical equipment contributed additional stress and vibration. Warning signs emerged. The store remained occupied.

These are not competing stories. They are layers of defense that failed in sequence.

A useful causal model separates capacity, demand, damage and exposure. Design and construction determine capacity. additions and equipment change demand. maintenance decisions can generate damage. management determines exposure by deciding whether people remain inside when evidence of failure appears.

Corruption belongs in the model differently. A bribe does not exert a vertical force on a slab. It can disable the public mechanism that is supposed to prevent unsafe changes from reaching occupancy.

This separation prevents two errors. The first is engineering reductionism: treating the collapse as if correct equations alone would have saved everyone. The second is moral reductionism: treating greed as if it explains the specific physics.

Sampoong was deadly because technical and institutional failures were coupled.
