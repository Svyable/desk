# Chapter 16 — Seventeen Days

The long-survival stories changed the meaning of time at Sampoong.

Contemporary Korean rescue chronologies record people recovered after ten and twelve days. Later Korean accounts remember Park Seung-hyun surviving seventeen days before rescue. These cases became national symbols because they violated the ordinary expectation that a collapsed building rapidly stops containing living people.

They also expose a difficult operational question. When does rescue become recovery?

The answer affects whether heavy equipment can be used aggressively, how much danger rescuers accept and how families interpret every change in activity. Extraordinary survival can justify persistence, but it can also distort future expectations if rare cases are treated as typical.

A scientifically responsible chapter must reconstruct each late rescue from contemporaneous records, not legend: location, void geometry, access to water, injuries, environmental conditions and detection method. This first pass does not pretend to have that full record.

What the late survivors establish beyond dispute is that rubble is heterogeneous. A collapsed floor system can create lethal crush zones centimeters from spaces that preserve air and volume.

Search doctrine therefore has to respect uncertainty in the debris long after public intuition has become pessimistic.
