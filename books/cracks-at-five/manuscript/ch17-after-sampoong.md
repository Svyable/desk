# Chapter 17 — After Sampoong

Disasters are frequently credited with laws they did not actually create.

That mistake matters here.

Korean safety commentary often groups Seongsu Bridge and Sampoong together as turning points. But the Special Act on the Safety Control of Facilities was enacted after Seongsu and before Sampoong collapsed. Sampoong therefore cannot honestly be presented as the sole origin of that law.

Its institutional effects were still substantial. The collapse intensified national inspection, public concern about existing buildings, rescue reform and scrutiny of construction and permitting. Korean Academy accounts connect the disaster to broad safety evaluation and expansion of central rescue capability.

The harder question is whether reform changed the underlying decision problem. Inspection regimes can find defects. They do not automatically empower workers to stop operations, protect experts who recommend closure, preserve as-built information, or prevent economic pressure from redefining a warning as a maintenance issue.

A society can become better at checking structures while remaining poor at acting on inconvenient information.

That is why the book ends not with a law but with a threshold: who can say `everyone out`, on incomplete evidence, before the public can see why?
