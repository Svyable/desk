# Chapter 10 — The Void

Rescue begins with a paradox: rubble must move, but rubble is also holding other rubble in place.

Early response at Sampoong brought firefighters, police, military personnel, volunteers, medical teams and heavy equipment into an unstable environment. Contemporary Korean reporting records hundreds of rescuers working dozens of locations while bodies and survivors were still being found.

The operational problem was not simply digging faster. It was preserving voids, locating voices, controlling fire and hazardous conditions, moving massive concrete without triggering secondary collapse, triaging patients and reconciling contradictory missing-person reports.

This is where the building's failure became a data problem again. Search sectors had to be mapped. Finds had to be logged. Names had to be compared against hospital lists, family reports and bodies. A rescue site is an information system assembled under dust.

The Seoul Archives preserves repeated situation reports from the response. Those records deserve far more attention in English-language accounts because they can show how a city learned in real time: what resources were requested, where bottlenecks appeared, how casualty estimates changed, and which procedures were improvised.

The collapse is often remembered through extraordinary survivors. The institutional story is the machinery that kept looking after ordinary expectations had failed.
