# Chapter 13 — Bribes

Corruption is often described as a moral stain added to an engineering disaster. At Sampoong it was closer to a control-system failure.

The criminal proceedings extended beyond the store's leadership to public officials. Contemporary Korean reporting from the first trial describes allegations involving payments connected to use changes, temporary approvals and completion inspection. Twenty-five defendants faced proceedings across negligence and corruption-related allegations.

The precise legal findings must be reconstructed defendant by defendant before every accusation is treated as proven. But the existence of the corruption cases is itself significant.

Building regulation exists because shoppers cannot inspect reinforcement, recalculate punching shear or verify every permit. The public delegates that work. A corrupt approval therefore alters the information available to every person who enters. The open door and official status become false reassurance.

This is why corruption can produce physical risk even when no official touches a column. It changes which warnings are amplified and which are converted into paperwork.

The deepest cost of bribery is not the money exchanged. It is counterfeit trust.
