# The Machine Needs You

In 2023 a group at the University of Texas at Austin published a machine that could do something sufficiently alarming that almost every description of it risked becoming wrong.

A person lay inside an fMRI scanner and listened to stories. The scanner watched blood oxygen change across the brain. A decoder learned the relationship between those measurements and semantic features of language. Later, given new recordings, it generated sequences of words that recovered portions of the meaning of speech the person heard, speech the person imagined, and silent videos the person watched.

You can see why *mind reading* arrived before the methods section had finished loading.

But the experiment contained another result. It is the result I cannot stop looking at.

The machine needed the person.

Jerry Tang and his colleagues reported that successful decoding required subject cooperation both to train the decoder and to use it. When subjects deliberately resisted by doing things such as naming animals or imagining another story, the reconstruction deteriorated. The paper was not a proof that mental privacy is safe. It was something more useful: a measurement of one boundary.

The boundary is not philosophical. It is engineering.

The decoder had no dictionary marked HUMAN THOUGHT. It had a relationship painstakingly estimated between one person's measured brain activity and a representational space. The subject supplied training data. The scanner supplied a slow hemodynamic proxy for neural activity. A computational model supplied candidate language. The experiment supplied constraints. Remove enough of that apparatus and the apparent telepathy becomes a different machine.

This is easy to miss because outputs are psychologically louder than pipelines. If a computer produces a sentence that resembles something you were thinking, the sentence feels like the fact. The hours of calibration disappear. So does the magnet surrounding your head. So does the cooperation. So does the statistical machinery that chose among candidate sequences.

We have made this mistake before. Cold War behavioral-control programs are remembered through the capability they wanted. Remote viewing is remembered through the target description that looked right. UAP cases are remembered through the velocity somebody inferred after range became uncertain. The desired noun colonizes the evidence.

Mind reading has the same problem.

In August 2023, two invasive speech-neuroprosthesis papers made the distinction harder to ignore. One system decoded attempted speech from intracortical microelectrode arrays at rates that began to approach useful conversation. Another used a high-density electrocorticography array to drive text, synthesized speech and an avatar. These were extraordinary clinical achievements. They were also exquisitely situated achievements: electrodes at known cortical sites, participants performing known tasks, supervised training, individualized models, and signals generated by attempted communication.

Three years later the clinical boundary moved again. A 2026 Nature Medicine report described a participant using an intracortical interface at home for more than 3,800 hours across nineteen months. He produced more than 180,000 sentences and used the same system for cursor control. This matters because laboratory tricks die when the graduate student leaves the room. This one did not.

But notice what survived with it: an implanted interface, an enrolled participant, a calibrated decoder, intentional use.

That is not a criticism. It is the capability.

The mistake would be to subtract those conditions in prose while keeping the result.

DARPA has been unusually helpful here because military research programs state their ambitions plainly. Its completed N3 program sought high-performance bidirectional brain-machine interfaces for able-bodied service members without surgery. The stated target was astonishing: read from and write to sixteen independent channels in a small volume of neural tissue, with tight spatial and temporal performance, using modalities that might include optical, acoustic or electromagnetic energy. The national-security use cases included unmanned systems and cyber defense.

There are two honest ways to read that page. One is to say the military seriously wanted non-surgical neural interfaces far beyond consumer EEG. True. The other is to say a program specification is not a fielded capability report. Also true.

The interesting territory sits between them.

Suppose the history of brain-machine interfaces is plotted not as a line from impossible to telepathy but as a series of burdens. First obtain a measurable signal. Then decode a constrained intention. Then generalize across vocabulary. Then maintain performance across days. Then operate outside the laboratory. Then reduce invasiveness. Then reduce calibration. Then generalize across people. Then decode without cooperation. Then decode content the system was never trained to recognize. Each step is a different problem. A result on rung four cannot be spent on rung nine.

This sounds obvious until you read almost anything written about secret neurotechnology.

Claims about remote neural surveillance often begin with a real result and quietly remove its geometry. Claims about thought decoding remove training. Claims about influence remove the difference between stimulating tissue and specifying what a person will believe. Claims about classified capability then use secrecy to fill every missing rung: perhaps the public system needs an implant, but the secret one does not; perhaps the public decoder needs calibration, but the secret one reads strangers; perhaps the published experiment requires cooperation, but the classified version defeated that years ago.

Maybe. Classification can hide engineering. It cannot make engineering burdens cease to exist.

This book is going to treat those burdens as evidence.

The strongest extraordinary model is not stupid. Governments have funded neural-interface research for decades. Classified programs can precede public disclosure. Sensor fusion, better models, individualized priors and new transduction methods can move thresholds. A state with access to a target's medical data, devices, behavior, voice, images and long-term physiological telemetry would not be solving the same problem as Tang's decoder from scratch. Intelligence work loves side channels.

So I do not intend to prove that covert neural inference is impossible by pointing at a 2023 scanner.

I intend to ask what a claimed capability would have to overcome.

There is a useful asymmetry here. Reading gets easier when the subject deliberately generates a decodable signal. Writing gets easier when the desired output is crude: evoke a phosphene, move a muscle, alter arousal, interrupt a circuit. The science-fiction version asks for the reverse combination: read rich arbitrary content from an uncooperative unknown person and write rich operator-chosen content back into that person's cognition. That is not one extrapolation. It is a stack of extrapolations, each with its own calibration, bandwidth, targeting and semantic problem.

The semantic problem is the one I suspect will survive longest.

A neuron does not contain the word *submarine*. A voxel does not contain your mother's face. Meaning is distributed through a biological history the decoder did not live. Even highly successful speech interfaces exploit structured motor intentions and enormous statistical regularities in language. The language model is not an innocent stenographer. It is part of the reconstruction.

That does not make the output fake. It changes what the machine has done.

And then there is resistance.

Tang's participants could degrade the non-invasive semantic decoder by choosing to think differently. This should not be romanticized into a permanent defense. Better systems may become more robust. Coercion can manufacture cooperation. Passive data can train models without a person understanding the eventual use. Medical interfaces create their own privacy problems precisely because they are good.

Still, resistance reveals something deep: the observed brain is not a radio station transmitting a fixed plaintext message. Measurement participates in a task. The subject can change the task.

The machine needs you because, so far, you are not merely the thing being measured. You are part of the calibration apparatus.

If that stops being true, I want to know exactly when.
