# Mind Reading Needs Permission

*What Brain Decoders Can Read, What They Cannot, and Why Cooperation Is the Missing Variable*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a phrase that should make us suspicious before it makes us afraid: *mind reading*.

A voltmeter reads voltage. A decoder does not read a mind in that sense. It measures a signal, under a task, from a particular person, through an instrument, then uses a model trained on examples whose labels somebody already knows. Every noun in that sentence matters.

This book is about the distance between the demonstration and the mythology.

It uses four evidence classes throughout. **Documented** means a primary record, official program description, or peer-reviewed experiment supports the stated fact. **Credible but incomplete** means the source is serious but the relevant denominator, protocol, replication, or operational detail remains missing. **Disputed** means an allegation lacks independent confirmation or conflicts with stronger evidence. **Speculative** means I am making the synthesis and saying so.

A patent is evidence that somebody filed a patent, not that the device works. A military research goal is evidence of a goal, not a capability. A neural decoder that works after hours of individualized training inside a scanner is not evidence that a stranger can read arbitrary private thought from across a room. A device designed to write signals into neural tissue is not evidence that it can install beliefs.

The most interesting boundary may be cooperation. If the subject has to spend hours teaching the machine what their neural activity means, and can defeat the decoder by deliberately changing what they attend to, then consent is not merely an ethical wrapper around the technology. It is part of the engineering stack.

That is a much stranger fact than the headline.
