# Document and Experiment Appendix

## 1996 — Bianchi et al., PNAS

**Question:** Can fetal cells from prior pregnancies remain in maternal blood long enough to confound cell-based prenatal diagnosis?

**Design:** Flow-sorted maternal blood cell populations; Y-chromosome PCR; pregnant women plus nonpregnant women with prior male births.

**Established:** Male fetal progenitor-cell signal persisted in some women years after pregnancy, including one 27 years after a male birth.

**Did not establish:** universal persistence, tissue function, disease effect, or persistence of cell-free fetal DNA.

## 1999 — Maloney et al., JCI

**Question:** Do maternal cells persist in immunocompetent offspring?

**Design:** HLA-specific PCR for nonshared maternal alleles; supporting sex-chromosome imaging in selected samples.

**Established:** maternal-origin microchimerism can persist into adult life despite HLA disparity.

**Did not establish:** a universal prevalence or specific physiological function.

## 2003 — postpartum plasma DNA study

**Question:** Does fetal DNA remain in maternal plasma long after pregnancy?

**Established:** no evidence for long-term persistence in plasma in the studied cohort; early postpartum signal cleared quickly.

**Importance:** separates persistent cellular microchimerism from cell-free fetal DNA.

## 2004 — female bone marrow and bone

**Question:** Can fetal-origin cells persist in long-lived maternal niches?

**Design:** XY FISH in marrow-derived mesenchymal cells and rib sections.

**Established:** male-origin cells in marrow/bone of women with previous male pregnancies in the reported cohort.

**Boundary:** small sample; male origin used as fetal proxy; does not prove every persistent lineage is mesenchymal stem cell.

## 2012 — human female brain

**Question:** Is male-origin microchimerism detectable in the human female brain?

**Design:** qPCR targeting DYS14 in autopsy brain from 59 women, with and without Alzheimer's disease.

**Established:** male-origin microchimerism in 63% of tested women and multiple brain regions.

**Boundary:** source pregnancy incomplete; no demonstration of neural function, memory transfer or causal protection from Alzheimer's disease.

## 2012 — injured maternal myocardium

**Question:** Do fetal-origin cells home to damaged maternal heart and express cardiac/vascular phenotypes?

**Design:** GFP-labeled fetal lineages in mice; maternal cardiac injury; tissue localization and phenotype.

**Established:** injury recruitment and reported contribution to several cardiac-associated lineages in the model.

**Boundary:** presence/differentiation evidence did not by itself prove improved cardiac function.

## 2017 — CCR2/CCL2 wound healing

**Question:** Is recruitment of a functionally important fetal-origin population mechanistically controllable?

**Design:** mouse wound model; phenotype of fetal-origin cells; chemokine-axis manipulation.

**Established:** a CCR2-associated fetal microchimeric population was recruited to wounds; CCL2 manipulation improved delayed healing in pregnancy/postpartum contexts in the reported model.

**Boundary:** host chemokine effects and translation to human chronic wounds require independent testing; positive skin-wound results do not establish positive effects in other organs.

## 2023 — preeclampsia immune subsets

**Question:** Does preeclampsia alter the composition of fetal microchimerism in maternal blood?

**Design:** matched cases/controls; flow sorting of immune and stem-cell subsets; fetus-specific nonshared polymorphism qPCR.

**Established:** higher concentrations in particular immune subsets, especially B and NK populations, in the small study.

**Boundary:** does not establish whether the altered population causes, mediates or follows placental disease.

## 2024 — selective cardiac depletion

**Question:** Do fetal-origin microchimeric cells materially change maternal cardiac outcome after myocardial infarction?

**Design:** wild-type females crossed with males carrying a diphtheria-toxin receptor; postpartum toxin treatment to reduce fetal-origin microchimerism; permanent left-descending coronary ligation; echocardiography before infarction and 28 days later.

**Established:** according to the paper's abstract, reducing fetal microchimeric cells **improved cardiac contraction** in the postpartum post-infarction model. The authors interpreted the microchimeric population as detrimental in this setting.

**Importance:** this is the manuscript's strongest warning against inferring benefit from injury homing or differentiation markers. The direction of a microchimeric effect has to be measured tissue by tissue.

**Boundary:** modest animal numbers, broad depletion of a heterogeneous population, acute postpartum timing and a mouse infarction model limit generalization to human or decades-old microchimerism.

## 2025 — cancer meta-analysis

**Question:** Across published studies, is detectable male-origin microchimerism associated with cancer risk?

**Design:** random-effects meta-analysis of 12 studies, 3,078 participants, multiple cancer types.

**Reported result:** pooled relative risk about 0.51 for cancer among microchimerism-positive women.

**Boundary:** observational heterogeneity, male-origin source ambiguity, reverse causation and publication bias prevent causal inference.

## Minimal evidence ladder used in the manuscript

1. Genetic signal.
2. Intact foreign-origin cell.
3. Anatomical localization.
4. Cell phenotype.
5. Prospective recruitment or temporal change.
6. Lineage-specific perturbation.
7. Functional outcome and its direction.
8. Independent replication.
9. Human prospective validation.

Claims are not promoted up this ladder by repetition in reviews or media.
