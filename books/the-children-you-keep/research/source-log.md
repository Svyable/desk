## Multigenerational and transmaternal transfer

- Karlmark KR, et al. “Grandmaternal cells in cord blood.” *eBioMedicine* 74 (2021), 103721. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ebiom.2021.103721 ; PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720789/ — Direct three-generation evidence. Healthy primigravid women were genotyped with maternal grandmothers and offspring; 28 cord-blood samples were informative for an unequivocal noninherited/unshared HLA grandmaternal marker. Grandmaternal microchimerism was detected in 5/28 (18%), while maternal microchimerism in those same 28 was 57%. Where quantities could be compared in whole blood/PBMC, grandmaternal levels were roughly two orders of magnitude below maternal levels. This establishes that a grandmother-origin cell lineage can cross through a pregnant daughter into cord blood in some pregnancies. It does **not** establish population prevalence beyond the selected/informative cohort, persistence after birth, tissue colonization, or functional effect. Associations with invasive prenatal procedures / aneuploidy-risk-related procedures and HLA compatibility are hypothesis-generating because only five grandmaternal-positive cords were observed.
- Gammill HS, et al. “Pregnancy, Microchimerism, and the Maternal Grandmother.” *PLoS ONE* 6(8), 2011, e24101. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0024101 — Tracks a pregnant woman's own maternal-origin microchimerism and reports gestational dynamics, including higher detectability later in pregnancy. This establishes persistence and pregnancy-associated variation of grandmother-origin cells **in the mother**; it is not direct evidence that those cells entered the fetus. Use it as the precursor observation that the 2021 cord-blood study later extends across the next placental boundary.
- Dierselhuis MP, et al. “Transmaternal cell flow leads to antigen-experienced cord blood.” 2012. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22627770/ — Reports HY-specific antigen-experienced responses in female cord blood and male microchimerism in a subset, supporting transmaternal influence from prior male pregnancies / older brothers. This is a sibling-associated route and immune-exposure result, not the same as directly genotyped grandmaternal transfer. Male-origin detection alone should not be assigned to a specific brother without source-resolving markers.

Claim boundary: three related observations must remain separate: (1) a pregnant woman can carry cells from her own mother; (2) grandmother-origin cells can be detected in a grandchild's cord blood in some directly genotyped families; and (3) prior pregnancies can create transmaternal sibling-associated cellular/antigen exposure. None by itself establishes a universal multigenerational traffic rate or long-term functional consequence.

## Heart and injury

- Kara RJ, Bolli P, Karakikes I, et al. “Fetal cells traffic to injured maternal myocardium and undergo cardiac differentiation.” *Circulation Research* 110, 2012. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22082491/
- Llorente V, López-Olañeta M, Blázquez-López E, et al. “Presence of fetal microchimerisms in the heart and effect on cardiac repair.” *Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology* 12, 2024, 1390533. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2024.1390533 ; PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39206089/

Claim boundary: the 2012 work established injury homing and reported cardiac-associated differentiation phenotypes in mice. The 2024 study then used a diphtheria-toxin-receptor strategy before myocardial infarction and reported improved cardiac contraction after treatment. Important design detail: the intended homozygous DTR-male breeding was unsuccessful, so heterozygous males were used; only the DTR-bearing fraction of fetal microchimeric cells was susceptible to toxin. The experiment therefore demonstrates a functional effect of **partial/selective depletion**, not complete removal of fetal microchimerism. In DTR-cross mothers, 28-day post-MI LVEF was reported as 18.89 ± 3.99% in controls versus 29.86 ± 3.85% after DT; stroke volume was 17.20 ± 2.41 versus 24.85 ± 2.15 µL. WT-cross mothers (n=4 per condition) did not show the same LVEF improvement with DT, arguing against a simple toxin-only explanation. Remaining limitations include incomplete depletion, uncharacterized responsible cell lineage, myocardial flow-cytometry samples from only two non-perfused hearts, and the possibility—raised by the authors—that killing DTR-positive cells altered the behavior of surviving DTR-negative microchimeric cells. This paper should not be cited as evidence that all fetal microchimeric cells are harmful, nor generalized from an acute postpartum mouse infarction to decades-later human persistence.

## Skin and wound repair

- “Fetal microchimerism in skin wound healing.” PubMed record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22627845/
- Castela M, Nassar D, Sbeih M, et al. “Ccl2/Ccr2 signalling recruits a distinct fetal microchimeric population that rescues delayed maternal wound healing.” 2017. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28516946/
- “Contribution of fetal microchimeric cells to maternal wound healing in sickle cell ulcers.” 2022. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36373248/

Claim boundary: the strongest positive repair evidence is experimental and largely murine. Human parity associations in sickle-cell ulcer burden are retrospective and confounded. These results should not be generalized to heart or other tissues; the 2024 cardiac depletion study points in the opposite functional direction.

## Sources not treated as proof

- Popular claims that mothers permanently retain cells from every conception in every organ.
- Claims that fetal-origin cells contain a child's memories, emotions or personality.
- Claims that microchimerism explains female autoimmunity in general.
- Patents or commercial assays as evidence of clinical utility.
- Evolutionary narratives that lack prospective predictions.
- Y-chromosome positivity assigned to a specific son without reproductive and medical source control.
