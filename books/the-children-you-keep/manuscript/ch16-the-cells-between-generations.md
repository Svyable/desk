# The Cells Between Generations

For years, multigenerational microchimerism lived in the most seductive part of biology: the part that was easy to imagine and hard to prove.

A woman can carry cells from her mother. She can later become pregnant. Cells cross the placenta in both directions. So could cells that originally came from the grandmother pass through the mother and appear in the next generation?

The route made sense.

That was not the same as showing it happened.

The technical problem is vicious. A maternal-origin cell in a pregnant woman is genetically very similar to the woman herself. If investigators then find a rare maternal-family sequence in the fetus or cord blood, ordinary inheritance is the first explanation. To prove a true grandmother-to-grandchild microchimeric lineage, the marker must identify something present in the grandmother, absent from the mother’s own inherited genotype, and absent from the child’s inherited genotype. Pregnancy history, transfusion history, vanished twins and other possible sources matter. The target is rare enough that contamination can imitate the entire story.

That is why the 2021 paper **“Grandmaternal cells in cord blood”** matters more than the popular version of the idea.

The investigators did not begin with women who had several previous pregnancies and then infer ancestry from a Y chromosome. They recruited healthy **primigravid** women—women in a first pregnancy—from three maternity centers in Marseille between 2014 and 2017. They excluded women with prior transfusion, autoimmune disease or cancer and built three-generation HLA pedigrees involving maternal grandmother, pregnant woman and child.

The design was aimed directly at the source problem.

Researchers identified HLA variants carried by the grandmother that were **not inherited and not shared** in a way that could be confused with the mother’s or child’s ordinary genotype. HLA-specific quantitative PCR was then used on cord blood. Twenty-eight families were informative enough for an unequivocal grandmaternal search.

Five were positive.

Five of twenty-eight.

Eighteen percent.

That is the first number this chapter should carry.

The paper reported maternal microchimerism in 57 percent of those same twenty-eight cord-blood samples. Grandmaternal microchimerism was therefore less frequent and, where quantified in whole blood and peripheral blood mononuclear cells, roughly **one hundred times lower** than maternal microchimerism.

The signal was small.

That makes it more interesting, not less.

The study also searched separated cell populations, including peripheral blood mononuclear cells, T cells, B cells, granulocytes and hematopoietic stem/progenitor-cell fractions. Its central claim is therefore not simply that a grandmaternal DNA fragment floated through plasma. The authors were looking for lineage markers inside cellular compartments.

Still, the result needs to remain exactly as large as it is.

It does **not** show that every child carries cells from a grandmother.

It does **not** show that the detected grandmaternal lineages persist into childhood or adulthood.

It does **not** show that they colonize organs.

It does **not** show that they alter immunity.

And it certainly does not establish a universal biological law in which grandmothers routinely populate grandchildren with a durable cellular inheritance.

What it does is retire one older sentence.

The route is no longer merely hypothetical.

Direct transgenerational transfer has been demonstrated in a small, carefully selected human cohort.

That distinction matters because scientific language tends to lag behind the evidence in both directions. Before 2021, some popular accounts spoke as though grandmother-to-grandchild transfer were established when it was still an elegant inference. After 2021, cautious summaries can make the opposite mistake and repeat that direct evidence is absent.

The right sentence is narrower and stranger:

**Grandmaternal microchimerism has been directly detected in cord blood using three-generation HLA markers, but its prevalence, persistence, tissue distribution and functional consequence remain poorly defined.**

That is enough to change how the family tree should be drawn.

Not as arrows replacing heredity.

As rare traffic crossing it.

The experiment also demonstrates why source attribution must be more demanding than “male cells found in a woman.”

Male microchimerism has several possible routes. A male fetus can contribute cells to a mother. A vanished male twin can be a source. Transfusion can matter. Cells originating from an older brother may, in principle and perhaps in some cases in practice, pass through the mother to a younger female sibling. A male cell in a woman is therefore evidence of male origin, not a paternity test for the microchimeric lineage.

This sibling route has its own evidence.

A 2012 cord-blood study reported male microchimerism in female newborns with older brothers and detected HY-specific T-cell responses, supporting the idea of **transmaternal cell flow**: cells or antigens associated with an earlier male pregnancy can shape a later pregnancy through the mother. That result is not identical to the 2021 grandmaternal experiment. It involves sibling-associated male microchimerism and immune experience rather than a directly genotyped grandmother-to-grandchild lineage.

The distinction is useful.

There may be more than one way for pregnancy history to become cellular history.

A mother can carry cells from her own mother.

She can carry cells from an earlier fetus.

She can carry maternal microchimeric cells acquired before she was born.

A later fetus develops inside that already mixed cellular environment.

The body entering pregnancy is not blank.

This does not mean every resident lineage crosses every later placenta. It means that the maternal bloodstream can contain genetically distinct populations whose ancestry predates the current pregnancy, and at least some of those populations can cross generations.

That result sharpens an older observation from 2011. Researchers studying microchimerism from a pregnant woman’s own mother found that maternal-grandmother-origin microchimerism in the pregnant woman’s blood increased in detectability as gestation advanced and peaked in the third trimester. That study asked whether pregnancy changes the preexisting maternal microchimerism already carried by the woman. It did not directly show those cells entering the fetus.

Ten years later, the cord-blood study closed that specific gap for at least some pregnancies.

The two experiments fit together without being the same experiment.

One says the grandmother’s lineage can still be present and dynamically detectable in the pregnant daughter.

The other says grandmaternal lineage markers can appear in the grandchild’s cord blood in a subset of informative families.

Now the interesting questions begin.

How long do those cells remain?

Do they disappear after birth?

Do particular cell types persist better than others?

Do they occupy marrow or tissue niches?

Are they immunologically silent passengers?

Can they present antigen?

Do they ever become clinically relevant?

The 2021 study itself provides one reason not to race past those questions. Grandmaternal microchimerism was associated in that small cohort with invasive prenatal procedures and/or aneuploidy-risk-related procedures, and the positive families showed different HLA-compatibility patterns. Those associations are intriguing and fragile. With only five positive cord-blood samples, they are not a stable epidemiology.

The sample size should discipline the imagination.

Five positive families can establish possibility.

They cannot map a population.

This is where the word *microchimerism* can mislead. It sounds like one phenomenon. In practice it is a family of lineage states with different sources, cell types, ages and tissue contexts.

A grandmaternal T cell is not functionally equivalent to a grandmaternal stem/progenitor cell.

A lineage detected at birth is not the same claim as a lineage persisting at age fifty.

A PCR-positive specimen is not automatically evidence that the detected cells are metabolically active, proliferating or immunologically consequential.

Presence comes first.

Phenotype comes second.

Function comes third.

Causality comes last.

The multigenerational story also changes how this book should discuss immune memory.

A transferred cell can persist and divide. If it survives, it carries a genome representing a specific family relationship. That is a kind of material continuity across pregnancies that antibodies and transient hormones do not provide.

But “material memory” should remain a biological metaphor.

The cell does not remember a grandmother.

It preserves ancestry.

Whether that ancestry changes the host depends on what the cell is, where it goes, whether it persists, what antigens it expresses and what surrounding immune cells do with it.

This matters in transplantation, where pregnancy history already has clear relevance through HLA sensitization and antibodies. Microchimerism is a separate layer. A recipient may carry rare maternal or pregnancy-derived cells not represented by the recipient’s ordinary genotype. That does not mean transplant programs should suddenly genotype every ancestral lineage. It means that immunological exposure and genetic identity are not perfectly synonymous.

The same caution applies to epidemiology.

Parity changes anatomy, vasculature, hormones, metabolism, immunity and behavior. An older brother changes family environment as well as potential cellular exposure. A correlation between reproductive history and later disease cannot be assigned to microchimerism merely because a plausible cellular route exists.

The 2021 grandmaternal result actually raises the evidentiary standard for those claims.

Now that direct transfer can be measured, researchers no longer need to use family structure as a proxy forever.

If older-brother exposure is supposed to matter through transmaternal cells, identify those cells genetically.

If grandmaternal microchimerism is proposed to influence a pregnancy outcome, measure grandmaternal lineage directly.

If immune effects are claimed, characterize the cell population and assay the response.

The next study should therefore be larger than the one this chapter originally proposed.

Enroll three-generation families prospectively, but collect enough family genotyping to identify **multiple private lineage markers**, not one convenient locus. Use whole-genome or dense HLA-informed data to define grandmother-, mother-, father- and child-specific targets. Pair ultrasensitive targeted assays with cell sorting and single-cell sequencing so ancestry and phenotype are measured in the same rare cell.

Then follow the child.

Cord blood answers whether transfer occurred by birth.

It does not answer persistence.

Repeat blood sampling at defined intervals. When clinically indicated tissue becomes available for unrelated reasons, preserve material for lineage-aware analysis under appropriate consent. Record later pregnancies, transfusions, twins and transplant exposures that can create new sources.

The output should be a traffic map with decay curves.

At birth: which ancestral lineages are present?

At one year: which remain?

At adolescence: which remain?

In adulthood: which remain?

Which cell types carry them?

Do their frequencies rise under injury, infection, pregnancy or immune activation?

This is the difference between an arresting fact and a biology.

The arresting fact is already here.

Some newborns carry detectable cellular material from a grandmother that they did not inherit as part of their own genome.

The biology of what those cells do next is still mostly unwritten.

That is a better mystery than the old one.

The question is no longer whether a cell can cross two generations.

It is what happens after it does.
