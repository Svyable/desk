# Back Matter

## Evidence note

Microchimerism is unusually sensitive to source attribution, detection threshold and causal direction. Throughout this manuscript, “fetal-origin” is used only where the study design assigns fetal origin with reasonable confidence; “male-origin” is retained when Y-chromosome detection establishes sex but not a specific pregnancy. Presence in diseased tissue is never treated as proof of causation.

The manuscript uses four working evidence classes:

1. **Verified / documented:** bidirectional maternal-fetal cell traffic; persistence of fetal-origin and maternal-origin microchimerism for years to decades; detection in multiple tissues; rapid postpartum clearance of cell-free fetal DNA as distinct from cellular persistence; specific animal lineage-tracing and ablation results.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** injury recruitment, defined repair functions in particular animal models, altered microchimeric populations in preeclampsia, some prospective or case-control disease associations.
3. **Disputed / unconfirmed:** broad causal roles in systemic sclerosis, thyroid autoimmunity, cancer protection, neurodegeneration or other human diseases; universal multigenerational transfer; simple HLA rules governing persistence.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** an adaptive “microchiome,” systematic fetal manipulation of maternal physiology, clinically useful microchimeric-cell therapy, or a general theory in which persistent cells explain long-term maternal disease.

## A note on language

“Child's cells” is sometimes useful shorthand and often scientifically too strong. Fetal-origin cells can derive from pregnancies that did not result in live birth. Male microchimerism can have sources other than a known son. A persistent cell decades later may be the descendant of a progenitor that crossed during pregnancy, not the original transferred cell. None of these cells contains the memories or identity of the person whose lineage it carries.

## What would change the argument

The book's functional thesis should weaken if large, family-genotyped prospective studies fail to reproduce long-term cellular persistence, if apparent long-term signals prove mostly noncellular or contamination, if injury-associated enrichment is not reproducible, or if selective depletion repeatedly fails to change outcome in well-controlled models.

It should strengthen if pregnancy-specific clones can be tracked prospectively for decades, shown to enter defined tissue compartments after injury, assigned reproducible cell states, and manipulated in lineage-specific experiments that change physiology.
