# Training on the Echo

Every model of the world is smaller than the world.

That is what makes it useful.

A map leaves out most of the landscape. A financial statement leaves out most of a company. A medical chart leaves out most of a patient. A photograph leaves out everything outside the frame. A language model compresses patterns from an enormous training distribution into parameters that can generate plausible continuations.

Compression is not a defect.

It is the capability.

The problem begins when the compressed representation becomes the material from which the next representation is learned.

A map can be copied from another map.

The second map may be excellent.

But if nobody returns to the territory, small errors become inherited facts.

Roads that were omitted remain omitted. A mislabeled stream gains legitimacy through repetition. Features that seemed unimportant to the first cartographer never get a second chance to matter.

The system learns the map's choices as though they were the landscape's choices.

This is training on the echo.

In July 2024, researchers led by Ilia Shumailov and Zakhar Shumaylov published a paper in *Nature* examining what happens when learned generative models are trained recursively on data generated by previous models. They called the resulting degeneration model collapse.

The finding is often summarized dramatically: AI trained on AI gets worse.

That summary is too crude.

The conditions matter.

The paper studies generational learning in which model-generated data progressively contaminate or replace access to the original distribution. It does not show that every use of synthetic data is harmful. Synthetic data can be useful, especially when curated, combined with real data, generated under controlled procedures, or used to target specific gaps.

The stronger and more interesting result is about indiscriminate recursion.

If models repeatedly learn from samples produced by prior models without preserving enough access to the original distribution, errors compound and the learned distribution drifts.

The researchers observed something particularly important for this book.

The tails disappear early.

That phrase sounds statistical.

It is also philosophical.

A distribution has common events near its center and less common events in its tails. A model trained on finite data does not reproduce the original distribution perfectly. Its samples reflect an approximation. When another model trains on those samples, lower-probability parts of the original distribution are easier to miss. Repeat the process and the omissions compound.

What is common survives.

What is rare becomes fragile.

This is not because the model hates rarity.

It is sampling.

A rare pattern has fewer chances to appear. If it is absent from one generation's synthetic sample, the next generation cannot infer that it was supposed to be there unless some original data remains.

The echo preserves the loudest frequencies.

Quiet details fade.

This is the most literal version of the argument in *Gradient Ascent*.

Signal can be hidden without anyone hiding it.

Noise can be generated without anyone intending deception.

A system can become less representative simply by learning too much from its own projections.

Consider language.

Human writing is not one distribution in any simple sense. It contains technical manuals, love letters, legal opinions, dialect, jokes, arguments, family histories, fan fiction, scientific notation, obscure hobbies, bad poetry, brilliant poetry, minority languages, local slang, old archives, new memes, private correspondence, bureaucratic forms, sermons, menus, recipes, transcripts, marginalia, and things nobody has named yet.

A model sees only a sample.

It learns patterns weighted by what appears in that sample and by the training process.

When the model generates text, it does not reproduce the sample exactly. It produces what its learned representation makes probable under the prompt and sampling procedure.

That output can be very good.

It can also be smoother than the source distribution.

More standard.

More legible.

More centered on familiar patterns.

If future systems learn heavily from that output, the synthetic corpus can gradually overrepresent what earlier models found easy to generate.

The center becomes louder.

This is where the phrase artificial noise needs care.

Generated text can be high quality and still contribute to a feedback problem.

Imagine a million accurate summaries of scientific papers.

Each summary may be useful.

But if those summaries begin to substitute for the papers in the available corpus, future systems see less method, less uncertainty, fewer failed hypotheses, fewer tables, fewer edge cases, and fewer awkward details that were compressed away.

The summaries are not false.

They are lossy.

Scale makes the loss structural.

Compression followed by compression is different from compression with repeated access to the source.

This is why archives matter.

Original observations matter.

Raw data matters.

Not because raw data is pure. Raw data is produced by instruments, choices, schemas, sampling procedures, and institutions. There is no view from nowhere.

But original records preserve information that later transformations may decide not to carry forward.

A photograph may preserve a background detail nobody noticed at the time.

A transcript may preserve a hesitation omitted from the summary.

A dataset may preserve an outlier that looks like noise until a later theory explains it.

An old web page may preserve a usage pattern that falls out of fashion.

A failed experiment may preserve a boundary condition.

A customer's unstructured complaint may preserve a failure mode that a satisfaction score averages away.

The value of original data often appears later.

That is why recursive synthetic environments can be dangerous even when current performance looks fine.

The system does not know which discarded detail will become important.

This is a problem of option value.

Keeping access to the original distribution preserves future options to ask different questions.

Replacing the original distribution with a compressed synthetic projection spends those options.

The model-collapse paper makes this concrete. In the authors' experiments, preserving some original data improved the situation relative to training only on generated data. The paper's broader conclusion emphasizes the growing value of access to genuine human-generated data and the need to distinguish generated material in future training corpora.

That conclusion should not be romanticized.

Human data includes spam, prejudice, error, repetition, propaganda, boilerplate, and nonsense.

“Human-generated” is not a quality certificate.

The value is not moral purity.

It is access to an external distribution the model did not create.

Externality is the scarce thing.

A model needs something that can surprise it.

This principle applies outside model training.

Organizations train on echoes too.

A company writes a strategy document.

The document shapes planning.

Planning generates project updates.

The updates feed dashboards.

The dashboards inform the next strategy document.

After several cycles, the company may be learning primarily from its own categories.

Market information gets translated into internal language, then future market information is interpreted through the same language.

The company becomes coherent.

It also becomes vulnerable to facts that do not fit the ontology.

Scientific fields can train on echoes.

A theory organizes observations.

The theory suggests experiments.

Experiments produce publishable results more often when they fit the field's available methods and vocabulary.

Those results train students.

Students choose new problems using the inherited vocabulary.

The theory gains empirical density partly because the field became good at seeing what the theory taught it to see.

Again, this is not evidence that the theory is wrong.

It is evidence that self-reference changes the evidence environment.

Markets train on echoes.

Investors build models from prices.

Trading based on those models changes prices.

The changed prices become data for future models.

Culture trains on echoes.

Popular formats receive attention.

Creators imitate them.

The imitations become the next generation's cultural environment.

Recommendation systems amplify what survived the previous round.

Generative systems make imitation instantaneous.

The general structure is recursive estimation.

We infer the world.

We act on the inference.

Our action changes the world.

We observe the changed world.

We treat the observation as fresh evidence without remembering how much of it we caused.

At each stage, the loop can forget its own authorship.

This is why provenance matters for data, not merely media.

When a training corpus contains a document, the important questions include more than who signed it.

Was it directly observed?

Was it generated from another document?

Was it a summary?

Was it synthetic augmentation?

Was it produced by a previous version of the same system?

Was it selected because an earlier ranking system made it visible?

Was it corrected by humans?

Was it copied from a copied source?

These relationships affect how much independent information the document contributes.

A billion rows can contain less information than they appear to contain if they share ancestry.

This is synthetic consensus in training-data form.

The quantity is large.

The number of independent roots is small.

Machine learning already has concepts for parts of this problem: data leakage, contamination, distribution shift, duplicate detection, provenance, weighting, curriculum design, active learning, and careful evaluation sets.

The synthetic era makes them more central.

A future dataset may need something like a genealogy.

Not just what is in it, but where the material came from and which transformations produced it.

That sounds expensive.

It is.

Verification is expensive because independence is expensive.

The cheap path is to scrape whatever is available and assume volume averages out error.

Recursive generation weakens that assumption.

Volume can contain correlation.

A million echoes do not recreate the original voice.

There is another reason this matters.

Generative systems do not merely reproduce central tendencies. They can also introduce new errors.

The model-collapse paper distinguishes losses in the original distribution from erroneous material that accumulates across generations. A model can simultaneously become narrower around common patterns and drift in strange directions elsewhere.

That combination is dangerous because it defeats a simple mental model of collapse as blandness.

The future corpus is not necessarily just more average.

It can become more average in some dimensions and more distorted in others.

The common survives because it is common.

Some errors survive because they are repeated.

Rare truths disappear because they lack reinforcement.

This is how artificial noise can become structurally stable.

Repetition gives an error apparent support.

Absence removes a correction.

The next model sees both as properties of the world.

The system becomes confident in a distribution partly authored by its ancestors.

This should change how we value old data.

Before mass generation, a large historical corpus looked obsolete compared with fresh data.

Freshness is often valuable.

But pre-synthetic corpora have another property: they were produced before current generators could influence them at scale.

That does not make them better for every purpose. Language changes. Facts change. Social attitudes change. Software changes. Scientific knowledge advances.

It makes them independent in a particular way.

They are a record from before the feedback loop intensified.

That can be useful as a baseline.

The same is true of physical measurement.

A sensor measuring temperature does not care what a language model predicted the temperature would be.

A telescope does not revise the photon because a prior model expected a different galaxy.

A laboratory assay does not become true through repetition in text.

Reality channels break recursion.

They reconnect the information system to something outside its own generated corpus.

This will make high-quality observation more valuable, not less.

As generation gets cheaper, measurement can become the premium input.

Experiments.

Surveys with defensible sampling.

Verified transactions.

Instrument readings.

Field work.

Audits.

Interviews.

Original reporting.

Longitudinal records.

Well-documented human interactions.

These are expensive because the world has to participate.

You cannot hallucinate a new temperature reading into existence and call it measurement.

You can generate a plausible temperature.

That is precisely the difference.

Training on the echo teaches a system what its ancestors believed was likely.

Training on reality gives the system a chance to be wrong.

That chance is precious.

A system that can no longer be surprised cannot correct itself.

This is why the first thing lost in recursive learning matters so much.

It is not always the center.

The center has redundancy.

The common case appears everywhere.

The fragile information sits at the edges.

Rare events.

Minority patterns.

Unusual combinations.

Low-frequency language.

Exceptions.

The cases that look statistically expendable until the day they are the case that matters.

The echo does not erase everything at once.

It fades from the outside inward.

That is collapse at the edges.
