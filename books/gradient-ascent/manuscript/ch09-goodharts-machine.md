# Goodhart's Machine

The most famous sentence about metrics is usually quoted in a form its namesake did not write.

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

The compact version is useful because it fits on a slide.

The history is messier, which makes it more valuable.

Charles Goodhart was writing about monetary policy in the 1970s, not artificial intelligence, social media, schools, hospitals, or software benchmarks. The Reserve Bank of Australia later identified his 1975 paper *Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience* as containing the first reference to what became known as Goodhart's Law. The underlying observation was about relationships that looked stable until policymakers tried to use them for control.

That is the important part.

The measure did not become corrupt because someone announced a target.

The world changed because the target changed behavior.

A statistical regularity that had been useful for observation became less reliable after institutions organized themselves around it.

The map changed the territory.

This is stronger than the ordinary warning that people cheat.

Cheating is one mechanism.

Adaptation is the larger one.

If a central bank targets a monetary aggregate, financial institutions adapt. If a school targets test scores, teachers adapt. If a company targets quarterly conversion, sales teams adapt. If a platform targets engagement, creators adapt. If a model developer targets a benchmark, researchers adapt.

The system does not need to be dishonest.

It only needs to be responsive.

That is why Goodhart's Law becomes more important as optimization gets cheaper.

Artificial intelligence is a general-purpose adaptation machine.

Give it a rubric and it can draft toward the rubric.

Give it a ranking objective and it can search variants for better rank.

Give it a sales script and it can personalize the script.

Give it a benchmark and it can help discover strategies that score better.

Give it a performance review framework and it can reshape evidence to fit the framework.

Give it a grant call and it can restate a project in the language of the call.

Give it a procurement matrix and it can organize the proposal around the matrix.

Many of these uses are beneficial.

A clear rubric should help people produce clearer work.

A safety standard should change behavior toward safety.

A procurement requirement should cause vendors to address the buyer's needs.

A benchmark should focus engineering effort on a capability we care about.

Goodhart's Law is not an argument against targets.

It is an argument against treating targeted measures as passive instruments.

Once the measure enters the incentive system, it becomes part of the causal system.

That sounds abstract until you watch what happens to a simple score.

Imagine a support organization that wants customers to get fast help.

It starts tracking average response time.

At first the metric is informative. Slow teams respond slowly. Fast teams respond quickly. Management sees a real operational difference.

Then response time becomes a target.

Teams reorganize around it.

They answer quickly with acknowledgments.

They split complicated issues into multiple tickets.

They close tickets and invite customers to reopen them.

They route hard questions elsewhere.

Some of these changes are genuine process improvements.

Some change the relationship between the number and the experience the number was meant to represent.

The measured response gets faster.

The useful response may not.

The metric is not broken in the sense that the database contains incorrect timestamps.

The metric is broken as a proxy.

This is the distinction the synthetic era forces us to learn.

A metric can be perfectly measured and increasingly misleading.

Artificial intelligence compounds the effect because it makes compliance surfaces cheap.

Suppose a regulator asks institutions to document a set of risk controls.

Before generative systems, producing the documentation required a meaningful amount of expert time. The document was never proof that the controls worked, but writing it often forced somebody to confront the process.

Now documentation can be produced quickly from templates, policies, prior reports, and a few prompts.

That is not inherently bad. Administrative burden can be wasteful. A system that turns genuine controls into clear documentation is useful.

But the old evidentiary relationship weakens.

A complete-looking document no longer implies the same quantity of investigation.

The measured artifact became cheaper.

If the institution continues to reward document completeness as if it still carried the old cost, the proxy inflates.

This is Goodhart's Machine.

The phrase does not refer to one algorithm.

It refers to an environment in which optimization capacity attaches itself to every legible target.

The machine can be a company.

A market.

A platform.

A bureaucracy.

A classroom.

A model-training pipeline.

A person with a spreadsheet and an assistant.

The important property is not artificiality.

It is responsiveness.

Targets create gradients.

Actors climb them.

The better they become at climbing, the more carefully we have to inspect whether elevation still means what it used to mean.

This helps explain why apparently strong governance can become weak through success.

A regulator creates a measurable standard because the old system lacked consistency.

Organizations conform.

The standard becomes common.

Consultants learn it.

Software encodes it.

Templates circulate.

Training courses teach it.

Audit practices stabilize.

Eventually the entire ecosystem becomes excellent at producing the observable signs of compliance.

That is an achievement.

It is also the moment when the signs become less discriminating.

The institution now needs a new question.

Which organizations have the control in reality, rather than merely the form?

The same pattern appears in hiring.

A resume once compressed a person's history under conditions of moderate production cost. Tailoring every application was possible, but expensive. Cover letters were imperfect signals of writing ability, motivation, and familiarity with the role.

Generative tools reduce the cost of customization.

That can make hiring fairer by helping candidates communicate relevant experience they already possess. It can also saturate employers with perfectly tailored surfaces.

The hiring institution must update.

It cannot keep assigning the same evidentiary weight to customization after customization becomes nearly free.

A signal that was partly valuable because it cost effort loses some value when effort collapses.

This does not mean the candidate became less qualified.

It means the measurement environment changed.

Education faces the same problem in a more emotionally charged form.

A take-home essay was never a pure measure of understanding. Students received help from parents, tutors, peers, spell-checkers, libraries, and the internet. But the cost structure placed limits on assistance.

Generative systems change those limits.

Institutions can respond badly by pretending nothing changed.

They can also respond badly by treating every polished sentence as evidence of misconduct.

The better response is Goodhart-aware assessment.

Ask what quality the essay was supposed to measure.

Writing?

Recall?

Reasoning?

Research?

Synthesis?

Argument under uncertainty?

Use of evidence?

Then design evaluation so the desired quality cannot be inferred from one cheap surface alone.

That may mean oral defense.

Process evidence.

In-class work.

Revision histories.

Novel material.

Open-book assessment.

Projects with external constraints.

Or explicit permission to use AI while evaluating the student's judgment in using it.

The point is not to make assessment harder for the sake of hardship.

It is to reconnect the measure to the thing.

Goodhart's Machine rewards institutions that perform this reconnection continuously.

The old habit was to validate a metric, deploy it, and assume the validation persisted.

The new habit must be to monitor the metric as an evolving market.

Who is optimizing against it?

What capabilities do they now have?

What behaviors raise the score without improving the outcome?

Which legitimate improvements would look like gaming under a simplistic analysis?

What alternate measures disagree?

What residuals are growing?

When did the metric last surprise us?

A metric that never surprises the people it measures may no longer be measuring much.

This is not always true. A thermometer can remain useful even if everyone knows the target temperature. Physical measurements often resist strategic adaptation because the measured system cannot cheaply manipulate the measurement.

Social measurements are different.

People see the rule.

Organizations learn the rule.

Algorithms infer the rule.

Generative systems can often reverse-engineer the visible preferences embedded in the rule.

The measurement participates in the behavior it measures.

This is why Goodhart's Law is frequently invoked in management and policy, but often too casually.

People use it as a punch line whenever a metric has flaws.

The more precise lesson is conditional.

Pressure matters.

The stronger the optimization pressure placed on a measure, the more likely the relationship between measure and goal is to change.

That gives us a practical variable.

Not every metric needs the same defense.

A low-stakes diagnostic used by a small team may remain informative for years.

A public leaderboard tied to prestige and capital will attract more optimization.

A school score tied to funding attracts more pressure than an internal classroom diagnostic.

A platform metric tied to creator income attracts more adaptation than a private quality measure.

A benchmark tied to model marketing attracts more effort than an obscure research probe.

The pressure predicts the need for maintenance.

Artificial intelligence raises pressure in two ways.

First, it increases the capability of the optimizers.

Second, it lowers the cost of running many attempts.

This second effect is easy to underestimate.

If a person can afford to produce one proposal, the proposal contains all the person's constraints.

If a system can produce one thousand variants and rank them, the process becomes a search over the evaluator.

A small bias in the evaluation can become a large selection effect after enough trials.

This is the same reason multiple testing matters in statistics.

Run enough experiments and some will look impressive by chance.

Generate enough candidates against a noisy evaluator and some will score well for reasons that do not transfer.

The winner can look exceptional.

It may simply be the luckiest fit to the measurement.

This matters for model evaluation, hiring, advertising, product testing, grant writing, content distribution, and any other domain where cheap generation feeds selection.

Optimization does not merely improve candidates.

It searches for the blind spots in evaluation.

Often without intending to.

That is the machine.

A well-designed institution therefore preserves holdouts.

Not necessarily statistical holdouts in the narrow machine-learning sense, though those matter.

Holdout judgment.

Holdout data.

Holdout outcomes.

Measures that are not fully optimized in public.

Audits sampled after behavior occurs.

Evaluation on cases the producing system did not select.

Customer outcomes that arrive later than the campaign.

Real-world tests that cannot be simulated perfectly.

Independent reviewers with different incentives.

The goal is to preserve some terrain the optimizer cannot completely pre-shape.

That sounds adversarial because it is.

Measurement becomes adversarial when the measured system adapts.

A tax authority knows this.

A cybersecurity team knows this.

A fraud investigator knows this.

A teacher eventually learns it.

A search engine has always known it.

More organizations will have to learn it as generative capability spreads.

But there is a deeper complication.

Goodhart's Law describes how targeting a measure changes the relationship between measure and goal.

What happens when the changed behavior becomes the next round of training data?

Then the system does not merely adapt once.

It learns from the consequences of its previous adaptation.

The ranking shapes behavior.

Behavior becomes data.

The data trains the next ranking.

The recommendation changes what people see.

What people see changes what they choose.

What they choose becomes evidence of what they wanted.

The observer has entered the loop.

Goodhart's Machine is not stationary.

It feeds itself.

That is the feedback loop.
