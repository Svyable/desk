# The Noise Factory

The easiest way to misunderstand information overload is to count messages.

A person who receives two hundred emails a day has more information to process than a person who receives twenty. A search engine indexing a trillion pages has a larger retrieval problem than one indexing a million. A company with ten dashboards can spend more time looking at numbers than a company with one.

But volume alone is not the interesting change.

Libraries contain enormous volumes of information. Nobody walks into a library, notices the shelves, and concludes that knowledge has become impossible. The collection has structure. Books have titles, authors, publishers, dates, subjects, citations, reputations, catalog records, physical locations, and histories. A reader does not evaluate every sentence in the building before choosing one book.

The harder problem begins when the cost of producing the signals used for selection falls faster than the cost of evaluating what they are supposed to represent.

That is the noise factory.

It does not manufacture randomness.

It manufactures things that look selectable.

A page that resembles a useful page.

A review that resembles an experience.

A summary that resembles comprehension.

An application that resembles preparation.

A photograph that resembles an event.

A research note that resembles a literature review.

A recommendation that resembles judgment.

A customer-service reply that resembles attention.

A status update that resembles progress.

The resemblance is often good enough to be useful. That is what makes the problem difficult.

If generated material were consistently ridiculous, the transition would be simple. We would laugh, filter it out, and move on. The disruptive technology is not nonsense. It is adequate form at low marginal cost.

Form used to carry information because form was expensive.

A hundred-page report suggested that somebody had spent time making a hundred-page report. A carefully written cover letter suggested at least a modest willingness to customize an application. A polished website suggested access to design and development skill. A long technical explanation suggested that the writer had either done the work or was taking an extraordinary risk pretending to have done it.

None of these inferences was ever perfect. People hired ghostwriters. Companies used templates. Students plagiarized. Marketers bought reviews. Propagandists forged documents. Fraud did not arrive with the transformer architecture.

The old signals were noisy too.

They were simply more expensive to counterfeit at scale.

That qualifier matters.

Scale changes the economics of defense.

Suppose a shop receives ten suspicious reviews in a month. An owner can read them, compare purchase records, look for repeated phrasing, and perhaps report the obvious fakes. Now suppose the shop receives ten thousand. Even if the proportion of suspicious material stays the same, the review problem changes. Human inspection no longer scales. The owner needs sampling, automated detection, identity signals, platform enforcement, or some combination of them.

Now make the reviews better.

Give them varied sentence length. Add minor complaints. Mention different products. Use local details. Avoid repeated phrases. Produce some positive, some negative, and some ambivalent. Make the distribution look human.

The problem is no longer writing reviews.

The problem is discrimination.

This is why artificial noise should not be defined as low quality.

Quality can increase while the signal deteriorates.

A beautifully written piece of irrelevant material is still noise relative to a question it does not answer. A perfectly grammatical fake review is more damaging to a reputation system than an obvious bot comment. A persuasive summary built on a weak source can consume more verification time than a clumsy one because the weak points are harder to see.

The relationship is contextual.

Signal is what helps you infer the thing you care about.

Noise is what makes that inference harder.

A weather station can produce an accurate temperature reading that is useless to someone trying to determine whether a bridge is structurally sound. A company's customer-satisfaction score can rise while its most profitable customers quietly leave. A model can improve on a benchmark while becoming less reliable on the particular workflow a hospital cares about.

The same artifact can be signal in one system and noise in another.

The internet has always had this problem, but generative AI changes its supply curve.

Google's search policies now use the phrase “scaled content abuse” for large amounts of unoriginal material produced primarily to manipulate rankings rather than help users. The policy explicitly says the problem does not depend on whether the material was made by automation, by humans, or by some combination. Google's separate guidance on generative AI says the tools can be useful for research and structure while warning that generating many pages without adding value may violate the scaled-content policy.

That distinction is important.

Generation is not the offense.

The problem is scale aimed at a proxy.

A search ranking is a proxy for usefulness. It has to be. No search engine can ask every person in advance which page will genuinely solve the problem they have not yet described. The system relies on signals: links, text, structure, behavior, freshness, reputation, context, and many others.

The moment enough economic value attaches to those signals, people optimize toward them.

Search-engine optimization can be helpful. A restaurant should make its opening hours legible to a search engine. A medical journal should structure pages so a paper can be discovered. A government agency should write titles that citizens can find.

Optimization becomes corrosive when the visible signals are produced mainly to win the ranking rather than serve the person the ranking is supposed to help.

Cheap generation lowers the cost of trying more variations.

One page becomes ten.

Ten become ten thousand.

A publisher can target narrow combinations of query, location, product, question, and phrasing that would never have justified a writer's afternoon. Each individual page may be passable. The aggregate effect can still be a worse search environment if the corpus fills with material that exists because the ranking opportunity existed.

That is the first defining feature of the noise factory:

**The artifact is created because the filter can see it.**

The second feature is more subtle:

**The factory learns from the filter.**

Every filtering system creates feedback.

An email provider blocks a certain kind of spam, so spammers change the wording.

A social network downranks a repetitive behavior, so growth marketers diversify it.

A fraud detector notices one transaction pattern, so fraud moves to another.

A hiring manager begins ignoring generic cover letters, so applicants generate more specific ones.

A teacher notices generated essays have a recognizable cadence, so tools get better at variation and students get better at prompting.

The defender is not sorting a stationary pile.

The pile adapts.

This produces a strange arms race. Better generators create a need for better filters. Better filters create incentives for generators to produce artifacts that survive them. The more important the filtering system becomes, the more valuable it is to approximate the signals the filter trusts.

Again, malicious intent is not required.

Consider a perfectly ordinary company using an AI system to answer customer questions.

The company wants shorter response times, so it measures response time.

The system drafts replies quickly. The metric improves.

Customers still reopen certain cases because the first answer sounds complete without resolving the underlying issue. The company adds a metric for reopen rates.

The system or workflow adapts. Replies become longer and more comprehensive. Reopen rates fall, but customers spend more time reading boilerplate. Another metric arrives.

Every metric is reasonable. Every optimization step can improve something real. Yet the communication can become increasingly shaped by what the dashboard can observe.

Eventually the customer may be reading a reply optimized for measurable satisfaction rather than one written by someone who understood the problem.

If the two coincide, excellent.

When they diverge, artificial noise appears inside the organization without a single bad actor.

This is why “AI slop” is too easy a phrase for the larger phenomenon.

Slop implies obvious inferiority. It invites a comforting response: quality will win.

Sometimes it will.

But the expensive problem is not the bottom of the quality distribution. It is the middle.

Material that is competent enough to demand attention.

A proposal that deserves a meeting before anyone realizes the assumptions are generic.

A code patch that passes the obvious tests but misunderstands the architecture.

A research summary that cites real papers while flattening the disagreement among them.

A photograph that is realistic enough to require provenance checking.

A personalized sales email that contains just enough accurate context to make deletion feel risky.

The noise factory becomes dangerous when ignoring its output also has a cost.

That creates what we might call the plausible-deniability problem of attention.

You cannot simply refuse to read everything generated, because some generated material will be valuable. You cannot simply trust everything polished, because polish is cheap. You cannot simply privilege human origin, because humans can be wrong and machines can be right. You cannot manually verify every claim, because verification capacity is finite.

The old binary categories fail.

Human versus machine is not enough.

Real versus fake is not enough.

High quality versus low quality is not enough.

We need to ask what evidence an artifact carries about the underlying thing we care about.

That is a harder question, but it produces better defenses.

Take customer reviews.

The real question is not, “Was this sentence written by a human?”

The real question is, “What does this sentence tell me about actual customer experience?”

A machine might help a real customer turn rough notes into a clear review. The wording is partly synthetic, but the experience is genuine. A human might be paid to write a review for a product never purchased. The wording is human, but the experience is invented.

Origin matters, but the causal chain matters more.

Did an event happen?

Did the writer observe it?

Can the platform establish that the transaction occurred?

Is the reviewer independent of the seller?

Does the review add information not already copied from elsewhere?

Once we ask those questions, better system design becomes possible.

The same shift applies to research.

A generated literature review is not useless because a model wrote sentences. It becomes risky when the reader cannot tell whether the underlying papers were actually retrieved, whether contradictory findings were represented, whether the citations support the claims, whether important negative results were omitted, or whether ten paragraphs ultimately trace back to one source.

The relevant unit is not prose.

It is evidence lineage.

Organizations will need to become much better at seeing lineage.

Where did this number come from?

Which raw records produced this chart?

Which customer conversations produced this summary?

Which experiments produced this conclusion?

Which independent observations support this recommendation?

Which parts were generated, and from what inputs?

Who checked the high-consequence claims?

The more production scales, the more valuable these questions become.

There is an uncomfortable implication here for people who make information for a living.

Volume is becoming less impressive.

For a long time, businesses treated production capacity as evidence of seriousness. More pages. More posts. More reports. More campaigns. More variants. More documentation. More features. More analyses. More decks.

When production is expensive, output can signal organizational capability.

When production becomes cheap, output can signal the opposite: that nobody imposed a stopping rule.

A company can now create more material than its own employees can read.

That should change management.

The question is no longer, “Can we generate this?”

Of course we can.

The question is, “What decision becomes better because this exists?”

That is a much higher bar.

The noise factory thrives when nobody asks it.

A system is given a target, output is cheap, and success is measured at the point of production rather than the point of use. The factory keeps running because stopping looks like underperformance.

Marketing must keep publishing.

Analytics must keep reporting.

AI assistants must keep answering.

Managers must keep receiving summaries.

Dashboards must keep filling.

The strange result is that institutions can become informationally poorer while producing more information-shaped objects.

People stop reading the reports.

Alerts are ignored.

Dashboards become wallpaper.

Search results become interchangeable.

Internal memos get summarized into other summaries.

Meetings exist to interpret documents nobody had time to inspect.

The signal is still present somewhere.

The customer did say what went wrong.

The experiment did fail.

The model did hallucinate the citation.

The employee did notice the safety issue.

The market did move for a reason not captured by the standard explanation.

But the organization has built a larger haystack around each observation.

That is the third defining feature of the noise factory:

**Production is counted immediately. Verification is deferred.**

The queue grows in the background.

This is where artificial intelligence can create a paradoxical new form of scarcity.

We get more answers and less certainty about which deserve action.

More analysis and less confidence that the analysis is independent.

More personalization and less evidence that a real person paid attention.

More polished work and less information in the polish.

The rational response is not to reject generation.

It is to make verification capacity part of the production budget.

If a team can generate one hundred experiments, it also needs a rule for choosing which ten to inspect and which one to run.

If a model can draft a report in minutes, the organization needs to know which claims require source checks and which can safely remain provisional.

If a platform can host infinite reviews, it needs stronger ways to establish transaction, independence, and manipulation risk.

If a search engine can index an ever-growing web, it needs defenses against content whose primary purpose is to exploit the ranking surface.

The constraint has moved.

That is what new technologies do when they work.

They remove one bottleneck and reveal another.

The first bottleneck was making the artifact.

The next is deciding what the artifact tells us.

The noise factory makes the distinction unavoidable.
