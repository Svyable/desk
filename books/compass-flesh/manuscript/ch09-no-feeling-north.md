# No Feeling North

Most people do not feel magnetism.

That mundane fact should not be argued away. If humans possess magnetoreception, the sensory experience is unlike vision, hearing, touch, smell, taste, or vestibular vertigo in one important respect: there is no widely recognized conscious quale for geomagnetic direction.

The temptation is to invent one retrospectively. A good sense of direction. An inexplicable preference for sleeping orientation. discomfort near electrical equipment. Headaches before storms. The feeling of being watched by a transformer. Once a hidden sense is proposed, ordinary variation becomes available for recruitment.

That is bad science and worse medicine.

A more disciplined possibility is subliminal processing. The nervous system continuously estimates blood pressure, carbon dioxide, joint position, visceral stretch, and many other variables without turning each into a named conscious sensation. Even familiar senses contain processing stages unavailable to introspection. If a geomagnetic input survives in humans, it could contribute weakly to orientation, spatial calibration, circadian biology, or some unknown function without producing "northness."

The 2019 EEG result fits that possibility because participants were not reporting conscious detection. Neural response and conscious experience were separated.

But subliminality raises a falsifiability problem. A sense with no report and no behavior can become immortal. Any failed conscious test is dismissed because the signal is unconscious; any failed navigation test is dismissed because culture suppressed it; any anatomical failure is dismissed because the receptor is distributed.

The way out is prediction. An unconscious magnetic signal should still alter something reproducibly. Reaction time, spatial updating, eye movements, vestibular integration, alpha dynamics, learning, adaptation, or neural connectivity could change under controlled field manipulations. Participants who show strong EEG responses could be compared with nonresponders in tasks chosen before the data are seen.

Conscious training is another useful test. If the signal exists but is ignored, feedback might make it behaviorally accessible. Humans have learned to use artificial sensory substitutions that map cameras, sonar, or orientation sensors into touch and sound. A biological signal already entering the brain should in principle offer something to condition, unless it is too weak or routed away from flexible behavior.

The hidden-sense hypothesis becomes interesting only when it stops needing invisibility.

A sense can be unconscious. It cannot be consequence-free forever.
