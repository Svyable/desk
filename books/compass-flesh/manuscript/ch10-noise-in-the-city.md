# Noise in the City

The geomagnetic field is not the only magnetic field a human lives inside.

Power systems, motors, trains, elevators, loudspeakers, appliances, vehicles, steel structures, wireless electronics, and the magnetized debris of an industrial civilization create a magnetic environment radically unlike the one in which hominins evolved. Static distortions and alternating fields overlap. Buildings bend local field geometry. A person carrying a phone and riding an electric train is not experiencing a pristine planetary compass.

This offers an elegant story: perhaps modern electromagnetic noise masks an ancestral magnetic sense.

Elegant stories are dangerous because they arrive before measurements.

Bird magnetoreception research has produced evidence that weak anthropogenic radiofrequency noise can disrupt magnetic orientation under some conditions. Radical-pair models offer specific reasons certain frequencies might matter. It is therefore reasonable to ask whether human magnetic responses differ between electrically quiet environments and ordinary urban ones.

It is not reasonable to leap from that question to electromagnetic hypersensitivity.

People who report symptoms around electromagnetic fields deserve careful study and humane treatment. But controlled provocation studies have generally struggled to show that self-identified electromagnetic hypersensitivity tracks actual field exposure reliably. Human magnetoreception, even if established, would not retroactively prove broad clinical claims about Wi-Fi, power lines, or cellular signals. A receptor can exist without producing disease. A geomagnetic transducer can have frequency and intensity characteristics irrelevant to modern exposures.

The city nevertheless provides a natural experiment.

If human magnetic transduction was selected in environments with relatively stable geomagnetic cues, chronic exposure to local anomalies might influence development, calibration, or use. People raised in rural versus dense urban environments could differ. Occupations with unusual magnetic exposure could be informative. Geographic regions with different field inclination and intensity could reveal developmental tuning.

These are hypotheses, not findings.

The most valuable urban study would probably be boring: carry calibrated magnetometers through ordinary human environments, quantify the actual static and time-varying fields people encounter, then use those distributions to design laboratory experiments. Magnetoreception research often speaks about "Earth strength" as though the comparison solves ecology. It does not. The relevant exposure is the field at the head, over time, in the environments where nervous systems develop and act.

Before asking whether civilization silenced a sense, measure the noise.
