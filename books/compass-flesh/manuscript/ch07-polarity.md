# Polarity

A compass needle cares which end is north.

A radical-pair inclination compass, in its simplest form, does not. Reverse the magnetic field vector by 180 degrees and the molecular axis relative to the field is unchanged. That distinction turns an abstract mechanism debate into an experimental lever.

The 2019 human EEG study leaned on exactly this point. Responses were sensitive to the polarity of the field. The authors argued that this behavior was inconsistent with a free-radical quantum-compass mechanism like the standard cryptochrome model and remained compatible with a ferromagnetic element such as magnetite.

This is one of the strongest conceptual parts of the paper because it asks a mechanism to risk death.

Frontier fields often accumulate mechanisms that explain everything after the fact. A useful mechanism forbids outcomes. If a receptor is axial, polarity reversal should not matter in the relevant way. If a receptor contains a permanent magnetic moment, polarity can matter. If a response comes from electrical induction in wires or tissue, static polarity should not create the same dependence.

The clean diagram becomes dirty inside a body. Real cryptochrome systems can involve downstream asymmetries, receptor orientation, retinal geometry, mixed mechanisms, and interactions with other cues. Magnetite models have their own degrees of freedom. A behavioral or EEG polarity effect does not automatically identify a mineral receptor.

Still, polarity gives the field something precious: a discriminator.

The next generation of human experiments should treat it as one dimension in a larger response surface. Field intensity can vary. Inclination can vary. declination can vary. rotation speed can vary. static fields can be inverted. oscillatory noise can be layered on top. light wavelength can be changed. participant orientation can be separated from field orientation. If a biological transducer exists, these manipulations should reveal a structure richer than "magnet on" versus "magnet off."

That structure would help locate the receptor before anyone sees it.

A ferromagnetic sensor should have thresholds and directional properties tied to magnetic torque, anisotropy, particle moment, mechanical coupling, and thermal noise. A radical-pair sensor should care about photochemistry, field orientation, molecular alignment, spin dynamics, and radiofrequency perturbation in mechanism-specific ways.

At the moment, human magnetoreception is a rare field in which the most useful arguments are not about whether the phenomenon is weird. They are about whether the response curve has the shape of physics.
