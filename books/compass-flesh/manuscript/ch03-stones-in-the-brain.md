# Stones in the Brain

In 1992, Joseph Kirschvink, Atsuko Kobayashi-Kirschvink and Bruce Woodford reported magnetite in human brain tissue. The result had a satisfying shape: a mineral already implicated in biological magnetism had turned up inside the organ that would have to interpret a magnetic sense.

This is exactly the kind of fact that becomes dangerous when it is made to carry too much.

Magnetite is ferrimagnetic iron oxide, Fe3O4. Small particles can interact mechanically with external fields. In principle, a biologically organized magnetite structure could exert torque, stress a membrane, alter an ion channel, or participate in another transduction scheme. Magnetotactic bacteria make the physical possibility difficult to dismiss. Magnetite has also been found or proposed in sensory structures of other animals. The human finding therefore supplied a plausible material substrate.

It did not identify a receptor.

A receptor is not merely a material with convenient physics. It needs anatomy. It needs organization. It needs coupling to cells. It needs a path by which field-dependent motion becomes electrical or biochemical activity. It needs enough signal above thermal and mechanical noise. Ideally it produces a prediction that distinguishes it from rival mechanisms. Human tissue containing magnetite satisfies almost none of that by itself.

The problem grew stranger in 2016, when Barbara Maher and colleagues reported abundant magnetite nanoparticles in human brains whose rounded morphology and associated metals were consistent with high-temperature combustion sources. Air pollution had entered a discussion that had often treated brain magnetite as though every particle were a tiny evolutionary artifact waiting to be assigned a sensory purpose.

The two findings do not cancel each other. Maher's group described a population of pollution-derived particles distinct from angular particles attributed to endogenous formation. But the pollution result changes the evidentiary discipline. "There is magnetite in the human brain" can no longer glide effortlessly into "the human brain has a magnetite compass." Origin matters. Crystal morphology matters. location matters. size distribution matters. whether particles are isolated or organized matters.

This is a recurring pattern in frontier biology: the component is real, the system is inferred.

If the 2019 EEG response is genuinely polarity-sensitive, ferromagnetic transduction becomes attractive because polarity sensitivity is natural for a permanent magnetic moment. But attraction is not localization. A serious magnetite theory of human magnetoreception has to find the particles that matter, distinguish them from environmental contamination, place them in a reproducible anatomical structure, connect that structure to neural circuitry, and perturb it without destroying half the organism.

Until then, human brain magnetite belongs in evidence class one as a material finding and in evidence class four as a sensory explanation.

That gap is not embarrassing. It is the work.
