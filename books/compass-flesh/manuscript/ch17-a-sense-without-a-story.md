# A Sense Without a Story

Evolutionary stories arrive faster than data.

Humans migrated across continents, crossed deserts, hunted, sailed, and found their way home. Therefore, the story goes, surely a magnetic sense would have been useful. Perhaps modern life buried it. Perhaps language displaced it. Perhaps northern and southern populations tuned differently. Perhaps iron-rich geology shaped culture.

Almost all of that is premature.

Usefulness is not evidence of retention. Humans solved navigation with vision, memory, social transmission, celestial observation, smell, terrain, and tools. Selection can maintain redundant systems, but it can also let expensive systems decay when other cues suffice.

The human case becomes more interesting if we remove the heroic migration story.

A magnetic receptor could have functions unrelated to navigation. It might calibrate vestibular estimates. It might contribute to spatial frames during development. It might influence circadian systems indirectly. It might be a vestige with no current adaptive function. The EEG response could even reflect neural processing of a field-sensitive molecule whose original role lies elsewhere.

This matters because narratives change experiments. If researchers assume navigation, they ask subjects to point north. If they assume a retinal compass, they manipulate light. If they assume magnetite, they reverse polarity. If they assume developmental calibration, they study childhood geography and hemisphere.

A field can become trapped by the first story that makes its phenomenon intuitive.

The strongest version of human magnetoreception therefore has surprisingly little to say about what ancient humans did. It says only that biology elsewhere has repeatedly evolved magnetic sensitivity, that human tissues contain plausible components, that at least one controlled EEG study reported field-specific neural responses, and that the causal chain remains incomplete.

Everything after that is earned hypothesis.

This restraint is not anti-imagination. It is what keeps imagination useful.

If we eventually discover a human magnetic system, the function may be stranger than the folklore. Sensory evolution does not owe us a compass because compasses make good book covers.
