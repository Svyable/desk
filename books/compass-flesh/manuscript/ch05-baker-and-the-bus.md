# Baker and the Bus

Human magnetoreception did not begin with EEG. It began, awkwardly, with people being taken somewhere and asked to point home.

Robin Baker's experiments in the late twentieth century became famous enough to haunt the field. Subjects were transported along unfamiliar routes, sometimes blindfolded, then asked for directional judgments. Some reports suggested that human homing performance deteriorated when magnets were attached to the head or otherwise exposed to altered magnetic conditions.

The claim was irresistible. It was also difficult to reproduce reliably.

This is where the sociology of a field can be mistaken for its evidence. Failed replication should reduce confidence in the specific effect. It should not create a permanent taboo against the entire sensory modality. Yet repeated controversy changes what researchers are willing to spend careers on. A human magnetic sense became the kind of topic that required an apology in the introduction.

The early behavioral work also suffered from a problem that remains relevant: humans are extraordinarily good at leaking cues into navigation experiments. Vehicle acceleration, turns, road noise, sun angle, wind, odors, slope, time estimates, investigator behavior, subtle vision, and memory can all become directional information. A good magnetic experiment is therefore less like giving someone a compass test and more like building a sensory deprivation machine that leaves one variable alive.

Behavior itself is a demanding endpoint. If a magnetic signal is weak, unconscious, developmentally neglected, or normally fused with other senses, asking a participant to point north may be equivalent to asking someone to report their baroreceptor activity. Failure to verbalize a cue is not proof that no neural system receives it.

That argument can become an excuse if used lazily. A phenomenon that never reaches behavior is harder to interpret as a sense. The proper response is not to lower the standard but to split the problem into levels: transduction, neural response, perception, behavior, ecological use.

The EEG work decades later changed the dependent variable. Instead of asking people what direction they thought they faced, researchers asked whether the brain's ongoing activity changed when the geomagnetic vector changed under controlled conditions. The experiment could succeed even if participants consciously felt nothing.

This is not a rescue of Baker. It is a change of question.

A recurring mistake in anomalous research is to treat every new method as vindication of every old claim. The opposite mistake is to let one unreliable paradigm exile an entire hypothesis. Human magnetoreception deserves neither inheritance nor guilt by association. It deserves a sequence of experiments that know exactly which layer of the problem they are testing.
