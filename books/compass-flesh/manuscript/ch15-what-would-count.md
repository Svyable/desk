# What Would Count

The easiest way to keep an anomalous field alive is to never say what would satisfy you.

Human magnetoreception needs a ladder of evidence.

At the bottom is material possibility. Magnetic minerals exist in human tissue. Human cryptochrome can participate in magnetosensitive chemistry under some experimental contexts. Neither establishes a sense.

Next comes reproducible transduction. Independent laboratories should show that controlled geomagnetic manipulations change a human biological signal under blinded, preregistered conditions. The signal should survive hardware redesign and disappear under conditions the proposed mechanism says should abolish it.

Then localization. The responsible tissue or pathway should be narrowed by perturbation, lesion, anatomy, pharmacology, wavelength, genetics, or direct recording.

Then coding. Neural responses should contain information about field properties in a way that can be decoded out of sample.

Then behavior. Magnetic information should alter a task, learning, calibration, or physiological decision in a predictable way.

Then ecology. The effect should make sense at field strengths, noise spectra, and exposure patterns humans actually encounter.

No single rung is mandatory in exactly that order, but a mature claim should eventually climb most of them.

The 2019 EEG paper occupies the reproducible-transduction rung only provisionally because independent replication is the unresolved issue. The magnetite and cryptochrome studies populate material and mechanism plausibility. The behavioral literature remains inconsistent. Anatomy is missing.

This framework is intentionally unfair to romance.

It also protects the interesting result from being forced to answer questions it did not test. An EEG response can be real even if humans cannot navigate by it. Cryptochrome chemistry can be real even if it is irrelevant to the EEG response. Brain magnetite can be real even if none of it is sensory.

What would disconfirm the book's strongest synthesis? Multiple high-powered, independent failures to reproduce the EEG effect under faithful conditions would sharply reduce the case for current human magnetic transduction. A demonstrated nonmagnetic artifact would collapse it faster. Anatomical work showing that candidate magnetite is entirely exogenous and unorganized would weaken ferromagnetic models. Human cryptochrome results that fail under Earth-strength conditions would narrow radical-pair arguments.

A field becomes science when it can lose.

Magnetoreception has spent too long being asked whether it is possible.

The better question is what result would make us stop.
