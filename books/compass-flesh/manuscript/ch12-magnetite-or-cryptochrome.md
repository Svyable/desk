# Magnetite or Cryptochrome

Scientific arguments love tournaments.

Magnetite versus cryptochrome has often been narrated as one: mechanical mineral compass on one side, quantum chemical compass on the other. Evidence accumulates, points are scored, and eventually a winner is expected to leave with magnetoreception.

Biology rarely respects the bracket.

Different animals may use different mechanisms. A single animal may use more than one magnetic channel. One system could provide compass direction while another contributes intensity or map information. A receptor could calibrate another cue rather than directly guide behavior. Evolution reuses available machinery.

Humans make premature exclusivity especially risky because the evidence streams do not even point to the same endpoint. Human CRY2 can support magnetosensitivity in a transgenic fly system. Human brain tissue contains magnetite, including both endogenous-looking and pollution-derived populations. A human EEG study reports a polarity-sensitive response that its authors interpret as inconsistent with the standard radical-pair compass.

These are three facts with three different inferential distances.

The right research program should make both mechanisms compete on predictions while leaving room for coexistence. Light dependence is obvious. If the human EEG response persists in complete darkness after adequate dark adaptation, that weakens a photochemical retinal mechanism for that response, though cryptochrome chemistry can be more complicated than a cartoon. Wavelength dependence can be tested. Radiofrequency fields chosen to perturb radical-pair spin dynamics can be tested. Static polarity, inclination, intensity, and mechanical perturbations can probe ferromagnetic models.

Genetics could eventually help. Cryptochrome variants might correlate with magnetic response if sample sizes are enormous and hypotheses are preregistered. Tissue studies could search for organized magnetite structures and molecular machinery associated with mechanotransduction. Neither approach is likely to produce a clean answer quickly.

The prize is not naming the receptor. It is closing a causal chain.

Field changes receptor. Receptor changes cell. Cell changes nerve. Nerve changes brain. Brain changes computation. Computation changes behavior or physiology.

At present, every human magnetoreception story has missing arrows.

That is why the mechanism fight is useful. Each candidate tells us which arrow to attack next.
