# The Southern Test

A Northern Hemisphere nervous system grows up under field lines that point downward into Earth. In much of the Southern Hemisphere they point upward.

The 2019 human EEG result made this geography scientifically useful. The reported alpha response appeared for certain rotations when the vertical component was directed downward, while corresponding conditions with an upward component did not produce the same response. The authors interpreted this as ecological tuning rather than a generic electromagnetic artifact.

If so, the obvious experiment lives south of the equator.

Recruit participants raised in Australia, New Zealand, southern Africa, southern South America, or other regions with upward-pointing geomagnetic inclination. Test them with the same apparatus. Do they respond preferentially to their native polarity? Do immigrants recalibrate? Does childhood exposure matter more than current residence? Are there sensitive periods? Does the response track magnetic latitude continuously rather than hemisphere as a binary?

This is the kind of experiment that can turn a curious laboratory effect into biology.

It can also kill the story.

If Southern Hemisphere participants show the exact same "Northern" preference, the ecological-tuning interpretation weakens. A hardware asymmetry, analysis artifact, universal anatomy, or some other explanation becomes more plausible. If no laboratory can reproduce the effect at all, the hemisphere question becomes irrelevant.

Geographic prediction is unusually powerful because it was not invented only to explain a failure. It falls directly out of the original reported dependence on vertical field direction.

The field should embrace such tests because the history of anomalous research is full of effects that remain alive by becoming more elaborate each time they fail. A good esoteric thesis should become more vulnerable as it becomes more specific.

There is also a larger evolutionary question. Many migratory animals use inclination rather than polarity precisely because inclination changes systematically across the planet. Humans, if they retained a different kind of detector, may not use magnetic information for long-distance migration at all. A polarity-sensitive response could reflect calibration to local geophysics rather than compass navigation.

The Southern test therefore does more than replicate a chamber.

It asks whether the phenomenon has a childhood.
