# Training a Sense

Suppose the signal is there.

Not a mystical compass. Not a conscious arrow. Just a weak, reliable neural variable correlated with magnetic-field rotation.

Could a person learn to use it?

Sensory substitution research makes the question less fanciful than it sounds. Humans can learn to extract spatial information from signals delivered through unusual channels when feedback connects sensation to consequence. Blind users can learn structured information from auditory or tactile mappings. Pilots learn instruments that translate invisible physical states into action. The brain is opportunistic.

A human magnetic signal already entering sensory circuitry would present a different problem: there may be information without a consciously accessible label.

One experiment could select participants with robust, repeatable EEG responses, then train them on a forced-choice orientation task with trial-by-trial feedback while controlling all nonmagnetic cues. The magnetic field could rotate independently of the participant and room. Sham blocks would measure strategy and expectation. Learning curves could be compared with EEG magnitude.

If performance rises above chance only in responders, survives blinding, generalizes to novel orientations, and collapses under mechanism-specific disruption, the field would change overnight.

If training fails, that result also matters. The neural effect might be noninformational, epiphenomenal, too weak, or routed into functions not available for conscious learning.

The experiment has to avoid becoming a stage act. No handheld magnets. No guessing games outside shielding. No investigator who knows the condition. No post hoc sorting of "gifted" participants until someone beats chance.

There is an ethical oddity here too. A successful training protocol would create a human experience without a familiar sensory vocabulary. Participants might report pressure, visual texture, intuition, certainty without sensation, or nothing at all while performance improves. Language would lag behind psychophysics.

That would be scientifically valuable because it would force a distinction we usually ignore: senses are not defined by what they feel like. They are defined by reliable information transduction and use.

The hidden human compass, if it exists, may never feel like a compass.

We may have to teach ourselves what to call it.
