# A Fungus Makes Frost into Hair

On certain damp, cold mornings, a dead branch in a forest can appear to have grown long white hair. The strands are astonishingly fine, sometimes curling into delicate clumps that look like cotton or silk. They are ice. The phenomenon is called hair ice, and its occurrence depends on a fungus living in the wood. The beautiful part is visible to any walker who happens to be in the right forest at the right temperature. The mechanism took researchers more than a century to investigate in detail.

In July 2015, Diana Hofmann, Gerhart Preuss and Christian Mätzler published [Evidence for biological shaping of hair ice](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/12/4261/2015/index.html) in *Biogeosciences*. They studied pieces of dead broadleaf wood under cold, humid conditions, examined the chemistry of the ice and looked for fungi. A species called *Exidiopsis effusa* was present on all the hair-ice-producing samples in their investigation. Heat treatment or fungicide suppressed the formation of the delicate filaments. The finding linked a particular biological activity to the unusual physical shape of frozen water.

The paper's historical trail is almost as good as the experiment. Alfred Wegener, better known for proposing continental drift, had suggested in 1918 that a fungus might contribute to the phenomenon. A geophysicist who helped change the scientific understanding of continents also thought about ice filaments on dead twigs. The two subjects are wildly different in scale. Both require attention to evidence that appears ordinary until someone notices a problem with the accepted explanation.

Hair ice grows under particular conditions, often on dead wood from broadleaf trees when the temperature lies slightly below freezing. Water in the wood moves toward the surface and freezes in very thin strands through a process associated with ice segregation. A fungus can influence how those crystals grow and persist. The exact chemical compounds responsible for suppressing recrystallization have been studied, but the mechanism is more complicated than saying that a mushroom spins ice like silk.

The [European Geosciences Union's research summary](https://www.egu.eu/news/180/fungus-shapes-hair-ice-researchers-identify-fungus-responsible-for-peculiar-ice-filaments-that-grow-on-dead-wood/) distinguishes the physical and biological components. The freezing process produces ice. The living fungus appears necessary to maintain the hairlike structure under the conditions studied, preventing the crystals from simply merging into more ordinary ice forms. Biology influences a physical process without creating a different substance. The white hairs are water that has frozen, not fungal threads coated in frost.

That distinction is important because the visible material looks biological. A person might assume the branch has grown a cottony mold. Another might think the strands are ordinary frost arranged by wind. Both guesses use familiar categories. The studied phenomenon is produced by their interaction: a dead piece of wood, available moisture, freezing conditions and fungal activity. It cannot be reduced to any one of those ingredients without losing the shape that makes it distinctive.

The people who eventually made the connection secure were an unlikely little research team, which makes the history more enjoyable than the phrase scientists have solved the mystery. Diana Hofmann was a chemist at Forschungszentrum Jülich in Germany. Gerhart Preuss was a biologist and keen observer of the phenomenon, and Christian Mätzler was a physicist at the University of Bern. Their project needed all three: field observations of when hair ice occurred, experiments on the physical behavior of water inside wood, and analysis of substances mixed into the melted filaments.

The [2015 paper in *Biogeosciences*](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/12/4261/2015/index.html) did not begin from a newly discovered species. Hair ice had been known and photographed before, and Wegener's fungal hypothesis was nearly a century old. The achievement was to make the observation reproducible and connect it with physical and chemical measurements. A fragile natural feature becomes much more informative when the same process can be recreated under controlled conditions.

Preuss collected pieces of wood that had produced hair ice in the field. The researchers kept track of the species of wood, the fungal growth and the environmental conditions that seemed to permit the phenomenon. They could then compare samples under cooling conditions, observe whether the characteristic filaments returned, and test what happened when fungal activity was suppressed. The work was not a single trip into a beautiful forest followed by a laboratory revelation. It was a series of observations and manipulations designed to isolate a cause.

There was an immediate experimental difficulty: hair ice forms under conditions that are narrow and transient. The temperature must be slightly below freezing, the wood must contain water and the surface must support a supply of liquid to the growing crystals. A piece of dead wood in a freezer may not reproduce the field conditions if it dries, freezes uniformly or lacks the relevant organism. Making the process happen repeatedly is part of identifying what the system requires.

The team succeeded in producing hair ice on samples from which it had previously been observed. The result connected a specific piece of wood with the ability to create the unusual ice under controlled circumstances. It also allowed them to examine the growth process more closely than a hiker watching an early-morning frost. The strands emerged from the wood's surface, not from a hidden spool or a growing fungal fiber simply coated in ice.

A central experiment asked whether the fungus was providing significant heat and thereby changing the freezing process. One might imagine that an organism's metabolism warmed a thin water layer, encouraging an unusual ice structure. The [2015 investigators](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/12/4261/2015/index.html) compared temperature profiles of wood samples with active fungi and samples whose biological activity had been disrupted. The temperature behavior was essentially the same. That was evidence against fungal heat being the primary explanation.

The result is one of the most interesting corrections in this chapter. The fungus was necessary for the special appearance under the studied conditions, yet it was not making the ice by heating the wood in the way the skunk cabbage later produces warmth. The physical formation of ice still followed the ordinary process of water moving toward a freezing front. The organism's more likely role was in controlling the structure and stability of the crystals that formed.

The mechanism of ice segregation deserves a little attention. In a porous material, water can move through small spaces under gradients created by temperature, pressure and the advancing freezing front. Liquid water within the wood may continue feeding a growing ice filament at the surface. The ice extends outward while water is supplied from behind. The process does not require a hollow hair-shaped mold. It requires that the ice maintain a narrow shape while new material is added.

Ordinary ice crystals often grow or merge into larger forms over time. If many very fine strands were allowed to recrystallize without interference, their delicate arrangement might disappear. The researchers proposed that substances associated with fungal activity inhibit recrystallization, helping preserve the thin strands. This is a strong mechanistic hypothesis linked to the observed chemical mixture, although the exact responsible compound was not identified in the original study.

The chemical investigation was unusually elaborate for a phenomenon that looks like a child's science-fair experiment. The team melted hair ice and analyzed dissolved organic material. The [published paper](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/12/4261/2015/index.html) describes thousands of compounds containing combinations of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and sometimes nitrogen or sulfur. The mixture resembled complex dissolved organic matter, with products associated with the breakdown of lignin in wood. The material was not a neat single substance that could be given a short chemical formula.

Lignin is part of the structural material that helps wood resist collapse. Fungi involved in wood decomposition can alter lignin and other polymers, producing smaller organic compounds. Some of those products can dissolve in the water moving through the branch. When the water freezes on the surface, dissolved material may influence crystal growth or persist in the thin film around the ice. A process that starts with the decay of wood can therefore end with a remarkable form of ice.

This is an example of biological chemistry altering the behavior of a nonliving material without changing its fundamental composition. The ice is water. The fungus influences conditions under which water crystallizes and remains in a particular structure. An observer sees an object that looks grown, but the formation depends on two interacting processes whose ordinary descriptions belong to different scientific subjects.

The [2015 European Geosciences Union account](https://www.egu.eu/news/180/fungus-shapes-hair-ice-researchers-identify-fungus-responsible-for-peculiar-ice-filaments-that-grow-on-dead-wood/) notes that the suspected fungus, *Exidiopsis effusa*, was present in every sample of hair-ice-producing wood examined. Heat treatment and fungicide suppressed the characteristic formation. Together, association and experimental intervention made the biological case much stronger than a simple observation that mold and frost were found on the same branch.

But a finding of necessity under the tested conditions should not be inflated into a statement about every visible filament on every cold twig. Different forms of needle ice, frost and fungal growth can resemble hair ice. Identifying the substrate and conditions remains essential. The team established a causal role in the particular phenomenon it investigated. A reader who sees white fibers in a photograph cannot establish *E. effusa* from appearance alone.

The [2024 ecological note on hair and needle ice](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/fee.2732) gives a useful comparison. Needle ice grows from soil and can lift or disturb small particles as water freezes near the surface. It can appear as narrow columns emerging from the ground without requiring the fungal mechanism associated with hair ice on dead wood. The two formations are cousins in physical appearance, not identical biological phenomena. Confusing them would hide exactly what the 2015 experiment discovered.

The human history is also unusual because Wegener's earlier suggestion survived for nearly one hundred years without a fully convincing demonstration. A geophysicist who later became famous for continental drift had paid attention to a small ice structure. The hypothesis was not an official truth waiting passively for confirmation. It was an explanation to be tested against alternative physical and biological accounts. Later researchers brought a different set of instruments and methods to the same observation.

The delay should not be interpreted as proof that scientists ignored an obvious answer for a century. Hair ice is rare and fleeting. It grows overnight and can melt after the sun reaches it. It can be obscured by ordinary snow or frost. Samples may stop producing it if they dry out or lose active fungal material. The conditions make repeated experiments difficult and create long gaps between interesting field observations. The logistical problem was genuine.

A camera can capture the shape, but the definitive work takes place when the researchers change the conditions and see which aspects of the outcome change. The fungus is suppressed; the fine hairs do not form. The temperature profile remains similar; the heating hypothesis weakens. The chemical analysis finds complex organic material; a possible mechanism for crystal stabilization becomes plausible. The argument is cumulative and specific. No one result carries every conclusion.

The story also illustrates a form of scientific success that has no obvious business plan attached. A team investigated white filaments on decaying twigs because the phenomenon was unexplained and because several branches of science could meet in the answer. The payoff was a better understanding of how fungal activity affects ice structures. It may contribute to wider work on crystallization, but no invented medical or industrial application is required to justify the study.

The little forest object is a record of a relationship between decay and freezing. Fungal metabolism alters chemical conditions in wood. Water moves through pores and freezes at the surface. The resulting ice is kept fine enough to resemble hair. None of the participants is attempting to make art, and the structure disappears with weather. The researchers have managed to reconstruct a process whose finished product may not last until lunch.

It would be easy to describe the effect as magic performed by a fungus. That phrase misses the most remarkable part. The organism influences the growth and stability of a material that obeys ordinary thermodynamics, but which takes a shape very different from familiar frost. A small biological intervention becomes visible through a larger physical process. The exact inhibitor remains a question, and the questions are more valuable than a false claim of complete knowledge.

Hair ice is not a new species, not an alien filament and not a fungal beard. It is water. The extraordinary part is the system that lets the water hold its form.

The experiments included a comparison between samples whose fungal activity remained and samples treated to suppress it. The physical freezing conditions were not dramatically altered by the loss of the fungus, yet the characteristic hair did not form in the same way. That result strongly suggested the fungus affected the structure rather than simply heating the wood enough to change its temperature. The research did not require the organism to create energy or force water to disobey freezing physics.

This is an elegant example of why controlled comparison matters. A field photograph can demonstrate that delicate ice strands exist on a branch. Finding a fungus on the same branch establishes an association. Treating samples and comparing the growth under similar temperature conditions moves the claim toward a causal explanation. The presence of a species alone is not enough; the experiment's intervention provides additional evidence about its role.

The researchers also analyzed melted hair ice and detected organic compounds, including material associated with decomposed lignin from the wood. They suggested that some of these substances might help maintain the thin ice strands by inhibiting recrystallization. The exact identity and behavior of the responsible compounds remain questions for further work. A chemical analysis can identify a complex mixture without revealing which molecule is essential for a particular physical effect.

The phenomenon has attracted attention because it looks like a small, harmless miracle. But the science is connected to serious questions about freezing and biological materials. The growth and stability of ice crystals matter in soils, plants, food processing and other settings. Organisms can produce compounds that alter ice formation or crystal size. The hair ice is not necessarily a direct model for each application, but it demonstrates that biological chemistry can substantially affect the shape and persistence of ice.

The physical scale is remarkable. Strands reported in the literature can be only about two hundredths of a millimeter in diameter. An ordinary human hair is of a comparable order of magnitude, though its width varies. Such a fine thread of ice might be expected to break, fuse or disappear quickly as environmental conditions change. Hair ice can persist long enough to be photographed when the weather remains suitable. Its fragility is part of its appearance, not evidence that it is imaginary.

A fallen branch is an unlikely museum for one of the more delicate shapes on the planet. The object is decaying, and a fungus is helping break down the wood. At a particular temperature and humidity, the interaction among the fungus, moisture and freezing produces a structure that seems designed for ornament. The organism is not attempting to decorate the forest. It is participating in decomposition, and the ice is a consequence of that activity under the right conditions.

The [2015 study](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/12/4261/2015/index.html) is careful about what it establishes. The association with *Exidiopsis effusa* was strong, and experimental suppression of fungal activity supported a causal role. The paper did not claim to have identified every molecule responsible for shaping the hair, nor that all frostlike structures in every forest share this origin. The distinction between hair ice and other ice formations matters.

Needle ice, for example, can grow from soil as freezing water is drawn upward. It may resemble tiny crystalline columns or bristles. It is not the same as hair ice emerging from decaying wood under fungal influence. The [2024 ecological explanation](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/fee.2732) distinguishes the phenomena. A single photograph of pale fibers on a cold surface can be misleading without information about the substrate and physical conditions.

Wegener's role adds a useful historical warning. A person can propose a plausible mechanism long before later experiments establish the details. The fact that he suspected a fungal connection does not make his idea equivalent to a completed modern analysis. It means a hypothesis survived because the observation continued to demand explanation. A century later researchers had better tools for identifying fungal species, manipulating biological activity and analyzing the chemistry of the melted filaments.

There is a quiet kind of scientific patience in a project that studies frost on dead branches. It is not the sort of subject that reliably produces commercial predictions or dramatic policy announcements. The experiment is valuable because it turns a curious visible pattern into a series of testable physical and biological questions. The researchers did not need the fungus to be intelligent. They needed to determine whether the fungal activity was necessary for the observed ice structure.

The case also challenges a crude distinction between living and nonliving things. Ice is nonliving water in a solid state. A fungal organism is alive. Dead wood is a biological material whose original cells are no longer carrying out the functions of a tree. The resulting white filaments arise because those categories interact. A photograph shows one object, but the explanation crosses several levels of organization. Natural phenomena do not respect the divisions of a school textbook.

There is a limit to the metaphor of a fungus making hair. The strands are not actual hairs in the biological sense, and the fungus does not sit at a loom. The name conveys the shape. The mechanism combines water migration, freezing and substances associated with the wood–fungus system. A person can enjoy the comparison without misunderstanding the material.

The scientists were also interested in the conditions that made hair ice disappear. A change in temperature can melt the filaments. Mechanical disturbance can break them. Recrystallization can alter their delicate form. The ability of the fungus to inhibit that process under particular conditions explains why the hairs survive at all. Their existence is therefore a balance between forces creating fine crystals and processes that would ordinarily erase them.

It is an odd outcome of decay. A fallen branch slowly loses its original structure as microorganisms break down its material. Under the right winter conditions, that decay contributes to the creation of a highly ordered visible shape. There is no general law that decomposition must look disordered. Matter can pass through unexpected forms along the way to becoming something else.

A hiker who sees hair ice may never know which fungus inhabits the branch. An investigator with samples and instruments can establish the connection more securely. Both encounter the same physical object. The surprise in the field is followed by the labor of figuring out which components matter.

I would rather keep the first sight of those delicate white strands and the lab report together. The former explains why someone cared enough to investigate. The latter shows why the appearance was possible. Hair ice is a small event, no danger to a city and no change to national policy. Its scale is precisely why it belongs here. The planet can be astonishing on a decaying twig.
