# A Fungus Makes Frost into Hair

On certain damp, cold mornings, a dead branch in a forest can appear to have grown long white hair. The strands are astonishingly fine, sometimes curling into delicate clumps that look like cotton or silk. They are ice. The phenomenon is called hair ice, and its occurrence depends on a fungus living in the wood. The beautiful part is visible to any walker who happens to be in the right forest at the right temperature. The mechanism took researchers more than a century to investigate in detail.

In July 2015, Diana Hofmann, Gerhart Preuss and Christian Mätzler published [Evidence for biological shaping of hair ice](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/12/4261/2015/index.html) in *Biogeosciences*. They studied pieces of dead broadleaf wood under cold, humid conditions, examined the chemistry of the ice and looked for fungi. A species called *Exidiopsis effusa* was present on all the hair-ice-producing samples in their investigation. Heat treatment or fungicide suppressed the formation of the delicate filaments. The finding linked a particular biological activity to the unusual physical shape of frozen water.

The paper's historical trail is almost as good as the experiment. Alfred Wegener, better known for proposing continental drift, had suggested in 1918 that a fungus might contribute to the phenomenon. A geophysicist who helped change the scientific understanding of continents also thought about ice filaments on dead twigs. The two subjects are wildly different in scale. Both require attention to evidence that appears ordinary until someone notices a problem with the accepted explanation.

Hair ice grows under particular conditions, often on dead wood from broadleaf trees when the temperature lies slightly below freezing. Water in the wood moves toward the surface and freezes in very thin strands through a process associated with ice segregation. A fungus can influence how those crystals grow and persist. The exact chemical compounds responsible for suppressing recrystallization have been studied, but the mechanism is more complicated than saying that a mushroom spins ice like silk.

The [European Geosciences Union's research summary](https://www.egu.eu/news/180/fungus-shapes-hair-ice-researchers-identify-fungus-responsible-for-peculiar-ice-filaments-that-grow-on-dead-wood/) distinguishes the physical and biological components. The freezing process produces ice. The living fungus appears necessary to maintain the hairlike structure under the conditions studied, preventing the crystals from simply merging into more ordinary ice forms. Biology influences a physical process without creating a different substance. The white hairs are water that has frozen, not fungal threads coated in frost.

That distinction is important because the visible material looks biological. A person might assume the branch has grown a cottony mold. Another might think the strands are ordinary frost arranged by wind. Both guesses use familiar categories. The studied phenomenon is produced by their interaction: a dead piece of wood, available moisture, freezing conditions and fungal activity. It cannot be reduced to any one of those ingredients without losing the shape that makes it distinctive.

The experiments included a comparison between samples whose fungal activity remained and samples treated to suppress it. The physical freezing conditions were not dramatically altered by the loss of the fungus, yet the characteristic hair did not form in the same way. That result strongly suggested the fungus affected the structure rather than simply heating the wood enough to change its temperature. The research did not require the organism to create energy or force water to disobey freezing physics.

This is an elegant example of why controlled comparison matters. A field photograph can demonstrate that delicate ice strands exist on a branch. Finding a fungus on the same branch establishes an association. Treating samples and comparing the growth under similar temperature conditions moves the claim toward a causal explanation. The presence of a species alone is not enough; the experiment's intervention provides additional evidence about its role.

The researchers also analyzed melted hair ice and detected organic compounds, including material associated with decomposed lignin from the wood. They suggested that some of these substances might help maintain the thin ice strands by inhibiting recrystallization. The exact identity and behavior of the responsible compounds remain questions for further work. A chemical analysis can identify a complex mixture without revealing which molecule is essential for a particular physical effect.

The phenomenon has attracted attention because it looks like a small, harmless miracle. But the science is connected to serious questions about freezing and biological materials. The growth and stability of ice crystals matter in soils, plants, food processing and other settings. Organisms can produce compounds that alter ice formation or crystal size. The hair ice is not necessarily a direct model for each application, but it demonstrates that biological chemistry can substantially affect the shape and persistence of ice.

The physical scale is remarkable. Strands reported in the literature can be only about two hundredths of a millimeter in diameter. An ordinary human hair is of a comparable order of magnitude, though its width varies. Such a fine thread of ice might be expected to break, fuse or disappear quickly as environmental conditions change. Hair ice can persist long enough to be photographed when the weather remains suitable. Its fragility is part of its appearance, not evidence that it is imaginary.

A fallen branch is an unlikely museum for one of the more delicate shapes on the planet. The object is decaying, and a fungus is helping break down the wood. At a particular temperature and humidity, the interaction among the fungus, moisture and freezing produces a structure that seems designed for ornament. The organism is not attempting to decorate the forest. It is participating in decomposition, and the ice is a consequence of that activity under the right conditions.

The [2015 study](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/12/4261/2015/index.html) is careful about what it establishes. The association with *Exidiopsis effusa* was strong, and experimental suppression of fungal activity supported a causal role. The paper did not claim to have identified every molecule responsible for shaping the hair, nor that all frostlike structures in every forest share this origin. The distinction between hair ice and other ice formations matters.

Needle ice, for example, can grow from soil as freezing water is drawn upward. It may resemble tiny crystalline columns or bristles. It is not the same as hair ice emerging from decaying wood under fungal influence. The [2024 ecological explanation](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/fee.2732) distinguishes the phenomena. A single photograph of pale fibers on a cold surface can be misleading without information about the substrate and physical conditions.

Wegener's role adds a useful historical warning. A person can propose a plausible mechanism long before later experiments establish the details. The fact that he suspected a fungal connection does not make his idea equivalent to a completed modern analysis. It means a hypothesis survived because the observation continued to demand explanation. A century later researchers had better tools for identifying fungal species, manipulating biological activity and analyzing the chemistry of the melted filaments.

There is a quiet kind of scientific patience in a project that studies frost on dead branches. It is not the sort of subject that reliably produces commercial predictions or dramatic policy announcements. The experiment is valuable because it turns a curious visible pattern into a series of testable physical and biological questions. The researchers did not need the fungus to be intelligent. They needed to determine whether the fungal activity was necessary for the observed ice structure.

The case also challenges a crude distinction between living and nonliving things. Ice is nonliving water in a solid state. A fungal organism is alive. Dead wood is a biological material whose original cells are no longer carrying out the functions of a tree. The resulting white filaments arise because those categories interact. A photograph shows one object, but the explanation crosses several levels of organization. Natural phenomena do not respect the divisions of a school textbook.

There is a limit to the metaphor of a fungus making hair. The strands are not actual hairs in the biological sense, and the fungus does not sit at a loom. The name conveys the shape. The mechanism combines water migration, freezing and substances associated with the wood–fungus system. A person can enjoy the comparison without misunderstanding the material.

The scientists were also interested in the conditions that made hair ice disappear. A change in temperature can melt the filaments. Mechanical disturbance can break them. Recrystallization can alter their delicate form. The ability of the fungus to inhibit that process under particular conditions explains why the hairs survive at all. Their existence is therefore a balance between forces creating fine crystals and processes that would ordinarily erase them.

It is an odd outcome of decay. A fallen branch slowly loses its original structure as microorganisms break down its material. Under the right winter conditions, that decay contributes to the creation of a highly ordered visible shape. There is no general law that decomposition must look disordered. Matter can pass through unexpected forms along the way to becoming something else.

A hiker who sees hair ice may never know which fungus inhabits the branch. An investigator with samples and instruments can establish the connection more securely. Both encounter the same physical object. The surprise in the field is followed by the labor of figuring out which components matter.

I would rather keep the first sight of those delicate white strands and the lab report together. The former explains why someone cared enough to investigate. The latter shows why the appearance was possible. Hair ice is a small event, no danger to a city and no change to national policy. Its scale is precisely why it belongs here. The planet can be astonishing on a decaying twig.
