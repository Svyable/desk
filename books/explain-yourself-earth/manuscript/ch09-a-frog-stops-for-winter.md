# A Frog Stops for Winter

A wood frog can survive freezing. During a winter cold spell, ice forms in parts of its body. Its heartbeat and breathing may cease while it remains frozen, and circulation is profoundly reduced. When conditions warm, the animal can thaw and resume activity. The claim sounds like a laboratory error because most vertebrates suffer severe damage when ice forms in their tissues. Yet wood frog freeze tolerance has been studied in controlled experiments for decades, and the phenomenon is a genuine part of the species' winter biology.

The [1987 physiological study by Kenneth Storey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3618830/) exposed wood frogs to repeated freeze–thaw cycles and measured changes in metabolism. During freezing, the animals mobilized large quantities of glucose from liver glycogen; during thawing, the glucose could be restored to storage. The result made a chemical pathway central to understanding an ability that otherwise seemed almost impossible for an animal with a heart, liver and brain.

The word frozen is not a universal state. In a wood frog, ice can form outside cells, while the animal's physiology limits the injuries that freezing would otherwise cause. Ice crystals inside cells can damage delicate structures; water movement, osmotic stress and temperature changes also matter. The organism's protective response includes the production or redistribution of compounds that help cells tolerate the changing physical environment. The frog is not simply a solid cube of ice that revives because the weather decides to be generous.

The ability to survive is also conditional. Temperature, cooling rate, duration and the animal's physiological state affect the outcome. A wood frog that tolerates freezing under particular natural conditions is not invulnerable to arbitrary cold. The experimental literature carefully distinguishes survival after controlled exposure from the fantasy of an animal that can be stored in a household freezer indefinitely and returned to life at will.

The controlled experiments are much more interesting than the common story of a frozen frog waking up. An especially revealing [1987 paper by Kenneth Storey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3618830/) studied animals through three consecutive freeze–thaw cycles. Each freezing interval lasted two days. The team examined biochemical changes across ten tissues and found that liver glycogen was broken down and large amounts of glucose accumulated. During the thawing intervals, the pattern reversed, with glucose restored to liver glycogen. The organism was not maintaining the maximum glucose concentration continuously throughout winter. It mobilized the protective material in response to actual freezing episodes.

That sequence changes how the physiological achievement should be described. A cryoprotectant is not simply an ingredient a frog carries like a bottle of antifreeze. The amount, location and timing of its production matter. An animal can have a large supply of stored glycogen yet remain vulnerable if glucose is not delivered to a particular tissue before freezing disrupts circulation. The protective system has a history measured in minutes and hours, and that history is part of the reason the animal survives.

The result is easy to summarize and difficult to reproduce. A researcher needs to control the animal's starting condition, cooling rate, minimum temperature and duration of exposure. The amount of ice that forms matters. Temperature sensors and biochemical samples help describe what happens in different organs. After thawing, the animal must recover integrated physiological function, not merely show that a small patch of tissue remains physically intact. A successful freeze tolerance experiment is a whole-animal outcome supported by organ-level measurements.

The [1991 study by Jon Costanzo and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1750578/) tested a specific weakness of the system. Rapid cooling can injure wood frogs because the animal has insufficient time to produce and distribute its own glucose before damage occurs. The researchers experimentally loaded some frogs with glucose and compared them with controls during rapid freezing. The work helped establish that the timing and amount of cryoprotectant were causal factors, rather than merely correlated measurements in an animal that happened to survive.

The experiment is especially effective because it changes one aspect of the system while preserving the broader freezing challenge. If protective glucose has already been supplied, the animal may tolerate a rapid change better than one forced to mobilize it from storage during cooling. The result does not mean external glucose is a general cure for freezing injury. It shows a particular concentration-dependent protective role under the study conditions.

A later [1993 experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8409827/) established an even more numerical relationship. Researchers studied wood frogs from southern Ohio and reported that control animals had difficulty surviving freezing below roughly minus three degrees Celsius under their conditions. Animals given additional glucose before freezing often survived lower temperatures around minus five degrees Celsius, while the corresponding saline-injected controls did not survive that exposure. The contrast provides evidence that more glucose can improve tolerance within a bounded physiological range.

This is a much better result than the phrase frogs can survive being frozen solid. It gives a location, temperature, comparison group and measured outcome. It does not establish that every wood frog from every region has the same limit. Northern populations can experience different seasonal and physiological conditions, and local adaptations may differ. A study using southern Ohio animals should not be silently used as a universal standard for Alaskan frogs.

The [1992 work on cooling rate and dehydration](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1569408/) adds another mechanism. Organs lose water during freezing as extracellular ice forms and osmotic conditions change. This can reduce the chance of damaging intracellular ice, but excessive dehydration can itself be harmful. The response must remain within a range compatible with recovery. The protective picture therefore includes sugar, water movement, tissue structure and the timing of both cooling and thawing.

A [study of water dynamics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/030645659290064M) reported that a wood frog frozen at minus 2.5 degrees Celsius could have around sixty-five percent of its body water frozen under particular experimental conditions. The phrase partly frozen is accurate and striking. It also prevents the false image of every cell becoming a solid ice crystal. The distribution of water matters more than the rough impression that a frog is hard to the touch.

The same research described changes in organ water content and differences in rehydration after thawing. Some tissues restored their ordinary condition more slowly than others. This makes recovery a sequence rather than one magical instant. An animal may resume some functions while other tissues are still adjusting after the frozen period. A photograph of a moving frog does not reveal all the physiology beneath that movement.

The heartbeat is one part of the experience that attracts attention because it seems to mark the boundary between living and dead. During a controlled freeze, the heart can stop, and ordinary breathing movements may be absent. The frog remains viable if cellular structures and biochemical mechanisms preserve the capacity to restore function. In another organism, the same outward appearance might indicate irreversible injury. The sign has to be interpreted in the context of the species and its specific physiology.

The role of the liver becomes almost architectural. It is an organ that stores resources and releases them when a threat arrives. The blood transports glucose while circulation still permits distribution. Cells alter their water balance as extracellular ice forms. After thawing, the pathways that were temporarily constrained need to restart in the correct relationship. There is no single command center making a plan. The result emerges through evolved regulatory systems whose timing and chemistry can be measured.

The fact that the process can be repeated is not evidence of limitless endurance. Repeated cycles use energy and impose stress. The [2016 paper on repeated freeze–thaw events](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27327184/) investigated Alaskan wood frogs under conditions intended to resemble natural temperature changes. Glucose levels increased in measured tissues across successive cycles, particularly when thawing intervals were short. The response was not necessarily a return to the initial baseline after each event. The organism's prior exposure affected the state in which it encountered the next freeze.

That history is biologically valuable. A frog entering a second freezing episode after only a brief thaw may retain elevated protective compounds from the first. The previous event becomes part of the body's current physiological condition. This is not memory in the conscious sense. It is a measurable persistence of substrates and responses across environmental changes. The timing of winter weather can therefore matter beyond the single coldest temperature reached.

The 2016 researchers also discussed differences between laboratory and natural overwintering conditions. Alaskan wood frogs can encounter prolonged subzero temperatures in their habitats, but interpreting those exposures requires attention to snow, soil, local temperatures and how the animals prepare. A laboratory freezer and an overwintering refuge do not reproduce identical circumstances. The research uses controlled experiments to investigate mechanisms and field observations to determine which conditions matter in nature.

The wood frog's early-spring breeding biology gives the adaptation a reason to exist in a life history. A species that can overwinter near shallow breeding pools may return to reproduction soon after warming, without spending the season in deep water. Temporary pools can be advantageous in some places because they may lack fish predators. But their availability changes with snowmelt, rainfall and climate. Survival through the cold is useful only if the animal can complete the rest of its cycle.

That relationship makes the organism vulnerable in more ways than freezing alone. The protective physiology does not create breeding ponds. It does not guarantee a food supply or shelter from predators. A shift toward unpredictable winter thaws may alter energy use and timing. Reduced snow insulation can expose animals to abrupt temperature changes even where mean winter temperatures rise. A species adapted to seasonal freezing can still face ecological problems under altered seasonal patterns.

The experiment also demonstrates why borrowing nature's solutions is complicated. A medical researcher interested in preserving tissues may study the wood frog's metabolism, but a human organ differs in size, architecture and the ability to tolerate high concentrations of glucose. Delivering cryoprotectants, avoiding toxic effects, controlling ice formation and restoring function are separate technical challenges. A natural example can suggest an approach without establishing a clinical technology.

A particularly dangerous popular claim is that any frog can be frozen and revived if it is warmed gently enough. Different species have different tolerances, and the experimental conditions matter. Trying to reproduce the phenomenon with a captive animal could cause severe injury or death. The wood frog is an extraordinary example of specialization rather than a general property of amphibians.

I would put the original glucose plots in a museum beside a photograph of an apparently frozen frog. Visitors would see the claim in two forms: a startling animal state and a measured physiological mechanism. The plots are less picturesque, but they show why the state can be reversible. The glucose concentration rises during freezing, the body water shifts, and recovery follows under controlled conditions. The natural event becomes more impressive when the measurements constrain it.

The point is not that death is an illusion. The frog can die from cold beyond its tolerance or from other causes. The point is that severe loss of visible activity during a particular frozen state is not necessarily the irreversible end of life for this animal. Biology has evolved mechanisms that widen the range of survivable physical conditions.

That distinction is why the wood frog deserves an entire chapter instead of a line in a collection of amazing animal facts. It changes the meaning of a familiar sign without changing the laws of chemistry. A frog can freeze, its heartbeat can cease, and it can later resume activity because its tissues were protected through a specific physiological sequence.

The winter is not an exception to the frog's life. It is a condition around which that life has been organized.

A [1991 study by Costanzo, Lee and Wright](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1750578/) examined the protective role of glucose under rapid cooling. A [1992 follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1569408/) investigated how cooling rate affected cryoprotectant distribution and organ dehydration. These papers are specific enough to show why the mechanism deserves serious work. The frog does not have one magic antifreeze molecule that solves every problem. Its tissues undergo coordinated physiological changes.

Most people encounter the word cryoprotectant in connection with laboratories and freezing biological materials for storage. In the wood frog, certain substances help reduce the damaging effects of extracellular freezing. Glucose is a familiar molecule because organisms use it as a source of energy. Here a high concentration also contributes to protection during an unusual physical condition. A substance can have different roles depending on its concentration and context.

The frog's liver plays a critical part. Glycogen stored in the liver can be broken down to release glucose, which circulates through tissues. As freezing progresses and circulation changes, the timing of this redistribution matters. A protective substance must reach relevant cells before the physical conditions become damaging. A response that begins too late may be insufficient. A species can survive by evolving a sequence of changes that is closely matched to the cooling patterns it ordinarily encounters.

Water movement is equally significant. When ice forms outside cells, the concentration of dissolved material in the remaining liquid changes. Water can move out of cells in response to osmotic gradients. That dehydration may help avoid the formation of destructive internal ice, but excessive water loss can also be harmful. The animal must manage an uncomfortable compromise between different kinds of injury. The [1992 experimental work](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1569408/) connected cooling rate with organ dehydration and the distribution of protective molecules.

The winter behavior of the species adds another dimension. Wood frogs may overwinter in shallow terrestrial refuges rather than migrating to deep, unfrozen water. That choice exposes them to temperatures at which freezing can occur. Their survival strategy is therefore tied to habitat and seasonal timing. The physiology is not a detached superpower; it has evolved within an ecological setting where the animal must endure a winter and then return to activity in time for the next part of its life cycle.

A [1986 comparative study of freeze tolerance in amphibians](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2870854/) examined several frog and salamander species. Some tolerated extracellular freezing under moderate subzero conditions; others were killed by exposure. This contrast matters because it prevents a popular story about frogs in general. The ability belongs to particular species and physiological systems. One animal's successful freezing strategy does not imply that all amphibians can endure the same treatment.

The phrase stops for winter is a metaphor, but one connected to a real loss of ordinary visible activity. The frozen frog can appear dead. Its heartbeat can be absent from the ordinary perspective of an observer, but the animal's cells have not simply ceased to exist or become irreversibly damaged. Recovery requires that the critical structures and functions remain sufficiently intact through the frozen interval. The apparent absence of life at the scale of a heartbeat is not identical to the permanent loss of biological viability.

The distinction between freezing and preserving is especially important. A successful freeze–thaw cycle in a wood frog depends on its evolved biochemistry and environmental conditions. It does not establish that large mammalian bodies can be frozen and revived by copying the same method. The scale and complexity of organs, the different distribution of water and protective compounds, and the vulnerability of particular tissues produce different engineering problems. A serious comparison with medical cryopreservation would require work specific to the materials being preserved.

Wood frog research has nevertheless influenced scientific interest in cryobiology, the study of life and biological materials at low temperatures. Understanding naturally evolved freeze tolerance can suggest mechanisms worth testing in cells, tissues or preservation systems. The relevance is scientific, not a commercial promise that an immortal human life has already been discovered in a swamp. The animal's physiology provides evidence and hypotheses; translating them into other systems remains a separate task.

A person might imagine a winter forest in which frogs are frozen beneath leaves, then emerge again when the snow melts. That general scene is consistent with the species' biology, but this chapter cannot pretend to have observed a particular frog through a specific winter. The laboratory studies provide controlled evidence of the phenomenon and its physiological responses. Field ecology can establish how the animals use winter habitats. The two kinds of evidence complement one another without making an invented anecdote necessary.

The frogs' ability also shows why a living organism cannot be evaluated through one visible function. Heartbeat is an important sign of activity, but the processes that make survival possible involve cellular structures and chemistry continuing in altered states. The organism's normal performance can be suspended without every part suffering irreversible injury. What matters is whether it can restore integrated activity after the stress passes.

The word pause may be too gentle for the physiology involved. Freezing imposes substantial stress. Cells must withstand changes in water content, oxygen supply, metabolism and physical structure. The organism's responses are costly and highly specialized. An ecological adaptation can allow survival in a dangerous condition without making the condition comfortable or desirable.

There is another surprising consequence. Repeated freezing and thawing are possible under some conditions. The 1987 experiment examined multiple cycles and the accompanying use and restoration of liver glycogen. The ability to recover after one event is impressive; doing so more than once requires a repeatable physiological process. Yet the number of cycles and exposure conditions in an experiment cannot be generalized to an unlimited number of winters or arbitrary temperatures. The evidence remains bounded by the methods.

A winter cold snap could expose the animal to freezing before it has had time to respond fully. Changes in snow cover, winter variability and habitat structure can affect the temperatures it experiences. A frog that evolved freeze tolerance in one climate may still be vulnerable to environmental changes that alter the timing and intensity of freezing. The adaptation solves a particular set of problems. It does not make the species independent of the climate.

The biology is so strange because the frog remains a vertebrate whose ordinary life depends on circulation, metabolism and cellular water. Its protective system uses familiar substances and physiological pathways in an unfamiliar combination. It does not add an entirely new law to nature. It organizes existing chemistry into a response that lets the animal occupy a habitat dangerous to many of its relatives.

If somebody asked whether one could freeze a frog and bring it back, the careful answer would specify the species, degree of freezing and conditions. A wood frog can tolerate extracellular ice and recover under suitable conditions. A pet frog placed in an ordinary freezer might die. The difference is the entire subject. Leaving it out converts a real adaptation into a myth and makes a dangerous experiment look like a harmless trick.

The laboratory result is more satisfying than the myth. A small animal has evolved a reversible response to an environmental condition that would destroy many similar bodies. Its liver supplies glucose. Its tissues change water balance. Its metabolism slows and later resumes. The winter is survived through a sequence of physical and chemical events, not an exemption from them.

The wood frog does not leave the planet to escape the cold. It lets part of the cold inside and survives. That is an astonishing local arrangement between a body and its environment.
