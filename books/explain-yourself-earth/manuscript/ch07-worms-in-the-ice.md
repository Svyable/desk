# Worms in the Ice

There are worms that live in glaciers. Not worms trapped in ice, awaiting an adventurous child to thaw them in a jar. Living worms, able to move on glacier surfaces and spend their lives in that environment. The [United States Geological Survey](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/do-iceworms-exist) answers the question as plainly as anyone could ask it: yes, ice worms exist. They are small annelids found in glaciers in Alaska, Washington, Oregon and British Columbia. They are generally dark in color, can be up to about an inch long, and come to the surface at certain times to feed on snow algae.

I want to begin with the myth because it explains why people sometimes dismiss the animal before they encounter the actual research. There are old stories about enormous glacier worms, monsters fifty feet long or creatures responsible for the blue color of ice. The USGS explicitly rejects those embellishments. The verified animal is tiny. Its habitat and physiology are extraordinary enough without turning it into a missing link between an earthworm and a sea serpent.

The puzzle is thermal. A glacier seems like the last place a soft-bodied animal should choose for permanent residence. Living cells depend on water and chemical reactions that are affected by temperature. Ice formation can damage tissue. Extended exposure to cold can make ordinary biological processes impossible for species not adapted to it. Yet glacier ice worms occupy a habitat close to freezing and have life histories organized around those conditions.

The organisms belong to the genus *Mesenchytraeus*, related to other segmented worms. The species most often discussed in western North America is *Mesenchytraeus solifugus*. The Latin name can be translated in a way that suggests avoiding the sun, an appropriate description for a creature whose surface activity often happens near morning or evening. The animal's behavior is connected to its physiological limits. It can thrive in cold conditions that would seriously stress many other worms, but comparatively high temperatures may be dangerous.

A [2004 biochemical study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14720608/) and a [2025 whole-animal thermal study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15230430.2025.2572150) investigated ice worm metabolism and temperature tolerance under controlled conditions. Researchers have examined how the organisms maintain activity, energy production and cellular function at temperatures near freezing. The key point is not that ice worms violate the need for chemistry. Their physiology is adapted to operating within an unusual part of the temperature range available to life.

The sharpest scientific correction came late in the story. A popular explanation once suggested that ice worms must die immediately if removed from the cold and would be happiest in the lowest temperature a glacier could provide. The [2025 study published in *Arctic, Antarctic, and Alpine Research*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15230430.2025.2572150) tested the limits more directly. The authors found that the animals survived brief exposures to surprisingly warm temperatures, around twenty-six degrees Celsius, but could not tolerate actual freezing of their bodies. The organisms live in glacier ice and snow, yet internal ice formation is lethal to them.

That is a far better paradox than the old story about a worm that instantly dies in a human hand. An ice worm is adapted to living in a narrow range close to the melting point. It can spend its life surrounded by frozen water without freezing internally. The conditions that keep the animal alive involve hydrated spaces and liquid water films, not a magical ability to turn its tissues to solid ice and recover. The same environment can contain both ice and an animal whose body must remain unfrozen.

The difference from the wood frog later in the book is fundamental. A wood frog can survive controlled formation of extracellular ice and recover after thawing under suitable conditions. The ice worm cannot tolerate freezing within its body, according to the new thermal work. Both animals are associated with cold environments, and the mechanisms they use are almost opposite. The worm depends on preventing destructive freezing, while the frog has evolved physiological ways to survive some freezing. A headline about two animals that love the cold would conceal the biological difference.

The [2025 researchers](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15230430.2025.2572150) collected ice worms on Paradise Glacier on Mount Rainier in Washington, with specimens gathered in 2019 and again for metabolic measurements in 2023. They transported the animals on ice and maintained them in cold laboratory conditions. Those methods may seem unnecessarily fussy until one recognizes that the animals' normal environment is close to zero degrees Celsius. The researchers had to control temperature precisely enough to distinguish a short stressful exposure from the conditions in which the species normally lives.

Their experiments examined metabolic rate across a range of temperatures. It increased as temperature rose, with a notable change in the relationship around sixteen degrees Celsius. The result does not mean a glacier worm can move to a warm garden and live normally throughout the year. Short-term survival under a test and sustained successful reproduction in a habitat are different outcomes. The experiment provides measurements of acute tolerance and metabolism, not a license to assume the species is independent of glacial conditions.

This is why the phrase ice worm loves cold is not enough. The species evolved a set of traits for a particular habitat. It can function in temperatures that slow many other worms, yet those same traits may produce limits on long-term performance outside the glacier. A short warm exposure may be survivable even while prolonged warmth remains ecologically harmful. The biological question concerns the actual duration and intensity of stress, and the organism's ability to continue its full life cycle afterward.

There is a biochemical clue to this specialization. In 2004, Michael Napolitano, Robert Nagele and Daniel Shain published [a study of adenylate levels in glacier ice worms](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14720608/). They compared energy-related molecules including ATP, ADP and AMP with those in more temperate relatives. Rather than following the usual expectation that low temperature simply reduces available metabolic energy, the ice worms showed increased adenylate levels as the temperature fell within the studied range. The response offered a potential compensatory mechanism for chemical reactions slowed by cold.

ATP is often called the energy currency of cells because it transfers energy into many biochemical processes. A cell's capacity to perform work depends not only on the amount of ATP present but on how quickly it is used and regenerated, the availability of substrates and the behavior of the relevant enzymes. The study's result was not that the ice worm had invented an entirely new chemical fuel. It was that its energy balance responded to low temperature in a way different from comparison organisms.

The underlying physics is difficult to escape. Chemical reaction rates tend to depend on temperature. A worm moving through near-freezing water must maintain cellular processes even when molecular motion and enzyme behavior are slowed. Accumulating energy-related molecules may help offset some of those constraints. The process can be investigated through measurements rather than inferred from the animal's ability to crawl on ice. The discovery makes a metabolic explanation available where a photograph supplies only an ecological surprise.

A [2018 molecular study of the ATP synthase ATP6 subunit](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6047255/) found an unusual histidine-rich extension in the glacier worm's mitochondrial gene sequence. The researchers proposed that the sequence might affect proton movement and ATP synthesis. They also raised the possibility that the genetic material involved a history of horizontal gene transfer from a microbial source. Those proposals were grounded in sequence analysis but did not prove that one insertion alone accounts for the worm's entire cold adaptation.

This is exactly the kind of finding a popular writer might misuse. A gene with an unusual sequence appears, and the story becomes the secret code that lets ice worms live forever. The actual study identified a feature worth investigating and possible functional consequences. Organismal temperature tolerance depends on many interacting systems. The relationship between one genetic variant and the complete ecological phenotype would require more experimental work.

Another [2022 study of mitochondrial polyphosphate](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9774917/) compared ice worms with other annelids under different forms of stress. The investigators found that glacier worms increased mitochondrial polyphosphate under thermal stress in a pattern unlike some temperate worms. Polyphosphate may participate in cellular stress responses and energy regulation. The result added a different biochemical pathway to the puzzle rather than simply repeating the ATP finding. The animal's unusual metabolism had become a research program with several distinct mechanisms under investigation.

A history of the species itself has also emerged through genetics. The [2012 phylogeographic study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22370043/) sampled dozens of worms from populations across the species' range and examined mitochondrial and nuclear sequences. It found structure among populations and suggested a historical connection to an aquatic ancestor in Alaska, with subsequent movement and separation across regions. This was not a simple tale of one glacier worm walking south along the ice. It was a reconstruction of lineage relationships and possible dispersal based on genetic evidence.

The paper reported a division between northern and southern population groups, with a distribution gap in southern Alaska that may have limited gene flow. Some populations in British Columbia, Washington and Oregon therefore appear connected through an evolutionary history more complicated than a single continuous band of worms across every glacier. This matters because the habitat is discontinuous. Glaciers form separated islands of cold conditions in mountains and coastal regions. A small organism's dispersal opportunities are not unlimited, even when its species can persist over a wide geographic range.

Researchers have also counted worms at surprisingly high densities. The [North Cascade Glacier Climate Project's field account](https://glaciers.nichols.edu/iceworm/) reports common densities around three hundred per square meter in some North Cascades settings, with much higher numbers in particular locations. The estimates depend on when and where researchers count. Because the animals emerge in shade and withdraw from bright conditions, a daytime survey may severely underestimate the population near the surface. The rhythm of the animal's activity becomes part of the measurement method.

A [2001 study of distribution and behavior on Byron Glacier in Alaska](https://doi.org/10.1139/cjz-79-10-1813) found large numbers of worms, described their daily surface emergence and investigated their movements within the glacier. The researchers reported animals using spaces beneath the surface and moving in ways that connect feeding with the timing of light. A glacier that looks empty at midday may host numerous worms emerging several hours later. An observer can miss a substantial living population simply by visiting at the wrong time.

The habitat becomes more surprising when a glacier is understood as a changing porous environment rather than one unbroken solid. Snow grains, ice crystals, meltwater, debris and algae create an intricate surface and near-surface structure. The worms can move through suitable spaces and return to places where food is available. They are not tunneling endlessly through solid clear ice as if equipped with tiny drills. Their movements depend on the actual texture and water content of the habitat.

Climate change then affects more than the thermometer the worms experience. Retreating glaciers reduce the area available for the species to occupy, fragment connections among populations and alter the seasonal structure of snow and ice. A brief warm day may be tolerable to an individual, while the disappearance of the glacier on which an entire population completes its life cycle can eliminate its habitat. This is the distinction between physiological resilience and ecological security.

The [North Cascades glacier monitoring project](https://glaciers.nichols.edu/intro/) describes persistent retreat and disappearance of some smaller ice bodies over recent decades. The measurements refer to particular glaciers, and the precise consequences for individual worm populations require ecological surveys. The broader vulnerability is still clear: a species tied to glacier habitat cannot simply continue its usual life cycle on bare rock after the ice disappears. Its adaptations cannot manufacture the physical environment they depend on.

The recent physiological experiments therefore complicate the popular conservation narrative in a useful way. It is not accurate to say an ice worm will instantly die whenever the air reaches a temperature comfortable to a human. It is accurate that the species depends on a much narrower ecological system than an ordinary soil worm. Survival during a short laboratory test and persistence of a population over generations are entirely different measurements. The most meaningful risk is not one hot afternoon but the loss of permanent ice and associated food-web conditions.

I would make the museum exhibit difficult for the visitor in a particular way. Place one worm in a cold controlled habitat with liquid water, then show the result of a study demonstrating that it cannot survive internal freezing. Follow with the graph of ATP and polyphosphate behavior under temperature changes. The visitor would have to abandon the simple idea that cold adaptation means being frozen. The body is surviving *near* ice, not surviving as ice.

The story begins with a government FAQ confirming that tiny worms really live in glaciers. It becomes more interesting when the biochemical and ecological evidence is added. These animals have specialized energy responses, a complicated dispersal history and a habitat that is shrinking in parts of their range. Their name sounds like a myth. Their limits are precise enough to study.

An ice worm is living proof that a glacier can be an ecosystem. It is also proof that the word ice is not a sufficient description of the temperature and water conditions inside an animal's cells. The organism survives because those details are compatible, not because it has found a way to ignore them.

Ice worms often emerge at the surface of glaciers when sunlight is lower. The surface can provide food in the form of algae or other organic material carried onto the ice. At other times the worms can occupy spaces within the ice or snow. The apparent uniformity of a glacier hides a microenvironment with liquid films, grains, channels, microbes and organic particles. A glacier is not a sterilized block of glass.

The relationship with snow algae is particularly good because it makes a habitat that looks empty into a small food web. Algae can grow on snow and ice when conditions permit, using light and nutrients. An ice worm feeding on them transfers that biological production into animal tissue. Other organisms and environmental processes may contribute. The entire system exists in a place that a visitor might regard as hostile to all life. The judgment is reasonable from the standpoint of a person without cold-weather equipment and wrong from the standpoint of the specialized organism.

Scientists have studied how ice worms respond to temperature. The [USGS FAQ](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/do-iceworms-exist) emphasizes the restricted geographical distribution and the fact that the worms do not appear to exist on glaciers in every cold part of the world. That caution matters. It would be easy to say that glaciers everywhere are full of worms. The documented range of particular species is limited, and differences among glacial environments can affect which organisms survive. The existence of one highly specialized lineage does not make the entire cryosphere a single habitat.

The animal's ability to live on or in ice also does not mean it is comfortable at any freezing temperature. Extreme cold can slow chemical reactions and damage cells; freezing and thawing conditions differ from a stable near-zero environment. Organisms can survive through particular biochemical adaptations without being invulnerable. Their activity depends on narrow physical circumstances, and the very adaptations that enable life in cold conditions can create vulnerability when the habitat warms.

Glaciers are moving environments. Ice flows downhill over time, crevasses open, surface melt changes seasonally and snowfall buries or exposes layers. An organism living there must contend with these processes. The worms are not stationary decorations embedded in a frozen crystal. They move, feed, reproduce and respond to changing microconditions. Their ecology is a living process within a geological one.

There is an interesting problem in collecting the animals for research. Removing a cold-adapted organism from a glacier changes its environment immediately. A container warmed by a person's hand, exposure to sun, or the time required for transport can alter the animal's condition. Experiments need temperature control and a clear account of handling effects. An investigator who finds a worm inactive after an overheated journey may have measured damage caused by the sampling method rather than normal behavior on the glacier.

That difficulty belongs to a larger class of field sciences where the instrument changes the thing observed. A deep-sea animal may be damaged by pressure changes during retrieval. A microbe taken from a specialized chemical environment may behave differently in a laboratory. An ice worm that dies at a temperature comfortable to the researcher is a warning that human convenience cannot define the experimental conditions. The point is methodological, but it emerges directly from the animal's body.

The [USGS educational account of glacier life](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/climate-research-and-development-program/science/glacier-and-ice-research) places these organisms within the broader study of ice environments. Glaciers store water, influence ecosystems downstream and change in response to climate. A small creature that depends on permanent ice may face habitat loss as the climate warms. The worm's existence is therefore relevant not only to curiosity about extreme life but also to conservation and the biological consequences of changing glaciers.

It is important not to turn the creature into a sentimental symbol without understanding the evidence. A photograph of an ice worm on a shrinking glacier does not prove that one particular animal has suffered because of a measured climate trend. The broader pattern of glacier retreat in many regions is well established, but population changes in ice worms require field surveys, taxonomic work and knowledge of how the species moves among habitats. The conservation concern is real. Its exact scale should be investigated rather than inferred from one image.

The life cycle also complicates a popular picture of adaptation. An ice worm has not decided to specialize in glacial habitat. Its lineage has evolved traits compatible with particular conditions over many generations. Those traits may make the organism unusually effective within its niche and unusually vulnerable outside it. A temperature that seems barely tolerable to another worm can be close to ideal for the ice worm. The word extreme often describes the observer's position more than the organism's own.

The public may be surprised that a glacier supports algae, worms and microbial communities because school illustrations commonly show glaciers as uniform blue-white masses. Real ice interacts with dust, minerals, organic debris, surface water and living organisms. Its surfaces and internal spaces contain gradients of temperature, light and chemistry. A glacier can be both an immense geological feature and a mosaic of local ecological conditions. The scale of the surroundings does not eliminate small habitats.

This also changes how one thinks about lifeless landscapes on other worlds. Glacial environments on Earth offer analogues for some problems of survival under low temperatures and limited resources, but the existence of an ice worm does not show that complex animals inhabit icy moons. The chemistry, pressures, energy sources and histories differ. The defensible claim is that terrestrial life has adapted to certain cold, icy niches that many people would initially regard as uninhabitable. That alone broadens the imagination without turning analogy into discovery.

There is something particularly satisfying about the government's tone. The USGS FAQ does not try to sell the animal as a miracle. It answers a slightly incredulous question, explains the worm's size and range, and gently disposes of the myths. The account is useful because it is not trying to prove a general thesis about nature. It is describing a specific creature in a specific habitat.

An ice worm is therefore a challenge to two intuitions: that a glacier is merely frozen water, and that extreme cold is always a barrier to active animal life. The real ecosystem depends on more than temperature alone. The worm needs food, liquid water at appropriate scales and conditions compatible with its physiology. None of these are waived by the romance of living on ice.

If I held one in my palm, it would probably be a mistake for the worm, and the question would be whether it could survive my body heat. That observation is hypothetical; I have not held one. The useful fact is that the animal has a temperature problem opposite to the one most people associate with cold wilderness. A glacier can be home. A warm hand can be hazardous.

The species is small enough that a person can walk over a glacier without noticing the life beneath or beside them. Its story is larger than its body because it asks what counts as habitable in a physical environment that seems designed to stop ordinary biological activity.

The ice does not need to melt before the worm can live. The worm lives there now.
