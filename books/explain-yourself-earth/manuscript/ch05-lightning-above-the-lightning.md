# Lightning Above the Lightning

For a long time people reported seeing brief, strange flashes above thunderstorms. Pilots sometimes described the light as red. The reports were difficult to verify because the events lasted only milliseconds and occurred high above clouds, where a person on the ground rarely had a clear view. In 1989 a low-light camera at the University of Minnesota recorded a pair of luminous shapes above a distant storm. The instrument had been set up for a different research purpose. It accidentally captured the first credible photographs of what scientists now call red sprites.

The [NASA history of its Spritacular citizen-science project](https://www.nasa.gov/general/spritacular-nasas-new-citizen-science-project-to-capture-elusive-upper-atmospheric-electrical-phenomena-on-camera/) describes the sequence. The original camera was being tested for a rocket-related project when the flashes appeared. Once the record existed, an oddity that had previously depended on fleeting witness reports could be investigated systematically. The phenomenon did not wait for an apparatus designed to study it. A camera happened to be pointed in the right direction at the right time.

The sprites appear high above thunderstorms, in a region of the atmosphere normally omitted from the familiar picture of lightning. A thundercloud produces bright electrical discharges at lower altitudes. Certain energetic events can be associated with changes in the electrical conditions above the storm. A red sprite can extend upward in branching or jellyfish-like shapes for tens of kilometers, but it lasts so briefly that ordinary visual observation is difficult. The color and form are real. The names were supplied by scientists who knew they were describing something that sounded implausible.

The old theoretical prediction makes the accident of 1989 particularly satisfying. Decades before anyone obtained a convincing photograph, the Scottish physicist C. T. R. Wilson had considered the electrical effects of thunderstorms in the atmosphere above them. Wilson was investigating the behavior of atmospheric electricity and the conditions under which high-altitude discharges might occur. His work did not give the public a clear picture of a red jellyfish-shaped light, but it established that the electrical environment above a storm deserved attention.

The [2001 review by Earle Williams at MIT Lincoln Laboratory](https://www.ll.mit.edu/r-d/publications/sprites-elves-and-glow-discharge-tubes) traces the field from Wilson's early prediction through reports by pilots and the eventual camera observations. The interval was not caused by physicists being unable to imagine any mechanism. The problem was that the relevant event was brief, high above storms and poorly suited to the normal instruments and viewing angles of weather observation. A theoretical possibility can remain without persuasive observational support for many years.

The first widely accepted camera record emerged through an experiment that was not primarily designed to hunt sprites. The University of Minnesota team was preparing optical instruments for a rocket flight. Robert Franz tested a low-light camera while John Winckler and colleagues worked on related sensors. The camera recorded two luminous structures above a distant thunderstorm on the night of July 5–6, 1989. Some later summaries give July 5 and others July 6 because the exposure occurred near the change of date or because accounts use different descriptions of the observing night. The important historical result is the independently captured image.

The [NASA technical history](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19980020650) describes the observation at O'Brien Observatory near Minneapolis and the subsequent scientific work. The camera was not looking at an object on the ground, a reflection in a window or an ordinary cloud illuminated by lightning from below. It recorded a brief structure in clear air over a distant thunderstorm, at an altitude much greater than most of the familiar lightning channel. The instrument provided something the pilot reports could not: a recording that other scientists could examine after the event.

Even then, an image could be misunderstood. The distance to the storm, the camera's field of view and the ambiguity of a two-dimensional photograph made it difficult to determine precisely where the luminous event occurred in three dimensions. Additional observations from different positions, timing with lightning measurements and color imagery were needed to characterize the phenomenon. A camera opened the field. It did not immediately provide every quantity required for a physical model.

The space shuttle contributed surprisingly quickly. The [NASA Earth Observatory history](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/elusive-sprite-captured-from-the-international-space-station-78487/) notes that Otha Vaughan and colleagues examined video from shuttle flight STS-34 in October 1989 and recorded additional evidence of unusual high-altitude flashes. This changed the observing perspective. From the shuttle, the researchers could look toward and above thunderstorms rather than trying to peer through clouds from beneath them.

By 1993, researchers were conducting deliberate field campaigns. Walter Lyons set up instruments at Yucca Ridge near the Rocky Mountains, while Davis Sentman and collaborators pursued airborne observations above the Great Plains. The [MIT Lincoln Laboratory retrospective](https://www.ll.mit.edu/r-d/publications/sprites-elves-and-glow-discharge-tubes) describes these efforts as early systematic studies of the phenomenon. The work moved from isolated sightings to a repeated pattern involving storms, altitude, shape and electromagnetic activity.

The 1994 color images gave the phenomenon one of its most memorable features. Red light dominated portions of the luminous structures, while other colors could appear in different regions. Color is not merely decorative evidence. It can provide information about which atmospheric molecules are excited and under what physical conditions. A photograph showing red tendrils has a physical explanation in the emission of light by nitrogen and other atmospheric constituents, not in a glowing object made of red material.

Some sprites look like columns or carrots. Others spread into branching forms that observers compare with jellyfish. The shapes can extend tens of kilometers and remain visible for only milliseconds. The [NASA Spritacular project](https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/spritacular/) explains that these forms are part of a family of transient luminous events with different observed geometries. Their diversity is useful because a model must explain why different electrical conditions produce different structures, not simply why a bright flash occurs somewhere above a storm.

Sprites are associated with some powerful cloud-to-ground lightning events, particularly those involving major charge transfer. A rapid change in the electrical structure of a storm can produce a field in the upper atmosphere strong enough to accelerate electrons and excite atmospheric gases. The light that reaches a camera is the visible consequence of those interactions. The whole process can develop and fade much faster than a person can consciously follow without an instrument.

It is possible for an observer to connect two flashes in time and assume one caused the other. A stronger scientific study compares the timing, polarity and electrical characteristics of the underlying lightning with high-speed images of the sprite. The causal relationship becomes convincing when repeated events display a systematic connection and when models reproduce the relevant conditions. A single beautiful photograph can demonstrate that something occurred; it rarely establishes the complete sequence of electrical events.

The altitude makes this more than a peculiar feature of local weather. Many sprites occur in the mesosphere, a region above much of the atmosphere in which ordinary weather develops but below the conventional edge of space. A thunderstorm is therefore capable of producing electrical and optical effects in an environment far removed from the cloud tops. The upper and lower atmosphere are coupled through electromagnetic processes that are invisible in a school illustration of a rain cloud.

The [NASA Spritacular overview](https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/spritacular/) also explains why observers continue to submit images. Even with satellites and research aircraft, coverage is sparse relative to the number of thunderstorms on Earth. A specialized camera operated by a citizen scientist can capture a rare event at a useful time and location. Researchers can compare submissions with lightning networks and meteorological data. An observation made outside a university laboratory can become part of a scientific record if its timing and provenance are sufficiently clear.

The citizen-science project also has a classification problem. A faint vertical streak in a nighttime photograph could be a camera artifact, a distant aircraft, an ordinary lightning discharge, a meteor or another light source. Identifying a sprite requires checking context and distinguishing features. The project does not become valuable because every submission must be extraordinary. It becomes valuable when the network of observations is large enough and careful enough to separate genuine events from mistakes.

I would not romanticize the early pilot reports as proof that scientists were maliciously ignoring the truth. A brief flash in a difficult observing environment is genuinely hard to evaluate. Other phenomena can produce visual impressions that resemble upper-atmospheric light. Scientists were justified in asking for records with sufficient spatial and temporal detail. The problem was partly that the right observing instruments had not been pointed in the right direction. Once they were, the field expanded rapidly.

There is a lesson about the value of accidental data in the first camera record. The instrument was not built to discover a new category of atmospheric light that night. But it had sufficient sensitivity to record one. The scientific response was to preserve the observation, compare it with possible alternatives and design later studies that could deliberately reproduce the circumstances of observation. Luck supplied an opening; methodological work turned it into a subject.

The Space Station cameras supplied a new kind of public image. An astronaut can photograph the curved Earth, clouds and a brief red structure appearing above a thunderstorm. The scale is hard to reconcile. The sprite is fleeting, while the surrounding cloud system may span hundreds of kilometers. The photograph can make the luminous structure look like a giant organism floating over the planet. In reality the form is an electrical discharge whose shape emerges from the physics of charged particles and gas.

The images are compelling enough to inspire claims about unidentified atmospheric beings or mysterious weapons. There is no need for either interpretation. A class of upper-atmospheric discharge has been studied for decades through optics, electromagnetism and meteorology. The remaining research questions concern details of occurrence, energy, morphology, chemical effects and connections with storms. Genuine uncertainty about one feature does not make the whole event unexplained in the supernatural sense.

There is a second historical surprise in Wilson's prediction. A physicist could derive the possibility from known principles and still wait almost seventy years for an accidental image to persuade a wider scientific audience. The atmosphere did not change its laws during that interval. The limiting factor was observation. Science sometimes advances by adding a new instrument to an old question rather than by inventing a new theory.

I would place the low-resolution 1989 image beside the later color photographs and the high-speed instrument data. The first looks almost disappointing. Two luminous blobs are hardly a dramatic picture of the fantastic structures now associated with sprites. But those small marks are historically more important than many spectacular later photographs because they helped change the status of the phenomenon from difficult report to recorded event.

The shape of the chapter should follow that progression: prediction, testimony, accidental detection, deliberate observation and physical analysis. Every stage makes a different claim possible. None requires a narrator to assert that nature had been hiding the truth from humanity. The problem was one of distance, brief duration and the limited vantage points available to observers.

The atmosphere can discharge electricity upward as well as through the familiar region beneath the clouds. A lightning event can affect gas tens of kilometers above its source. The resulting light may be red, branching and far too brief for an ordinary camera. The person watching from a field or an airplane sees part of a complicated physical system. The instrument reveals another.

It is unusual to find a genuine natural phenomenon whose public name sounds like a practical joke. Red sprites have earned theirs through images and measurements. The light has no opinion about the name, and its existence is not in doubt.

The [NASA Earth Observatory photographs from August 2015](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/red-sprites-above-the-us-and-central-america-86463/) show sprites recorded by astronauts aboard the International Space Station. In one image the red tendrils reach toward the upper atmosphere above a storm near the central United States. The astronauts were in orbit, looking across an enormous portion of the planet. What appears like an elaborate electric sculpture above the clouds occupied only a fraction of a second.

There is something strange about seeing familiar weather from the wrong direction. From the ground, lightning appears as a brilliant line running through clouds or to the surface. From space, the full vertical structure of the storm system becomes visible, including the upper region where transient luminous events occur. The usual storm narrative is incomplete partly because our position as observers is below it. The phenomenon belonged to the atmosphere long before people had a convenient way to watch from above.

Sprites are not the only objects in this remarkable family. Researchers use the term transient luminous events to describe several forms of short-lived upper-atmospheric electrical activity. Some are called blue jets, gigantic jets, halos or ELVES. The names sound less like a scientific classification than the cast of a fantasy novel, but they refer to different observed phenomena with distinct geometries, altitudes and physical mechanisms. A [NASA account from 2022](https://www.nasa.gov/general/spritacular-nasas-new-citizen-science-project-to-capture-elusive-upper-atmospheric-electrical-phenomena-on-camera/) describes how public photographs can help expand the observational record.

The speed creates a difficult experimental problem. A normal video camera may record frames too slowly or with too little light sensitivity to capture a sprite clearly. An ordinary observer can blink and miss an event entirely. Specialized cameras, sensors and coordinated observations allow researchers to connect a luminous shape with electrical activity in a storm below it. The goal is not simply to prove that a red object appeared. It is to establish when and where the event occurred, which storm caused it and which electrical processes were involved.

The physical mechanism concerns electrical discharges at high altitude. Sprites are associated with some powerful lightning events, often those transferring significant electrical charge within a thunderstorm system. The atmosphere above the storm can respond to a rapid change in the electric field. A brief discharge can cause gases to emit light under conditions different from those near the ground. The red appearance reflects properties of atmospheric molecules and the excitation process. It is not a small fire burning over the cloud.

The [NASA image analysis](https://eol.jsc.nasa.gov/Collections/EarthObservatory/articles/RedSprites.htm) describes sprites as a kind of cold plasma phenomenon rather than ordinary hot lightning. This distinction is useful because a visually similar flash can be produced through different physical conditions. A person who sees a bright red structure should not infer a conventional lightning channel heated to the same temperatures as the familiar white bolt. The event occurs in a different part of the atmosphere and operates through different discharge dynamics.

An old description of sprites might have been dismissed as a hallucination or a trick of the eye. That response was not completely irrational. Brief lights at night can have many causes, including reflection, optical effects and the behavior of the observer. A competent scientist wants evidence sufficient to distinguish the extraordinary claim from the ordinary alternatives. What changed in 1989 was the availability of a recorded event and the development of techniques to confirm later observations. The right conclusion was not that all earlier pilots had been correct about every detail. It was that the atmosphere really did contain a class of luminous events they could have been seeing.

There is a slight irony in the dependence on technology. A species can travel thousands of kilometers in aircraft, build an international network of weather stations and place satellites in orbit, while still discover a common feature of thunderstorms only when low-light cameras happen to catch it. The discovery is recent not because the phenomenon is new, but because certain observations were historically difficult. A system can be visible in principle and still remain outside routine scientific knowledge for a long time.

Modern instruments mounted on the International Space Station, including the Atmosphere-Space Interactions Monitor, collect data on high-energy processes associated with thunderstorms and transient luminous events. A [NASA overview of red sprites photographed in June 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/a-midsummer-red-sprite-seen-from-space/) describes how orbiting cameras, photometers and related sensors can contribute. The result is a connection among atmospheric electricity, high-energy radiation and the visible forms that first caught public attention.

The visual drama is almost unhelpful because it encourages supernatural analogies. A sprite may resemble an inverted jellyfish with long tentacles descending toward a cloud. A well-framed photograph can make it look like a giant living object above the weather. That image is real as a record of light. It is not evidence of a biological creature. The category error arises when a person assigns the familiar shape to the unfamiliar cause.

A better story follows the field of observation. First, there were brief reports from people who saw lights in conditions difficult to check. Then a camera recorded a phenomenon nobody intended to film that night. Researchers developed techniques for observing it and related events, including from space. The category grew as different luminous processes were distinguished. The names became theatrical, but the methods became increasingly quantitative.

There is still scientific work to do. Particular sprite forms, their connection to different lightning discharges, and their contribution to the electrical and chemical environment of the upper atmosphere continue to be studied. A photograph confirms appearance and location; it does not by itself give the complete sequence of charges and fields that produced the event. A model can explain important features while leaving questions about rare forms or unusual conditions.

The existence of sprites also changes the ordinary geography of thunderstorms. The visible storm may occupy a few kilometers of cloud thickness, while its electrical consequences reach much higher. A local weather event can have a physical signature in a region many people associate with the boundary between atmosphere and space. The Earth is not built from neatly separated layers whose activities are independent of one another.

A storm changes air pressure, moves heat and moisture, produces lightning and affects the electrical environment above it. A person standing below sees a thundercloud and rain. A camera on the space station may see the brief red shape above the cloud. Both are seeing aspects of one system. The different vantage points create different stories unless the evidence is combined.

The names sprites, jets and elves have done the field a small favor. Scientists who work on difficult phenomena sometimes choose memorable terms that make the subject easier to discuss. The risk is that a term borrowed from fantasy can lead the public to assume the underlying observation is fanciful too. Here the whimsical language follows the evidence, not the other way around.

The red sprite has a particular beauty. It appears and disappears faster than many people can register, sometimes with a structure extending tens of kilometers. The instrument captures it because the atmosphere emits actual photons in a measurable pattern. There is no need to turn the event into a message or a supernatural sign. It is electrical weather occurring in a part of the sky that ordinary storms never taught most of us to look at.

The pilots had reported lights for years. The first good image arrived by accident. Subsequent images arrived by design. The Earth had been holding its own brief fireworks above the clouds, and most of the species living beneath them had not noticed.
