# EXPLAIN YOURSELF, EARTH

*Twenty True Stories From a Planet That Will Not Behave*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

## A Note to the Planet

I have a small complaint about the Earth. It is presented to us as the dependable part of the arrangement. Human beings are allowed to have impractical ideas, governments are free to make expensive mistakes, and engineers may spend their lives building devices that nobody wants. But the ground is supposed to stay put. Water should behave like water. A frog ought to require functioning circulation. A plant should not count how often something touches it. The planet should, within reason, observe the conventions.

It does not.

A rock in Death Valley can move across a desert floor without a person pushing it. The trick is ice, water and a certain kind of wind, but it took researchers years to see the movement happen. Under an Antarctic glacier, saltwater escapes and turns the ice red. Dunes can produce a low sustained note. A form of lightning makes red shapes above thunderstorms where few people thought to point a camera. A fish in the Nevada desert lives in one hot, oxygen-poor cavern, and a great deal of American water law has had to make room for it.

None of this needs a supernatural explanation. That is what makes it so rude. I would be less troubled by a ghost moving the stones than by a few millimeters of ice managing the job, given the right weather. The ghost would at least have a motive.

This book is not a collection of the twenty most unusual things ever seen. I have no way of knowing what that list would include. It is a selection of particularly good documented cases where an observation forces a person to revise an ordinary assumption, and where the scientific work is interesting enough to follow after the surprise has worn off. Each chapter begins with a fact, experiment, specimen or field record. The explanations are part of the pleasure, not the price of admission.

There will be limits. A glass frog concealing red blood cells while asleep does not solve the problem of human blood clotting. A sea slug's severed head regenerating a body does not mean a human head can do the same. A giant virus does not prove that viruses are secretly bacteria. The scientific literature is full of distinctions that spoil a good headline and rescue a much better question.

The world is allowed to be stranger than our first description of it. The writer is not allowed to claim more than the evidence shows. That seems a fair trade.

I have not watched the Death Valley rocks move, descended into the mine where a bacterium was collected, or handled a living glacier worm. Where the record belongs to researchers and field workers, the credit and the evidence belong there too. I am interested in the distance between what they saw, what they could explain, and what the rest of us have chosen to repeat.

It is a long distance. Sometimes it contains an entire ocean glowing in the dark.
