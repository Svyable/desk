# 1 — The Wrong Question

There is a sentence that can ruin an investigation before it begins.

**Is it real?**

It sounds rigorous. It sounds like the adult question. It is often neither.

In 1995 the Central Intelligence Agency inherited a peculiar problem from the Defense Intelligence Agency: a government program that had spent years asking people to describe distant or hidden targets without ordinary sensory access. The vocabulary shifted over the decades. The offices shifted. The sponsors shifted. The paperwork accumulated. The underlying proposition remained indecently simple.

A person sits here.

The target is somewhere else.

Tell me something true about it.

That proposition is so clean that almost every later argument manages to make it dirtier.

Believers ask whether the program proved psychic functioning. Skeptics ask how intelligent adults could have spent government money on it. Both questions are irresistible because both turn a difficult measurement problem into an identity test. You are either the sort of person who thinks minds can reach across space or the sort who knows better.

The documents are less cooperative.

The final evaluation commissioned by CIA produced one of those bureaucratic artifacts that deserves to be read slowly because it refuses to collapse into the story everybody wants. Statistician Jessica Utts looked at the experimental record and concluded that anomalous effects had been demonstrated strongly enough that chance was not an adequate explanation. Psychologist Ray Hyman did not accept that inference. He argued that the result was premature without stronger independent replication and tighter exclusion of methodological artifacts.

That disagreement is usually where the story gets sold.

Government scientists admit psychic powers are real.

Or:

CIA debunks psychic spying.

Neither is the interesting part.

The interesting part is what happened when the phenomenon had to go to work.

The government did not need a metaphysics seminar. It needed intelligence. Somewhere there was a hostage, a submarine, a facility, a weapon, a person, a room. The customer did not need a beautiful p-value. The customer needed to know whether to move an aircraft, redirect a satellite, send a team, search one building instead of another, trust one source instead of another.

The anomaly had to choose.

That is where things became ugly.

A declassified 1995 operational summary says that from 1986 through the first quarter of fiscal 1995 the program received more than two hundred tasks from operational military organizations. By 1 May 1995, three viewers had produced material for forty tasks from five operational organizations under a newer numerical evaluation scheme. The resulting products generated ninety-nine accuracy scores and one hundred value scores.

Notice the nouns.

Tasks. Products. Scores. Value.

This is no longer a séance. It is a system.

And systems have denominators.

If one session contains a startling correspondence, what happened in the other sessions? If a viewer says `large structure`, how many possible targets contain a large structure? If the report includes twenty descriptive fragments and three appear correct after the target is known, what happened to the other seventeen? If an analyst recognizes a match, was the matching rule written down before the target was revealed? If several viewers contribute overlapping fragments, who decides which fragments become the product? If the customer already knows half the target, does the remote-viewing material add information or merely decorate information already in the room?

The operational question is cruel because it removes the privilege of being interesting.

A weak signal can be real and useless.

That sentence should bother both camps.

Skeptics sometimes behave as though operational failure proves that every laboratory deviation must have been fraud, leakage or statistical error. It does not. A phenomenon can fail engineering long before its underlying scientific status is settled. Plenty of measurable effects are too weak, unstable, expensive, slow or context-dependent to become useful instruments.

Believers make the opposite mistake. If an aggregate experimental result appears unlikely under chance, they allow the statistical anomaly to inherit capabilities it has never demonstrated. A deviation becomes information. Information becomes perception. Perception becomes remote viewing. Remote viewing becomes intelligence collection. Intelligence collection becomes a successful spy program.

That ladder has missing rungs.

The missing rungs are this book.

## The difference between information and a decision

Suppose I build a machine that predicts a hidden binary target correctly 51 percent of the time.

If the experiment is enormous, clean and preregistered, 51 percent may be scientifically astonishing. It might force us to investigate the apparatus, the randomization, the analysis pipeline and, eventually, the physics.

Would you use it to decide whether to raid a house?

No.

Now suppose the machine is correct 60 percent of the time but does not tell you when it is likely to be wrong.

Would you launch a missile from it?

Still no.

Now suppose it produces paragraphs rather than binary choices. The paragraphs contain sensory adjectives, shapes, temperatures, textures and impressions. After the target is revealed, a human judge can often find correspondences.

What is the machine's accuracy?

That question no longer has an innocent answer.

The judge has entered the instrument.

This is the part of remote viewing that matters to me. Not because it resolves psi. It doesn't. It matters because it is an unusually naked version of a problem that appears everywhere people encounter weak, ambiguous or anomalous information.

The signal does not walk into the decision by itself.

Somebody carries it.

They select. Weight. Match. Ignore. Translate. Combine. Remember. Forget. Sometimes those operations are legitimate. Sometimes they are the entire effect. Often nobody can tell afterward because the decision rule was never frozen before the answer arrived.

The more extraordinary the proposed channel, the more seductive the retrospective fit becomes.

A drawing looks vaguely like a crane after you learn the target was a dockyard. A phrase about `metal, movement, cold` acquires meaning when somebody tells you it was a submarine. The human visual and linguistic system is magnificent at completing patterns. This is usually a feature. In anomaly research it can become a counterfeit sensor.

The cure is not cynicism.

The cure is to make the anomaly choose before you tell it what the choices mean.

## Raw intelligence

One declassified remote-viewing session from March 1980 concerns the hostage crisis at the U.S. Embassy in Tehran. The document is stamped with the bureaucratic seriousness of the period and describes the viewer's impressions as `raw intelligence data`. The phrase is perfect.

Raw intelligence is allowed to be wrong. Conventional sources are wrong all the time. Human agents misunderstand. Radar generates clutter. Intercepts are ambiguous. Satellite images need interpretation. A technology does not have to be infallible to be useful.

But conventional intelligence disciplines have spent enormous effort learning how to characterize those failures. Sensor resolution. Source reliability. Circular reporting. False alarms. Deception. Confidence. Access. Placement. Collection bias. Analysts are not supposed to ask whether a source sometimes says something true. They ask what kind of source it is, what access it could plausibly possess, how often it has been right, how independently the report was obtained and what decision changes if the report is believed.

Remote viewing entered an institution that already knew this.

That makes the long life of the program more interesting, not less.

It is tempting to say the government funded it because Cold War agencies were credulous. That explanation is too cheap. Intelligence organizations also investigate low-probability threats because the cost of ignoring an adversary capability can be enormous. If Soviet services appeared interested in psychotronics or anomalous cognition, an American program could be rational as threat assessment even if the underlying phenomenon ultimately failed. A program's existence proves interest, not efficacy.

That distinction will recur until it becomes annoying.

It should become annoying.

A budget line is evidence that somebody authorized spending. A classified file is evidence that somebody classified a file. A tasking record is evidence that somebody asked a question. None of those facts is proof that the proposed phenomenon worked.

Likewise, termination is not proof that it did not.

The program's bureaucratic life and the phenomenon's scientific life are different variables.

## The split verdict

The 1995 review matters because it refuses the neat ending.

Utts's argument deserves to be represented at full strength. If controlled experiments repeatedly produce target-identification performance that exceeds chance expectations, and if ordinary sensory leakage and known statistical artifacts are adequately controlled, then the result is not made less interesting because the mechanism sounds impossible. Science does not get to reject data because the data have embarrassing friends.

Hyman's objection also deserves its full strength. A statistical departure from chance does not identify its cause. Revolutionary claims should survive independent replication. Experimental systems can carry subtle dependencies their designers do not recognize. A scoring procedure can manufacture confidence. A laboratory tradition can reproduce its own assumptions. The absence of an identified artifact is not evidence that the remaining explanation is paranormal.

Those positions are not symmetrical in every detail, but together they create a useful trap.

Assume, temporarily, that the laboratory anomaly is genuine.

Now what?

This is the move the public argument rarely makes. Grant the strongest disputed premise for a moment. Do not spend the next hundred pages trying to win it. Ask what follows.

Surprisingly little.

If an effect is small, unstable, judge-dependent, target-dependent or impossible to confidence-calibrate on a single trial, it may be useless for intelligence even if it is scientifically profound. A spy service cannot average a thousand hostage crises. It receives this crisis. This building. This Tuesday.

The universe may contain an anomaly that refuses to become a tool.

That possibility is more interesting than either `psychics are real` or `psychics are fake` because it creates a third category we are bad at thinking about: **effects that may deserve scientific attention but do not deserve operational authority.**

I call the distance between those two states the actionability gap.

It can be measured.

How much information arrives per trial? How specific is it? How often does it discriminate the true target from plausible alternatives? Does confidence predict accuracy? Does the effect survive a new laboratory? Does it survive a hostile evaluator? Does it survive when the scoring rule is fixed in advance? Does it survive when the output has to trigger a costly decision rather than impress a judge afterward?

The last question is the one I care about most.

## Which door?

Imagine four identical doors.

Behind one is the target.

The viewer never sees the doors. The experimenter interacting with the viewer does not know which door is correct. The target is chosen after every procedural detail has been frozen. The viewer can return anything they like—drawing, words, numbers, impressions—but before the door is opened the protocol must convert that output into one choice and one confidence score.

No post-hoc matching.

No analyst selecting the most evocative fragment.

No `this part is remarkably similar` after the photograph appears.

Choose.

Then open the door.

Repeat it at another laboratory.

Repeat it with skeptics running the randomization and proponents designing the viewing conditions. Publish the misses with the hits. Freeze the code. Measure whether confidence calibration exists. Put money on the decisions if you want to make the operational problem visible.

This experiment would not answer every philosophical question about consciousness. Good.

It would answer a smaller question brutally well.

Can the anomaly choose?

That is where I want to begin, because twenty-three years of government interest left us with an archive full of stranger things than psychic spies.

It left us with a case study in how easily humans confuse an effect with an instrument.

And we are building instruments out of uncertain effects everywhere now.
