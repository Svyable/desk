# Fifty Experiments

The number that survived the 1966 test phase was 82 percent. More than fifty clouds were seeded over southern Laos; according to the January 1967 State Department review, 82 percent produced rain within a brief period. Defense called the result outstanding. State went further: the experiments were “undeniably successful.”

A percentage that large invites the eye to supply the experiment it wishes had been run. Forty-odd successes out of fifty-something trials feels decisive. But the memorandum gives no untreated comparison group, no cloud-by-cloud table, and no derivation of the baseline behind its claim that 82 percent was “appreciably higher than normal expectation in the absence of seeding.” The statistic records a rain rate after treatment. By itself, it does not tell us how much additional rain the treatment caused.

That distinction was not invented decades later by skeptics of secret programs. It was the central scientific problem of weather modification at the time. In a federal review published just before Popeye, the National Science Foundation’s Special Commission on Weather Modification noted that, nearly twenty years after the first cloud-seeding experiments, conclusive evidence acceptable to the scientific community about effects on ground precipitation was still lacking. The same review did not dismiss seeding. It reported positive statistical findings from a National Academy of Sciences analysis of commercial projects and described a field in which plausible mechanisms, encouraging results, and arguments over experimental proof coexisted.

Popeye entered that argument with a military customer.

The missing record matters because operators do not seed random clouds. They look for clouds that appear physically susceptible to intervention. That is sensible engineering and awkward experimental design. If crews became good at selecting clouds already near the threshold of rain, a high post-seeding rain rate could reflect useful meteorological judgment, a seeding effect, or both. To separate them now would require the material the policy summary omits: selection criteria, cloud conditions, seeding material and quantity, timing, radar or gauge observations, untreated candidates, the rule for declaring a rainfall response, and whatever data produced the “normal expectation” used as comparison.

There is also a counterargument to demanding laboratory neatness from a battlefield test. The government did not need to settle cloud physics for all climates. It needed to decide whether repeated seeding under the weather and terrain conditions of the Lao panhandle was useful enough to continue. The January review was explicit about that narrower claim. Its authors tied their confidence to conditions like those in the test area, and the observations they cared about extended beyond rain: saturated ground, damaged trafficability, and the possibility of keeping roads difficult into the dry season. An operational decision can rationally use evidence that would be insufficient for a general scientific law.

But the narrower standard changes what the 82 percent can prove. It can help explain why officials became confident. It can show that rain repeatedly followed seeding in clouds selected for the experiment. It cannot, from the surviving summary alone, give us the causal increment attributable to seeding or tell us how often equally promising unseeded clouds would have rained.

This becomes more important as the outcome moves downstream. The test could watch a cloud and ask whether rain followed. An operational campaign had to ask whether extra rain softened a particular route, whether the route would otherwise have been passable, whether trucks were delayed, whether engineers repaired the damage, and whether supplies still arrived by another path. Each link added another natural process and another adaptive human system between the aircraft and the military result.

The strongest case for Popeye therefore does not require turning 82 percent into a magic number. The test persuaded senior officials who knew the technique’s limits well enough to worry that the rain might spread beyond the intended area and who were acting in a scientific field that was promising without being settled. That combination is historically more interesting than either caricature: a proven weather weapon on one side, an elaborate rain dance on the other.

The fifty experiments still deserve reconstruction one mission at a time. Until the untreated comparison logic and the “normal expectation” behind the State Department’s sentence are recovered, 82 percent is best read as a historically consequential response rate, not a measured causal effect size.
