# ENMOD

On December 10, 1976, the United Nations General Assembly adopted the Convention on the Prohibition of Military or Any Other Hostile Use of Environmental Modification Techniques. It opened for signature in May 1977 and entered into force in October 1978.

The treaty's existence is sometimes narrated as if the world discovered Operation Popeye, recoiled, and banned weather warfare. The causal history is more complicated. Environmental modification had already become an international scientific and strategic concern. U.S.-Soviet arms-control politics mattered. Congressional pressure mattered. Broader environmental diplomacy mattered. Popeye was an important precedent inside that convergence, not a single sufficient cause.

The distinction is worth defending because simple origin stories are seductive. They turn treaties into moral reactions and erase negotiation. ENMOD emerged from states with their own military interests, scientific ambitions, and definitions of acceptable conduct. A government could oppose hostile environmental modification while preserving peaceful research. It could favor broad principle while arguing over thresholds.

Article I prohibits military or other hostile use of environmental modification techniques having widespread, long-lasting or severe effects as a means of destruction, damage, or injury to another state party. The conjunction is important in ordinary reading: widespread, long-lasting, or severe. The treaty did not simply outlaw any human attempt to influence weather during conflict. It created a thresholded prohibition tied to hostile use and significant effects.

That structure reflects the technical problem. Environmental intervention exists on a spectrum from local fog dispersal to imagined planetary manipulation. A treaty broad enough to prohibit every deliberate atmospheric change could sweep in activities states considered benign or operationally necessary. A treaty too narrow could leave the most dangerous future techniques untouched.

Popeye sits awkwardly against the threshold language. Its intended effect was localized enough to target routes and seasonal enough to focus on trafficability. Whether particular operations would meet ENMOD's later standards is a legal question this manuscript does not resolve by retroactive assertion. The more important point is historical: Popeye demonstrated that hostile environmental modification could exist below the scale of cinematic catastrophe.

That makes the treaty both stronger and stranger. It is stronger because it recognizes the category before technology matures. Stranger because it must govern techniques whose causal effects may themselves be hard to attribute. How does a state prove that unusual rain was deliberately induced? How do inspectors distinguish hostile intervention from natural variability or peaceful activity? A legal prohibition can be clear in principle while verification remains difficult.

The problem resembles arms control only partially. Nuclear tests leave characteristic physical evidence. A cloud-seeding flight can leave rain indistinguishable from rain except through operational records, aircraft observation, material traces, or intelligence. The environment can hide the intervention inside its own normal behavior.

That verification difficulty creates incentives for both secrecy and accusation. A state with a real program may deny it. A state facing damaging weather may allege hostile manipulation without proof. Once capability is known to exist in limited form, natural disasters can acquire political authors.

ENMOD did not solve that epistemic problem. It changed the normative baseline. Hostile manipulation of environmental processes was no longer merely another technological option waiting for better engineering. It became a category states had reason to disavow.

Popeye's legacy belongs partly here: not in proving that weather can be controlled, but in helping make weather warfare something governments would need to deny, constrain, or prohibit.
