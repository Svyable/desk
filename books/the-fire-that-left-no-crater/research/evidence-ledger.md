# Evidence Ledger

## Class 1 — verified / documented

- LDG exists as a widespread silica-rich natural glass field in the Great Sand Sea near the Egypt-Libya border.
- Published ages place formation near 28.5 Ma.
- LDG contains high-temperature phases and textures requiring rapid extreme heating.
- Published work reports shocked quartz in regional bedrock.
- Published zircon work reports microstructures interpreted as reversion from high-pressure reidite.
- Some chemical/isotopic work reports a small meteoritic component in portions of the glass population.
- The central scarab in a Tutankhamun pectoral has been identified as LDG.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete inference

- A crater-forming impact is presently a better integrated explanation than an airburst-only model.
- The parent structure may be deeply eroded, buried, displaced from intuitive search centers, or otherwise difficult to recognize.
- Multiple target lithologies likely contributed to the glass.
- Spatial shock mapping could materially narrow the source region.

## Class 3 — disputed / unconfirmed

- Any specific candidate crater as the demonstrated parent structure.
- Exact projectile type, size, or trajectory.
- Claims that ancient Egyptians understood the glass as impact-produced.
- Strong cultural or ritual interpretations inferred solely from the material’s unusual origin.
- A pure giant-airburst origin that also accounts for the reported tens-of-GPa shock evidence.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis

- LDG may be a model case for a broader class of terrestrial impacts whose topographic craters are lost while mineral-scale shock archives survive.
- Integrated shock-gradient, provenance, geophysical, and ejecta modeling may localize the parent structure without first recognizing crater morphology.
- The known terrestrial impact record may contain a preservation bias stronger than crater catalogs alone imply.
