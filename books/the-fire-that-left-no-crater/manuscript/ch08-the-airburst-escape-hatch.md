# The Airburst Escape Hatch

The airburst hypothesis deserves better than being treated as the losing side of an old debate.

Its attraction came from a real observational problem. Here was a vast field of glass requiring extreme heat and no obvious crater of matching age and scale. Tunguska had already shown that cosmic energy could be deposited catastrophically in the atmosphere without a conventional crater. Numerical work demonstrated that sufficiently energetic low-altitude bursts could expose the surface to intense thermal radiation.

That made airburst a serious hypothesis, not pseudoscience.

The problem is that “can melt” is not the same as “matches the mineral record.” Once high-pressure shock evidence entered the case, a pure thermal airburst had to explain pressures it was not designed to produce. Some modelers explored very large, low, shallow-angle events. The more strongly the event couples to the ground, however, the closer it approaches an impact in its geological effects.

The distinction should therefore be parameterized. Altitude of disruption. Remaining projectile mass. Entry angle. Velocity. Composition. Fragmentation cascade. Ground-coupling pressure. Thermal pulse duration. Melt depth. Excavation. Those variables produce a continuum of outcomes.

This matters beyond one desert. Planetary defense often divides hazards into impactors and airbursts because the consequences differ operationally. Chelyabinsk taught the modern world that an object too small to form a major crater can still break windows across a city. Tunguska demonstrates a larger atmospheric devastation regime. At still greater energies, the boundary between airburst and impact may become physically messy.

Libyan Desert Glass was once invoked as evidence for an enormous craterless airburst. The reidite work pushed back sharply on that interpretation. This is an example of why geological analogs used in risk models need mineralogical auditing. A spectacular deposit should not become a calibration point merely because it resembles the outcome a model can produce.

The airburst hypothesis remains valuable even if wrong because it forced impact researchers to specify what a crater-forming model must explain. Why is the crater missing? How can melt be distributed so broadly? What pressure-temperature trajectories do individual inclusions record? What parts of the target were excavated versus radiatively heated?

A hypothesis can lose and still improve the science. The only embarrassing outcome is when a field protects a category after the discriminating evidence has moved on.
