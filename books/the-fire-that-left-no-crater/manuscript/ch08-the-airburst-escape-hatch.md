# The Airburst Escape Hatch

One way to make Libyan Desert Glass without leaving an obvious crater is to send a 300-meter stony asteroid into the atmosphere at about 35 kilometers per second, almost sideways.

Eight degrees above the horizon, in one published simulation.

That is not a reconstruction of what happened in the Great Sand Sea. It is a useful reminder that “airburst” covers more physical territory than the word suggests. In 2020, Vladimir Svetsov, Valery Shuvalov, and Igor Kosarev modeled stony and cometary impactors with coupled hydrodynamics and radiation transfer, then searched for cases in which a near-surface fireball could melt an area roughly comparable to the Libyan Desert Glass field without producing a substantial crater. Their craterless solutions included that shallow stony entry and several faster cometary cases. In the models, thermal radiation could melt a few centimeters of silica in the asteroidal case and as much as about ten centimeters in some cometary cases, over tens to hundreds of seconds.

So the old airburst argument had real physics behind it. A sufficiently hot fireball near the ground can, in a numerical model, pay a large thermal bill without excavating the kind of hole everyone has been looking for.

Then the rocks became less cooperative.

The shocked bedrock reported in 2019 requires pressures of at least about 16 gigapascals. Zircon textures in the glass interpreted as the aftermath of reidite point to pressures above roughly 30 gigapascals. Thermal radiation does not do that. A fireball can roast a surface; it cannot explain high-pressure crystal transformations merely by shining harder.

The interesting part is what happened next. Svetsov and colleagues did not ignore those results. They wrote them into the problem. The high-pressure evidence, they argued, leaves at least two broad possibilities: a crater-forming impact, or an event in which a projectile breaks up in the atmosphere but some fragments still reach the surface fast enough to shock the target. Their own calculations therefore include both craterless radiative-melting cases and cases where atmospheric disruption, ground contact, shock, and radiation occupy the same event.

At that point, “airburst versus impact” starts to become bad bookkeeping.

A body can fragment violently in the atmosphere, transfer much of its energy to a fireball, strike the ground in pieces, shock limited patches of target rock, and still radiatively heat a much larger surface. The event may be atmospheric in one part of its energy deposition and geological in another. A small or degraded crater does not decide which mechanism made most of the glass.

This is also the strongest counterargument to treating the reidite result as a complete victory for a conventional crater-and-ejecta story. High-pressure minerals show that something struck hard enough to generate impact shock. They do not, by themselves, prove that every yellow-green fragment was produced by shock melting or launched as ordinary ejecta from one preserved bowl. A hybrid model can concede the shock and still ask radiation to do part of the melting.

But the hybrid has to earn that freedom. Other observations push toward substantial ground interaction: shocked local bedrock, meteoritic material mixed into some glass, and high-pressure zircon. Work on the glass has also emphasized that its textures and chemistry do not behave exactly like a simple blanket of surface sand melted in place by distant heat. Moving from a pure airburst to a fragmenting, ground-coupled event may save the missing-crater problem, but each added contact with the ground makes the model look less like Tunguska and more like an impact whose obvious structure has been lost.

That distinction matters because Libyan Desert Glass has sometimes been recruited as a calibration case for enormous craterless airbursts. The deposit is too unsettled for that job. A model showing that radiation can melt the right amount of silica establishes physical plausibility. Shocked bedrock and former reidite establish that the historical event reached a pressure regime the purely radiative version does not explain. Neither result gets to borrow the certainty of the other.

The discriminating evidence is spatial. If radiation made much of the glass while fragment impacts supplied the high-pressure minerals, heat and shock should separate in the landscape in intelligible ways. Glass populations, target chemistry, meteoritic contamination, shocked bedrock, and high-pressure zircon should not all peak for the same reasons or necessarily in the same places. A conventional crater-and-ejecta model makes a different set of spatial and petrographic predictions. The missing crater turns those differences from an inconvenience into the experiment.

The useful question is no longer whether the object exploded in the air or hit the ground. It is how much of the object became atmosphere before the rest became geology.
