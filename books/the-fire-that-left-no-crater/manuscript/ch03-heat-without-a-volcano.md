# Heat Without a Volcano

Before pressure entered the argument, heat dominated it.

The glass contains mineralogical evidence of extraordinary temperatures. Lechatelierite records melting of quartz. Cristobalite and other high-temperature phases constrain thermal histories. Work on inclusions and streaks has inferred rapid heating into roughly the 1,500–1,700 °C regime, followed by rapid cooling. Whatever happened was not a warm geological process patiently cooking silica underground.

That eliminated a great deal of imaginative clutter. Low-temperature sol-gel ideas became increasingly implausible. Ordinary volcanism did not fit the geography or composition. Lightning can make glass, but fulgurites are narrow, branching products of localized electrical discharge; they do not naturally explain a vast field of high-purity silica melt with the observed inclusion record.

The heat problem, however, created the airburst hypothesis. If a cosmic body exploded low enough in the atmosphere, thermal radiation and hot gas might melt surface material over a broad area without excavating a conventional crater. This was attractive because it simultaneously explained the temperature and the missing hole in the ground.

Attractive explanations are dangerous when they solve two embarrassments at once. They become sticky. Researchers begin asking whether each new fact can be made compatible with the model rather than whether the fact discriminates among models.

The thermal record is a good example. Both a crater-forming impact and some extreme airburst scenarios can create enormous heat. Temperature alone is therefore poor evidence for choosing between them. A mineral that only requires high temperature cannot tell us whether the pressure pulse came through rock or atmosphere. To separate those histories we need phase transformations and deformation features whose formation pressures are far above what an atmospheric blast can transmit into ground at scale.

The glass also records short timescales. Partially melted inclusions survive. Chemical heterogeneity remains. Those features imply heating intense enough to liquefy silica without homogenizing every component. The event was violent, fast, and inefficient in the geological sense: it did not hold the target at equilibrium long enough for all minerals to negotiate a common composition.

That matters for reverse reconstruction. If we build a model that produces the right peak temperature but keeps the material hot for too long, it may erase exactly the disequilibrium textures we observe. If the model creates a shallow melt sheet that should incorporate abundant substrate fragments but the glass does not show them, that discrepancy matters. Thermal history is not one number.

A useful catastrophe model has to produce a movie, not a thermometer reading: heating rate, peak temperature, pressure, duration, melt fraction, acceleration, transport, quench rate, and later alteration. The desert glass preserves frames from that movie. The challenge is that no single fragment contains the whole reel.

The absence of a volcano was the first narrowing. The absence of a crater became the next obsession. But the decisive shift came when researchers stopped asking only how hot the glass became and started asking what else happened to the minerals while they were hot.
