# The Crater Problem

A crater is an argument you can see from orbit. Its absence is not the opposite kind of evidence.

For Libyan Desert Glass, the missing source crater has been the central discomfort for decades. Impact glass is easiest to understand when it sits inside or near a recognized impact structure. Here, the glass field is obvious and the parent structure is not. That mismatch encouraged airburst models, remote-source ideas, and repeated searches for candidate craters in Egypt and Libya.

The temptation is binary: crater present means impact; crater absent means airburst. Geology is rarely generous enough to preserve the variable we most want.

A crater can be buried beneath younger sediment, eroded down through its rim and breccia lens, tectonically modified, or misidentified because its remaining geophysical expression is subtle. A highly oblique impact complicates expected symmetry. A source outside the densest modern glass field is possible if ejecta transport and subsequent redistribution are substantial. But each rescue has a cost. A model that moves the crater farther away must explain transport. A model that erases it must explain denudation. A model that buries it predicts subsurface structures detectable by gravity, magnetics, seismic data, drilling, or remote sensing.

This is where “absence of evidence” becomes useful only after an explicit search model exists. How much of the plausible source region has been mapped at what spatial resolution? Which geophysical datasets are available? What crater diameters would remain visible after plausible erosion? Which lithologies would retain shatter cones, breccias, shocked quartz, or structural uplift? If the search is weak, the missing crater tells us little. If the search is strong over a physically justified area, its absence becomes quantitative evidence.

The 2019 report of shocked quartz in bedrock within the LDG region changed the balance because bedrock cannot be transported as casually as loose grains. The authors interpreted planar deformation features and related structures as evidence of shock pressures of at least about 16 GPa and argued for a physical impact with a crater that was later largely eroded. That does not hand us coordinates. It shifts the burden.

The same year, high-pressure evidence from zircon in the glass strengthened the case that the event involved pressures characteristic of impact shock rather than a purely atmospheric blast. Again, this did not reveal the crater. Instead it made the missing crater more interesting.

A weak mystery asks why the crater is absent. A stronger one asks what geological history is required for a crater-forming event to leave widespread glass, shocked minerals, and shocked bedrock while losing its obvious topographic structure.

That is a solvable class of question because it generates subsidiary predictions. Erosion should expose deeper target rocks. Shock intensity should vary spatially. Buried structural remnants may produce geophysical anomalies. Zircon populations may preserve pressure gradients. The glass distribution may be offset from the impact center in ways that encode trajectory.

The hole in the ground is no longer the only place to look.
