# Tektites and Other Orphans

Libyan Desert Glass is not the only impact product whose parentage has been awkward.

Tektites are natural glasses distributed in strewn fields, generally understood as terrestrial target material melted and ejected during hypervelocity impacts. Several fields have secure or strongly supported source craters. Others have spent long periods as geological orphans.

The Australasian tektite strewn field is the great comparison. It is enormous, young by geological standards, and despite decades of work its source crater has remained disputed. The existence of abundant distal impact glass without an agreed crater demonstrates that crater absence is not unique to LDG.

That does not mean every orphan glass shares one mechanism. Tektites have characteristic aerodynamic shapes, chemistry, distribution, and formation histories. LDG differs in important ways. Comparison works only when the relevant variables are explicit.

The useful lesson is search bias. If a crater is buried beneath sediment, offshore, in dense vegetation, or under difficult political geography, a strewn field may be discovered long before the structure. Geological expectation can also narrow search areas incorrectly. Distal ejecta trajectories from oblique impacts may not center neatly on the source.

Former reidite evidence has also been discussed in relation to other disputed-origin glasses, reinforcing the idea that mineral pressure histories can identify impact even before cartography identifies the hole.

An orphan impact product is therefore not a contradiction. It is a localization problem.

The harder question is how long we should tolerate orphan status before revising the model. That depends on the strength of positive impact evidence and the completeness of crater searches. A robustly shocked glass with no crater after weak regional surveying is not mysterious. The same glass after exhaustive geophysical coverage of every physically plausible source zone would be.

This book keeps pushing toward that missing denominator: how hard have we actually looked?
