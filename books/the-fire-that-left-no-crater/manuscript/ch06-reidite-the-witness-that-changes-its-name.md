# Reidite, the Witness That Changes Its Name

Some witnesses survive by ceasing to exist.

Zircon is common, durable, and beloved by geochronologists. Under extreme pressure its crystal structure can transform into reidite, a denser polymorph. When pressure falls, reidite may revert to zircon. If all we did was ask whether reidite is present now, the answer could be no even though the grain once crossed the relevant pressure boundary.

The trick is that reversion can leave crystallographic fingerprints. In Libyan Desert Glass, granular zircon textures and systematic orientation relationships have been interpreted as evidence that reidite formed and then transformed back. The inferred pressure is on the order of tens of gigapascals. That is the kind of number that changes a geological argument.

This is stronger than saying the glass got hot. It means at least some zircon experienced a shock state consistent with crater-forming impact processes. The 2019 work by Cavosie and Koeberl argued that this evidence precluded formation by an airburst alone. Their reasoning was physical: atmospheric airbursts generate overpressure orders of magnitude below the shock pressures required for reidite.

The interesting caveat is spatial. A zircon grain tells us what happened to itself. It does not tell us where the crater center was, how large the projectile was, or whether every gram of glass experienced the same pressure history. Ejecta can mix. Target lithologies can vary. One high-pressure survivor may represent a small component of a large melt volume.

A 2026 study of a dendritic zircon inclusion added another layer by reconstructing extreme thermal conditions and rapid kinetics. That work was explicitly cautious about using its specimen alone to discriminate cratering from airburst formation because the particular evidence emphasized temperature rather than an independent high-pressure marker. This apparent tension is useful. Different grains answer different questions.

The lesson is methodological. Never ask a mineral to testify beyond its jurisdiction.

Reidite evidence is jurisdictionally strong on pressure. Melt phases are strong on temperature. Isotopes can be strong on source mixing. Distribution can be strong on transport geometry only after post-depositional movement is modeled. Archaeological context is strong on human use, not Oligocene impact physics.

Once the evidence is partitioned this way, the mystery gets harder to sensationalize and easier to work. There is no need for one perfect sample. The event can be reconstructed from witnesses that each remember one part.

The zircon’s peculiar contribution is almost literary: it remembers an identity it no longer has. The high-pressure phase vanished, but the route back to ordinary zircon left geometry behind. The absence of reidite is therefore not evidence that reidite was absent. In a book organized around a missing crater, that is more than a mineralogical curiosity. It is a warning about how geological absence works.
