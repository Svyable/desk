# Bedrock Under Oath

Loose grains travel. Bedrock makes a stronger alibi.

Early shocked-quartz reports from the Libyan Desert Glass region were vulnerable to a familiar objection: detrital grains can be transported from distant impact structures. A shocked mineral found loose in sediment proves that an impact happened somewhere in its provenance history, not necessarily beneath the collector’s boots.

That is why the 2019 bedrock work matters. Researchers sampled basement outcrops and reported planar fractures, planar deformation features, and feather features in quartz consistent with moderate shock pressure. Their interpretation was direct: the local rocks themselves had been shocked, strengthening the case for an impact in the region rather than merely imported impact debris.

Bedrock does not end the argument. Outcrops can be structurally complicated. Shock-feature identification requires crystallographic care. A region can contain older impact-affected material unrelated to the glass if geological histories overlap. But these alternatives can be tested. Petrography, geochronology, structural mapping, and spatial gradients can link or separate events.

What I like about this evidence is that it changes the geometry of skepticism. If high-pressure signatures occur only inside transported glass, the source might be elsewhere. If target bedrock within the strewn field also carries shock, a local high-pressure event becomes much harder to avoid.

The authors inferred pressures of at least about 16 GPa for the investigated samples. That is not atmospheric blast damage. It belongs to a regime where rock behaves briefly in ways our everyday intuition does not recognize: minerals deform along crystallographic planes, pressure waves compress material violently, and structures can survive after the macroscopic crater is degraded.

The next step should be systematic rather than rhetorical. Map shock intensity across the region. Collect bedrock on transects extending well beyond the known glass field. Use blind petrographic classification where possible. Date reset or newly formed phases. Compare target-rock chemistry to glass compositions. The goal is to turn isolated “proof of impact” samples into a field-scale pressure map.

If the crater is deeply eroded, such a map may be more useful than topography. In a simple impact structure, shock intensity generally declines outward from the center, though real geology and oblique trajectories complicate the pattern. A coherent spatial gradient could point toward the missing structure even if its rim is gone.

This is the book’s recurring inversion. The crater search should not begin by drawing circles on satellite images. It should begin by asking the rocks which direction the violence increases.
