# Catastrophes That Lose Their Craters

Impact craters are temporary structures pretending to be permanent.

On the Moon, a crater can survive for billions of years because there is little atmosphere, liquid water, vegetation, sediment transport, or plate tectonics. Earth is hostile to geological memory. Weathering lowers rims. Rivers cut ejecta. Sediments bury basins. Tectonics deforms crust. Oceans recycle seafloor.

The terrestrial impact record is therefore biased toward young, large, stable, exposed structures. This matters whenever we use the known-crater catalog to estimate how often impacts occurred in deep time.

Libyan Desert Glass offers a case study in preservation asymmetry. If the high-pressure evidence is correctly interpreted, then a crater-forming event occurred and its most obvious structure is now sufficiently altered or hidden that researchers still debate its location. The glass outlived the crater as a recognizable object.

That seems backwards only because humans privilege landforms. A glass fragment is chemically durable. Zircon is famously durable. Microscopic shock textures can persist inside resistant grains while kilometers of landscape are lowered.

This suggests a broader research program: search for impact residues where crater morphology is absent. Distal ejecta layers, shocked detrital minerals, spherules, geochemical anomalies, high-pressure phases, and unusual glass populations may reveal a larger terrestrial bombardment record.

But expanding the impact record this way raises false-positive risk. Shocked grains can be reworked from older craters. Wildfire and volcanism make unusual layers. Industrial contamination confuses recent deposits. The methodology must therefore demand convergent evidence and provenance.

A missing crater should not lower the evidentiary standard. It should change the kind of evidence we seek.
