# The Crater We May Never Find

There is a chance nobody will ever stand on the rim.

That possibility used to sound like failure. It no longer does.

A geological event can be reconstructed without recovering every macroscopic structure it created. We infer vanished volcanoes from ash, extinct rivers from sediments, erased mountain belts from metamorphic minerals, and ancient oceans from chemistry locked in rock. Impact science should be comfortable doing the same.

For Libyan Desert Glass, the evidentiary trajectory has moved from visual mystery toward mineral physics. High-temperature phases established extreme heating. Meteoritic tracers supported extraterrestrial involvement. Shocked quartz and high-pressure zircon histories strengthened the case for hypervelocity impact. The lack of a secure parent crater remains unresolved, but it no longer has veto power over positive shock evidence.

The remaining uncertainty is physically interesting. Where did the impact occur? How large was the body? What was its trajectory and composition? Which source rocks became glass? How was melt distributed? How much of the apparent strewn field reflects primary emplacement versus millions of years of redistribution? What structural remnants survive below the desert?

A good mystery gets smaller as evidence improves without becoming boring. LDG has done exactly that. Lunar volcanism and lightning no longer deserve equal seats at the table. A purely thermal airburst faces serious high-pressure objections. A crater-forming impact is strongly favored by several lines of evidence, while the crater itself remains missing.

The speculative synthesis of this book is that this mismatch should be treated as a preservation experiment. If we can reconstruct how one impact loses its crater while retaining glass and microscopic shock, we gain a template for finding other erased events. Earth’s impact record may be less incomplete because projectiles were rare than because geology is efficient at destroying the structures we trained ourselves to look for.

The desert did not leave us nothing. It left the wrong shape of evidence.

That is often what nature does when our categories are too tidy.
