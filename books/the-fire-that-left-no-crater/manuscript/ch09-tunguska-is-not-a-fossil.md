# Tunguska Is Not a Fossil

Tunguska is irresistible because it gives us a craterless cosmic catastrophe with witnesses.

In 1908 an atmospheric explosion over Siberia flattened forest across a vast area. No large parent crater has been accepted. The event remains the canonical modern example of an airburst. It is therefore repeatedly recruited into arguments about ancient glasses, unusual burn layers, and proposed prehistoric disasters.

The problem is preservation.

Tunguska occurred in a living landscape with trees, soils, peat, lakes, and twentieth-century investigators. Much of its most obvious evidence was biological and geomorphic. If we fast-forward twenty-eight million years, almost none of that archive survives in recognizable form. The question is not whether Tunguska made a crater. The question is what a Tunguska-class or much larger airburst would leave in deep geological time.

That is surprisingly difficult. Melt products may be localized or absent depending on height and energy. Shocked minerals at extreme pressures should be scarce in a purely atmospheric event. Geochemical traces of the projectile can be diluted. Fire signatures are environmentally contingent. Sediments may rework rapidly.

This makes Tunguska a poor one-to-one analog for Libyan Desert Glass but an excellent epistemic control. We know a craterless cosmic event happened there. We can therefore test which candidate geological signatures actually survive a century of weathering and which might plausibly survive millions of years.

Chelyabinsk adds another point on the spectrum. Its 2013 airburst was instrumented by satellites, infrasound networks, dashcams, seismic stations, and an accidental global sensor web. Meteorite fragments reached the ground, including a large mass recovered from Lake Chebarkul, but the principal damage came from atmospheric blast.

Neither event melted thousands of square kilometers of silica into Libyan-Desert-Glass-like material. Extrapolating upward in energy is not linear. Fragmentation, entry angle, altitude, radiative transfer, and ground coupling all change.

The useful comparison is therefore mechanistic. Tunguska tells us crater absence can coexist with extraterrestrial catastrophe. Chelyabinsk tells us atmospheric fragmentation can dominate consequences. LDG tells us high-temperature glass can preserve high-pressure histories. The task is to determine whether one physical continuum connects these observations or whether the desert glass belongs firmly on the crater-forming end.

A modern event is not automatically a fossil analog. It becomes one only after we ask what evidence time would erase.
