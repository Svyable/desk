# How Much Desert Can Melt?

A spectacular mechanism is cheap until somebody pays the energy bill.

Libyan Desert Glass exists in enormous quantity over a broad field. Estimates vary because the original deposit has been redistributed, buried, collected, and eroded, but any formation model must heat a substantial mass of silica-rich target above melting temperature and then quench it rapidly enough to preserve glass.

The energy budget begins with sensible heat: raising target material from ambient desert or subsurface temperature to well above 1,500 °C. Then comes latent heat of fusion. Some material vaporizes. Some energy leaves as radiation, atmospheric heating, projectile kinetic energy, excavation, seismic waves, and acceleration of ejecta. Only a fraction of the impactor’s kinetic energy becomes useful glass-making heat.

This inefficiency matters. A model that says a body carried enough total energy is not finished. It must show that sufficient energy couples into the right depth and area of target material on the right timescale.

Airburst models face a surface-heating problem. Radiative and convective flux can be immense, but silica is not a good thermal conductor on event timescales. Extremely shallow layers can melt while material centimeters below remains cooler. To produce thick melt bodies, the thermal pulse must be intense and sustained or coupled mechanically.

Impact models have the opposite embarrassment. They are excellent at creating pressure and heat locally, but must explain widespread distribution of relatively homogeneous silica glass without preserving an obvious source structure. Ejecta dynamics, plume transport, and target composition become central.

The quantity of glass therefore contains hidden geometry. If average fragment thickness, original areal coverage, and density can be bounded, we can estimate melt volume. If target chemistry identifies specific lithologies, we can estimate excavation depth. If inclusions constrain peak temperature and duration, we can narrow coupling efficiency.

These calculations are not glamorous compared with reidite images, but they are where hypotheses die. An airburst that requires a thermal fluence inconsistent with plausible entry physics should be rejected even if it solves the crater problem aesthetically. An impact model that cannot distribute the inferred melt volume across the field without impossible velocities has the same problem.

The right answer may involve multiple melt populations: proximal impact melt, ballistic ejecta, vapor-plume condensates, and redistributed fragments later mixed by desert processes. The glass field need not be one perfectly uniform depositional event simply because it shares an age.

Catastrophe science improves when the word “enormous” is replaced by kilograms, joules, square kilometers, seconds, and meters of melt depth.
