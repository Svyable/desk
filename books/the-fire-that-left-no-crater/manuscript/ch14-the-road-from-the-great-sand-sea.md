# The Road From the Great Sand Sea

A stone’s archaeological biography begins where geology stops following it.

Once a fragment of Libyan Desert Glass is picked up, it leaves the natural distribution and enters a network of decisions. Somebody carries it. Somebody recognizes it as worth keeping. Somebody may exchange it, cut it, polish it, drill it, or place it into an object whose symbolism has nothing to do with the glass’s actual formation.

For the Tutankhamun scarab, the gap between source and tomb invites reconstruction. The Western Desert was not an empty blank to ancient Egyptians. Oases, caravan routes, mineral expeditions, military movements, and political frontiers created episodic corridors through terrain that modern readers often imagine as impassable.

Yet a plausible route is not a documented route. This is where historical anomaly research frequently goes wrong. Once a path can be drawn on a map, possibility quietly becomes narrative. The responsible reconstruction should rank routes by archaeological evidence, water availability, known expeditionary behavior, and chronological fit, while preserving ignorance about the exact collector.

The glass may have traveled in stages. A desert traveler could have picked up an attractive fragment without knowing its rarity. An intermediary could have moved it eastward. A royal workshop could then have treated it simply as an unusual stone.

That mundane chain is more interesting to me than a lost-knowledge story because it demonstrates how objects detach from their origins. By the time the scarab reached the tomb, nobody needed to preserve where it came from for the geological fact to remain true.

This is analogous to the impact problem itself. A fragment can preserve pressure without preserving crater coordinates. An artifact can preserve material provenance without preserving the names of its handlers. Both sciences infer missing links from partial chains of custody.

The difference is timescale, not method.
