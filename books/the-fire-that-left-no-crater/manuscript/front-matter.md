# The Fire That Left No Crater

The Great Sand Sea contains an argument disguised as yellow glass.

The pieces are too pure to be ordinary slag, too widely distributed to be one campfire accident, too old to belong to any civilization, and hot enough in their mineral history to make most terrestrial explanations uncomfortable. For decades this was the invitation to speculate. Volcanoes were invoked where there are no useful volcanoes. Lightning was asked to melt a landscape. A body exploding in the atmosphere became attractive because it could provide violence without leaving the embarrassing thing a surface impact normally leaves: a crater.

Then the minerals became less cooperative with the airburst story.

This book is about what happens when one missing object—the crater—has more psychological weight than several positive measurements. It is also about the reverse mistake: treating one high-pressure mineral as if it tells us the entire event history. Libyan Desert Glass is not solved by choosing the most dramatic explanation. It is solved, if it is solvable at all, by making every piece of evidence answer to a common physical chronology.

I use four evidence classes throughout. Class 1 is verified or directly documented: measured mineral phases, dated glass, mapped distribution, peer-reviewed shock features, museum objects. Class 2 is credible but incomplete inference: likely target lithologies, likely transport paths, likely impact geometries. Class 3 is disputed or weakly supported: specific crater identifications, exact projectile composition, strong archaeological stories built on one object. Class 4 is synthesis: the larger propositions developed here about lost craters, preservation bias, and how catastrophe science should reason from residue.

The central wager is modest. The desert did not forget the event. It merely kept the parts geologists were not initially trained to treat as a complete archive.
