# Research brief — Recycled

## Working thesis

*Recycled: A New Future for Sustainability* argues that recycling is only one stage in a larger problem: preserving the useful work already embodied in physical things.

A manufactured object contains more than raw material. It contains extraction, refining, forming, machining, assembly, testing, certification, installation, maintenance, software, documentation, and accumulated knowledge about performance. When a useful product is crushed, shredded, melted, pulped, or chemically reduced to feedstock, some material value can be recovered while higher-order functional value is intentionally destroyed.

The book's core decision principle is:

**Destroy useful structure only when there is a better reason than habit.**

That principle is not a claim that reuse always beats recycling. The hierarchy is an order of questions. Whole-system evidence can still justify replacement, material recovery, energy recovery, treatment, or disposal because of safety, operating efficiency, transport, cleaning burden, contamination, obsolete technology, poor remaining performance, or negative economics.

The book should therefore preserve three distinctions throughout:

1. **Technical possibility is not realized circularity.** A product can be recyclable, repairable, or reusable on paper and still fail to circulate in the market where it is sold.
2. **Circularity is not environmental goodness by definition.** Toxic materials can circulate. Reuse can perform badly. Recycling can require substantial energy. Safe exit can be preferable to continued circulation.
3. **The environmental benefit is usually counterfactual.** Repair, reuse, remanufacturing, or recycling matter because they can avoid some future replacement production, primary extraction, pollution, or disposal—not because the activity itself is automatically virtuous.

## The conceptual contribution

The book extends familiar waste hierarchies and circular-economy loops with a more explicit theory of **completed work**.

A motor should be valued first as a motor, then as working components, then as material. A building should be considered first as usable space and structure, then as reusable systems and components, then as recoverable material. A battery's afterlife should depend on measured remaining capability and safety rather than a categorical switch from product to waste.

The argument is deliberately wider than "urban mining." A city is not merely a concentration of recoverable atoms. It is an inventory of materials already transformed into useful geometries and systems. The manuscript calls this **an urban warehouse of partially completed futures**.

The economic corollary is that destruction often wins because it restores standardization. Scrap steel is easier to price than a used beam with a biography. Recycled feedstock is easier to aggregate than heterogeneous returned products. High-value circularity therefore requires richer information, testing, markets, warranties, standards, storage, and liability rules.

## What the book is not

### Not an anti-recycling argument

Recycling remains essential. The book repeatedly describes it as the material-recovery floor beneath a larger preservation system. Metals, paper, glass, batteries, construction materials, organics, and other streams need effective recovery after higher useful states are exhausted.

The criticism is of **premature recycling**—destroying useful structure simply because a lower-value recovery route is institutionally easier.

### Not "reuse everything"

Reuse can lose a lifecycle comparison. An inefficient old appliance can deserve replacement. A damaged battery can be inappropriate for second life. A reusable package can perform poorly if return rates are low or reverse logistics are burdensome. A building can be unsafe, inflexible, badly located, or too costly to adapt.

The hierarchy must remain subordinate to evidence.

### Not perfect closed-loop physics

Every material cycle has losses. Materials degrade, mix, corrode, dissipate, or become contaminated. Energy is required to maintain order. Some substances should leave circulation because continued cycling would perpetuate toxicity. The manuscript explicitly rejects perpetual-motion versions of circularity.

### Not a consumer morality book

The household recycling bin appears at the beginning because it is where public responsibility is often concentrated. The argument moves upstream: design, software support, maintenance, finance, procurement, producer responsibility, standards, data, and industrial markets determine most afterlife options before a consumer reaches the bin.

The successful system should require **less moral homework at the end**.

### Not a product-passport manifesto

Digital product passports can preserve useful technical information, but they do not guarantee truth, repair, reuse, a buyer, a warranty, or safe processing. The manuscript treats passports as one layer of circular infrastructure and keeps privacy, interoperability, persistence, access control, corporate failure, and data quality in scope.

## Evidence spine

### 1. Resource extraction is rising fast enough that material productivity matters at global scale

UNEP's *Global Resources Outlook 2024* reports that global extraction of natural resources has roughly tripled over the previous five decades and warns that extraction could rise another 60% from 2020 levels by 2060 without major change. Use this as the scale argument, not as evidence that every category of consumption should shrink equally.

The report also emphasizes large inequalities in resource use and impacts between high-income and low-income countries. That supports the manuscript's distinction between material **build-out** where infrastructure is still scarce and material **churn** in mature high-consumption stocks.

### 2. Waste hierarchies already rank prevention and reuse above recycling

The U.S. EPA's Sustainable Materials Management hierarchy places source reduction and reuse above recycling/composting, followed by energy recovery, treatment, and disposal. EPA recycling guidance likewise emphasizes reduction and reuse before recycling.

Use this to ground the book's ordering without pretending the manuscript invented the hierarchy. The new contribution is the completed-work explanation for why the ordering matters.

### 3. Global municipal waste continues growing despite decades of recycling policy

UNEP's *Global Waste Management Outlook 2024* estimates municipal solid waste at roughly 2.1 billion tonnes in 2023 and projects about 3.8 billion tonnes by 2050 under prevailing trajectories. Use this to show why downstream processing capacity alone cannot be the sustainability strategy.

### 4. Construction and demolition reveal the scale of high-mass completed work

EPA estimates U.S. construction and demolition debris at roughly 600 million tons in 2018, more than twice municipal solid waste. EPA guidance explicitly includes preserving existing buildings, designing for adaptability, and optimizing components for disassembly and reuse.

Use C&D as the strongest physical example of the difference between landfill diversion and preservation of useful structure.

### 5. Material efficiency can reduce clean-energy material demand

The IEA's work on material efficiency in clean-energy transitions includes longer building lifetimes, renovation, reuse of steel, improved design, and other measures as strategies for reducing primary material demand and associated emissions.

This is important because the book should not sound as if material efficiency is a niche waste-sector concern. It affects the scale and emissions of energy-transition infrastructure itself.

### 6. Plastics show the gap between technical recyclability and real circulation

OECD's *Global Plastics Outlook* estimates 460 million tonnes of global plastics production and 353 million tonnes of plastic waste in 2019, with only 9% ultimately recycled after losses. UNEP's *Turning off the Tap* strategy combines reduction of problematic and unnecessary plastics, redesign, reuse, and recycling rather than treating recycling as a standalone answer.

Use this to support the systems claim. Do not use the 9% figure as evidence that all plastic applications are inappropriate or that all recycling technologies have failed equally.

### 7. E-waste demonstrates the loss of complex organization

ITU's *Global E-waste Monitor 2024* reports 62 billion kilograms of e-waste generated in 2022 and a documented formal collection/recycling rate of 22.3%. The book uses electronics to distinguish a functioning device from the same device treated as a mine.

Preserve the global labor and environmental boundary: secondhand trade can extend access, while exports of nonfunctional or hazardous material can shift disposal burdens to weaker systems.

### 8. Batteries make product memory economically important

Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 requires, from February 18, 2027, an electronic battery passport for specified electric-vehicle, light-means-of-transport, and larger industrial batteries. The regulation includes information relevant to the battery model, individual battery, dismantling, state of health/lifetime, and access for actors involved in repair, remanufacturing, repurposing, and recycling under the applicable rules.

Use this as evidence that product biography is moving into regulation. Do not imply the passport itself certifies second-life safety.

### 9. Digital Product Passports are becoming broader product infrastructure

The EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 establishes a framework for product-specific requirements concerning durability, reusability, upgradability, reparability, remanufacturing, recycling, resource efficiency, recycled content, and related information. It creates the Digital Product Passport mechanism, with standardization work continuing through 2026.

Use the regulation as contemporary evidence that afterlife information is moving upstream into product policy.

### 10. Right-to-repair policy recognizes repair as part of normal product life

The EU adopted its common right-to-repair rules in 2024; the directive entered into force July 30, 2024 with Member State transposition due by July 31, 2026. Use the official EU policy pages for current implementation status and avoid claiming every product category is covered identically.

### 11. NIST models reuse, remanufacturing, recycling, and disassembly as distinct manufacturing activities

NIST's circular-economy manufacturing models distinguish activities such as reuse, repair/remanufacturing, and recycling, and NIST has separately published an information model for disassembly, reuse, recycling, and remanufacturing. Use these sources to ground the reverse-factory chapters and the claim that information is a manufacturing input in circular systems.

### 12. Critical-mineral recycling grows in importance as installed stocks mature

The IEA's critical-mineral recycling work shows substantial long-run potential for recycled lithium, nickel, cobalt, copper, and other materials if collection and recovery improve, while also making clear that rapidly growing clean-energy demand still requires primary supply because much of the material stock has not yet reached end of life.

Use this to preserve the build-out-versus-stewardship timing distinction. Do not imply recycling can immediately replace mining.

### 13. Current critical-mineral outlooks still show primary-supply pressure

The IEA's 2026 critical-minerals outlook identifies potential supply deficits or concentration risks for key minerals under current project pipelines. Use these current projections cautiously: they are scenarios based on announced projects and demand assumptions, not certainties.

### 14. Extended producer responsibility reconnects sale with post-consumer responsibility

EPA's extended battery producer responsibility framework describes EPR as extending producer responsibility into the post-consumer stage, potentially including financial and physical responsibility. Use this as a concrete institutional model for sending afterlife cost information back toward product design.

The manuscript should keep the limitation visible: EPR can become a fee-paying compliance exercise if it does not create differentiated design signals or support higher loops.

### 15. Federal procurement already recognizes remanufactured categories

The current Federal Acquisition Regulation distinguishes reconditioned and remanufactured supplies, and relevant solicitation clauses can permit new, reconditioned, or remanufactured supplies rather than treating newness as the only category.

Use this as a narrow example of procurement language legitimizing non-new performance categories. Do not generalize one clause to every federal purchase.

### 16. Water reuse is explicitly fit-for-purpose infrastructure

EPA defines water reuse as reclaiming water from a range of sources, treating it, and reusing it for beneficial purposes. EPA launched Water Reuse Action Plan 2.0 in April 2026. Use current EPA material for the policy and infrastructure framing.

The water chapter should preserve the basin boundary: local reuse can affect downstream flows, and repeated cycling can concentrate contaminants.

### 17. Food waste hierarchy makes the higher-use principle unusually visible

EPA's Wasted Food Scale puts prevention, donation, and upcycling above lower pathways such as composting and anaerobic digestion. UNEP's *Food Waste Index Report 2024* estimates 1.05 billion tonnes of food waste in 2022 at household, food-service, and retail levels.

Use food to show that recovery can be successful while still representing a lower-value outcome than delivering the original intended service.

## Falsification notes

The central thesis weakens if the following conditions dominate in practice:

- high-value reuse, repair, and remanufacturing consistently require more lifecycle energy, transport, labor, or new material than replacement plus high-quality recycling;
- product information and testing fail to reduce enough uncertainty to create meaningful secondary-market value;
- automated and advanced recycling becomes so efficient, low-impact, and inexpensive that preserving product/component specificity rarely provides additional environmental benefit;
- rapid technological efficiency improvements make old products environmentally inferior so quickly that life extension usually increases total lifecycle burden;
- secondary markets expand total consumption so strongly that reuse and repair rarely displace new production;
- safety/liability requirements remain intrinsically incompatible with reuse in most high-value product categories;
- recycled output regularly fails to displace primary material because quality, market saturation, or demand response absorbs the supply;
- products become materially lighter and less complex enough that preserving completed work matters less than reducing the original manufacturing burden;
- the administrative cost of passports, standards, testing, and reverse logistics outweighs the material value preserved across most sectors.

The book should not argue these possibilities away. It should make them measurable.

## Reporting discipline

- Prefer primary government, standards, and first-party technical sources for current regulations and policy claims.
- Date all claims about policy implementation, recycling rates, product-passport requirements, and current critical-mineral outlooks.
- Distinguish a legal requirement already in force from a future applicability date.
- Never describe "recyclable" as equivalent to "recycled in practice."
- Never describe "recycled content" as equivalent to a system-wide increase in recycling.
- Preserve boundaries around reuse: actual displacement, safety, logistics, remaining efficiency, and market demand matter.
- Do not imply that every form of product tracking is desirable; minimize personal data and separate technical product memory from user surveillance.
- Do not use circularity rates as universal measures of environmental performance; include scale, toxicity, and primary-material displacement where relevant.
- Do not describe informal waste workers only as a problem to be formalized; distinguish unsafe conditions from the economic and technical value already created by repair/recovery networks.
- Do not turn critical-mineral recycling into a claim that near-term mining is unnecessary.
- Treat future scenarios as scenarios, not reports.
