# The Work Inside Things

Walk through a scrapyard slowly enough and the word *material* begins to feel inadequate.

A pile of copper wire is material. So is a motor with copper windings. Chemically, the distinction may look minor. Economically, mechanically, and historically, it is enormous.

The copper in the motor has already been drawn into wire, insulated, wound into coils, arranged around a magnetic circuit, connected, housed, balanced against bearings and a shaft, integrated with other parts, tested, rated, shipped, installed, and perhaps operated successfully for thousands of hours. The motor is not a container that happens to hold copper. It is a record of completed transformations.

Melt it and the copper survives.

Most of the record does not.

This is the central accounting mistake of a material economy. We are good at recognizing what objects are made *of*. We are less good at recognizing what has already been done *to* them.

A steel beam contains iron, carbon, and alloying elements. It also contains ore extraction, concentration, transport, reduction, refining, casting, rolling, straightening, cutting, drilling, coating, inspection, engineering, and documentation. A pane of architectural glass contains sand and other inputs, but nobody wants a bucket of those inputs where the window is supposed to be. What civilization values is the controlled transformation that made a transparent, dimensionally precise, installable building component.

A product is matter plus a history of useful decisions.

Some of those decisions are visible in shape. Some are invisible in heat treatment, tolerances, purity, software, test results, traceability, and standards. Some survive physically but disappear institutionally because nobody preserves the record.

This is why an old component can become waste even while the component itself remains capable of working.

It loses its biography.

An engineer sees an unknown beam and sees risk.

A technician sees an unlabeled battery and sees risk.

A hospital sees a device with uncertain maintenance history and sees risk.

An insurer sees a reused component without certification and sees risk.

The object may still contain useful physical performance. The economy cannot safely recognize that performance, so it sends the object downward toward a state that is easier to standardize. A recycler turns the unknown beam into known scrap. A furnace turns the scrap into known steel. The system sacrifices completed work in exchange for renewed legibility.

That trade can be rational. It is also expensive.

Circular-economy diagrams often show a series of loops. The innermost loops keep a product in use through maintenance or direct reuse. Wider loops refurbish or remanufacture it. Wider still recover components or materials. Recycling sits farther from the user because it typically destroys more of the original product's embedded value. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation's technical-cycle explanation makes the point directly: recycling retains material value but loses the time and energy invested in making the product. NIST's circular-economy manufacturing models likewise distinguish reuse, disassembly, remanufacturing, and recycling as different end-of-life activities rather than interchangeable versions of the same act.

The diagrams are useful. But the visual language of loops can hide what makes the hierarchy powerful.

The hierarchy is really a staircase of completed work.

Imagine a bicycle.

At the highest level, the bicycle is useful as a bicycle. If a rider no longer wants it, the best next owner may simply ride it.

If the bicycle is damaged, maintenance can restore it while preserving almost all of the original product. A tire, cable, chain, bearing, or brake pad is replaced. The frame, wheels, fork, drivetrain, controls, fasteners, and accumulated fit remain.

If the bicycle is too damaged or obsolete to remain intact, components can still move. A wheel can become another bicycle's wheel. A saddle can move. A crank can move. A derailleur can move. The product disappears, but some subassemblies preserve their function.

If component reuse fails, metals and polymers can be separated and recycled. The material remains, but geometry, assembly, and most component function disappear.

At each step, society is not merely deciding where matter goes. It is deciding how much completed work to erase.

This perspective changes the meaning of durability.

Durability is usually described as an environmental virtue because a durable product lasts longer. True. But durability also increases the time over which a product's accumulated manufacturing work can deliver service. The longer the structure remains useful, the longer society benefits from transformations already paid for.

Repairability does something similar. It allows a local failure to be corrected without discarding successful work elsewhere in the object.

A broken bearing is not a broken motor in the same sense that a broken motor is not broken copper.

Our economic systems often blur those distinctions because replacement can be simpler than diagnosis. A sealed assembly turns a small failure into a large disposal event. A glued battery can convert a worn cell into a discarded product. Unsupported software can retire working hardware. A missing proprietary part can make an otherwise sound machine uneconomic to repair.

The product's physical life becomes shorter than the life of most of its parts.

That is a design choice disguised as fate.

The same logic appears in buildings, where the scale becomes almost absurd.

A building can be structurally sound but functionally obsolete. The floor plan no longer fits the market. Mechanical systems are old. The facade performs poorly. The neighborhood changed. A particular tenant leaves. In a system optimized for the first use, these mismatches can push an enormous stock of already-completed work toward demolition.

But buildings are bundles of different lifetimes.

A carpet may last years. A chiller lasts longer. Interior partitions can move. Wiring can be replaced. Windows can be upgraded. A facade can be modified. A structural frame may remain useful across many interior generations. A foundation can outlive multiple businesses, families, or institutions.

Treating the building as one indivisible object makes the shortest-lived layer capable of condemning the longest-lived layers.

Design for adaptation breaks that coupling.

The International Energy Agency's work on material efficiency identifies building lifetime extension, retrofit, repurposing, improved design, and steel reuse among strategies that can reduce demand for energy-intensive materials. The idea is not exotic. The challenge is that construction practice, finance, codes, leases, component interfaces, and ownership records must support the physical possibility.

A structure can be technically adaptable and commercially impossible to adapt.

Completed work therefore exists in layers.

The first layer is physical transformation. Matter has been refined and shaped.

The second is functional integration. Parts have become assemblies and systems.

The third is verification. Someone has measured, tested, inspected, or certified performance.

The fourth is contextual knowledge. Someone knows where the object is, what it has experienced, how it was maintained, and what it can safely do next.

The fifth is institutional acceptance. Codes, contracts, warranties, insurance, and procurement rules allow the object to be used.

Lose the upper layers and the lower layers become harder to preserve.

This is why product passports matter, but also why a passport alone is not enough. Information can preserve the possibility of reuse, but information does not create a buyer, a warehouse, a warranty, or a standard. A thirty-year-old beam with a perfect digital history still needs a project that can use it and an engineer willing to sign off. A battery with excellent state-of-health data still needs a second application whose performance requirements fit the battery's remaining capability.

The afterlife economy is not a database project.

It is a coordination project.

The forward economy coordinates extraordinarily well because it has a dominant state transition: raw material becomes product, product becomes revenue.

That direction organizes incentives.

A miner does not need to know which exact building will eventually contain a tonne of steel. A steelmaker does not need to know which family will live behind a particular beam. Standardized intermediate markets connect the chain. The product acquires more specificity as it moves forward.

The reverse economy moves in the opposite direction and confronts heterogeneity immediately.

Every returned object arrives with a different age, condition, configuration, location, owner, use history, and documentation record. Reverse systems must decide whether to preserve specificity or destroy it.

Recycling is attractive because it destroys specificity.

A recycler can aggregate many different objects into a material stream. Hundreds of products can become kilograms of a known commodity. The history of each individual object becomes less important. Standardization returns.

Reuse and remanufacturing try to preserve specificity longer.

That can save more completed work, but it requires richer information and more judgment. The returned motor has to be inspected. The laptop has to be tested and wiped. The battery needs diagnosis. The beam needs traceability. The office chair needs cleaning and perhaps new fabric. The valve needs to be disassembled and rebuilt. The product's individual history matters.

In other words, high-value circularity is information intensive.

That is one reason it was historically easier to build giant linear supply chains than giant reuse systems. Virgin production begins from materials standardized precisely for manufacturing. Reuse begins from a population of objects shaped by different lives.

The more complicated the object, the more important the information.

This creates a surprising connection between sustainability and digital infrastructure.

A low-carbon future is often imagined in physical hardware: turbines, batteries, heat pumps, transmission lines, efficient buildings, electric vehicles. But a high-reuse future depends on an information layer that allows those physical assets to keep moving through useful states.

What is this part?

What is it made from?

When was it produced?

What revision is it?

How has it been used?

What failures has it experienced?

Which parts are replaceable?

What tools open it?

What software supports it?

Which safety procedures matter?

What performance remains?

What standard did it originally meet?

Can it be tested against a current standard?

Who accepts responsibility for the next use?

Without answers, the object's value collapses toward scrap.

With answers, more of the staircase can remain standing.

There is a second reason to think in terms of completed work: it prevents sustainability from becoming a morality play about materials.

No material is inherently circular because it can be recycled, and no material is inherently bad because it cannot circulate forever. The environmental result depends on the service delivered, the lifetime, the production process, the energy system, the toxicity, the logistics, the recovery infrastructure, and what the recovered output displaces.

A durable plastic component that prevents repeated replacements can be sensible. A theoretically recyclable package that is never collected is not circular in practice. A reusable container that travels enormous distances empty can perform badly. A lightweight disposable product can sometimes use fewer resources than a heavy reusable one if the reuse system is weak. A forty-year-old appliance can be repairable yet still waste enough operating energy that replacement becomes reasonable.

Preserving completed work is not a command to preserve everything.

It is a demand to count what destruction costs.

That cost is frequently missing because conventional accounting treats manufacturing as sunk once the product is sold. The energy was used yesterday. The labor was paid yesterday. The emissions occurred yesterday. The ore was mined yesterday. If a company compares repairing an old machine with buying a new one, the old machine's original manufacturing burden may disappear from the financial calculation.

From the perspective of future decisions, some of that history is indeed sunk. We cannot unmine the ore. But we can decide whether to invoke the process again for the replacement.

The environmental value of keeping the old machine comes from avoiding some portion of the new machine's future burden.

This is why life extension can matter even though the original manufacturing impact has already occurred.

The question is not whether reuse refunds the past.

It is whether reuse avoids repeating it.

That distinction is important because environmental arguments sometimes become trapped in retrospective guilt. A product was carbon-intensive to make, therefore we should regret it. Perhaps. But the more actionable question is whether the product can keep doing useful work long enough that its replacement is delayed or avoided.

A city already contains buildings whose concrete has been poured.

A factory already contains machines whose steel has been refined.

A grid already contains transformers whose copper has been mined.

A household already contains furniture, appliances, electronics, tools, and vehicles.

A country already contains bridges, rails, pipes, ports, schools, hospitals, and warehouses.

These stocks are not merely old stuff.

They are environmental assets because much of their production burden is already behind us.

Maintaining them can be a climate strategy.

The idea becomes even more powerful when applied to clean-energy technology. The transition away from fossil fuels requires a large initial build-out of physical systems. If those systems are designed for short lives, opaque components, difficult repair, and poor recovery, the transition creates a future extraction treadmill. If they are designed for maintenance, repowering, component exchange, remanufacturing, and high-quality material recovery, the same initial build-out becomes the beginning of a durable stock.

The first generation of a solar farm is not only an electricity project. It is the ancestor of a future material system.

The first large wave of electric-vehicle batteries is not only transportation hardware. It is a future inventory of already-refined critical materials.

The first offshore wind turbines are not only power plants. They are future towers, metals, magnets, cables, and components entering circulation.

The design question is whether we intend to inherit those stocks intelligently.

A linear economy behaves as though each generation of technology will be fed indefinitely by fresh extraction and will exit through disposal or low-value recovery.

A recycled economy tries to turn the first great material build-out into a reservoir from which later generations can draw.

That does not eliminate mining. Growing systems need new material. Losses require replacement. Some technologies need inputs that older stocks do not contain in sufficient quantities. The IEA's critical-minerals work is explicit that recycling can make an increasingly important contribution without eliminating the need for primary supply.

The aim is not purity.

It is compounding.

Every tonne kept useful reduces the amount that has to be started again.

Every component with a second life gives the work already inside it another return.

Every building adapted instead of demolished extends the dividend from construction that already happened.

Every product designed for disassembly gives a future owner more choices than a glued box gives them.

Every durable record attached to an object preserves the possibility that someone later will recognize what the object still is.

This is why a used object can be more valuable than an equivalent mass of new raw material even when commodity accounting suggests otherwise.

The raw material is possibility.

The used object is possibility plus progress.

We have spent a century learning how to manufacture progress into matter.

The next century has to learn how not to erase it by default.
