# Back Matter

## A Note on the Argument

*Recycled* makes a simple claim and then spends a book complicating it: sustainability should preserve useful work before reducing useful objects back to material.

That claim is not a rule that reuse always beats recycling, that old always beats new, or that every material belongs in a loop forever. The manuscript repeatedly preserves the exceptions because they are part of the argument. Safety can require retirement. Toxic substances can require deliberate exit. A highly inefficient product can deserve replacement. Transport, cleaning, inspection, repair labor, and operating energy can overturn an intuitive reuse advantage. Growing societies still need primary material because no circular system can recover stocks that have not yet been built.

The hierarchy matters because it establishes an order of questions, not a predetermined answer.

Can the need for new material be avoided?

Can the product remain useful?

Can a local failure be repaired without discarding successful work elsewhere?

Can the product be refurbished or remanufactured to a credible performance state?

Can components retain their function?

Can the material be recovered at a quality that displaces primary production?

What should leave circulation because keeping it would be unsafe or environmentally worse?

The research trail in this Desk records the factual sources used to anchor those questions and, for each chapter, a falsification note describing evidence that would weaken the argument.

## The Principle in One Sentence

Destroy useful structure only when there is a better reason than habit.

## What “Completed Work” Means

The phrase *completed work* is used throughout the book to describe the transformations already embodied in a useful object: extraction, refining, forming, machining, assembly, testing, certification, installation, software, documentation, and accumulated knowledge about performance.

It is not a new physical unit and should not be treated as one. It is a decision lens. A motor and a kilogram of its constituent metals are physically related but economically and functionally different states. A building and its demolition debris contain much of the same matter but not the same useful organization. The book argues that sustainability decisions should recognize the value lost when a system moves irreversibly from a higher useful state to a lower one.

## Source Discipline

The manuscript prefers primary or first-party sources for concrete regulatory, statistical, and technical claims where those are available: UNEP and OECD resource and waste reports, ITU's e-waste data, European Union regulations and official implementation pages, U.S. EPA waste and water guidance, NIST circular-manufacturing models, IEA material-efficiency and critical-minerals work, and the current Federal Acquisition Regulation.

The accompanying source ledger is not intended to turn the book into a literature review. It records the evidentiary spine behind the factual claims most likely to be checked or to change over time. Future editions should date-check regulations, policy implementation, waste statistics, recycling economics, and technology-specific claims rather than assuming the first edition remains current.

## About the Author

Sven Hardy Benson writes about the systems beneath apparently simple outcomes: intelligence, infrastructure, institutions, incentives, and the physical arrangements that decide what society can do next.

In *Recycled*, the familiar recycling symbol becomes a starting point rather than an answer. The book asks what sustainability looks like when civilization treats its existing physical world not as a stream of future waste, but as an inheritance of partially completed futures.
