# The Necessary Exit

A circular economy needs a door marked exit.

That sounds like a contradiction only if the circle has become a religion.

Some materials should leave circulation.

Lead paint should not be preserved because the pigment represents completed work.

Asbestos should not be lovingly recovered into new consumer products because keeping matter in use is always virtuous.

Persistent toxic chemicals should not be cycled through new goods indefinitely merely to improve a recycling statistic.

Contaminated food should not be redistributed because prevention ranks above composting.

A structurally compromised component should not be reused because reuse sits above recycling.

A battery damaged beyond safe second life should not be forced into another application to satisfy a circularity target.

The hierarchy has an escape clause.

Health and safety.

This is not a small caveat. It defines the moral boundary of the whole project.

Circularity is valuable because it preserves useful value while reducing unnecessary extraction and waste. If circulation preserves harm, the system is working against its purpose.

The material economy has a long history of learning this late.

Products were manufactured with substances whose hazards were poorly understood, discounted, or hidden. Lead entered paint, gasoline, pipes, batteries, and other products. Asbestos entered insulation, fireproofing, flooring, roofing, gaskets, and industrial systems. Persistent organic pollutants entered pesticides, electrical equipment, flame retardants, and manufacturing. Mercury moved through instruments, lamps, industrial processes, and products. PFAS chemistry created remarkable resistance to heat, oil, water, and stains while raising increasingly serious concerns about persistence and health effects across many uses.

The circular economy inherits this history.

An old building can be a warehouse of useful components and a warehouse of legacy hazards at the same time.

A recycler can recover material and spread contamination at the same time.

A recycled product can carry substances that new products are no longer allowed to contain.

This creates one of the hardest design problems in material policy: how do we preserve value without preserving yesterday's mistakes?

The first answer is information.

Hazardous substances need to remain identifiable across the product life cycle. A building deconstruction crew needs to know where asbestos is before opening walls. A recycler needs to know which battery chemistry or refrigerant is present. A plastics processor needs information about restricted additives where relevant. A repairer needs to know stored electrical, chemical, thermal, or mechanical hazards.

The product passport has a safety role before it has a circularity role.

A circular system that increases disassembly increases worker contact with products that the original user may never have opened. The afterlife worker inherits hidden risks.

Design for disassembly must therefore include design for safe disassembly.

Can the product be made electrically safe before opening?

Can pressure be released?

Can refrigerants be recovered?

Can a battery be isolated?

Can hazardous modules be removed before shredding?

Can chemical identity remain legible after labels fade?

Can protective procedures be understood decades later?

These are afterlife safety requirements.

The second answer is selective destruction.

Some molecules need to be destroyed or permanently contained rather than recirculated. Some contaminated materials need hazardous-waste treatment. Some mixtures need stabilization. Some residues belong in engineered disposal facilities precisely because the alternative is uncontrolled dispersion.

Landfill is not always the enemy.

Poorly managed dumping is destructive. Unnecessary landfilling of recoverable material wastes resources. But a well-designed disposal facility can serve an essential environmental function: isolating residuals that should not be returned to the economy or the biosphere casually.

The waste hierarchy places disposal last, not nowhere.

Last is a meaningful category.

This distinction matters because zero-waste rhetoric can create perverse pressure to find a nominal reuse for material whose safest fate is controlled disposal. A material may be technically usable as fill, additive, fuel, or low-grade product while carrying contaminants that make such use questionable.

The highest diversion rate is not the highest environmental performance.

Sometimes the responsible loop ends.

This is especially important for legacy material stocks. A new circular product can be designed around safer chemistry. An old product may contain substances acceptable at the time of manufacture and unacceptable now. The reuse decision has to respect current knowledge.

This creates tension with the principle of preserving completed work.

Imagine an old window assembly containing hazardous sealants, a building component with lead paint, or electronic equipment with materials no longer desired in new products. Reusing the whole component can delay exposure during processing and avoid new manufacturing. Recycling can concentrate or redistribute hazardous substances depending on the process. Disposal can isolate them but destroy useful material.

There may be no perfect pathway.

Circularity cannot promise perfect answers in contaminated systems.

It can demand transparent tradeoffs.

What exposure occurs during reuse?

What exposure occurs during removal?

Can the hazardous layer be removed while preserving the rest?

Does recycling destroy or concentrate the contaminant?

Will the recovered material enter sensitive applications?

Does the new use create future exposure?

How long can the component remain safely in service?

The hierarchy becomes conditional.

This is good environmental practice rather than a failure of theory.

The broader circular-economy literature sometimes uses the phrase "safe and circular" to capture this combination. Safety has to qualify circularity because material cycles can otherwise become contamination cycles.

The problem is particularly clear with persistent chemicals.

A substance designed not to break down can be extremely useful during product life and extremely difficult after it. Durability at the molecular level can become persistence in the environment. Recycling can return the substance to commerce repeatedly or move it into products where exposure pathways differ.

The same property has opposite meanings at different system boundaries.

This is why chemistry belongs at the beginning of circular design.

A product should not merely be durable, repairable, and recyclable. Its materials should be compatible with repeated use and eventual recovery without building up unacceptable hazards.

Substitution is complicated because alternatives can have their own impacts. Removing one chemical does not guarantee the replacement is safer. Performance losses can shorten product life. A coating that prevents corrosion for decades may reduce material demand enough to justify complexity.

Safer chemistry requires comparative assessment, not simple bans by aesthetic preference.

But circularity makes one demand stronger: uncertainty compounds across loops.

A hazardous ingredient placed into a disposable product creates one waste problem.

The same ingredient placed into a highly circulating material stream can create many future decision points.

The more confidently we plan to recirculate material, the more carefully we should choose what enters the loop in the first place.

The third answer is loss accounting.

Every recycling process generates residues.

Every sorting process has rejects.

Every remanufacturing system has components that cannot be restored.

Every water-reuse system has contaminants that must be managed.

Every composting system has unsuitable inputs.

Every material cycle leaks some fraction through wear, breakage, corrosion, contamination, oxidation, dissipation, or economic loss.

A mature circular economy measures the losses instead of hiding them behind gross recovery rates.

Suppose a process collects one hundred units and recovers ninety units of material. Where did the other ten go?

Wastewater?

Slag?

Ash?

Dust?

Filter cake?

Rejected product?

Emissions?

Landfill?

The environmental result depends on the fate of the residuals.

A process that boasts 90 percent recovery can still have serious pollution if the remaining 10 percent contains concentrated hazards and is poorly managed.

Yield and residue quality belong together.

The same is true of energy recovery.

Burning waste can produce electricity or heat. It can also emit carbon dioxide and other pollutants, require air-pollution controls, and leave ash. In some systems it can reduce landfill volumes and recover value from material unsuitable for recycling. In others it can compete with recycling for feedstock or lock cities into long-term waste generation to supply facilities.

The waste hierarchy places energy recovery below recycling for a reason, while still recognizing it as a management option for non-recyclable waste.

The correct role depends on local energy systems, material composition, emissions controls, alternatives, and contractual incentives.

Again, circularity should resist binary thinking.

A technology can be useful in the last stages and dangerous when allowed to pull material prematurely down the hierarchy.

Incineration capacity can become an appetite.

Recycling capacity can become an appetite.

Landfill capacity can become an appetite.

Every downstream facility has fixed costs and business incentives. Once built, it needs feedstock or revenue.

This is a structural reason to protect prevention and higher-value loops in policy rather than assuming downstream infrastructure will voluntarily leave itself underused.

A circular system needs the right amount of exit capacity without becoming organized around filling the exit.

The fourth answer is honesty about entropy.

Matter can be rearranged. Energy quality degrades. Materials become mixed. Metals corrode. Fibers shorten. Polymers degrade. Surface treatments wear. Molecules disperse. Tiny quantities become uneconomic to recover. Products become physically distributed across billions of users.

No amount of digital intelligence makes separation free.

The circular economy is not a perpetual-motion machine.

This physical reality should be liberating.

It means we do not have to design an impossible world in which nothing is ever discarded. We can design a much better world in which disposal is smaller, safer, more deliberate, and reserved for material that has genuinely reached the bottom of the useful hierarchy.

The goal is not zero exit.

It is earned exit.

An object earns exit when higher uses are unsafe, uneconomic in whole-system terms, or technically exhausted.

A material earns exit when recovery would create more environmental harm than controlled disposal.

A hazardous substance earns exit because society has decided it should no longer circulate.

This is different from the current system, where many products exit because nobody can repair them, nobody has the documentation, the contract demands newness, the software expired, the return system is inconvenient, or the recycler's commodity bid is easier than finding a second owner.

Those are premature exits.

The book's entire argument can be understood as shrinking premature exit while preserving necessary exit.

This distinction also protects circularity from a common criticism: that recycling merely delays landfill.

Sometimes it does.

A material recycled into a lower-grade product may eventually be landfilled. A bottle reused twenty times may eventually break and be disposed. A building adapted for a century may eventually come down. A battery can have two lives and still require final treatment.

Delay is not meaningless.

If the material delivered useful service during the delay and displaced new production, the environmental benefit was real. A human life also ends; medicine is not therefore pointless.

The question is what happened before the end.

How much service did the material deliver?

How much extraction did it displace?

How much pollution did the cycle create?

What quality remained at the final stage?

Circularity is a strategy for extracting more value from a finite material history, not a promise of eternity.

The necessary exit also creates a financial obligation.

Hazardous disposal costs money. Product systems should not rely on the value of recoverable material to fund every afterlife because some products become net liabilities. Producer-responsibility systems can help ensure funds exist for collection and safe treatment even when commodity prices fall.

This is particularly important for products with batteries, refrigerants, toxic components, or difficult composites. If the afterlife economics depend entirely on recovered commodity value, the least valuable and most hazardous products are the ones most likely to leak into informal or uncontrolled disposal.

Responsibility has to survive negative value.

That is a crucial phrase.

When an object is worth money, markets tend to find it.

When an object costs money to handle safely, policy has to ensure someone remains responsible.

The linear economy often loses responsibility at exactly that moment.

The owner abandons.

The municipality pays.

The waste moves to a weaker jurisdiction.

The informal worker absorbs the health risk.

The river becomes the cheapest disposal route.

Circular justice begins where market value becomes negative.

Who pays then?

The producer?

The current owner?

A collective system?

The public?

The answer varies, but "nobody" is not acceptable.

The same question will become larger as clean-energy technologies reach end of life. Solar panels, wind blades, batteries, power electronics, heat pumps, and grid equipment will create future material streams at scales larger than today's pilot recycling systems. Most contain valuable recoverable material. Some contain difficult composites, hazardous components, or low-value fractions.

The green transition should not repeat the fossil era's habit of celebrating use while leaving decommissioning vague.

Decommissioning belongs in the business case from the beginning.

Financial assurance can require funds for closure or recovery. Take-back obligations can preserve responsibility. Design standards can reduce future hazards. Material records can tell dismantlers what they face.

The afterlife should be financed while the product still has revenue.

That is another form of stewardship.

The necessary exit also has cultural value because it keeps circularity from becoming hoarding.

People should not feel obliged to preserve every object because throwing anything away is morally suspect. Homes can fill with things kept for hypothetical future reuse. Organizations can store obsolete equipment indefinitely because disposal feels like failure. Cities can maintain derelict structures whose social and environmental opportunity cost exceeds their remaining value.

Letting go can be rational.

The discipline is to let go intelligently.

Sell while value remains.

Donate while function remains.

Repair before damage spreads.

Recover components while they can be identified.

Recycle materials before contamination destroys quality.

Dispose safely when the remaining material should leave circulation.

Timing matters all the way down.

A circular economy is not a world without trash cans.

It is a world in which the trash can receives fewer mysteries.

The things entering it have been considered.

Hazards have been identified.

Higher uses have been exhausted or rejected for reasons strong enough to explain.

Recoverable material has been removed where sensible.

Responsibility remains attached to the residual.

The exit is controlled.

This is what environmental maturity looks like.

Not infinite circles.

Good endings.
