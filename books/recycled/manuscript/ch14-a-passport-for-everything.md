# A Passport for Everything

A passport does not make a person good.

It makes a person legible to a border system.

That distinction is useful because product passports are beginning to attract the kind of optimism that follows every new information technology. Attach a digital identity to a product, record its materials, repairs, environmental attributes, and instructions, and the circular economy will supposedly become obvious. The recycler will know what to do. The repairer will know what to replace. The buyer will know what is sustainable. The regulator will know whether requirements were met.

Some of that is plausible.

None of it happens because a QR code exists.

A product passport is valuable for the same reason the biography of a beam or battery is valuable: it preserves facts that otherwise disappear while the object remains physically present. Its contribution is not moral certification. It is continuity of information.

The European Union's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation makes this idea unusually concrete. Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 creates a framework through which product-specific ecodesign requirements can address durability, reusability, upgradability, reparability, remanufacturing, recycling, resource efficiency, recycled content, substances that hinder circularity, and environmental footprints. It also creates the Digital Product Passport as an information mechanism for products brought under the regulation.

In 2026 the EU continued building the standards infrastructure underneath that system. This is not a small labeling exercise. It is an attempt to make product sustainability information travel with products across companies, owners, borders, and time.

The battery passport goes further in a sector where afterlife information has immediate economic value. From February 18, 2027, the EU Batteries Regulation requires an electronic battery passport for electric-vehicle batteries, light-means-of-transport batteries, and industrial batteries above the specified capacity threshold. The passport includes model-level and individual-battery information, and the regulation explicitly contemplates access needed by repairers, remanufacturers, second-life operators, and recyclers.

The wording matters because a passport only becomes circular when somebody downstream can use it.

A database that proves compliance to the original seller but cannot help a repairer identify a part has limited afterlife value.

A material record that disappears when the manufacturer exits the market is not durable memory.

A recycling instruction that uses a proprietary format no future system can read is merely a temporary brochure.

A circular passport therefore has to survive both the product and, potentially, the company that issued it.

That is a demanding design requirement.

Consumer electronics illustrate the problem. A phone can remain physically useful after a cloud service closes. A smart appliance can outlive the startup behind it. A building can outlive software companies, database vendors, property managers, and several owners. A structural component can remain in service for a century. If the information needed for reuse depends on one firm's authentication server, circularity inherits the firm's commercial lifetime.

The information architecture must have an afterlife too.

This is easy to miss because digital information feels weightless and permanent. In practice it decays through forgotten passwords, dead domains, obsolete file formats, expired certificates, changed schemas, unsupported APIs, broken links, lost encryption keys, inaccessible proprietary systems, organizational mergers, and simple neglect.

Paper records can disappear in a flooded basement.

Digital records can disappear behind an HTTP error.

Neither is automatically durable.

Long-lived product information needs governance.

Who hosts it?

Who pays after the product is no longer profitable?

Which fields are public?

Which are restricted for safety or commercial reasons?

Who can update a repair record?

How is a fraudulent update detected?

What happens when a component is replaced?

Can an owner transfer the record without transferring personal data?

Can an independent repairer append evidence?

Can a recycler access disassembly information without receiving unrelated customer information?

What happens if the producer goes bankrupt?

What happens if the passport provider goes bankrupt?

What information must remain human-readable if software fails?

These questions determine whether the passport becomes infrastructure or decoration.

The privacy issue deserves special care.

A product can have a biography without becoming a surveillance device.

For a battery, useful afterlife data may include chemistry, design, manufacturing date, relevant performance history, safety incidents, state of health, and repairs. It does not require a permanent record of where the driver went for dinner. A refrigerator passport may need model, parts, refrigerant, service procedures, and repair history. It does not need a database of what the household ate.

The circular economy has a legitimate appetite for technical data and an illegitimate temptation toward total data.

Those should be separated deliberately.

Data minimization is not an obstacle to circularity. It is a design discipline that can make circular systems politically durable.

A product passport also should not become a truth badge.

Manufacturers make mistakes. Suppliers misreport. Records become stale. Sensors fail. Fraud exists. A digital record can preserve a false statement just as efficiently as a correct one.

The passport should therefore distinguish claims from measurements and provenance from verification.

This part was declared by the manufacturer.

This part was tested by an accredited laboratory.

This repair was recorded by a service provider.

This state-of-health estimate was produced by a specified method on a specified date.

This material composition is derived from a supplier declaration.

This component was replaced, but the identity of the replacement is uncertain.

Good information systems make uncertainty visible rather than hiding it behind a green icon.

That principle is familiar in finance, aviation, medicine, and science. Records have sources. Measurements have methods. Certifications have scopes. Audit trails exist because the record itself can become contested.

Circularity needs the same epistemic maturity.

The temptation will be to compress complex product histories into scores.

Repairability score.

Circularity score.

Recycled-content score.

Carbon score.

Durability grade.

Scores can help consumers and procurement systems compare products quickly. They can also become new proxies that manufacturers optimize around. A product can improve the metric while degrading something the metric does not capture. A battery can be easy to disassemble yet have poor durability. A durable product can use hazardous chemistry. A lightweight product can score well on material use while being impossible to repair. A reusable package can score well in design and perform badly because return rates are low.

A passport should preserve the evidence underneath the score.

Otherwise we have replaced an opaque object with an opaque number.

The deepest value of the passport is not consumer shopping advice.

It is machine-readable continuity across afterlives.

Imagine a building demolition inventory assembled automatically from component passports. The owner can see what will become available. A marketplace can match components to future projects. Engineers can retrieve original specifications and maintenance records. The demolition contractor can identify hazardous materials before work begins. Recyclers can plan separate streams.

Imagine a repair shop scanning an appliance and receiving the exact disassembly sequence, electrical hazards, parts list, software version, and compatible replacements.

Imagine a remanufacturer receiving industrial equipment with verified operating hours, overhaul history, material specifications, and test data.

Imagine a recycler knowing not merely that a device contains a battery, but where the battery is, what chemistry it uses, how to make it electrically safe, and which fasteners or release features allow controlled removal.

The passport turns future uncertainty into present design work.

That is its strongest sustainability contribution.

The information can also change markets before the product reaches end of life.

A buyer of a used machine can inspect the service history.

An insurer can price risk.

A lender can estimate residual value.

A fleet owner can identify which assets deserve life extension.

A manufacturer can see failure patterns across generations.

A government buyer can verify whether a product satisfies circular procurement requirements.

The passport becomes part of the asset rather than a regulatory attachment.

This creates incentives to keep it accurate.

A used car with a reliable service history is worth more than an otherwise identical car with uncertain history. A product passport can create the equivalent effect in categories that currently lose documentation at resale.

Information increases residual value when the market trusts it.

That is important because circularity often fails at the second transaction. The first seller has enough knowledge to make the product. The second seller receives an object. The information gap becomes a price discount. A sufficiently large discount can push the object toward scrap.

A passport narrows the gap.

But only if rights to information travel with rights to the object.

This is where commercial tension enters.

Manufacturers can argue that detailed repair information reveals intellectual property. Component suppliers can resist disclosure of formulations. Cybersecurity considerations can justify restricting some system details. Safety-critical procedures may require trained access. Data can reveal competitive information about production.

These interests are real.

The answer is not universal public disclosure.

It is layered access designed around legitimate need.

The consumer may need durability, repair options, warranty, material, and environmental information.

A repairer may need diagnostic and parts information.

A recycler may need chemistry, disassembly, hazard, and composition data.

A regulator may need compliance evidence.

A remanufacturer may need performance history unavailable to the general public.

The EU battery regime explicitly uses differentiated access in this spirit. The model is important because it recognizes that a product can be more legible without every fact being public to everyone.

Layered access also creates an interoperability challenge.

If every manufacturer invents a proprietary passport platform, downstream actors will face hundreds of portals, schemas, terms, credentials, and APIs. The circular economy will recreate the fragmentation it was trying to solve.

Standards are therefore central.

Not standards for the product itself in every detail, but standards for identity, access, data fields, links, persistence, and machine readability. The passport needs to be discoverable by a future actor who did not participate in the original supply chain.

The standards cannot be frozen forever either.

A product may last longer than a data schema. The system needs versioning and migration so old records remain interpretable.

This is archival engineering disguised as product policy.

Libraries understand this problem. Aviation understands it. Nuclear waste management understands it over extreme timescales. The circular economy is now discovering that materials can outlive the information technology used to describe them.

A steel beam can survive several software generations.

Concrete can survive several computer industries.

A transformer can outlive a database platform.

The most durable object in the system may be the least digital part of it.

This should make designers humble.

The passport also gives regulators a new tool against greenwashing, but again only if the underlying claims are verifiable. A company can make broad sustainability statements today that are difficult for buyers to inspect. Product-specific data can make claims more granular: recycled content, repair availability, material composition, expected durability, environmental footprint under defined methods.

Granularity raises the cost of vague virtue.

It also raises the administrative burden on small firms and complex supply chains.

A manufacturer may rely on thousands of suppliers. Material information can be commercially sensitive or difficult to obtain. Changes propagate through bills of materials. Suppliers change. Recycled feedstocks vary. A passport regime that demands impossible precision can generate expensive paperwork without improving decisions.

The information requirement should follow use.

Ask for data because a future actor needs it, not because databases can store it.

This is another recurring principle: more circular data is not automatically better circular data.

The useful record is the minimum durable set that preserves high-value options.

For a building component, that may include identity, dimensions, material grade, relevant certifications, installation context, fire or damage events, maintenance, and disassembly information.

For an appliance, parts, refrigerant, electrical characteristics, repair procedures, compatibility, and service history may matter.

For packaging, material identity and recovery route may matter more than a detailed individual history.

For food, a permanent passport would be absurd; short-lived traceability and safety information matter instead.

The data architecture should match the asset's clock.

This prevents product passports from becoming a universal ideology.

Not everything needs a digital twin.

A simple standardized glass bottle circulating in a deposit pool may need almost no individual identity. The system can manage the container as a class. A sophisticated battery with variable degradation may benefit from individual history.

The more heterogeneous future value depends on the object's personal history, the stronger the case for individual records.

The passport is therefore a response to variance.

It helps a used object remain specific without becoming unknowable.

That makes it the information counterpart to design for disassembly.

Physical reversibility preserves the object.

Informational reversibility preserves understanding.

A product designed to come apart but documented poorly can still be difficult to reuse.

A beautifully documented product glued into a permanent block can still be difficult to reuse.

The two systems have to meet.

This is why the future product-development meeting will eventually include a strange question alongside cost, performance, safety, manufacturing, and user experience:

What will a stranger need to know about this thing twenty years from now?

The stranger might be a repairer.

A building owner.

A recycler.

A firefighter.

A customs officer.

An insurer.

A remanufacturer.

A regulator.

A child inheriting a house.

A city trying to rebuild after a disaster.

A company that does not exist yet.

Design usually privileges the people present at creation. Circular design extends duty toward people absent from the room.

The passport is a message to them.

It says: this is what we made, this is what we know, this is how it has changed, these are the hazards, these are the parts, these are the options we tried to preserve.

That message can save material because it saves future people from having to rediscover the object by destroying it.

A recycler should not need to grind something apart to learn what it contains if the manufacturer already knew.

A repairer should not need to reverse-engineer an assembly if a service procedure exists.

An engineer should not need to assume a beam is worthless because a certificate vanished.

A second-life battery operator should not need to treat every pack as equally uncertain when years of operational data were generated and then discarded.

Information that dies early can cause materials to die early.

That is the reason for a passport.

Not because every object needs paperwork.

Because valuable objects should not have to become anonymous before they become recyclable.
