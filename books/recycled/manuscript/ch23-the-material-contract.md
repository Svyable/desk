# The Material Contract

Every generation inherits two material worlds.

The first is geological.

Ore bodies.

Forests.

Soils.

Aquifers.

Fossil carbon.

Sand.

Stone.

Biomass.

The planetary stocks and flows that existed before us.

The second is artificial.

Cities.

Roads.

Pipes.

Bridges.

Buildings.

Machines.

Vehicles.

Power plants.

Transmission lines.

Data centers.

Ports.

Landfills.

Warehouses.

Billions of products and components made from earlier extraction.

The twentieth century expanded the second world faster than any period before it. The twenty-first century will decide whether that artificial stock becomes an inheritance or merely a temporary staging area between mines and waste.

This is the material contract between generations.

A generation receives natural resources and completed infrastructure. It uses them to live, grow, and build. It passes forward what remains.

The contract is not literal. Future people cannot negotiate with us. That is exactly why the terms can be unfair.

We can consume a resource and leave the waste.

We can build infrastructure and leave maintenance debt.

We can create products whose hazardous afterlives outlast their useful lives.

We can extract materials cheaply while passing climate, pollution, or ecosystem costs forward.

Or we can invest in durable stocks, maintain them, preserve their information, recover their materials, and pass future people both physical assets and options.

Sustainability is often defined as meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. The phrase has become familiar enough to lose some force.

The material version is concrete.

What will they receive?

A bridge maintained or a bridge failing?

A city of adaptable buildings or demolition liabilities?

A stock of batteries designed for recovery or millions of opaque packs with fragmented data?

Landfills full of recoverable material mixed with persistent contaminants?

Mines exhausted after one-use product cycles?

Recycling infrastructure capable of producing high-quality secondary feedstock?

Products whose repair knowledge survives their original companies?

The future is not merely affected by how much we consume.

It is affected by the condition of what we leave.

That insight changes the debate about growth.

A society can invest heavily in material infrastructure and still improve intergenerational sustainability if the infrastructure delivers valuable services for a long time and remains adaptable. A society can consume less material in one year while creating fragile, disposable assets that impose repeated replacement burdens later.

The stock matters.

This is why development cannot be judged only by annual material throughput.

A country building its first modern water network, grid, housing stock, or transit system can have high material demand while creating durable public wealth. A wealthy country replacing functioning kitchens, offices, vehicles, and electronics rapidly can have similar material demand with a very different social return.

The material contract should distinguish build-out from churn.

Build-out creates capacity that did not exist.

Churn replaces capacity that already existed.

Both can be necessary. Churn can introduce efficiency, safety, or technology improvements. Build-out can be wasteful or corrupt. The distinction is not moral by itself.

It is analytically essential.

Global resource policy will fail politically if it treats all tonnes as equal while ignoring what the tonnes are doing for people.

UNEP's Global Resources Outlook emphasizes both rising resource use and enormous inequality in its distribution. High-income countries consume far more resources per person and generate much larger associated environmental impacts than low-income countries.

This creates a responsibility asymmetry.

A mature high-consumption economy has more opportunity to obtain additional prosperity from existing stocks.

Maintain the building.

Use the spare room.

Repair the appliance.

Retrofit the grid.

Share underused equipment.

Recover material.

Adapt infrastructure.

The society without basic infrastructure cannot repair a bridge that was never built.

Circularity is therefore not a universal command to close loops at the same pace everywhere.

It can be a mechanism of material convergence.

Wealthy societies reduce replacement demand and primary extraction intensity.

Developing societies gain more room for the material build-out required for health, housing, energy, mobility, and resilience.

Secondary materials can also become globally available sources of lower-impact feedstock as mature stocks retire.

The result is not a world without extraction.

It is a world in which access to material service becomes less tightly coupled to continuous virgin throughput.

This matters for climate justice because the energy transition itself is materially unequal.

Rich countries can finance new grids, vehicles, storage, and efficient buildings sooner. Many of the minerals and manufacturing processes supporting those technologies occur elsewhere, sometimes in countries receiving a smaller share of the final economic value while bearing local environmental and social costs.

A circular clean-energy system can reduce future pressure by keeping minerals in circulation after the first build-out.

But circularity does not absolve the first extraction.

Mining conditions matter now.

Community rights matter now.

Worker safety matters now.

Tailings matter now.

Water impacts matter now.

Biodiversity matters now.

A promise to recycle the battery in twenty years does not repair a badly governed mine today.

The material contract has terms at every stage.

This is important because sustainability narratives can use future circularity to make present extraction feel morally frictionless.

The opposite is more accurate.

If a material is valuable enough that we expect to preserve it for generations, we should take its first extraction more seriously, not less.

The mine begins a long chain of custody.

The circular economy is not permission to extract carelessly because the atoms will eventually come back.

It is an argument that the first extraction should be treated as a capital event whose value society intends to preserve.

This framing can change resource nationalism too.

Countries rich in minerals reasonably want more than royalties from exporting raw material. They may seek refining, manufacturing, technology transfer, jobs, infrastructure, and greater participation in value chains. A circular future adds another question: who controls the secondary material later?

If batteries manufactured from one country's minerals end up recycled in another country decades later, the geography of resource power changes.

The mineral economy gradually moves above ground and toward the places where products accumulate.

That could reduce the long-term leverage of primary producers and increase the resource security of consuming countries.

This transition has geopolitical consequences.

A fair material contract should recognize them.

Producer countries need opportunities to build capabilities beyond extraction before the world becomes less dependent on some primary resources. Consuming countries should not use circularity as a new form of resource capture in which value migrates permanently away from places that supplied the original material.

There is no simple policy answer.

Trade, development finance, industrial policy, technology, and domestic governance all matter.

The principle is that circularity should not become a second extraction—this time of economic value rather than ore.

The same warning applies to waste trade.

Wealthy countries have historically exported some waste and used products to lower-income countries. Trade can support legitimate reuse and efficient recycling. It can also export environmental burdens, unsafe work, and residuals that local systems are poorly equipped to manage.

The material contract cannot be fulfilled by moving the mess beyond the jurisdiction that consumed the product.

Responsibility should follow the material far enough to ensure that claimed reuse is real and recycling occurs under adequate environmental and labor conditions.

This does not mean every product must remain in the country where it was first sold.

That would waste global secondary markets.

It means the environmental boundary cannot end at customs.

The destination matters.

The afterlife worker matters.

The final residue matters.

The circular economy should improve the conditions of material work, not merely rearrange where difficult work occurs.

Informal waste workers deserve particular attention.

Millions of people around the world collect, sort, repair, trade, dismantle, and recover materials outside formal municipal systems. Their work can contribute significantly to recycling rates and material recovery while exposing workers to unstable income, dangerous substances, fires, sharp materials, smoke, and other hazards.

Formalization is often proposed as the solution. Done badly, formalization can erase livelihoods and hand valuable waste streams to large companies without improving conditions for the people who built the recovery system.

A just circular transition should upgrade the worker, not simply replace the worker.

Safety equipment.

Training.

Secure access to material.

Fair contracts.

Recognition.

Healthcare.

Mechanization of the most dangerous tasks.

Opportunities to move into repair, refurbishment, and higher-value processing.

The hierarchy of material value can become a hierarchy of job quality too.

Sorting mixed waste at the bottom is often dangerous and poorly paid.

Diagnosing a device, remanufacturing equipment, certifying a building component, or maintaining infrastructure can require more skill and capture more value.

Circular policy should help workers climb the same staircase we want materials to climb.

This connects circularity to education.

The future economy needs people who can repair things that did not exist when today's vocational systems were designed.

Battery technicians.

Heat-pump technicians.

Power-electronics repairers.

Building retrofit specialists.

Material auditors.

Deconstruction crews.

Remanufacturing engineers.

Water-reuse operators.

Recycling chemists.

Digital product-passport architects.

Secondary-material quality specialists.

The green transition is often described as a race to build new technology. It is equally a race to maintain and recover the technology after deployment.

A country can install millions of devices and still fail sustainability if it lacks the workforce to keep them operating.

Maintenance capacity is national infrastructure.

The material contract also extends between rich and poor people within the same country.

Durable goods can lower lifetime cost but require higher upfront spending. Efficient appliances can save money but be unaffordable. A home retrofit can reduce bills while renters cannot authorize it. Repair can be cheaper than replacement but still require cash at the wrong moment. Reusable systems can demand deposits that burden low-income users.

Circular policies can unintentionally become regressive if they assume everyone has the liquidity, time, storage, transport, or housing control to act like an ideal circular consumer.

The system should absorb complexity where possible.

Repair financing.

Deposit designs that do not exclude people.

Landlord obligations.

Public retrofit programs.

Accessible refurbished markets.

Reliable warranties.

Convenient take-back.

Public transport and sharing systems.

Circularity should make good material choices cheaper and easier for households under constraint.

The poor already practice forms of circularity because replacement is expensive.

Secondhand markets.

Repair.

Hand-me-downs.

Reuse of containers.

Maintenance beyond official product life.

Affluent sustainability culture sometimes rediscovers these practices and sells them back as lifestyle innovation.

The material contract should be more humble.

Circularity did not begin in a design conference.

It has always been part of life where materials and money are scarce.

The task is to combine that resource discipline with safety, convenience, modern standards, and dignity.

This is especially important in rapidly growing cities. Informal repair economies, construction salvage, refill systems, and reuse practices can be displaced by modernization even when they conserve material. New formal systems can improve hygiene and safety while also increasing disposability.

Development should not require passing through a high-waste stage simply because rich countries did.

Digital payments, product passports, modern sanitation, efficient logistics, and distributed renewable energy can enable different pathways.

A city can build new while designing for future recovery from the beginning.

A country can leapfrog directly to extended producer responsibility.

A manufacturer can enter new markets with repair networks rather than disposable products.

A building code can require adaptability before the building stock becomes old.

The material inheritance can be designed at birth.

This is the hopeful side of the contract.

Later industrializers have the advantage of hindsight.

They can see the waste systems mature economies are now spending heavily to retrofit.

They can avoid some mistakes.

The global circular transition should help finance that avoidance rather than treating environmental standards as a barrier to development.

Development banks can support modern waste collection, recycling, wastewater reuse, repair ecosystems, resilient infrastructure, and cleaner industrial capacity. Trade rules can distinguish legitimate secondhand goods from waste dumping. Technology partnerships can build local processing and remanufacturing capability.

The circular economy can become development infrastructure.

The contract also reaches beyond human generations to ecosystems.

Materials come from landscapes.

Mining changes landscapes.

Forestry changes landscapes.

Agriculture changes landscapes.

Sand extraction changes rivers and coasts.

Waste changes landscapes.

The more service society obtains from material already extracted, the less pressure it needs to place on some new ecosystems at the margin.

That is not a license to treat nature as an inventory serving humans. Biodiversity has value beyond material supply.

Circularity helps precisely because it can reduce the frequency with which prosperity requires opening new extraction fronts.

The extraction dividend becomes a conservation dividend.

This is one reason recycling and reuse should be connected to land policy rather than siloed inside waste agencies.

A tonne of recovered metal can affect future mining demand.

A building reused can affect quarry and cement demand.

Food waste prevented can affect agricultural land pressure.

Water reused can affect freshwater withdrawals.

The loop reaches outward into landscapes even when the circular facility sits inside a city.

The material contract is therefore broader than ownership.

It is a rule about inheritance:

Do not pass forward a smaller set of options merely because destruction was easier today.

Future people should inherit functioning assets where useful.

Recoverable material where function is gone.

Records that explain what they received.

Safer chemistry.

Less maintenance debt.

Smaller uncontrolled waste burdens.

Institutions capable of repair and recovery.

And natural systems less depleted than they would be under repeated one-use extraction.

We cannot guarantee them abundance.

We can stop throwing away some of the abundance already built.

That is not everything sustainability requires.

It is a meaningful term in the contract.
