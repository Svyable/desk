# The Circularity Trap

The easiest way to ruin a good idea is to give it a percentage.

Circularity is especially vulnerable.

A company is 62 percent circular.

A product is 90 percent recyclable.

A city diverts 75 percent of waste from landfill.

A package contains 50 percent recycled content.

A building recovers 95 percent of demolition material.

The numbers can be useful. They can also become substitutes for the thing the numbers were meant to describe.

This book has spent twenty-one chapters arguing that material states are not equivalent. A working motor is not merely a kilogram of copper and steel. A reusable beam is not merely scrap. Edible food is not merely compost feedstock. A battery with remaining service is not merely a mineral deposit. A building adapted to a new use is not merely demolition material avoided.

Any circularity metric that counts mass without counting state risks flattening that hierarchy.

Imagine two factories.

Factory A repairs returned products and sends only irreparable components to recycling. Because the products remain intact, the factory processes relatively few tonnes of recycled material.

Factory B replaces returned products entirely but achieves near-perfect material recycling of the old units.

Which factory is more circular?

A mass-based recycling metric may reward Factory B.

A completed-work metric should often reward Factory A.

The difference is not philosophical. It changes investment.

If managers are rewarded for recycling tonnage, they may build larger recycling systems rather than reducing the flow reaching those systems. If municipalities are rewarded for landfill diversion, they may count crushed demolition concrete while ignoring buildings preserved through adaptive reuse. If packaging companies are rewarded for recycled content, they may optimize the percentage while total packaging volume rises.

Metrics create industries around themselves.

The circular economy needs measurement, but measurement has to resist the instinct to compress everything into one score.

The first useful distinction is between *flow metrics* and *stock metrics*.

Flow metrics describe what moves during a period.

Tonnes of waste generated.

Tonnes collected.

Tonnes recycled.

Tonnes of virgin material purchased.

Products returned.

Components remanufactured.

Water reused.

Stock metrics describe what remains available and useful.

Buildings still in service.

Average product age.

Installed material stock.

Residual asset value.

Share of equipment receiving maintenance.

Inventory of reusable components.

Useful life extension.

Circular policy has historically measured flows more easily because flows pass through gates where they can be weighed.

Stocks are harder.

The building that was not demolished does not cross a scale.

The phone used for an extra year does not trigger a recycling receipt.

The motor repaired in place does not appear in waste statistics.

The absence of an event is difficult to count.

This measurement bias can make prevention invisible.

EPA's waste hierarchy places source reduction and reuse above recycling, yet recycling is often easier to report because facilities record tonnage. The hierarchy says one thing; the data architecture rewards another.

A serious circularity system needs to measure avoided replacement.

That requires counterfactuals.

How many new products would have been purchased without the repair program?

How much new material would a renovation have required if the existing structure were demolished?

How much virgin feedstock did recovered material actually displace?

How much food purchasing fell after waste prevention rather than merely shifting waste elsewhere?

Counterfactuals are uncertain by nature. They depend on assumptions.

That is not a reason to ignore them.

Climate policy already lives with counterfactuals. Energy-efficiency programs estimate savings against baselines. Carbon projects estimate emissions relative to alternatives. Infrastructure planners compare scenarios. Economists estimate substitution.

Circularity needs similar analytical maturity.

The alternative is exact measurement of the wrong thing.

A second distinction is between *technical potential* and *realized outcome*.

Recyclable is potential.

Recycled is outcome.

Repairable is potential.

Repaired is outcome.

Reusable is potential.

Reused is outcome.

A product can score beautifully on design and circulate poorly because return infrastructure is weak. Another product can be difficult to recycle on paper but operate inside a closed industrial loop with near-complete recovery.

The system matters more than the label.

This is why claims about recyclability should increasingly be tied to real collection and processing conditions. A package should not be described as circular merely because a laboratory can recover it under ideal conditions. If the product is sold into a market without the necessary collection or sorting, the real afterlife differs from the technical afterlife.

The same applies to repairability scores.

A device can be opened with ordinary tools but have no affordable replacement parts.

A part can be available but software can reject it.

A manual can exist but labor can cost more than replacement.

Repairability is an ecosystem property.

The score should not pretend the screwdriver is the whole system.

A third distinction is between *gross recovery* and *net displacement*.

Suppose a recycling process recovers ninety kilograms from one hundred kilograms of input. That is a 90 percent recovery yield.

Excellent.

But what happens to the recovered material?

If it replaces ninety kilograms of virgin material in a comparable application, the extraction dividend is strong.

If it replaces another secondary material, the net displacement is lower.

If it becomes a low-value product that would not otherwise have been made, the effect differs again.

If processing requires enough energy or chemicals to create large environmental burdens, recovery yield alone is incomplete.

The circular economy needs both mass balance and consequence.

This is why lifecycle assessment remains important even though it can be complicated and assumption-heavy. A loop that looks elegant in a flow diagram can perform poorly once transport, washing, processing, energy, breakage, replacement rates, and behavior are included.

Reusable packaging is the obvious example. The environmental break-even point depends on how many times the container actually returns and what the logistics system requires. A durable container lost after one use can be worse than the disposable product it replaced.

The metric should count successful cycles, not intended cycles.

A fourth distinction is between *circularity* and *toxicity*.

Material can circulate perfectly and remain harmful.

A recycled stream contaminated with persistent chemicals can spread those chemicals across new products. A high diversion rate can reward uses that disperse contaminants rather than isolate them. A circularity score that treats all retained mass as positive can encourage the wrong loop.

The necessary-exit chapter exists because circularity needs a negative term.

Some material leaving circulation is success.

A hazardous chemical phased out and destroyed safely reduces circularity in a crude mass sense while improving environmental performance.

The metric must know the difference.

A fifth distinction is between *rate* and *scale*.

A company can reduce material per product while selling enough additional products that total material use rises.

A country can increase recycling rates while waste generation grows faster.

UNEP's Global Waste Management Outlook projects municipal solid waste rising from roughly 2.1 billion tonnes in 2023 to 3.8 billion tonnes by 2050 under prevailing trajectories. Improving waste management is essential, but a higher recycling rate applied to a rapidly growing waste stream can still leave society processing enormous additional volumes.

Percentages hide denominators.

Always look at the denominator.

This is the rebound problem in accounting form.

A city that moves from 40 percent recycling of one million tonnes to 60 percent recycling of two million tonnes can celebrate a better rate while sending the same 400,000 additional tonnes to disposal? Let's calculate carefully: the first system disposes 600,000 tonnes; the second disposes 800,000. The rate improved. Disposal increased.

Nothing fraudulent occurred.

The metric answered a different question.

Circularity needs absolute material-flow indicators alongside rates.

Virgin material consumed.

Waste generated.

Hazardous residuals.

Energy used in recovery.

Landfill volume.

Primary extraction displaced.

These keep the scale visible.

A sixth distinction is between *ownership loops* and *environmental loops*.

A company can take back products and keep them inside its own system. That looks circular from the company's boundary. If returned products are destroyed prematurely and replaced with new ones, the closed ownership loop may still have high throughput.

Conversely, a product can leave the original manufacturer and circulate through independent repair, resale, and recycling markets. The manufacturer has no closed loop, yet the material can remain highly circular at the economy level.

Corporate circularity is not the same as system circularity.

This matters because companies naturally measure what they control.

A take-back rate is easy to claim.

The broader afterlife may happen elsewhere.

Policy should avoid rewarding vertical control simply because it is measurable.

The goal is preserved value, not manufacturer possession.

A seventh distinction is between *value* and *mass*.

A kilogram of working electronics can contain more completed work than a kilogram of crushed concrete. A kilogram of a critical catalyst can have different strategic significance from a kilogram of ordinary aggregate. A single reused industrial component can avoid a complex manufacturing chain disproportionate to its mass.

Mass is environmentally relevant. Extraction, transport, and waste are physical.

But circular priority sometimes follows complexity or impact rather than weight.

A city that focuses only on heavy construction debris may miss tiny electronics containing scarce materials and hazardous components. A company chasing tonnage may ignore high-value parts because they barely affect the chart.

The material hierarchy needs multidimensional measurement.

This can sound like an argument for dashboards so complicated nobody uses them.

That would be another failure.

The answer is not to measure everything.

It is to choose a small number of metrics tied to the decision being made.

A product designer needs different metrics from a city waste manager.

A recycler needs yield, contamination, energy, and output quality.

A procurement officer needs lifetime cost, warranty, performance, and material requirements.

A manufacturer needs return rates, repair rates, remanufacturing yield, residual value, and afterlife cost.

A national government needs primary-material demand, secondary supply, waste generation, material productivity, and environmental impacts.

One universal circularity score would destroy information in the same way melting a beam destroys geometry.

Measurement itself has a hierarchy.

Preserve the dimensions that matter before compressing them.

The digital-product-passport era will make this temptation stronger because data availability will explode. Products can carry material composition, repair history, carbon footprints, recycled content, durability information, and other attributes. Algorithms can combine them into scores instantly.

The existence of data does not mean the aggregation is wise.

Different environmental impacts are not naturally commensurable. Carbon, toxicity, biodiversity, water use, labor conditions, repairability, and durability can conflict. Weighting them requires values and context.

A transparent multidimensional record can be more honest than a single green grade.

Consumers may still need simplified information. Simplification should link back to evidence and disclose what the score does not mean.

This is another place where sustainability can learn from nutrition labels. A nutrition label does not usually declare one food universally good or bad. It presents several dimensions and lets context matter, even if front-of-pack systems add simplified signals.

A material label can do something similar.

Durability.

Repair support.

Material composition.

Recycled content.

Take-back.

Hazardous substances.

Expected energy use.

The consumer may not inspect every field. The fields remain available to procurement systems, repairers, regulators, and researchers.

The passport becomes infrastructure rather than advertising.

Metrics also require falsification.

Every circularity program should ask what evidence would show it is not working.

A reusable-container system should specify a minimum return rate or cycle count below which the model underperforms the disposable alternative.

A repair program should measure whether replacement purchases actually decline.

A recycled-content policy should examine whether secondary supply expands or merely shifts between buyers.

A building-reuse policy should track operating performance and adaptation cost, not assume preservation is always best.

A battery second-life program should compare additional service with safety, economics, and delayed material recovery.

The metric should be capable of delivering bad news.

That is how it remains a measurement rather than a marketing asset.

This principle is especially important as circularity enters corporate reporting. Once executive compensation, investor scores, or regulatory compliance depend on a metric, the organization will optimize it.

Optimization is not corruption. It is what organizations do.

The metric has to survive being optimized.

A recycling-rate target that ignores prevention will produce recycling activity.

A recycled-content target that ignores total material use can produce heavier recycled products.

A reuse target that ignores actual displacement can produce warehouses full of nominally reusable inventory.

A repairability target that ignores parts pricing can produce technically openable but economically disposable products.

The best metrics are hard to game because they sit closer to the underlying objective.

The underlying objective of this book is not circularity.

It is human service with less unnecessary extraction, less pollution, less waste, and more preserved useful work.

Circularity is a strategy.

That distinction protects us from circularity theater.

A company may choose a linear pathway in one case because the lifecycle evidence is better. A hazardous substance may exit permanently. A highly efficient new product may replace an old one. A recycling process may beat a cumbersome reuse system.

The strategy should be allowed to lose individual comparisons without losing its broader value.

That is what mature frameworks do.

They guide decisions rather than predetermine them.

The final metric is therefore a question rather than a percentage:

How much new environmental burden did we avoid while delivering the service people needed?

The answer can be estimated in carbon, material, water, toxicity, cost, and other relevant terms.

It will rarely be one number.

Good.

The physical economy is not one number either.

The pursuit of a perfect circularity score can become another form of waste: expensive reporting that creates the appearance of precision while destroying the information needed to make good choices.

We should not recycle complexity into a single metric too early.

Even ideas have higher useful states.
