# Food Has a Higher Use

A compost bin can be the wrong place for a tomato.

That sounds like an attack on composting until the tomato is placed back into its full history.

A field was prepared. Seed was produced. Water moved. Fertilizer, machinery, labor, land, sunlight, fuel, packaging, refrigeration, transport, warehousing, retail space, and time helped carry the tomato from biology into food. Someone selected it, harvested it, sorted it, perhaps cooled it, packed it, moved it, displayed it, and offered it to a person.

If the tomato is spoiled, composting can recover nutrients and organic matter. Anaerobic digestion can recover energy and produce digestate. Those are useful afterlives.

If the tomato is edible, those afterlives are downgrades.

The tomato's highest useful state is food.

This seems obvious, yet food waste reveals how easily environmental systems can celebrate recovery after abandoning the original purpose of a product.

UNEP's Food Waste Index Report 2024 estimated that 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022 at the retail, food-service, and household levels—roughly one fifth of food available to consumers. Households accounted for the majority. The report translated the scale into a vivid fact: more than one billion meals' worth of food are wasted each day while hunger persists elsewhere.

The instinctive environmental response is to find a better destination for the waste.

Compost it.

Digest it.

Feed it to animals where safe and lawful.

Convert it into another product.

Those destinations matter. But the EPA's Wasted Food Scale puts prevention, donation, and upcycling above lower pathways such as composting, anaerobic digestion, incineration, sewer disposal, and landfill. The hierarchy reflects the same logic running through this book: preserve the highest useful function before recovering lower-order value.

Food makes the principle emotionally legible because nobody mistakes compost for dinner.

The challenge is that food is perishable in a way steel is not.

A beam can wait in a warehouse.

A ripe peach cannot.

Food's afterlife clock can be measured in hours or days. This makes information, logistics, and timing more important than almost anywhere else in the circular economy.

A restaurant can have surplus food at 10 p.m. that a shelter could use at 10:30 and that becomes unsafe or undesirable by morning. A farmer can have a field of vegetables whose market price suddenly falls below the cost of harvest. A supermarket can reject produce for cosmetic reasons. A household can buy more than it can eat because package sizes, promotions, schedules, and plans did not line up.

The food is not waste because its molecules changed.

It becomes waste because the coordination window closed.

This is why food waste is an information problem before it is a composting problem.

Who has surplus?

When will it become available?

How long is it safe?

Who can collect it?

Who can use it?

What temperature must it remain at?

What allergens does it contain?

Can it legally be donated?

Can a kitchen accept it in the quantity offered?

Does the recipient have storage?

The same city can contain surplus food and hungry people while failing to connect them because time and logistics separate the two.

Technology can help, but again software is only part of the system. A surplus-food marketplace can identify a match, yet somebody still needs to package, store, transport, receive, and distribute the food safely. Rescue organizations need refrigerators, vehicles, workers, volunteers, liability protection, predictable funding, and relationships with donors.

The reverse supply chain for food is a race against biology.

Prevention is therefore especially valuable.

Every edible item prevented from becoming surplus avoids the need to rescue it at all.

A retailer can improve forecasting.

A restaurant can adjust portions.

A household can plan meals and understand date labels.

A manufacturer can choose package sizes that fit real consumption patterns.

A cafeteria can measure what returns uneaten and redesign service.

A farm can find secondary markets before harvest decisions are made.

The environmental benefit of prevention is larger than the mass of waste avoided because food contains an upstream production chain. Preventing waste can avoid the land, water, energy, fertilizer, processing, refrigeration, transport, and packaging associated with food that would never nourish anyone.

This is the completed-work thesis in biological form.

A carrot is not merely carbon, water, nitrogen, potassium, and minerals.

It is agriculture completed into nutrition.

Composting recovers part of the material cycle after the nutritional function has been lost.

That is why the word *organic recycling* can mislead. It can describe useful processing of unavoidable food scraps and inedible material. It should not make us indifferent to the meal that could have happened first.

Food also exposes the moral limits of efficiency language.

Surplus-food donation is not a substitute for a functioning social safety net. A system that relies on unpredictable excess from supermarkets to feed people should not congratulate itself for circularity while ignoring poverty. Food banks can rescue value and serve urgent needs without making retail waste the foundation of food security.

The highest social use and the highest material use do not always reduce to the same optimization.

A restaurant may prevent waste most efficiently by buying less, leaving less surplus for donation. That is still a better environmental result even if the donation program receives fewer meals.

A serious system can hold both truths: hunger deserves direct policy, and edible surplus should not be destroyed while people need food.

Food waste also demonstrates why date labels matter.

Consumers encounter phrases such as best before, sell by, use by, or other formulations that can mix quality guidance with safety guidance. Confusion can cause edible products to be discarded prematurely. Standardized, well-understood labeling can preserve food's useful state by reducing uncertainty.

The underlying principle is the same as a battery passport, in miniature.

Information extends life when it helps a user distinguish "not new" from "not safe."

Uncertainty shortens life.

A person who cannot tell whether yogurt is still good may throw it away. A buyer who cannot tell whether a used beam is structurally sound may reject it. A second-life operator who cannot tell whether a battery has been damaged may recycle it. In each case, missing trust pushes the object downward through the hierarchy.

Food simply makes the decision happen faster.

Packaging sits in an interesting position because it can both create waste and prevent it.

A package has an environmental burden. It can also extend shelf life, prevent damage, provide portion control, protect against contamination, and communicate storage instructions. Removing packaging can be counterproductive if food waste rises enough to outweigh the saved material.

This is another place where anti-material instincts fail.

The correct unit is the system, not the package.

A slightly more complex package that prevents a high-impact food from spoiling may be environmentally sensible. A lavish package that adds little protection may not be. Reusable food containers can work in closed systems. Lightweight disposable packaging can sometimes outperform heavy reusable formats if return rates are poor.

The hierarchy has to remain empirical.

This does not mean paralysis by analysis.

Some interventions are robust across many settings. Better inventory management. Clearer labeling. Donation pathways. Smaller or flexible portions. Measurement of kitchen waste. Cold-chain improvements. Secondary markets for cosmetically imperfect produce. Planning around predictable events. Training staff to distinguish edible surplus from unavoidable scraps.

Measurement changes behavior because food waste is often invisible inside purchasing systems.

A commercial kitchen may know food cost as a percentage of revenue while knowing little about which ingredients repeatedly enter the bin. Weighing and categorizing discarded food can reveal patterns: preparation trim, spoilage, overproduction, plate waste, forecasting errors.

The bin becomes a diagnostic sensor.

That is a more interesting use of waste data than simply reporting tonnage diverted from landfill.

If a kitchen sends all food waste to composting and celebrates a high diversion rate, it can conceal overproduction. A lower compost tonnage can be evidence of improvement if fewer edible ingredients were purchased unnecessarily.

Again the successful circular economy may generate less material for recyclers.

This creates an important accounting principle: prevention must be visible even though it produces no recovered output.

How do you count the meal never wasted?

How do you count the building never demolished?

How do you count the phone kept an extra two years?

How do you count the replacement motor never manufactured?

Our environmental statistics are often better at counting flows than avoided flows. A tonne sent to compost can be weighed. A tonne never purchased because forecasting improved is a counterfactual.

The bias toward what is easy to weigh can push policy downstream.

Food policy has begun correcting this through prevention targets and measurement methodologies that treat waste reduction as an outcome in its own right. The broader material economy needs the same discipline.

Upcycling adds another layer.

Food byproducts or surplus ingredients can sometimes become new food products. Spent grain from brewing can become ingredients. Imperfect produce can become sauces. Trimmings can become stocks. Whey, once treated as a disposal problem, has become a valuable ingredient stream in many contexts. Citrus peels, coffee grounds, and other residues can feed food, chemical, agricultural, or material applications depending on safety and economics.

The interesting question is where the boundary between waste and coproduct moves.

Industry becomes more circular when it recognizes useful outputs earlier.

A brewery that designs a reliable market for spent grain is not merely recycling waste. It has turned a side stream into a product specification. A food processor that separates clean byproducts before they mix with contaminants preserves higher options. Once streams are mixed, wet, spoiled, or chemically contaminated, lower-value processing becomes more likely.

Separation is preservation.

The same is true in buildings, plastics, metals, and electronics. Clean streams retain options. Mixed streams lose them.

Food makes that physical law visible because a clean tray of edible surplus can be donated while the same food scraped into a mixed garbage bin becomes unrecoverable for human consumption in seconds.

The act of mixing is an irreversible decision.

This suggests a hierarchy inside facilities.

Edible surplus should remain identified as food.

Inedible but safe coproducts can be routed to other productive uses.

Animal feed can be appropriate under relevant health rules.

Industrial uses may recover value.

Anaerobic digestion can recover energy from organics.

Composting can return nutrients and organic matter to soils.

Landfill is a last resort because organic decomposition can generate methane and because the upstream production effort is entirely lost.

The exact ordering of some middle pathways depends on local conditions, and regulatory frameworks differ. The key is that facilities should not mix material in ways that foreclose higher uses before those uses are considered.

Food systems also connect circularity to water, land, and climate in ways that make prevention unusually powerful.

Agriculture is a major user of freshwater and land. Fertilizer production uses energy. Livestock and rice generate methane. Refrigeration consumes electricity. Transport uses fuel. Deforestation can be connected to agricultural expansion. Throwing away food throws away a share of those resource burdens without delivering nutrition.

This does not mean each discarded apple carries a precise moral quantity of climate damage. Food impacts vary enormously by product and production system. A kilogram of beef, tomatoes from a heated greenhouse, dry grains, and seasonal vegetables do not have equivalent footprints.

Mass alone can mislead.

A waste-prevention program that saves high-impact foods can have a larger environmental effect than one that saves a greater weight of low-impact material. Again, the system needs more than a tonnage target.

The social texture matters too.

Household waste is shaped by income, family size, work schedules, food access, cultural habits, refrigeration, storage space, transport, package sizes, and the unpredictability of life. Advice to plan perfectly can sound absurd to a parent whose schedule changes daily or a household that must buy whatever is affordable in bulk.

Circularity should reduce the burden on ordinary people, not convert every refrigerator into a moral examination.

Better system design can make the low-waste choice easier.

Flexible package sizes.

Resealable packaging where useful.

Clear labels.

Retail pricing that does not punish smaller quantities.

Easy freezing guidance.

Donation channels for businesses.

Apps or tools that help households remember what they have without demanding obsessive tracking.

Community fridges where appropriate.

Retail markdown systems that sell food before expiration rather than after demand disappears.

The principle is familiar now: design the reverse path before the surplus occurs.

Food also complicates the concept of permanence that appears in other chapters. A building can be designed to last a century. Food should not last forever.

Sustainability is not maximum lifetime.

It is enough lifetime to deliver the intended service with minimal unnecessary loss.

A tomato that reaches a plate has completed its purpose even though its material will be metabolized and transformed. We should not preserve the tomato after eating. The highest use was consumption.

This helps clarify the phrase *preserving completed work*.

Preservation does not always mean preserving the object.

It means preserving the intended value long enough for that value to be realized.

For food, the completed work is nutrition and enjoyment.

For fuel, it may be energy, though a decarbonizing economy should choose better energy carriers.

For medicine, it is therapeutic use.

For a filter, it is contamination removed.

Some products are consumptive by nature. Their circular strategy focuses on preventing unnecessary production, recovering byproducts, choosing safer chemistry, and returning materials where possible after service.

This prevents the book's thesis from becoming an absurd command to keep every object intact.

The highest useful state is contextual.

The tomato belongs on the plate.

After the plate, what remains belongs in a different hierarchy.

That sequence is the whole point.

A compost bin can be an excellent place for a tomato after the tomato has truly stopped being food.

The mistake is sending it there early and calling the recovery a complete success.
