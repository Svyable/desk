# Steal the Machine

*What Real Reverse Engineering Leaves Behind*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a useful mistake hiding inside the phrase *reverse engineering*. It sounds like a room. Put the object in the room, close the door, add clearances, wait thirty years. Out comes the future.

That is not what the public record of foreign-materiel exploitation looks like.

When states acquire an adversary's machine, the machine creates work. It has to be found, moved, hidden, inventoried, disassembled, measured, powered, flown or fired or otherwise made to betray itself. Specialists have to be read into the problem. Test infrastructure has to exist. Contractors may have to fabricate fixtures or reproduce components. Intelligence has to be extracted and translated into doctrine, countermeasures, procurement or new engineering. The secret can be real while much of its industrial shadow remains stubbornly physical.

This book starts with cases where we know the secret was real: Soviet MiGs acquired and tested by the United States; captured missiles and weapons exploited by defense intelligence; Project AZORIAN, in which the CIA built an extraordinary ship and capture system to recover part of a Soviet ballistic-missile submarine from more than three miles down. These are not analogies offered as proof against extraordinary claims. They are controls. They show what retrieval and exploitation mean when the noun being retrieved is not in dispute.

Only after that control is built does the book turn to allegations that the United States recovered craft of unknown or non-human origin and attempted to reverse-engineer them. Those allegations deserve neither automatic belief nor automatic ridicule. They deserve the same question asked of every other exploitation program: **what traces should this activity produce, and which of those traces actually exist?**

The evidence classes remain separate throughout.

**Class 1 — verified, declassified, documented.** Official records, authenticated archival material, contemporaneous documents, inspectable hardware, and program histories whose existence and basic purpose are established. Project AZORIAN and documented U.S. foreign-materiel exploitation belong here.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete.** Named testimony, reporting, recollection or documentary fragments from sources with plausible access, where important parts of the chain remain unavailable or unverified.

**Class 3 — disputed allegations.** Claims of retrieval, bodies, non-human origin, hidden reverse-engineering programs, extraordinary materials or technical breakthroughs for which independent public confirmation is absent or materially contested.

**Class 4 — speculative synthesis.** My attempt to infer what a genuinely unprecedented exploitation program would have to look like: its labor, metrology, containment, testing, security, waste, procurement, failure modes, scientific bottlenecks and institutional scars. This class is not evidence that such a program exists.

A patent proves that somebody filed a patent. A clearance proves access to something, not to the thing a later story assigns to it. A witness can establish that a claim was made and, depending on proximity, may substantially raise its credibility; testimony does not transmute itself into a recovered vehicle. A classified program can be misidentified by people outside its compartment. An official denial can be wrong. None of those sentences cancels the others.

The strongest skeptical model is not that governments never keep astonishing secrets. They plainly do. It is that authentic secrecy, authentic foreign-materiel exploitation, real black programs, folklore, inference, compartmented glimpses and repeated stories can become entangled until the existence of the machinery of secrecy is mistaken for proof of the extraordinary object allegedly inside it.

The strongest extraordinary model is harder and therefore more interesting: a small, deeply compartmented exploitation effort could preserve the most diagnostic evidence inside a narrow access structure while distributing ordinary-looking engineering tasks outward under cover. If that model is right, absence of a single public program name means little. But it does not mean evidence disappears. It changes where we should look.

That is the wager of this book. Not: *Could they hide it?*

Something more mechanical.

**If they had it, what would they have to do with it?**
