# STEAL THE MACHINE

## How States Learn From Technology They Did Not Build

### A note on what counts

This book uses four evidence classes and does not let evidence migrate upward by rhetoric.

**Class 1 — verified / declassified / documented.** Released government records, official histories, contemporaneous technical literature, physical artifacts with provenance, court or congressional records, and peer-reviewed measurements.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete.** Attributable testimony or reporting from people plausibly positioned to know, where important records, samples, denominators or independent confirmation remain absent.

**Class 3 — disputed allegation.** Claims of programs, events, materials, access or capabilities without independent confirmation sufficient to establish the underlying proposition.

**Class 4 — speculative synthesis.** My inference. Useful only insofar as it makes predictions that can lose.

The central discipline is simple: **institutional precedent is not event evidence**. If the CIA once recovered Soviet machinery in secret, that proves a state can attempt secret recovery. It does not prove that a later alleged recovery happened, that its object was anomalous, or that its origin was non-human.

The inverse mistake is just as bad. A spectacular claim does not become impossible because the required bureaucracy sounds too large. AZORIAN was large, expensive, strange and successfully concealed during its operational phase. The correct question is not whether a secret program *could* exist. It is what physical and organizational traces its claimed scale should generate, which of those traces secrecy could suppress, and which should leak into the world anyway.

### Governing test

For every retrieval/reverse-engineering claim, reconstruct the stack:

**target → localization → access → recovery → custody → transport → containment → characterization → exploitation → testing → downstream consequence.**

Then ask where the evidence actually enters the stack. A witness to budgeting is not a witness to material. A witness to a container is not a witness to origin. An isotopic anomaly is not a chain of custody. A program nickname is not a capability. A security compartment is not a recovered craft.

This book will be generous about what secrecy can hide and severe about what physics, logistics and engineering still require.
