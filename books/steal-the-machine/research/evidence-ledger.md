# Evidence ledger — STEAL THE MACHINE

## Class 1 — verified / declassified / documented

### Project AZORIAN

CIA publicly documents a six-year effort to recover part of Soviet Golf II submarine K-129 after the United States located the wreck on the Pacific floor. CIA describes the wreck as roughly 16,500 feet deep and the target section as approximately 1,750 tons / 132 feet. A purpose-built ship, Hughes Glomar Explorer, used a large capture vehicle, pipe string, hydraulic lifting system and an internal moon pool. Howard Hughes supplied the commercial deep-sea-mining cover. During the 1974 lift the submarine section broke apart; only a portion remained in the capture vehicle. Six Soviet submariners were recovered and buried at sea. Planning for a second attempt ended after public exposure.

Primary/official trace:
- CIA, Project AZORIAN museum history: https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/exhibit/project-azorian/
- CIA, “The Exposing of Project AZORIAN,” 17 Mar 2020: https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/the-exposing-of-project-azorian/
- CIA, *The Glomar Explorer in Film and Print*, Studies in Intelligence 56(1), 2012, with citation to the declassified 1978 internal history: https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/The-Glomar-Explorer.pdf
- CIA, “Honoring the Mission of Project AZORIAN,” 29 Jul 2022: https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/honoring-the-mission-of-project-azorian/

Important contradiction to preserve: CIA public pages differ on details such as distance northwest of Hawaii (roughly 1,500 vs 1,800 miles), duration at the site, and whether the target is described as the submarine or a section. Manuscript must normalize against the declassified internal history before treating those values as exact.

### Positive-control proposition

Documented foreign-materiel exploitation establishes that intelligence services have historically sought adversary hardware for technical intelligence. AZORIAN additionally demonstrates that extraordinary recovery infrastructure can be hidden behind a plausible commercial cover for a bounded period. It does **not** establish any modern anomalous-material program.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- Public accounts that specify exactly what intelligence was recovered from the raised K-129 section require reconciliation against released CIA history. CIA says the intelligence made a significant contribution to understanding Soviet strategic capabilities but its current public summaries do not enumerate every recovered technical/intelligence item.
- Contractor recollections are valuable for engineering practice and compartmentalization but must be separated from documentary proof of mission-level outcomes.
- Historical exploitation of acquired Soviet aircraft should be reconstructed from official HAVE DOUGHNUT / HAVE DRILL / HAVE FERRY records before using MiG exploitation as a second control.

## Class 3 — disputed allegations

- Claims of contemporary U.S. crash-retrieval or reverse-engineering programs involving non-human craft/material remain allegations unless independently supported by documents, hardware with provenance, or other evidence sufficient to establish the underlying program and material.
- Claims that particular isotopic, layered or metamaterial samples derive from anomalous vehicles must be evaluated separately at the levels of composition, manufacture, provenance and origin.
- A security clearance, SAP/SCI/Q vocabulary, contractor affiliation or claimed briefing is not by itself evidence of recovered non-human technology.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis / open conjecture

### Retrieval stack

Real retrieval has conservation laws of bureaucracy. Mass must be moved. People must be cleared. Machines must be built. Samples must be logged. Tests consume time, instruments and expertise. The larger and stranger the claimed object, the more places the program must touch ordinary engineering.

This does not imply inevitable public disclosure. It predicts **trace structure**: procurement oddities, facility requirements, specialized contractors, transport constraints, test signatures, budget displacement, safety regimes, documentation, personnel overlap and downstream technical questions. The hypothesis loses if well-documented historical secret exploitation programs routinely leave none of these traces even after declassification windows mature.

### Reverse-engineering distance

Possession and comprehension are different achievements. Exploiting a foreign machine built inside a shared technological civilization is easier than exploiting an artifact whose materials, manufacturing, energy source or operating principles fall outside the exploiter’s scientific stack. The more exotic the claimed origin, the less justified the phrase “reverse engineered” becomes without demonstrated intermediate steps.

## Quantitative extraction burden

For each historical control: target mass/dimensions; location uncertainty; depth/altitude/environment; recovery hardware; lift/transport capacity; personnel; contractor count; schedule; cost where releasable; facility footprint; number of samples/components; analysis modalities; failure rate; intelligence yield; time to downstream use; cover mechanism; exposure pathway.

For each anomalous claim: same fields, with `unknown` preserved rather than filled by inference.

## Defeat condition

The book’s governing synthesis is weakened if mature declassification of genuine high-complexity exploitation programs shows that operational scale has little relationship to recoverable physical/organizational trace, or if a modern anomalous-retrieval claim produces authenticated primary records and independently examinable material whose provenance and properties survive the full retrieval stack. In the latter case, the book must update toward the extraordinary evidence rather than explain it away.
