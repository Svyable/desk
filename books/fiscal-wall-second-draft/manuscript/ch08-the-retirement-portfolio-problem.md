# The Retirement Portfolio Problem

Retirement is a promise made by a younger person to an older stranger who happens to be the same person.

Finance exists partly to keep the two from cheating each other.

A worker saves today because future income from labor will eventually stop. A pension fund collects contributions because promises made to current workers will become checks written decades later. An insurer takes premiums now in exchange for claims that may arrive far in the future. Social Security collects payroll taxes from today's workers and pays today's beneficiaries under rules that create expectations for tomorrow's.

Every retirement system is therefore a machine for moving purchasing power through time.

Interest rates are inside the machine.

When safe yields fall toward zero, retirement becomes more expensive in one sense. A dollar saved today produces less future income without taking additional risk. When yields rise, new saving can earn more. But the transition between those regimes can damage the value of bonds already owned and change the present value of promised liabilities.

This is why retirees and pension funds can feel both relieved and injured by the same rate increase.

The fiscal wall runs through that contradiction.

Start with the simplest retiree.

Imagine someone with $1 million who wants to spend $40,000 a year without exhausting principal quickly. When Treasury bills and high-quality bonds yield less than one percent, the safe portfolio cannot generate the desired income. The retiree can spend principal, lower consumption, delay retirement, or take more market risk.

At a five-percent Treasury yield, the picture changes. Safe fixed income can cover much more of the spending goal.

For that retiree, higher federal borrowing costs arrive as higher income.

The Treasury's expense is the household's yield.

This fact complicates every claim that interest is “money wasted.”

Interest paid to domestic holders stays in the domestic income stream. Mutual funds distribute it. Pensions use it to meet obligations. Banks and insurers earn it. Households spend or reinvest it.

That does not make the federal cost imaginary.

A dollar of tax revenue used for interest cannot simultaneously fund another federal priority unless the government borrows another dollar. The distributional effect depends on who owns the debt and how the interest is taxed and spent.

The same payment can support private retirement security and reduce public fiscal room.

The United States is unusual because its public and private retirement systems meet inside the Treasury market.

Social Security is financed primarily through payroll taxes and trust-fund accounting, not a private portfolio of marketable securities. The trust funds hold special Treasury securities. Those securities represent claims of one part of the federal government on another and are part of gross federal debt, not debt held by the public.

Economically, the ability to pay future Social Security benefits still depends on future taxes, borrowing capacity, and the size of the economy.

The trust-fund mechanism matters legally and politically because it records dedicated revenues and provides rules for benefit authority. It does not create a vault of independent economic resources outside the federal government.

As the population ages, the relationship between workers and beneficiaries changes. CBO's 2026 projections placed the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund on a path toward exhaustion in the early 2030s under then-current law. The exact date will move with wages, employment, demographics, immigration, disability, mortality, and policy.

The direction matters because it creates another fiscal deadline near the same period in which interest costs are projected to keep rising.

Retirement politics and debt politics are approaching each other.

This is one reason the fiscal wall cannot be solved by treating Social Security as either untouchable or solely responsible.

Social Security is a large program with a dedicated financing structure. It is also a central part of retirement security for tens of millions of people. Changes to taxes, benefit formulas, retirement ages, or covered earnings can improve solvency. Abrupt changes can harm people who organized decades of work around existing rules.

The right time for retirement reform is therefore early.

Duration is mercy when it is used to phase change.

A worker twenty-five years from retirement can respond to a gradual rule change in ways a seventy-year-old cannot. The longer policymakers wait, the more adjustment must be concentrated on people with less time to adapt.

The same principle appears in pension finance.

A defined-benefit pension promises a future stream of payments. Actuaries discount those payments back to a present value using assumptions about interest rates and investment returns. Small changes in those assumptions can produce large changes in reported funding status because the liabilities extend for decades.

Low rates increase the present value of a fixed future payment when the discount rate falls. At the same time, low safe yields can reduce expected returns on the assets used to meet the payment.

That combination pressures pension sponsors.

Some respond by increasing contributions.

Some change benefits for new workers.

Some shift toward defined-contribution plans.

Some take more investment risk.

The last response is where the zero-rate era became dangerous.

If a pension assumes it must earn seven percent and safe bonds yield two, the portfolio can reach for return through equities, private equity, real estate, private credit, hedge funds, infrastructure, or leverage.

These assets may be entirely appropriate in a diversified long-horizon portfolio. A pension with patient capital can tolerate volatility that an individual retiree cannot.

The risk appears when the return assumption becomes a requirement rather than an estimate.

If the plan needs seven percent to look funded, it may choose assets because the accounting target demands them.

The portfolio starts serving the assumption.

Higher bond yields can improve this situation over time. A pension that can buy long high-quality bonds yielding four or five percent may need less risky return elsewhere. Insurers can match long-dated liabilities more comfortably. Retirees can hold more safe assets.

This is the healthy side of the post-zero world.

The transition is still difficult because existing bond portfolios lose market value when yields rise.

A pension with long liabilities may be less concerned about mark-to-market losses if the duration of assets and liabilities is matched. In fact, higher discount rates can reduce the present value of liabilities at the same time asset values fall.

The net effect depends on the structure.

This is why “bonds fell” tells us little about a pension's actual solvency.

Liability matching is a balance-sheet problem, not an asset-performance contest.

The United Kingdom's 2022 gilt episode showed what happens when liability matching becomes entangled with leverage and liquidity. Some pension schemes used liability-driven investment strategies involving derivatives and leveraged gilt exposures to hedge long-term liabilities while keeping capital available for growth assets. When gilt yields rose violently, collateral calls forced sales, which pushed yields higher and generated more collateral pressure.

The Bank of England intervened temporarily to stabilize the market.

The pensions were not wrong to care about duration.

The system became vulnerable to the speed and liquidity demands of the hedge.

This is a useful analogy for the broader Treasury market.

A portfolio can be economically hedged over thirty years and still fail at noon because it cannot meet a margin call.

Solvency and liquidity live on different clocks.

Retirement systems understand this better than most institutions because their promises are so long.

The federal government should too.

The fiscal wall is partly a mismatch between the horizon of political decisions and the horizon of obligations. An election occurs every two years for the House, four for the presidency, six for the Senate. Retirement promises run for lifetimes. Treasury bonds can run thirty years. Demographic changes unfold over generations.

A political system optimized for the next election can create a duration mismatch even without any financial derivatives.

Benefits are promised long.

Taxes are decided short.

Debt fills the gap.

The retirement portfolio problem also has an ownership dimension.

Who benefits when the federal government pays more interest?

Treasury securities are held through many channels. The Federal Reserve holds a large portfolio. Mutual funds hold Treasuries. Banks, insurers, pensions, foreign institutions, households, hedge funds, and state and local governments all appear in the ownership structure.

CBO reported that as of September 2025 roughly seventy percent of debt held by the public was held by domestic entities and about thirty percent by foreign investors, using Treasury and other data. The largest domestic categories included the Federal Reserve and mutual funds.

These categories blur the ultimate person.

A mutual fund holding Treasuries may belong to a teacher's 401(k).

An insurer may be investing premiums for future annuity payments.

A bank may be holding liquidity for depositors.

The Federal Reserve returns most net earnings to Treasury when earnings are positive, though higher rates after 2022 changed that flow because interest paid on reserve balances and other liabilities rose.

Foreign ownership may sit behind pension beneficiaries abroad or sovereign reserve policy.

The slogan “we owe it to ourselves” is therefore partly true and badly incomplete.

Some interest circulates domestically.

Some goes abroad.

Even domestic interest changes distribution because taxpayers and bondholders are not the same people in the same proportions.

A young worker with little financial wealth can help service debt held in a retirement account owned by someone older and wealthier. The same young worker may later become the bondholder. Intergenerational transfer is not a one-way line.

It is a web.

The retirement portfolio problem is especially sensitive to inflation.

A nominal bond promises dollars, not purchasing power. Unexpected inflation reduces the real value of those dollars. For a government with fixed-rate nominal debt, that can reduce the real burden of old liabilities.

For a retiree living on fixed nominal income, it can be devastating.

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities exist partly to separate those risks. Their principal adjusts with inflation, protecting investors while preserving real obligations for the government.

Social Security benefits also receive cost-of-living adjustments tied to inflation measures, so the federal government cannot simply inflate away all retirement-related obligations. Higher inflation can reduce the real value of nominal debt while raising indexed payments and future nominal borrowing costs.

The idea of “solving” the fiscal wall through inflation therefore asks retirees to absorb one kind of adjustment while many government obligations adjust in the other direction.

That is not a clean strategy.

It is redistribution through the price level.

There is a more constructive relationship between retirement and the fiscal wall.

Higher safe yields can help rebuild a culture of funded patience.

When bills yield five percent, a household can hold an emergency reserve without feeling punished. When long bonds yield enough to match pension liabilities, institutions can reduce the amount of heroic return they need from risk assets. When annuity providers can earn more on high-quality fixed income, products can price differently.

The federal government may pay more for capital, but the financial system can allocate retirement savings with less dependence on leverage and speculative appreciation.

This is one reason fiscal policy should not aim simply to force rates back down.

Low government borrowing costs are not the same as social welfare.

A healthy rate reflects a healthy balance among saving, investment, inflation, growth, and risk. Artificially suppressing yields to reduce the Treasury's interest bill can tax savers indirectly and encourage new risk-taking.

The objective is sustainable debt at market rates consistent with macroeconomic stability.

That requires facing the retirement promises directly.

The hard political truth is that aging makes the fiscal wall less abstract. Social Security and Medicare are not wasteful line items waiting to be deleted. They are the structure through which older Americans receive income and health coverage. Cutting them suddenly would create real hardship and could reduce aggregate demand. Raising taxes enough to preserve every promise also creates costs, particularly for younger workers.

There is no solution in which one generation pays nothing.

There are solutions in which generations have time.

A phased increase in dedicated revenue can spread cost.

Changes in benefit formulas can protect lower-income retirees while slowing growth for higher earners.

Retirement ages can be reconsidered carefully, with attention to differences in health, occupation, and life expectancy.

Health-care payment reforms can reduce costs without simply shifting them onto patients.

Immigration and higher labor-force participation can increase the worker base.

Productivity growth can raise wages and payroll-tax revenue.

None is sufficient alone.

All are easier before trust-fund deadlines and interest costs collide.

This is why the retirement portfolio belongs in a book about Treasury refinancing.

The same society is lending to the government, depending on government promises, and aging into the period when those promises become cash flows.

The borrower and the saver are not separate populations.

They are the same people at different ages, holding different claims through different institutions.

The fiscal wall is sometimes described as if creditors stand outside the country waiting to collect from taxpayers trapped within it.

A large share of the wall is internal.

The government pays interest into portfolios that help finance retirement while collecting taxes from workers whose own retirement depends partly on government programs.

That circularity does not make the debt harmless.

It makes adjustment political rather than mechanical.

A company can renegotiate with bondholders.

A country has to renegotiate with itself.

That negotiation will define the next retirement regime.

The goal should not be to choose between the old and the young as if prosperity were a fixed inheritance to divide.

The goal is to make the promises fit the productive capacity available to honor them.

Growth helps.

Saving helps.

Higher safe yields can help private portfolios.

Earlier policy changes help public promises.

The one thing that does not help is pretending time is not passing.

Retirement is duration made personal.

The fiscal wall is duration made national.

Both become expensive when adjustment is left to the last years of the promise.
