# A Generation Priced at Zero

For anyone who came of age in finance after 2009, zero did not feel temporary.

It felt like climate.

Young analysts learned valuation models in a world where the risk-free rate could sit near the floor for years. Homebuyers learned to compare monthly payments at mortgage rates their parents would have considered impossible. Startups learned that a compelling story about a distant market could attract capital long before profits arrived. Governments learned that debt could rise sharply without producing an equally sharp rise in interest expense.

Older investors remembered another world.

They remembered certificates of deposit paying rates that seemed generous by later standards. They remembered mortgages in the teens. They remembered Paul Volcker, money-market funds as a novelty, bond coupons that looked like stock-market returns, and a time when the phrase *fixed income* meant income rather than duration exposure disguised by tiny yields.

Both groups were rational to learn from the environment they inhabited.

That is what humans do.

The problem begins when an environment lasts long enough to become a theory of nature.

A generation priced at zero does not literally believe rates can never rise. It does something subtler. It builds habits, institutions, contracts, and expectations in which persistent low rates occupy the center of the probability distribution.

The difference matters.

A tail risk can be acknowledged and still be structurally unprepared for.

Before the global financial crisis, the federal funds rate had spent much of modern history well above zero. After the crisis, the Federal Reserve cut the target range to essentially zero and kept it there for years. The recovery was slow. Inflation remained subdued. When the Fed eventually raised rates, it did so gradually, only to reverse direction as conditions changed. Then the pandemic struck, rates returned to the floor, and the central bank purchased enormous quantities of Treasury and agency mortgage-backed securities.

By 2020, an entire cohort of professionals had never managed money through a sustained inflationary tightening cycle.

This did not make them foolish.

It made them historically situated.

Every generation is.

The fiscal wall is partly the collision between two historical situations: liabilities accumulated under one rate regime and budgets required to service them under another.

To see why that matters beyond Washington, start with savers.

The low-rate era was frustrating for anyone who wanted safe income. Bank deposits yielded almost nothing. Treasury bills could offer returns close to zero. High-quality bonds provided little coupon cushion. Retirees who had expected to live partly on interest faced an uncomfortable choice: spend principal, accept less income, or take more risk.

Many took more risk.

They moved into dividend stocks, longer-duration bonds, corporate credit, real estate, private funds, structured products, or whatever else promised yield.

This was not necessarily reckless. A diversified portfolio should contain risk assets. But the disappearance of safe yield altered the boundary between what investors *wanted* to own and what they *needed* to own to meet return targets.

Pension funds felt this acutely.

A pension promises payments extending decades into the future. The present value of those liabilities depends heavily on discount rates and actuarial assumptions. When safe yields fall, the mathematical value of future obligations can rise, while the returns available from low-risk assets fall.

The institution can respond by contributing more money, reducing promised benefits where legally possible, changing assumptions, or investing in riskier assets in search of return.

Public pensions, corporate pensions, insurers, endowments, and foundations all faced versions of this problem.

Low rates did not remove risk.

They relocated it.

The same relocation appeared in corporate finance.

Companies could issue debt cheaply and use the proceeds to invest, buy competitors, repurchase shares, or simply extend their financial runway. Healthy firms sensibly locked in low coupons. Weaker firms could sometimes refinance rather than confront a failing business model.

The phrase *zombie company* became popular because cheap credit can keep low-productivity firms alive longer than a harsher financing environment would. The term is often used too loosely. A company with low profits and cheap debt is not automatically economically dead. Innovation can require long periods of weak cash flow. Cyclical firms can recover.

The broader point survives the rhetoric.

The hurdle rate fell.

When the safe return is near zero, more projects can plausibly earn more than the cost of capital.

That is one reason low rates can stimulate investment.

It is also why higher rates reveal which projects depended on the hurdle staying low.

Real estate made the mechanism visible.

A commercial property is often valued by capitalizing expected income at a rate related to financing conditions and risk. When cap rates fall, a given stream of rent supports a higher valuation. When borrowing costs rise and cap rates move higher, the same building can be worth less even if every tenant still pays.

If the building was purchased with debt, the equity can be compressed dramatically.

Nothing physical changed.

The discount rate did.

The federal budget is a larger and slower version of the same duration problem.

Government promises often extend decades. Social Security benefits, Medicare, pensions for federal workers and veterans, defense commitments, infrastructure maintenance, and interest on outstanding debt all occupy future budgets. Unlike a private pension, the federal government can change taxes and laws. It does not need to pre-fund every liability in a portfolio of assets.

Yet the present political value of a promise is affected by what people believe the future will cost.

Low rates made long-lived commitments look easier to finance.

This is not the same as saying the commitments were mistakes. A bridge that lasts fifty years may be *more* sensible to finance when rates are low. Long-lived public investment can justify borrowing because future users share the cost. A recession can justify deficits because the alternative is a deeper loss of output and employment.

The institutional mistake is treating a favorable financing environment as permanent rather than using it to choose which long-lived investments deserve the privilege.

A generation priced at zero also learned a particular relationship between central banks and markets.

During several episodes after 2008, market stress was followed by easier monetary policy, liquidity facilities, or asset purchases. Investors coined phrases like the *Fed put* to describe the belief that severe asset-price declines could trigger policy support.

The phrase was always an exaggeration. The Federal Reserve has a mandate concerning employment and price stability, not the S&P 500. Financial conditions matter because they affect the economy, and market dysfunction can impair monetary transmission, but the central bank does not promise to protect speculative positions.

Still, repeated interventions shape expectations.

When a generation observes that severe market stress often brings policy support, leverage can feel safer than it is.

Treasury markets themselves participated in this learning.

In March 2020, the world's benchmark safe-asset market experienced extraordinary dysfunction as investors rushed for cash. Dealers struggled to absorb selling. Hedge funds reduced positions. Foreign official institutions sold. Bid-ask spreads widened and market depth collapsed. The Federal Reserve bought Treasuries at enormous scale to restore market functioning.

The lesson was not that Treasuries were unsafe in credit terms.

It was that a safe asset can become illiquid when too many holders want cash at once and the intermediation system cannot process the flow.

A low-rate generation therefore inherited two contradictory beliefs.

Treasuries are the ultimate safe asset.

Treasury market functioning may require public intervention under extreme stress.

Both can be true.

The fiscal wall increases the importance of the second because the market itself is larger.

As more debt is outstanding, the private financial system must hold, trade, finance, hedge, and clear more of it. The question is not simply whether investors have enough money. It is whether the plumbing scales.

This is where the zero-rate era leaves behind another structure: leverage built around tiny spreads.

When yields are low and safe arbitrage opportunities offer only a few basis points, investors can increase returns by borrowing. The Treasury cash-futures basis trade is a prominent example. A hedge fund can buy a Treasury security, finance it in repo, and short a related futures contract, seeking to profit as the small price difference converges.

The trade can support market efficiency by connecting cash and futures prices.

It can also be highly leveraged because the expected spread is small and the collateral is considered exceptionally safe.

Federal Reserve research published in June 2026 estimated that large hedge funds' gross Treasury exposures had doubled between 2023 and September 2025 to about $4 trillion, with roughly $830 billion attributed to the cash-futures basis trade under the researchers' methodology. The estimates have limitations, as the authors emphasized. The scale is enough to show how deeply modern Treasury market liquidity and leveraged private strategies can become intertwined.

Zero was never really zero.

Someone was always carrying risk.

The rate regime determined who could carry it cheaply.

When policy rates rose after 2022, some of the old relationships reversed.

Savers suddenly had options.

Treasury bills yielded enough to compete with risk assets for the first time in years. Money-market funds grew. Households bought Treasury securities more directly. Banks had to pay more for deposits or watch money move elsewhere. High-quality bonds could once again provide meaningful income.

Higher rates created winners.

This is important because fiscal discussions often describe interest only as a government cost.

Interest is also private income.

A retiree who receives five percent on a Treasury bill is not experiencing a fiscal abstraction. The government is paying that retiree for the use of money.

A pension can lock in higher yields against future liabilities.

An insurer can match claims with better-returning fixed-income assets.

A young saver can earn something on cash without buying speculative assets.

The return of yield can improve financial behavior even as it worsens the Treasury's budget arithmetic.

This is another reason the fiscal wall is politically complicated.

Lower rates help the borrower and punish the saver.

Higher rates help the saver and punish the borrower.

The federal government is the largest borrower in the system.

It is also the issuer of the asset many savers want.

The conflict is built into the same security.

A generation priced at zero may therefore misread higher rates as pure damage because it learned to associate falling yields with healthy asset prices and easier financing.

An older generation may make the opposite mistake and treat high nominal yields as evidence of normality without asking what inflation or debt stock sits behind them.

Neither memory is enough.

The relevant question is what rate is consistent with price stability, sustainable growth, and a financial system that can allocate capital without depending on perpetual rescue.

That rate is not set by nostalgia.

It can move as productivity, demographics, global savings, fiscal policy, risk appetite, inflation expectations, and monetary credibility change.

The federal government must be able to finance itself across regimes.

That is the deeper meaning of debt maturity.

A well-managed sovereign balance sheet should not require one generation's interest-rate environment to persist forever.

The same should be true of private institutions.

Pensions should survive a low-return era without taking existential risk.

Banks should survive a rapid increase in rates without discovering that “safe” assets were safe only if no depositor wanted cash.

Households should understand that adjustable debt can adjust.

Companies should be able to refinance without assuming the spread of the previous cycle.

Governments should distinguish investments whose benefits outlast their financing from recurring consumption financed indefinitely with debt.

The zero-rate era exposed how difficult that discipline is because the immediate cost of violating it was small.

There is a human tendency to confuse low observed volatility with low underlying risk.

If a river has not flooded for twenty years, building closer to the bank starts to feel rational. Property values rise. Insurance premiums may fall. Roads and utilities extend into the floodplain. The absence of water becomes part of the valuation.

Then a storm reveals that the floodplain was always there.

Interest-rate risk works similarly.

The low-rate era did not repeal duration. It rewarded anyone who could borrow long and cheap. It punished savers and institutions that needed safe income. It encouraged investors to move outward on the risk curve. It allowed governments to carry more debt at modest visible cost.

Then rates rose and the old exposures reappeared.

The question for a generation priced at zero is not whether it was wrong to believe the evidence in front of it.

The question is whether it can update without demanding the old climate back.

That update is already visible in markets.

Venture investors ask harder questions about profitability. Homebuyers calculate payments at higher rates. Corporate borrowers term out debt when they can. Banks manage deposit betas and duration with new urgency. Pension funds can buy bonds at yields that better match liabilities. Treasury investors demand a meaningful real return.

Washington's update is slower.

The budget still contains commitments made across multiple rate regimes. The debt portfolio still carries securities issued at different coupons. Congress still debates taxes and spending in ideological categories that can obscure the cost of time.

The fiscal wall is therefore partly a generational handoff.

One era decided what to build when money was cheap.

Another era has to refinance it.

That does not mean the second era should resent the first.

Every generation inherits infrastructure, institutions, debts, assets, mistakes, discoveries, and promises.

The useful question is what should be carried forward at the new price.

Some low-rate investments will prove extraordinarily valuable.

Some will look foolish.

Most will be mixed.

The federal debt itself will not provide a verdict because debt finances everything from wars to research, tax cuts to unemployment insurance, routine operations to emergency rescues. The balance sheet does not label dollars as wise or wasteful after they are borrowed.

It only carries the claim.

That is the harsh neutrality of refinancing.

The auction does not care why the debt was issued.

The buyer asks what the security is worth now.

A generation priced at zero learned to make promises in one financial language.

The next generation is translating them.

The exchange rate is interest.
