# The Choices Before the Wall

A fiscal wall is useful only if there is still road before it.

Otherwise it is a crash report.

The United States still has road.

Treasury markets are open. The dollar remains central. The economy is large and productive. Tax rates can be changed. Spending laws can be changed. Retirement programs can be adjusted gradually. Health systems can be reformed. Immigration can alter the labor force. Housing and energy policy can affect productivity. The maturity structure of public debt can be managed. The Federal Reserve retains strong institutional credibility.

These are options.

A fiscal strategy is a decision about how to use them before circumstances use them for us.

The first choice is whether to stabilize the debt ratio at all.

This sounds absurd only because most fiscal arguments skip it.

A country can deliberately choose a rising debt ratio for a period if it believes the borrowing finances extraordinary emergencies or investments whose future returns justify the cost. War, depression, pandemic, and infrastructure can all make higher debt rational.

The relevant objective is not a fetishized number.

It is a path that preserves confidence and room.

If debt held by the public rises faster than GDP indefinitely under plausible assumptions, interest eventually consumes an increasing share of national income or some adjustment occurs through taxes, spending, inflation, or other channels. “Indefinitely” is doing the work. A growing economy can carry growing nominal debt. It cannot promise a claim growing faster than its capacity forever without changing something.

So the first practical objective is to bend the ratio.

Not tomorrow.

Credibly.

That distinction creates room for design.

A plan to stabilize debt over ten or fifteen years can phase changes, protect vulnerable people, and avoid tightening into recession. A plan that demands balance next year may damage growth and collapse politically. A plan that promises balance in year ten by placing all hard choices in year nine is theater.

Credibility lives between speed and evasion.

The second choice is revenue.

The United States collects less tax revenue as a share of GDP than many other wealthy democracies while also choosing a mix of social programs, defense responsibilities, tax preferences, and federal structures unlike theirs. International comparisons do not tell Americans what government should cost. They demonstrate that advanced economies can sustain different revenue levels when political institutions support them.

At the scale of the current fiscal gap, revenue options eventually reach beyond a handful of billionaires.

High-income households can pay more. Corporate taxes can change. Capital gains can be taxed differently. Estate taxes can be broadened. Enforcement can improve. Tax expenditures can be reduced.

These measures can raise meaningful sums.

The arithmetic of structural deficits measured in trillions makes it difficult to preserve the entire middle-class tax base untouched forever if spending commitments remain near current trajectories.

This is the political fact almost every revenue debate tries to avoid.

A broad consumption tax, such as a value-added tax, could raise large revenue with relatively low evasion if designed well. It would also be regressive relative to income without rebates or credits and would create an entirely new federal tax instrument. The United States has resisted such a tax politically for generations.

A carbon tax could raise revenue while pricing an environmental externality, but revenue would decline if emissions fall as intended and energy prices would have distributional consequences.

Higher payroll taxes can strengthen Social Security financing but increase the tax wedge on work. Raising or eliminating the cap on taxable earnings changes the program's relationship between contributions and benefits.

Income-tax increases can be progressive but alter incentives, avoidance, and the timing of income. Broadening the base can sometimes raise revenue with lower marginal-rate increases, but every deduction and exclusion has a constituency.

There is no tax without behavior.

There is also no large revenue increase without taxpayers.

The third choice is retirement.

Social Security reform is often treated as politically impossible because beneficiaries vote and the program is popular. That is precisely why early reform matters.

A sudden benefit cut after trust-fund depletion would be politically and socially destabilizing. A phased package enacted years in advance can combine revenue, benefit adjustments, and protections.

Options include changing the payroll-tax base or rate, modifying the benefit formula, increasing the retirement age gradually, changing cost-of-living rules, creating minimum benefits, or adjusting benefits for higher earners.

Each choice reflects a theory of what Social Security is for.

Insurance against poverty in old age?

A universal earned benefit tied to lifetime contributions?

A replacement-income program?

The answer affects design.

The fiscal wall cannot decide the values.

It makes delay more expensive.

Medicare and Medicaid create a different challenge because the underlying price of health care matters as much as the eligibility formula.

The United States spends more per person on health care than peer countries for a mix of reasons including prices, administrative complexity, technology, utilization, market power, wages, pharmaceuticals, and the structure of insurance and delivery.

Reducing federal health spending by shifting costs to beneficiaries or states can improve the federal line while leaving national health costs unchanged.

A durable fiscal reform tries to lower the underlying resource cost where possible.

Drug-price negotiation, payment reform, competition, site-neutral payments, fraud reduction, primary care, preventive health, administrative simplification, and delivery-system changes all belong in the discussion. None offers a guaranteed painless trillion-dollar saving.

Health reform is hard because every unnecessary cost is somebody's revenue.

The fourth choice is discretionary spending.

This category gets outsized political attention because Congress votes it annually. It includes defense and a wide range of nondefense functions: research, infrastructure, law enforcement, diplomacy, education grants, environmental programs, administration, and more.

Cutting discretionary spending can contribute to deficit reduction.

It cannot carry the entire long-run adjustment without changing the character and capacity of the federal government.

Defense alone is a major strategic commitment. Nondefense discretionary programs include many of the investments most relevant to future productivity and state capacity.

The choice is therefore composition, not just cap.

A spending review should ask which functions the federal government must perform, which programs work, which can be consolidated, which can be devolved, and which investments should be protected even during consolidation.

The fifth choice is tax expenditures.

The federal government subsidizes activities not only by writing checks but by excluding, deducting, crediting, or preferentially taxing income. Mortgage-interest deductions, employer health exclusions, retirement preferences, state and local tax treatment, business provisions, and many other rules reduce revenue while encouraging particular behavior.

Economically, a tax expenditure can resemble spending delivered through the tax code.

Politically, it can feel like keeping one's own money rather than receiving a benefit.

That makes reform difficult and potentially fruitful.

A fiscal settlement that examines only outlays leaves a shadow budget untouched.

The sixth choice is growth.

Growth deserves its own chapter because it is not a residual. The practical agenda includes housing supply, energy, permitting, infrastructure, research, education, immigration, competition, tax incentives, and fiscal stability itself.

Every deficit-reduction package should be scored not only for static savings but for its likely effect on productive capacity, while avoiding the opposite mistake of declaring every favored tax cut or subsidy self-financing through implausible growth.

Dynamic effects are real.

So is wishful thinking.

The seventh choice is debt management.

Treasury can smooth the wall through maturity choices, but it cannot solve the primary deficit. A diversified issuance strategy can reduce rollover concentration. Bills can serve strong money-market demand. Longer bonds can lock funding. TIPS and floating-rate notes can broaden the investor base. Buybacks can support liquidity and cash management. Central clearing and market reforms can expand intermediation capacity.

These choices matter because market dysfunction can create crisis even when fiscal solvency is not the immediate problem.

They are insurance, not revenue.

The eighth choice is monetary integrity.

Protecting Federal Reserve independence may be one of the cheapest fiscal reforms available because credibility reduces inflation risk without appearing as a budget saving. A central bank trusted to maintain price stability can help keep long-term inflation compensation and term premiums lower than they would otherwise be.

The Fed should not set rates to finance Treasury cheaply.

That discipline can make Treasury cheaper to finance over time.

The ninth choice is timing.

A durable package can begin with measures that are least damaging in the current economy and phase others. If unemployment is low and growth is solid, gradual structural tightening can occur. If recession arrives, automatic stabilizers should operate and some measures can pause.

A plan that cannot survive a business cycle is not a plan.

Triggers can help if designed carefully. For example, some policies could phase based on dates while emergency provisions activate during severe downturns. The danger is creating excuses that turn temporary pauses into permanent repeal.

The tenth choice is distribution.

Every fiscal adjustment creates winners and losers unless growth does all the work. Political durability depends on whether the distribution feels reciprocal enough to survive elections.

A package that protects every benefit for older voters while raising taxes only on younger workers will fail generationally.

A package that cuts safety-net programs while preserving expensive tax preferences will fail distributionally.

A package that raises broad taxes without reforming visible waste will fail symbolically even if the arithmetic works.

Fairness is not a cosmetic feature of fiscal policy.

It is part of credibility because unfair plans are repealed.

This suggests the shape of a plausible American settlement.

Not a balanced-budget amendment.

Not a single tax.

Not a commission that produces a report nobody implements.

Not a promise that growth pays for everything.

Not an overnight entitlement cut.

A settlement would probably include broad elements that both parties dislike: more revenue than anti-tax conservatives prefer, slower spending growth than progressives prefer, changes to retirement programs with long phase-ins, health-cost reforms that reduce industry revenue, protection for low-income households, stronger investment in capacity, and budget rules that make future unfunded expansions harder.

It might set a debt-ratio objective rather than annual balance.

It might distinguish emergency borrowing from structural borrowing.

It might include automatic adjustments if debt or interest deviates materially from the path, though automatic mechanisms should not be allowed to create procyclical disaster.

It would need to be simple enough for voters to understand and broad enough that no single constituency can overturn it without reopening the entire bargain.

This sounds politically impossible.

So did many fiscal agreements before they happened.

The 1983 Social Security reforms combined payroll-tax changes, benefit taxation, a gradual retirement-age increase, and other measures under intense political pressure. The 1990 and 1993 budget packages made politically difficult changes. The late-1990s surpluses emerged from policy, growth, and geopolitical circumstances few expected to combine as they did.

None created permanent discipline.

Fiscal settlements decay because politics continues.

That is not an argument against them.

A bridge does not fail as infrastructure because it eventually needs maintenance.

Institutions need maintenance too.

The fiscal wall will require repeated adjustment, not one grand act that solves federal finance forever.

This is another reason to avoid crisis rhetoric.

If voters are told that one package will “fix the debt,” they will reasonably feel betrayed when deficits return after a recession, war, tax change, or demographic shift.

The objective is to change the trajectory enough that future shocks occur from a stronger starting point.

A good settlement buys option value.

That is the product.

Lower expected debt means more room for emergency borrowing.

Lower interest means more room for priorities.

More credible policy can reduce risk premiums at the margin.

Protected investment can support growth.

Gradual reform can preserve trust.

The choices before the wall are therefore not between pain and no pain.

They are between chosen adjustment and inherited adjustment.

Taxes enacted through law or purchasing power lost through inflation.

Benefits changed gradually or suddenly.

Investment protected deliberately or crowded out accidentally.

Market yields accepted honestly or suppressed through financial repression.

Reform during growth or austerity during panic.

The United States can still choose.

That is the entire advantage of being early.
