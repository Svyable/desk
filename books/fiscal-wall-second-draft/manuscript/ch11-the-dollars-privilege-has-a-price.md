# The Dollar's Privilege Has a Price

The United States borrows in a currency the world already wants.

That sentence explains more of American fiscal resilience than most budget charts.

A Japanese insurer buying Treasuries, a European bank posting dollar collateral, a commodity exporter holding reserves, a multinational managing cash, a hedge fund financing a basis trade, and a central bank intervening in foreign-exchange markets can all create demand for dollar assets for reasons unrelated to the current mood of Congress.

The dollar is not just America's money.

It is part of the operating system of global finance.

Treasury securities are among its most important files.

This arrangement is often called the dollar's *exorbitant privilege*, a phrase associated with complaints in France during the Bretton Woods era. The privilege is real: the United States can issue liabilities in its own currency to a global investor base that values them for liquidity, safety, reserves, collateral, and settlement. It can often borrow more cheaply and in greater volume than another country with the same headline fiscal ratios.

Privilege is a dangerous word because it sounds permanent.

No monetary constitution says the dollar must remain dominant forever.

Its position is reproduced every day by choices.

Companies invoice in dollars because counterparties already hold dollars. Banks fund in dollars because customers and markets use dollars. Central banks hold dollar reserves because intervention, trade, and financial stability often require them. Investors use Treasuries because the market is vast and liquid. Derivatives are margined against dollar assets. Commodity contracts use dollars. Legal systems, custody networks, clearing arrangements, and financial habits reinforce the choice.

This is a network effect.

Network effects can persist long after competitors emerge because every participant benefits from using what everyone else already uses.

The fiscal wall is testing how much stress the network can absorb without changing the choice.

The wrong way to think about reserve-currency status is as a trophy that foreign governments award for good behavior.

A country does not need to admire the United States to use dollars.

It needs dollar assets to be useful relative to alternatives.

China can want a more multipolar monetary system and still conduct trade in dollars. A foreign central bank can dislike U.S. sanctions policy and still hold Treasuries because it needs liquid reserve assets. An international bank can diversify currency exposure while relying heavily on dollar funding.

Geopolitical preference and financial infrastructure are not the same thing.

This is why predictions of an imminent dollar collapse recur so often and fail so often. They treat dissatisfaction as substitution.

Substitution is harder.

A reserve asset needs scale.

It needs open capital markets.

It needs deep secondary trading.

It needs trusted custody and settlement.

It needs a legal regime investors believe will protect claims.

It needs enough government or high-quality private securities to hold.

It needs a currency used by counterparties.

Europe has scale and sophisticated markets, but the euro area's sovereign debt remains divided among national issuers and institutions. China has enormous economic scale, but capital controls, legal uncertainty for foreign investors, and the structure of its financial system limit the renminbi's ability to replicate the dollar's role. Gold is politically neutral but produces no sovereign cash flow and cannot support modern collateral markets at the same scale. Cryptocurrencies are technologically interesting and useful for some forms of transfer and speculation but do not yet provide the depth, legal integration, price stability, and institutional reach of dollar markets.

The relevant competitor to the dollar is not one currency waiting backstage.

It is a gradual diversification of functions.

Trade can shift at the margin.

Reserves can become more diversified.

Regional payment systems can grow.

Sanctioned countries can develop alternatives.

Digital settlement can reduce some frictions.

The dollar can lose share without losing centrality.

That matters for the fiscal wall because the United States does not need perfect dominance to retain a large financing advantage.

It needs enough global demand that Treasuries remain unusually useful.

The privilege appears in several forms.

First, the United States can issue debt in its own currency. A country that borrows heavily in a foreign currency can face a devastating mismatch: its tax revenue is domestic, but its debt service rises when the domestic currency falls. The United States does not have that problem with Treasury debt.

Second, global demand creates a convenience yield. Investors may accept a lower return on Treasuries than they would require from an otherwise similar asset because Treasuries provide liquidity, safety, collateral value, and regulatory usefulness.

Third, crises can increase demand for the very liabilities whose issuer is running larger deficits. During global stress, investors often seek dollars and Treasuries. The U.S. government can therefore borrow more during a recession while yields fall, a combination that would be difficult for many sovereigns.

Fourth, the Federal Reserve sits at the center of a global dollar system. Swap lines and other facilities can provide dollar liquidity to foreign central banks during stress, reducing the risk that foreign institutions must dump dollar assets simply to obtain funding.

These advantages make the United States harder to force into a conventional sovereign debt crisis.

They do not make borrowing costless.

The privilege has a price because maintaining the network requires policy that preserves its usefulness.

Start with inflation.

A foreign investor buying a thirty-year Treasury accepts a nominal dollar promise. If U.S. policy allows persistently high inflation, the real value of that promise erodes. Investors can demand higher yields, shorten maturities, buy inflation-protected securities, hedge the currency, or diversify.

The United States can pay every nominal dollar and still make the asset less attractive.

This is why Federal Reserve credibility belongs on the national balance sheet.

Price stability is not only a domestic objective. It protects the real usefulness of dollar claims.

Now consider legal and political reliability.

Treasuries are valuable partly because payment is assumed to be mechanically boring. Debt-limit brinkmanship threatens that feature. A missed or delayed payment caused by a self-imposed statutory constraint would not prove the United States lacked resources. It would demonstrate that the political system was willing to introduce uncertainty into the world's benchmark safe asset for bargaining purposes.

That is an extraordinary thing to risk.

A privilege becomes durable when users stop thinking about it.

Making them think is expensive.

Sanctions create another price.

The United States gains geopolitical power from the dollar's central role because it can restrict access to its financial system and freeze assets under law. Those tools can be powerful and, in some circumstances, justified. They also give targeted countries incentives to reduce dependence on dollar infrastructure.

This does not mean using sanctions causes an automatic exodus from Treasuries. Alternatives carry their own risks, and allies may support the policy. It means monetary dominance is also geopolitical infrastructure, and every use changes incentives at the margin.

The reserve currency is strongest when its usefulness exceeds the political risk users attach to dependence.

Fiscal policy itself is part of that calculation.

An abundant supply of Treasuries can strengthen the dollar system by providing more safe assets. This is one of the paradoxes of U.S. debt. A larger federal debt can increase the quantity of high-quality collateral and reserve assets the world wants.

Too little Treasury debt could create scarcity in the plumbing of global finance.

Too much can raise questions about inflation, future taxes, crowding out, and the amount of duration investors must absorb.

The privilege is not a coupon for unlimited issuance.

It is demand with a slope.

Higher supply can require higher yield even while the asset remains dominant.

This is why foreign holdings should be interpreted carefully.

Treasury's International Capital data provide valuable information about cross-border positions, but custodial structures make country attribution imperfect. A security managed by an institution in one jurisdiction may economically belong to an investor elsewhere. Federal Reserve research has shown that hedge-fund structures can further complicate the apparent geography of holdings.

A monthly decline in one country's Treasury position is therefore not automatically a geopolitical sale.

The data can reflect reserve management, currency intervention, private flows, custody, valuation, hedging, or changes in portfolio structure.

The most recent TIC release available in August 2026, covering June, showed sizable net foreign purchases of long-term U.S. securities overall even as foreign holdings of Treasury bills declined that month. That combination is a useful reminder: “foreigners buying America” is not one trade.

Maturity matters.

Asset class matters.

Private and official investors behave differently.

A reserve manager may care most about liquidity and capital preservation. A private insurer may care about yield and duration. A bank may need dollar assets for regulatory liquidity. A hedge fund may hold Treasuries as collateral or an arbitrage leg. A sovereign wealth fund may prefer equities.

The dollar system aggregates these motives.

This diversity is one reason foreign demand can be resilient.

It is also why a single-country political narrative can mislead.

Suppose China gradually reduces the share of reserves held in Treasuries while other foreign private investors, U.S. households, money funds, pensions, insurers, and hedge funds increase holdings. The ownership map changes. The auction can still clear.

The question becomes price, not patriotism.

The federal shadow reaches the currency through that price.

If long Treasury yields rise because investors demand more compensation for fiscal uncertainty, dollar effects are ambiguous. Higher yields can attract capital and strengthen the currency. If the yield rise reflects unanchored inflation expectations or declining institutional trust, the dollar could weaken.

A strong dollar is therefore not proof that fiscal policy is sustainable.

A weak dollar is not proof of imminent sovereign crisis.

The combination of signals matters.

A concerning pattern would be rising long yields, rising inflation compensation, a falling currency, weak auction demand, and political pressure on the central bank occurring together over time.

Even then, interpretation would require care because growth, commodity prices, and global shocks influence all these variables.

The fiscal wall is not diagnosed by one screen.

The dollar's privilege matters most in the crisis that has not happened.

Imagine a severe global recession in 2030 with federal debt already much larger than today. Tax revenue falls. Automatic stabilizers increase spending. Congress passes emergency support. The deficit widens sharply.

If the dollar remains the world's preferred safe-haven currency, investors may buy Treasuries despite the larger borrowing need. The Federal Reserve can ease policy if inflation permits. Financing costs can fall while deficits rise.

The privilege creates room to fight the crisis.

Now imagine the same recession after years of politically induced inflation, payment brinkmanship, capital restrictions, or weakened central-bank credibility. Investors may still buy Treasuries because alternatives remain limited, but the safe-haven response can be weaker. More of the adjustment may arrive through higher yields, a weaker dollar, or inflation risk.

The debt stock did not create the difference alone.

Institutions did.

This is why the reserve currency should be treated as fiscal capacity rather than fiscal permission.

Capacity is most valuable when preserved for uncertainty.

A government that knows the world will lend to it in a crisis can spend that advantage in two ways.

It can use it when extraordinary events arrive.

Or it can use it continuously to avoid ordinary budget tradeoffs.

The second strategy leaves less distinction between emergency and normality.

The market may finance both for a long time.

The political system loses a valuable discipline.

There is another price to the privilege: the United States must supply the world with dollar assets, which can interact with trade and capital flows. Persistent global demand for dollar claims can support a strong currency and capital inflows. Economists have long debated the relationship between reserve-currency demand, U.S. current-account deficits, domestic investment, and the need to provide safe assets to the world.

The so-called Triffin dilemma originated under a different monetary system, but the underlying tension survives in altered form: the world's demand for dollar liquidity can encourage the United States to supply liabilities on a scale that eventually raises questions about the issuer.

There is no simple mechanical requirement that America run a federal budget deficit to supply safe assets. Private dollar assets exist, and current-account and fiscal balances are not the same. The broader tension is useful because it shows that the world's appetite for U.S. liabilities is both an advantage and a responsibility.

The dollar system wants Treasury securities.

Washington should not interpret wanting as indifference to quantity or policy.

The most durable reserve currencies are embedded in credible institutions.

Open markets.

Property rights.

Independent monetary policy.

Deep financial infrastructure.

A government capable of taxing and governing.

A political system that pays its bills.

Military and diplomatic power matter. Economic scale matters. Network effects matter. So does boring administration.

This is why the fiscal wall is not just a debt story.

It is an institutional-quality story.

The United States can carry more debt because the world assigns extraordinary value to its promises.

If that value is treated as infinite, the country begins consuming the reason it can carry the debt.

The irony is severe.

The reserve currency protects the United States from being forced into abrupt fiscal adjustment.

That protection can make gradual adjustment easier to postpone.

A weaker country may reform because the market closes.

America can keep refinancing.

The privilege buys time.

Time is valuable only if used.

The price of the dollar's privilege is therefore restraint where restraint protects the network: price stability, reliable payment, predictable debt management, open and deep markets, and enough fiscal credibility that investors continue treating Treasuries as infrastructure rather than merely a high-yielding claim on a polarized government.

No competitor can take that privilege in a single auction.

The United States can spend it one avoidable doubt at a time.

## The Privilege Can Shrink Without Disappearing

Reserve-currency debates often suffer from a binary imagination.

Either the dollar dominates forever or it collapses and is replaced.

The fiscal risk lives in the middle.

The dollar can remain the world's primary reserve and funding currency while the financing advantage attached to that role becomes smaller.

Suppose foreign institutions still need Treasuries for reserves and collateral but demand fifty additional basis points of compensation to hold long duration because supply is larger and fiscal uncertainty is higher.

The dollar has not lost reserve status.

Treasury has lost part of the subsidy.

On a large and growing debt stock, a small persistent change in the required premium can matter enormously once it rolls through maturities.

That is why the right question is not *Will the dollar be dethroned?*

It is *How much convenience yield does the dollar system continue to provide, and what behavior preserves it?*

Convenience is an economic asset.

Investors accept somewhat lower yields on an asset that is easy to trade, easy to pledge, legally predictable, widely accepted, and useful in regulation and settlement. The premium does not appear on a federal balance sheet, but it lowers financing costs relative to a less useful security.

Market plumbing therefore has fiscal value.

A Treasury market with deep two-way liquidity is more valuable to investors than one that becomes fragile under ordinary flows.

Reliable settlement has fiscal value.

Central-bank credibility has fiscal value.

The absence of arbitrary capital controls has fiscal value.

The expectation of payment on time has fiscal value.

These institutional assets can offset some of the burden of a large debt stock.

They can also be degraded independently of the debt stock.

This is what makes the debt limit so economically strange. A country can spend decades building a security that functions like financial bedrock and then voluntarily introduce legal uncertainty into the payment schedule.

Even if markets expect resolution every time, the repeated exercise teaches investors that a political variable sits inside an asset valued partly for removing political variables.

The cost may be tiny in any one episode.

The principle is large.

The same is true of inflation credibility.

A reserve manager does not require zero inflation. Modern fiat systems are built around low, positive inflation targets. The investor needs confidence that the inflation regime is not being subordinated to the government's desire for cheaper real financing.

If fiscal pressure makes that confidence weaker, the rational response is not necessarily to abandon dollars. It can be to shorten maturity, buy TIPS, demand higher nominal yields, hold more gold, diversify some reserves, or hedge more aggressively.

Each response can preserve the dollar's central role while raising Washington's price of time.

This is how privilege can erode quietly.

It is also how it can be strengthened quietly.

A credible fiscal adjustment does not need to balance the budget immediately to improve the long-run risk premium. Market reform can deepen liquidity. Reliable institutions can make Treasuries more useful even as supply rises. Strong productivity can expand the economy behind the debt. An independent Federal Reserve can anchor inflation expectations.

The network is dynamic.

The United States does not inherit reserve-currency privilege once.

It earns part of it every day through boring competence.

That may be the most important asset in the entire fiscal-wall story because it is the asset most easily mistaken for nature.

The dollar's privilege is powerful precisely because users rarely need to think about it.

The best fiscal policy keeps it that way.
