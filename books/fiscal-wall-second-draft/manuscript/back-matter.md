# A Note on the Numbers

This book is about a moving target.

Federal debt changes every business day. Treasury auction sizes change. The government's cash balance moves. Interest rates move continuously. Economic forecasts are revised. Tax laws and spending laws change. A maturity schedule that is exact on one date is already slightly different on another.

For that reason, the numbers in *Fiscal Wall* are evidence of scale and mechanism, not prophecy.

The central factual distinction is the one the book returns to repeatedly: gross refinancing is not the same thing as net new borrowing.

A Treasury bill can mature and be replaced several times in a year. Counting each maturity produces a very large gross financing flow without creating an equally large increase in debt outstanding. Notes and bonds also mature and are normally refinanced. New deficits add fresh borrowing on top of those rollovers. Treasury's cash-management decisions add another layer.

A headline describing “$10 trillion to refinance” can therefore be directionally useful and numerically ambiguous until the measurement is defined. The book deliberately avoids treating that phrase as a single official Treasury forecast. Depending on the date, horizon, securities included, and treatment of short-term bills, reasonable gross-refinancing totals can differ materially.

The official measures behind the manuscript come primarily from the U.S. Department of the Treasury, the Congressional Budget Office, the Federal Reserve System, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Social Security Administration, and other primary public sources.

Treasury's Debt to the Penny dataset is the canonical source for daily public debt outstanding. Treasury's Monthly Statement and Fiscal Data products provide budget and debt information. Quarterly refunding statements and Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee materials explain financing plans, maturity strategy, auction sizes, and market conditions. Treasury International Capital data describe cross-border holdings and flows, with important limitations around custody and country attribution.

The Congressional Budget Office supplies the baseline projections used throughout the book. A baseline is not a forecast in the ordinary conversational sense. It applies specified assumptions about current law and the economy to show where the budget would go under those assumptions. Congress can change the law. The economy can surprise. The value of the baseline is that it makes the consequences of the current policy structure visible before the surprises arrive.

The February 2026 CBO outlook projected a fiscal-year 2026 deficit of roughly $1.9 trillion, debt held by the public around 101 percent of GDP, and net interest above $1 trillion. Under that baseline, debt held by the public rose to about 120 percent of GDP by 2036 and net interest to about 4.6 percent of GDP. Those figures anchor the manuscript's claim that the refinancing period is primarily a loss-of-room problem rather than a prediction of imminent default.

Treasury's August 3, 2026 borrowing estimate projected $739 billion of privately held net marketable borrowing for the July-through-September quarter and $628 billion for October through December, subject to the usual assumptions about cash balances and legislation. The August quarterly refunding illustrated the relationship between rollover and new borrowing: Treasury announced $125 billion of three-, ten-, and thirty-year securities to refund approximately $96.3 billion of privately held notes and bonds maturing that month and raise roughly $28.7 billion of new cash.

These examples are snapshots. They should not be frozen into folklore.

The Treasury market itself is changing while the government asks it to carry more debt. The SEC's central-clearing rules move eligible cash Treasury transactions toward central clearing by the end of 2026 and eligible repo transactions by mid-2027 under the compliance schedule current when this draft was written. The reforms are intended to improve risk management and netting in a market where dealer balance sheets, principal trading firms, money funds, banks, pensions, insurers, foreign institutions, and hedge funds all perform different roles.

Federal Reserve research provides important context for the leveraged part of that ecosystem. A June 2026 FEDS Note estimated about $4 trillion of gross Treasury exposures among large hedge funds as of September 2025, including roughly $830 billion associated with the cash-futures basis trade under the authors' estimation method. The researchers emphasized limitations in the data and methodology. The numbers are included here to establish scale, not to claim that every leveraged Treasury position is dangerous. Relative-value trading can support liquidity and price alignment. Leverage becomes relevant when funding and margin move faster than positions can be reduced safely.

The March 2020 Treasury-market dysfunction remains a central case because it demonstrated that a security can be nearly free of conventional credit risk and still experience severe liquidity stress. Federal Reserve intervention restored market functioning during an extraordinary dash for cash. Later market reforms and standing facilities should be understood partly as attempts to make the next large market more resilient than the last one.

Foreign demand is treated cautiously throughout the manuscript. Treasury International Capital data are useful but not a perfect map of ultimate ownership. Securities can be held through custodians in jurisdictions different from the beneficial owner. Official and private flows behave differently. Changes in one country's holdings do not automatically represent geopolitical rejection of the dollar.

The book likewise avoids a single debt threshold. Historical and cross-country evidence does not support a universal ratio at which sovereign crisis mechanically begins. Currency denomination, maturity, inflation, tax capacity, growth, domestic saving, investor composition, financial regulation, central-bank credibility, external balances, political institutions, and the role of the debt in the financial system all matter.

This is why comparisons with households, Japan, Greece, Italy, Canada, Sweden, the United Kingdom, or the postwar United States are used only for mechanisms. None is a template for the contemporary United States.

The manuscript also distinguishes four ideas that are often collapsed in public debate.

A government can be solvent in nominal terms and still impose large real costs through inflation.

A Treasury market can remain liquid enough to finance the government while the resulting yields become fiscally painful.

A high debt ratio can be sustainable under one growth-and-rate regime and difficult under another.

A fiscal adjustment can improve the government's accounting while damaging productive capacity if designed badly.

Those distinctions are the reason the book spends so much time on duration, growth, institutions, and market structure rather than simply adding zeros to a debt counter.

No quotation in the manuscript should be read as invented authority. Where historical language or named concepts are used, the research trail points to primary or established sources. The prose generally paraphrases rather than reproduces long passages because the purpose is to explain mechanisms, not build an anthology.

The source ledger in the research folder records the main factual lanes chapter by chapter and includes a falsification question for each. That last column matters most.

A book with a title like *Fiscal Wall* carries an obvious temptation: interpret every rise in yield as evidence of approaching disaster and every large borrowing number as proof that the thesis is right.

That would make the book useless.

Evidence that should weaken the central concern includes sustained faster productivity growth, a durable improvement in the primary balance, lower real interest rates consistent with stable inflation, deeper long-duration demand, successful market-structure reform, a stable or declining debt ratio, and continued strong reserve demand without rising risk premiums.

If those things happen, the wall moves farther away.

That is not a failure of the argument.

It is the outcome the argument is asking policy to make possible.

The numbers will change.

The mechanism remains simple.

Debt moves purchasing power through time.

Refinancing resets the price of that movement.

Growth determines how much future capacity exists to carry the claim.

Politics decides who adjusts when the claim becomes expensive.

Everything else in this book is an attempt to make those four sentences harder to misunderstand.
