# Banks, Insurers, and the Wrong Yield

The most dangerous asset on a balance sheet is sometimes the one everyone agrees is safe.

Not because it secretly contains bad credit.

Because safety encourages size.

A bank can own an enormous Treasury portfolio precisely because Treasury credit is treated as exceptionally strong. An insurer can hold long government bonds because future claims need long reliable cash flows. A pension can use them to hedge liabilities. A money-market fund can hold bills because investors expect cash-like stability.

The safer the asset, the easier it is to forget which risks remain.

Interest-rate risk remains.

Liquidity risk remains.

Funding risk remains.

Concentration remains.

Market structure remains.

The fiscal wall matters partly because the United States is asking the financial system to absorb more of an asset whose credit quality makes it foundational while whose quantity and duration can still strain balance sheets.

Banks are the natural place to begin because modern banking is a business built on mismatched clocks.

Deposits can leave quickly.

Loans repay slowly.

Securities mature on schedules.

The bank earns money by turning short, liquid liabilities into longer, less liquid assets while maintaining enough capital and liquidity to survive withdrawals and losses.

This maturity transformation is not a bug. It is one of banking's core economic functions.

The problem is not that the clocks differ.

The problem is believing they cannot suddenly start moving at different speeds.

During the low-rate era, deposits flooded into banks, especially during the pandemic when fiscal transfers, reduced spending opportunities, and monetary expansion increased cash balances. Many banks had more deposits than attractive loans. They invested part of the excess in securities, including Treasuries and agency mortgage-backed bonds.

The yields were low because the market rate was low.

The securities looked safe because they were safe in credit terms.

Then rates rose.

New bonds paid more.

Old bonds fell in price.

At the same time, depositors gained alternatives. Treasury bills and money-market funds offered yields that ordinary bank accounts did not. Some banks raised deposit rates. Others were slower. Money moved.

For a well-managed diversified bank, this is a normal interest-rate cycle to be managed through asset-liability policy, hedging, liquidity buffers, capital, deposit pricing, and supervision.

For a bank with concentrated uninsured deposits, large unhedged duration, weak governance, or an unusually unstable funding base, the transition can become existential.

The 2023 failures made this visible.

Silicon Valley Bank held large portfolios of securities whose market values had fallen. Its depositor base was unusually concentrated among technology companies and investors. When confidence broke, withdrawals accelerated with the speed of digital banking and networked communication.

The failure was not evidence that Treasuries had become bad assets.

It was evidence that a balance sheet can fail while holding assets that eventually pay in full.

Time and liquidity can kill before maturity arrives.

This is a crucial idea for the Treasury market as a whole.

The United States does not merely need buyers with enough wealth to own its debt.

It needs holders whose liabilities, capital, hedges, and liquidity make the ownership stable under stress.

A dollar held by a pension for thirty years is not equivalent to a dollar held by a leveraged hedge fund financed overnight.

A dollar held by a money-market fund in a four-week bill is not equivalent to a dollar held by a bank in a ten-year note.

A dollar of foreign reserve demand is not equivalent to a dollar held by a momentum trader.

Aggregate demand can look deep while the behavior of the marginal holder changes quickly.

This is why the composition of Treasury ownership matters as much as the total.

Banks occupy an ambiguous position in that ownership structure.

Regulation encourages them to hold high-quality liquid assets, and Treasuries are central to those requirements. Treasuries also receive favorable treatment in many capital and liquidity frameworks because their credit risk is low and their markets are deep.

At the same time, bank balance sheets are constrained. Leverage ratios, risk management, internal limits, dealer inventories, and funding conditions can limit how much Treasury exposure banks are willing to intermediate even if they regard the securities as safe.

This distinction became important during market stress.

In March 2020, investors sold Treasuries in enormous volume to raise cash. Primary dealers absorbed substantial flows, but their balance sheets and risk capacity could not expand enough to keep market liquidity normal. Bid-ask spreads widened, market depth collapsed, and the Federal Reserve intervened with large purchases and repo operations.

The problem was not a shortage of belief in U.S. credit.

It was a shortage of intermediation relative to selling pressure.

A larger Treasury market makes this plumbing problem more important.

If debt outstanding rises faster than the capacity of banks and dealers to intermediate it, the market can become more dependent on principal trading firms, hedge funds, central clearing, and official backstops. That is not necessarily bad. Financial markets evolve. Nonbanks can provide valuable liquidity and arbitrage.

The question is whether the new architecture is resilient when everybody wants to move at once.

Insurers reveal another side of the same problem.

An insurance company receives premiums today and promises to pay claims later. Some claims are short term. Life-insurance and annuity liabilities can extend decades.

This makes long-duration bonds naturally useful.

Unlike a bank funded by runnable deposits, an insurer with predictable liabilities can often hold long bonds through market volatility without being forced to sell. Higher long-term yields can therefore be welcome because the insurer can lock in better returns against future obligations.

The wrong yield for the Treasury can be the right yield for the insurer.

This is one of the stabilizing forces behind the fiscal wall.

As government borrowing costs rise, demand from liability-driven investors can increase. A pension or insurer that found three-percent bonds insufficient may find five-percent bonds attractive. Higher rates summon buyers.

Yet insurers face their own constraints.

Credit ratings, regulatory capital, policyholder behavior, derivatives collateral, asset-liability matching, and competition affect the portfolio. A rapid rate rise can cause policyholders to surrender older products in favor of new higher-yield alternatives. Insurers may need liquidity. Long assets can still become awkward when liabilities move faster than expected.

Again, the safe asset lives inside a balance sheet.

The federal government cannot evaluate demand simply by asking who likes Treasuries.

It has to ask which maturity each buyer wants and under what conditions the buyer remains stable.

This leads to the concept of preferred habitats.

Different investors naturally concentrate in different parts of the yield curve. Money funds dominate very short maturities. Banks may favor bills and intermediate securities depending on liquidity and asset-liability needs. Pensions and insurers can have natural demand for long duration. Foreign reserve managers may prefer liquid benchmarks. Relative-value funds move where pricing discrepancies appear.

Treasury debt management tries to finance across these habitats rather than flooding one part of the curve.

That is why a giant gross refinancing number can obscure the actual market problem.

Ten trillion dollars of mixed maturities is not one asset seeking one buyer.

It is a series of securities offered to different pools at different times.

The system's strength comes from this segmentation.

Its complexity does too.

The phrase *wrong yield* captures what happens when an institution buys an asset appropriate in one environment and carries it into another without matching the rest of the balance sheet.

For a bank in 2020, a 1.5-percent long Treasury may have looked better than holding cash at near zero.

For the same bank in 2023, the bond could be far below market while deposits demanded four or five percent.

The bond had not become dishonest.

The yield had become wrong for the liability structure.

The federal government faces the mirror image.

A two-percent coupon issued for ten years was wonderful funding in 2020.

When it matures in 2030, the relevant question will be the yield then. If the replacement costs five percent, the old rate was not a permanent characteristic of the debt. It was a temporary contract.

The government, like the bank, cannot assume the old asset-liability relationship persists after reset.

There is another bank connection: Treasury securities are collateral in the repo market.

Dealers, hedge funds, money funds, banks, and other institutions lend and borrow cash against Treasuries, often overnight. Repo allows enormous positions to be financed at low cost because the collateral is highly liquid and creditworthy.

This makes Treasuries more valuable as financial infrastructure.

It also means Treasury-market stress can propagate through funding.

If repo rates spike, haircuts rise, or lenders retreat, leveraged holders may have to sell Treasuries. Selling can push cash prices down, widening discrepancies with futures and generating more pressure. The asset's safety can support leverage that becomes destabilizing when funding conditions change.

Federal Reserve research on hedge-fund exposures illustrates the scale. By September 2025, according to a June 2026 FEDS Note, large hedge funds had about $4 trillion in gross Treasury exposures and roughly $3 trillion in repo cash borrowing, with highly leveraged arbitrage strategies accounting for a large portion of long positions under the authors' estimates.

Those numbers should not be read as four trillion dollars of speculative danger. Gross positions include offsetting trades and legitimate market-making functions. The basis trade can improve price alignment between cash Treasuries and futures, helping Treasury financing by supporting demand.

The point is structural.

A meaningful share of Treasury-market capacity is connected to leverage and short-term funding.

The market can therefore be deep and fragile in different dimensions at once.

This is why central clearing is receiving so much attention.

The SEC's Treasury clearing rules are intended to bring more eligible cash and repo transactions into central clearing, improve risk management, increase netting, and reduce bilateral exposures. Compliance dates were extended to the end of 2026 for eligible cash-market transactions and June 2027 for eligible repo transactions.

Central clearing is not a magic shield.

It can concentrate risk in clearing infrastructure and impose margin and operational requirements. It can also reduce redundant gross exposures and make the system more transparent and efficient.

For the fiscal wall, market-structure reform matters because the buyer question is partly an infrastructure question.

How much Treasury can the system hold and trade for a given amount of dealer balance sheet, collateral, margin, and liquidity?

Improving that ratio is economically similar to increasing productive capacity in a factory.

The government can finance a larger debt market more safely if the financial machinery processes it more efficiently.

This is where banks and insurers stop being passive holders and become part of national fiscal capacity.

A strong banking system supports Treasury liquidity.

Well-capitalized insurers and pensions provide stable duration demand.

Money-market funds provide bill demand.

Central clearing can improve netting.

Repo facilities can reduce funding stress.

A diverse investor base reduces dependence on any one channel.

These are not substitutes for fiscal reform.

They determine how much time reform receives.

A country with weak market plumbing can face a technical crisis before its long-run fiscal arithmetic becomes impossible. A country with strong plumbing can finance itself smoothly while the budget deteriorates for years.

The United States has mostly lived in the second world.

That is an advantage, not a reason for complacency.

It means the financial system may continue saying yes while the political system avoids the harder question of what the higher yield is doing to the budget.

Banks and insurers see that price immediately.

They have to.

Their assets and liabilities are repriced by markets, regulators, customers, and accounting rules.

The federal government sees it slowly through net interest.

This difference in speed can create a dangerous illusion.

If banks have survived the rate shock and insurers are buying more bonds, perhaps the transition is over.

From the federal perspective, the average debt cost may still be climbing because old low-coupon securities have not all matured.

The private system can finish repricing before the public budget does.

That is the essence of the wall.

The financial institutions around Treasury discover the new yield first.

The taxpayer receives it later.

By then, the banks may be healthy, the insurers may be earning more, the retiree may be pleased with a five-percent bond ladder, and the Treasury auction may be clearing beautifully.

The wrong yield will not look wrong to anyone except the borrower.

That is enough.

## The Capacity Behind the Buyer

A useful way to evaluate Treasury demand is to stop counting buyers and start counting balance-sheet capacity.

A bank can want Treasuries and still be unable to intermediate another large block at the same price.

An insurer can prefer long bonds and still have limits determined by liabilities, capital rules, ratings, and portfolio concentration.

A pension can desire duration while keeping enough liquid assets to meet collateral calls and benefit payments.

A hedge fund can provide powerful marginal demand only while repo and margin economics support the trade.

The willingness to own is not the same as the capacity to own through stress.

This distinction changes the meaning of the question *Who will buy all the debt?*

The answer is not a list of institutions.

It is a map of constraints.

Money funds have asset-composition and maturity constraints.

Banks have capital, leverage, liquidity, and funding constraints.

Dealers have inventory, risk, and balance-sheet constraints.

Insurers have liability and regulatory constraints.

Pensions have funding and liquidity constraints.

Foreign investors have currency and hedging constraints.

Leveraged funds have repo and margin constraints.

Households have wealth and convenience constraints.

As Treasury issuance grows, yields move partly to compensate the next constraint that becomes binding.

This is why a market can appear to have bottomless demand at one maturity and weak demand at another.

There can be trillions of dollars in money-market funds ready for bills while a thirty-year auction still needs a concession because money funds do not want thirty-year duration.

Aggregate savings do not erase preferred habitats.

The policy implication is subtle.

Treasury cannot solve a long-term refinancing problem merely by issuing to whichever buyer class currently has the most cash. Doing so can shorten the portfolio and transfer rate risk back to taxpayers.

Nor can it force long-duration supply into the market without paying the premium required by the investors suited to hold it.

Good debt management is partly the art of respecting the balance sheets on the other side.

Market-structure reform can expand those balance sheets at the margin.

Central clearing can allow netting that reduces redundant gross exposures. Better transparency can make risk easier to price. Well-designed leverage rules can preserve bank resilience while avoiding unnecessary constraints on low-risk intermediation. Standing repo facilities can reduce the chance that a temporary cash shortage turns good collateral into forced selling.

None of these tools creates genuine end-investor demand from nothing.

They make existing demand easier to connect with supply.

That difference is important because fiscal policy can be tempted to treat financial regulation as a hidden financing lever.

If banks are required to hold more government debt, Treasury may gain demand.

If the rule is genuinely justified by liquidity and prudential needs, that can make the system safer.

If regulation is altered mainly to create captive demand and suppress the government's borrowing rate, financial repression begins to enter the picture.

The line between safety and subsidy becomes politically important as debt grows.

Banks and insurers are therefore more than buyers in this story.

They are a test of whether the Treasury market can scale without forcing private finance to reorganize around the government's needs.

A healthy system lets institutions own Treasuries because the assets fit their purposes.

A strained system increasingly needs institutions to own Treasuries because the government needs them to.

The securities can be identical.

The institutional relationship is not.

That is another way the wrong yield can reveal the wall before any default risk appears.
