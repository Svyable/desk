# Austerity Is Not a Plan

Cutting is an action.

Austerity is a condition.

A plan requires more.

The distinction matters because the fiscal wall will eventually make spending restraint unavoidable in some form, just as it will make revenue part of the argument. The federal government cannot permanently spend materially more than it collects without increasing debt. If the debt grows faster than the economy and interest rises with it, arithmetic eventually demands adjustment.

The mistake is assuming that any reduction in spending improves the fiscal position by the amount printed in the budget table.

Economies react.

People react.

Institutions react.

Timing matters.

Composition matters.

Credibility matters.

A government can cut a dollar and lose more than a dollar of future capacity.

It can also cut a dollar that never should have been spent and improve both growth and the budget.

Calling both austerity obscures the work.

The word acquired much of its modern political force after the global financial crisis and the European sovereign-debt crisis. Countries facing high deficits and market pressure reduced spending, raised taxes, or both. The outcomes differed dramatically depending on starting conditions, exchange-rate regimes, banking systems, external demand, monetary policy, and the design and pace of consolidation.

Greece became the most extreme symbol because its crisis combined unsustainable public finances, a deep recession, a banking crisis, weak administrative capacity, membership in a currency union it did not control, and external creditor programs. Output collapsed. Unemployment soared. Debt restructuring occurred. Spending cuts and tax increases interacted with a shrinking economy.

The Greek experience is not a template for the United States.

America issues debt in its own currency, controls national fiscal policy, has a central bank capable of creating dollar liquidity, and sits at the center of global capital markets. Importing the emotional lessons of a euro-area crisis without the institutional differences produces bad analysis.

The relevant lesson is narrower.

Fiscal consolidation imposed during a severe downturn can worsen the denominator at the same moment it improves the numerator.

If spending cuts reduce incomes and tax revenue sharply, the deficit may improve less than expected. If public investment is slashed, future growth can suffer. If households are already deleveraging and monetary policy is constrained, simultaneous public retrenchment can deepen recession.

This does not mean deficits should never be reduced in weak economies.

It means the macroeconomic environment changes the cost.

A country with the privilege to choose should prefer adjustment before crisis because early adjustment can be gradual and countercyclical.

That is the strongest argument for acting on the fiscal wall now.

Not fear of imminent default.

Avoiding the conditions in which adjustment becomes austerity.

When markets remain open, unemployment is low, and the economy is growing, lawmakers can phase changes over years. They can protect people near retirement. They can pair revenue changes with growth reforms. They can preserve automatic stabilizers for recessions. They can distinguish public investment from recurring consumption.

During crisis, the clock chooses.

Asset sales occur at bad prices.

Tax increases arrive when households are weak.

Spending cuts hit programs before reforms can be designed.

Pension promises are changed for people with little time to adapt.

Emergency lenders or market conditions set terms.

The political system loses sequencing.

That is austerity.

A plan keeps sequencing.

The American debate often avoids this distinction because “cut spending” is rhetorically satisfying. Federal outlays are large. Waste exists. Procurement fails. Programs persist after their usefulness fades. Agencies duplicate work. Fraud and improper payments occur. Congress sometimes creates subsidies with weak evidence and tax expenditures that function like spending without appearing on the spending side of the ledger.

Every serious fiscal program should attack these problems.

The arithmetic will not stop there.

CBO's 2026 baseline placed federal outlays around $7.4 trillion for the fiscal year. Mandatory programs accounted for the largest share, with Social Security and Medicare central to the long-run growth. Discretionary spending included both defense and nondefense priorities. Net interest itself exceeded $1 trillion.

The structural gap is too large to close by firing a few bureaucrats or canceling notorious small grants.

Waste reduction is good government.

It is not a fiscal strategy at trillion-dollar scale unless the definition of waste expands to include programs with large and politically powerful constituencies.

That is where slogans stop.

Austerity becomes a plan only when it answers four questions.

What are we trying to preserve?

Who has time to adjust?

What spending produces future capacity?

What happens in a recession?

The first question is moral and strategic.

A budget is not a list of numbers to minimize. It is the operating plan of a state. Defense, courts, public health, retirement security, research, transportation, environmental protection, income support, diplomacy, law enforcement, and administration all serve purposes that voters value differently.

Fiscal sustainability is not the objective function of civilization.

It is a constraint that keeps future choices open.

A consolidation that stabilizes debt while destroying core state capacity is not success.

This is why failed states can have small formal budgets.

The relevant question is not how little government spends.

It is whether the state can finance what it has democratically chosen to do over time.

The second question—who has time to adjust—is where duration becomes policy design.

A twenty-five-year-old can respond to a retirement-age change differently from a sixty-four-year-old. A hospital can adapt to a payment reform phased over five years differently from a sudden reimbursement cut. A defense contractor can restructure a program if procurement rules change predictably. A household can save more if tax changes are announced gradually.

Slow policy can be stronger policy because it preserves planning.

The common objection is that future Congresses can repeal future cuts.

True.

A reform delayed too far can become a fake savings device. Budget legislation is full of cliffs, sunsets, and phase-ins designed to manipulate scoring windows.

The solution is not immediate pain for its own sake.

It is institutional credibility: changes that begin soon enough to be real, phase predictably, and create constituencies for continuation rather than repeal.

The third question is investment.

Public accounting often makes a repaired bridge and a routine office expense look like the same category of annual outlay. Economically, they are different. One creates an asset that may reduce costs or increase productivity for decades. The other funds current operations.

The distinction is not clean. Maintenance can be investment. Education can create human capital. Defense research can have spillovers. Social spending can improve future health and labor-force participation. A tax administration system can raise revenue by improving compliance.

Still, a fiscal adjustment that cuts everything proportionally because proportionality looks fair can be strategically stupid.

The easiest spending to postpone is often the spending with the longest benefit.

Maintenance has no immediate constituency because the bridge still stands.

Research has no guaranteed discovery date.

Prevention has no visible patient.

Infrastructure can be delayed one budget at a time until the eventual replacement becomes much more expensive.

A good fiscal plan protects high-return capacity even while reducing total borrowing.

This may require explicit capital budgeting concepts or spending reviews that estimate long-term value rather than simply carrying forward last year's categories.

The fourth question is recession.

A fiscal rule that demands the same consolidation during a deep downturn as during a boom can destabilize the economy. Automatic stabilizers such as unemployment insurance and progressive taxes exist partly so government can absorb shocks. Emergency borrowing can be socially valuable when private demand collapses.

The objective is structural improvement over the cycle, not mechanical balance every year.

This is another place household analogies fail. A family cannot print money or act as macroeconomic stabilizer. The federal government can and should run deficits during severe downturns.

The fiscal problem arises when large deficits persist in normal conditions and leave less room for the next downturn.

A plan therefore tightens in good times and loosens when conditions require it without losing the long-run anchor.

Fiscal rules can help, but rules are only as strong as their exceptions and political legitimacy. A debt target can be ignored. A deficit cap can encourage accounting games. A spending ceiling can push activity into tax expenditures. A balanced-budget amendment can force procyclical policy or creative definitions.

The best rule is often a transparent process that makes deviations visible and costly rather than impossible.

Sweden's post-crisis fiscal framework is frequently cited because it combined expenditure ceilings, surplus targets over the cycle, and institutional transparency after the country's early-1990s banking and fiscal crisis. Canada in the 1990s reduced deficits through substantial spending restraint alongside growth and favorable rate developments.

These cases show that durable consolidation is possible.

They do not provide copy-and-paste instructions for the United States.

Canada is smaller, has different federal responsibilities, and adjusted under different demographics and external conditions. Sweden has a different tax-and-spending social contract. The United States has unique defense obligations, health programs, tax politics, and reserve-currency advantages.

Cases are useful for mechanisms, not costumes.

The mechanism is that credible adjustment often works through a package rather than a slogan.

Spending restraint.

Revenue.

Growth.

Institutional reform.

Time.

Political durability.

The package matters because voters tolerate pain differently when they believe others are participating.

A spending-only plan can look like an attempt to protect taxpayers who benefit most from lower taxes while reducing benefits for others. A tax-only plan can look like an attempt to preserve every program regardless of efficiency. A package creates the possibility of reciprocity.

Nobody gets everything.

That can make it survive.

The fiscal wall also raises the question of defense.

Debt-service pressure can collide with geopolitical pressure. The United States may need to spend more on military capability, industrial capacity, cyber defense, alliances, or strategic competition at the same moment aging pushes entitlement spending higher.

Austerity framed as across-the-board cuts can fail strategically if it hollows out the capabilities the country needs to protect the economic system supporting the debt.

Defense spending is not automatically productive or efficient. Procurement programs can be spectacularly wasteful. Strategic priorities change. Bases, weapons systems, and contracts develop constituencies.

The answer is not exempting defense from discipline.

It is recognizing that fiscal strategy and national strategy cannot be designed in separate rooms.

The same is true of health care.

Medicare and Medicaid are large not simply because government writes generous checks but because American health care is expensive. Cutting federal reimbursement without changing prices, incentives, delivery, or competition can shift costs rather than reduce them. Hospitals may charge private payers more. States may cut other services. Patients may face access problems.

A durable health fiscal strategy asks why the underlying care costs what it does.

That is harder than imposing a budget cap.

It is also more likely to produce real savings rather than exported bills.

Austerity is not a plan because an aggregate target cannot tell us how the system will adapt.

A ten-percent cut to a bad program can be too small.

A ten-percent cut to a critical understaffed function can be destructive.

A ten-percent cut to a benefit can be devastating for a poor household and barely noticed by a wealthy one.

A ten-percent cut to capital maintenance can create a future liability larger than the current saving.

Fiscal policy is allocation under constraint.

The wall strengthens the constraint.

It does not eliminate judgment.

There is a final reason to reject austerity as an identity.

Countries sometimes begin to treat fiscal pain as evidence of seriousness. The harsher the cut, the more responsible the government appears. This can become as irrational as treating spending as evidence of compassion.

Pain is a cost, not a credential.

The objective is to stabilize the debt path while preserving as much social value and productive capacity as possible.

That may require painful choices because decades of promises cannot be reconciled by efficiency alone.

The pain should earn something.

A higher retirement age should materially improve solvency and be designed around workers who cannot easily extend careers.

A tax increase should raise reliable revenue with as little distortion and avoidance as practical.

A program cut should target weak value rather than weak political protection.

A defense reform should improve capability per dollar rather than merely lower the top line.

A health reform should reduce underlying cost rather than move the invoice.

The fiscal wall is not asking the United States to become austere.

It is asking the United States to become selective.

That is harder.

Austerity can be announced in one number.

A plan has to know what the country is for.
