# Growth Is the Only Gentle Exit

Every fiscal solution eventually asks somebody for something.

A taxpayer pays more.

A beneficiary receives less than expected.

A department loses funding.

A bondholder accepts lower real returns.

A worker retires later.

A patient faces a different payment system.

A saver absorbs inflation.

A future Congress inherits the bill.

Growth is the exception because it can make the burden smaller without requiring an equal transfer from a named loser.

If the economy becomes more productive, the same tax rates can produce more revenue. Wages can rise. Companies can earn more. The debt ratio can fall because GDP grows faster than debt. Public programs can be financed from a larger base.

This is why growth is the only gentle exit from a fiscal wall.

It is also why politicians invoke it far more easily than they produce it.

The debt arithmetic is simple enough to fit on a napkin and difficult enough to occupy careers.

What matters for the debt ratio is the relationship among the existing debt stock, the government's primary balance, the effective interest rate on the debt, and the growth rate of the economy. If the economy grows faster than the effective interest cost and the primary deficit is small, a country can sometimes stabilize or reduce its debt ratio without running large primary surpluses.

If the effective interest cost exceeds growth and the government continues running primary deficits, the arithmetic becomes harsher.

No single year's values settle the future because rates and growth change.

The relationship is still the most useful way to think about why the same debt ratio can be manageable in one era and dangerous in another.

The United States benefited for years from a favorable version of this relationship. Interest rates were extraordinarily low. Nominal GDP grew. Even as federal debt increased, the effective cost of the debt portfolio remained modest relative to historical fears.

Then rates rose.

The question became whether growth could rise with them.

This is where the fiscal wall intersects with nearly every optimistic story about the American economy.

Artificial intelligence.

Energy abundance.

Advanced manufacturing.

Biotechnology.

Automation.

Robotics.

Immigration.

Housing supply.

Permitting reform.

Education.

Infrastructure.

Each can be described as fiscal policy even when it never appears in a deficit-reduction bill because each can affect the denominator.

The denominator is not a trick.

A richer country can carry more debt.

Suppose federal debt grows by four percent while nominal GDP grows by six. The debt ratio falls. The government can roll a larger dollar stock and become less indebted relative to national income.

Suppose debt grows eight percent while nominal GDP grows four. The ratio rises quickly even if every Treasury auction is successful.

The same word—*trillion*—can describe opposite fiscal directions depending on the denominator.

This is why proposals to balance the budget through spending cuts alone can be self-defeating if they damage growth enough, and why proposals to borrow for every program can be self-defeating if the spending fails to raise future capacity.

The composition of policy matters.

Public investment is not automatically productive because government calls it investment.

A bridge to nowhere is still concrete.

A rail project can be badly managed.

A subsidy can finance activity that would have happened anyway.

A tax incentive can reward politically favored firms without increasing total investment.

The word *infrastructure* does not suspend cost-benefit analysis.

But neither does the existence of waste make public investment economically trivial.

Roads, ports, transmission lines, water systems, scientific research, basic education, disease prevention, and digital infrastructure can increase private productivity in ways no single company can fully capture. The government can finance projects with broad spillovers precisely because private markets may underinvest in benefits that cannot be easily owned.

The fiscal question is whether the future increase in productive capacity justifies the financing cost.

Higher rates make that question more demanding.

They do not make the answer automatically no.

Artificial intelligence has become the most tempting growth deus ex machina of the current era.

If AI produces a sustained acceleration in productivity, the fiscal effect could be meaningful. Workers could produce more per hour. Firms could lower costs and create new products. Scientific research could accelerate. Government itself could become more efficient. Higher incomes would generate more tax revenue without necessarily requiring higher statutory tax rates.

A few tenths of a percentage point of additional annual productivity growth compounded over decades can produce a materially larger economy.

That is real fiscal help.

It is not a budget line Congress can spend before the productivity appears.

Technological revolutions often arrive unevenly. Firms need complementary investment, organizational change, training, energy, data centers, and infrastructure before inventions translate into measured productivity. Some gains accrue as consumer surplus rather than taxable corporate profit. Automation can displace workers before new tasks and industries absorb them. Capital income and labor income can be taxed differently.

An AI boom can improve the denominator and create distributional challenges at the same time.

The prudent fiscal stance is to make the economy capable of capturing the upside without assuming the upside solves arithmetic already on the books.

Energy offers a similar opportunity.

Cheap reliable energy can raise productivity across manufacturing, computing, transportation, and households. The United States has major resources in natural gas, renewables, nuclear technology, and an enormous emerging demand for electricity from data centers and electrification.

The constraint is increasingly physical.

Transmission takes years to permit and build. Transformers and other equipment can face long lead times. Generation must connect to grids. Local opposition can delay projects. Data centers compete for power. Industrial investment depends on reliable capacity.

A country expecting AI-led growth while unable to connect new power is planning productivity with a missing input.

Permitting reform can therefore be fiscal policy in disguise.

So can housing.

Expensive housing in productive cities prevents workers from moving toward opportunity. It raises wage demands and employer costs. It lengthens commutes. It channels subsidies into land values when supply is constrained. Local zoning can therefore affect national productivity and federal transfer needs.

Building more housing in high-demand regions is not usually described as debt reduction.

It can improve the denominator more honestly than a spreadsheet assumption.

Labor-force growth is another lever.

An aging society has fewer workers relative to retirees unless labor-force participation, immigration, fertility, retirement patterns, or productivity compensate. More workers mean more production and more taxpayers. Immigration can improve the fiscal outlook when newcomers work, earn, start businesses, and pay taxes, though fiscal effects differ by age, skill, family composition, public-service use, and level of government.

The political debate often reduces immigration to either pure benefit or pure cost.

For the fiscal wall, composition and integration matter.

A younger working-age population is an asset in an aging country if institutions convert newcomers into productive participants quickly.

Education belongs in the same category, with a long delay.

A child who learns to read better today may contribute more tax revenue decades later. High-quality early childhood programs can have long-run effects. Universities train workers and conduct research. Apprenticeships build technical skill. Immigration can import human capital. Employer training can adapt skills to technology.

The difficulty is that not every dollar labeled education produces equal returns, and the fiscal system must pay the cost long before the return is measurable.

Growth policy requires patience at exactly the moment the debt burden is making patience expensive.

This is why fiscal consolidation and investment cannot be designed independently.

A government under pressure may cut capital spending because it is politically easier to postpone a bridge than reduce a current benefit. The budget improves this year. Growth potential may worsen later.

A government can make the opposite mistake and classify ordinary consumption as investment to escape discipline.

The solution is not a slogan about cutting or investing.

It is separating high-return capacity from spending whose benefits are immediate and then financing each honestly.

Private investment is just as important and often larger.

The federal government can improve growth by making it easier for private capital to find productive uses. Tax policy influences investment incentives. Regulatory clarity affects planning. Competition policy can influence innovation. Trade policy affects access to inputs and markets. Fiscal stability itself matters because uncertainty about future taxes, inflation, and rates can make long-lived investment harder.

A large public debt can crowd out some private capital if government borrowing raises rates or absorbs saving that would otherwise finance business investment. The magnitude depends on global capital flows, monetary conditions, saving behavior, and the economic environment.

The effect is not a fixed one-for-one subtraction.

It is another reason the debt path can influence the denominator that supports it.

Debt can become self-reinforcing in the wrong direction if higher borrowing raises rates, reduces private capital formation, slows productivity, and then worsens the debt ratio.

It can also finance investments that raise productivity and partially or fully offset the burden.

The label *debt* does not tell us which.

History is full of episodes where growth did heavy fiscal work.

After World War II, the U.S. debt ratio fell dramatically over decades. The government did not pay off the wartime debt in a giant cash transfer. Nominal GDP expanded through real growth and inflation. Primary fiscal outcomes were generally more favorable than the wartime period. Financial regulation and periods of negative real rates also mattered.

The postwar case is often invoked as proof that today's debt can simply be grown away.

The conditions were unusual.

The United States emerged from war with enormous industrial capacity, favorable demographics, pent-up consumer demand, a housing boom, expanding labor force, and a uniquely strong global economic position. Wartime spending also fell sharply, mechanically improving the primary balance.

Today's aging population and structural programs create a different starting point.

The lesson is not that history cannot repeat.

It is that growth works best when fiscal policy does not simultaneously add a larger structural gap.

The 1990s offer another American example. Strong growth, low unemployment, policy changes, lower defense spending after the Cold War, and buoyant revenues combined to move the federal budget from deficits to surpluses.

Few forecasts at the beginning of the decade captured the exact result.

This is important because it shows why long-term fiscal baselines should inspire action without pretending to be destiny.

The economy can surprise positively.

Policy can change.

The debt path can bend.

The same uncertainty argues against relying on growth alone.

A plan that balances only if productivity suddenly accelerates is not a plan.

It is a forecast with constituencies.

The gentleness of growth can also hide distribution.

A larger economy does not guarantee broadly shared prosperity. If productivity gains accrue mostly to capital owners, federal revenue may rise but workers can still feel insecure. If housing supply remains constrained, higher incomes can bid up rents and home prices. If health and education costs outpace wages, aggregate GDP growth can coexist with household frustration.

A fiscal settlement built on growth needs political legitimacy as well as arithmetic.

People have to experience some of the larger denominator as a larger life.

This matters because fiscal reform will probably require visible compromises too.

Growth can make tax increases smaller, benefit adjustments gentler, and debt stabilization easier. It cannot guarantee that nobody pays.

CBO's 2026 baseline projected a primary deficit throughout the next decade. Closing a structural gap of that scale through growth alone would require a large and sustained improvement relative to baseline, especially because higher incomes can also increase some indexed spending and political demand for services.

Growth is the best lever.

It is not a magic lever.

The phrase *only gentle exit* should therefore be read literally.

There are other exits.

Higher taxes.

Lower spending growth.

Inflation.

Financial repression.

Asset sales.

Default or restructuring in extreme cases.

Each assigns loss more visibly or damages institutions more directly.

Growth expands the amount available to divide.

That is gentler.

It also takes time.

The fiscal wall is a time problem.

This creates the central policy challenge of the next decade: improve the debt trajectory without sacrificing the investments and reforms most likely to improve productive capacity.

A crude austerity package can save dollars and lose denominator.

A crude stimulus package can add denominator and even more debt.

The optimal path is not found by choosing one word.

It is found by asking of every major policy: what does this do to future productive capacity, future primary balances, and future resilience?

Some answers will be politically inconvenient.

A popular tax break may fail the test.

A popular spending program may fail it.

An unpopular infrastructure reform may pass.

An entitlement adjustment may improve fiscal room while harming vulnerable people unless designed carefully.

An immigration reform may be economically valuable and politically difficult.

A permitting change may create local costs and national gains.

There is no growth agenda without tradeoffs.

The difference is that the tradeoffs can produce a larger future rather than merely redistribute a smaller one.

That is what the fiscal wall needs most.

Not a miracle.

Compounding.

One more tenth of productivity.

One more worker connected to a productive job.

One less year of delay on a transmission line.

One city where housing supply can respond to demand.

One tax provision that stops rewarding unproductive behavior.

One public investment that lowers private costs for decades.

One budget rule that protects capital spending from being the easiest thing to cut.

None looks like an escape.

Together, sustained long enough, they change the denominator.

The only gentle exit is not one doorway.

It is an economy that grows faster than the wall.

## Growth Quality Matters as Much as Growth Quantity

Fiscal discussions often treat an extra point of GDP as though every point were equivalent.

They are not.

A burst of nominal spending financed by debt can raise measured GDP this year without increasing the economy's long-run ability to produce. A housing boom driven mostly by land-price appreciation can make households feel wealthier without adding enough housing units. A commodity-price surge can lift nominal income temporarily. A productivity improvement that lets workers produce more with the same inputs has a different fiscal character.

The wall cares about persistence.

Tax revenue generated by a one-time boom is useful, but a permanent improvement in productivity changes the denominator year after year. Compounding is what makes growth gentle enough to matter at sovereign scale.

This suggests a fiscal rule of thumb: do not spend temporary growth as though it were permanent.

The rule sounds obvious and is violated constantly.

A strong capital-gains year produces revenue. Commodity royalties jump. Corporate profits surge. Legislators see room. If the revenue disappears in the next cycle while permanent spending or tax cuts remain, the structural deficit grows.

The same problem appears in reverse when temporary weakness is mistaken for permanent decline and productive investment is cut too aggressively.

A growth-aware fiscal system distinguishes cycles from capacity.

That distinction should shape how policymakers evaluate AI and other emerging technologies. The relevant fiscal question is not whether an AI boom raises stock prices or data-center construction this year. It is whether the technology raises total factor productivity, expands taxable incomes, lowers real costs, and diffuses through enough industries to change the economy's trend.

The same standard applies to energy.

An investment boom financed by expensive electricity can look large in national accounts while failing to deliver its full productivity potential. More generation and transmission can convert capital spending into durable capacity.

The same standard applies to immigration.

A larger population increases aggregate GDP almost mechanically. The fiscal improvement depends more on employment, earnings, age structure, skills, integration, and per-capita productivity.

The same standard applies to public investment.

The bridge matters if it reduces transport costs, expands labor markets, or unlocks development—not merely because concrete was poured.

Growth quality is the difference between a larger economy and a more capable economy.

That difference matters because debt is a claim on future capability.

A government can service a large debt more comfortably if citizens are richer in real terms even when tax rates do not change. It cannot sustainably service debt by making nominal numbers larger while real capacity stagnates.

This is why inflation is a poor substitute for growth and why austerity that destroys productive capacity can be self-defeating.

The fiscal objective is not to maximize this year's GDP or minimize this year's deficit.

It is to maximize the economy's ability to support valuable public and private claims over time while keeping those claims politically legitimate.

That is a harder optimization problem than a balanced-budget slogan.

It is also the only one that offers a genuinely larger future.

The gentle exit requires patience because productivity reforms can take years to show up in measured output.

That patience must be paired with discipline because structural deficits compound during the wait.

The practical answer is a two-track strategy: improve the primary balance in ways that do the least damage to growth while aggressively removing barriers to productive capacity.

One track without the other is fragile.

Consolidation without growth can stabilize a ratio by shrinking ambition.

Growth policy without consolidation can allow every new dollar of revenue to be pre-spent.

The fiscal wall is defeated when the denominator grows and the numerator stops outrunning it.

That is why growth is the only gentle exit.

It is gentle only when fiscal policy lets the gain accumulate.
