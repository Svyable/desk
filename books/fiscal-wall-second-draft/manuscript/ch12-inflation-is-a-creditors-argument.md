# Inflation Is a Creditor's Argument

Inflation changes who wins an argument after the contract is already signed.

A lender agrees to receive dollars in the future.

A borrower agrees to pay them.

Neither side controls what those dollars will buy.

That uncertainty is normally hidden inside the interest rate. The lender asks for enough return to compensate for expected inflation and for the risk that inflation will be higher than expected. The borrower accepts the rate because the money is useful now.

If inflation later surprises to the upside, the borrower repays in dollars worth less than the lender anticipated.

If inflation surprises lower, the lender receives more real purchasing power than expected.

This is why inflation is a creditor's argument.

It decides how a nominal promise is translated into real resources.

Governments have understood this for centuries, sometimes explicitly and sometimes by accident. A state that owes fixed nominal debt can reduce the real burden of that debt through unexpected inflation. No formal default is required. Coupons are paid. Principal is returned. The legal promise is honored.

The economic promise changes.

This has led generations of fiscal pessimists to imagine inflation as the hidden escape hatch from the federal debt.

The idea contains enough truth to be dangerous.

The United States does have a large stock of nominal fixed-rate debt. If the price level rises unexpectedly, the real value of those fixed claims falls. In that narrow sense, inflation transfers wealth from Treasury holders to the federal government and other nominal borrowers.

The transfer can be large when the debt stock is large.

It is also temporary, uneven, and self-defeating if investors expect it.

A government cannot repeatedly surprise sophisticated creditors with the same trick.

Once investors believe inflation will be used to reduce the real value of debt, they demand compensation in advance. Nominal yields rise. Maturities shorten. Buyers prefer inflation-protected securities, floating rates, or other currencies. The government's future interest bill increases.

The fiscal wall accelerates this feedback because so much debt refinances continually.

Inflation erodes old fixed-rate debt.

Refinancing writes the new inflation expectation into the coupon.

The shorter the maturity structure, the faster creditors reopen the argument.

This is why the idea of “inflating away the debt” is much easier after a war than in a modern market with frequent issuance and transparent monetary policy. A one-time change in the price level can reduce the real burden of outstanding nominal claims. Persistent inflation becomes part of the price of every new claim.

The United States also issues Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities. TIPS principal adjusts with the consumer price index, so inflation directly increases the nominal amount the government owes. Floating-rate notes reset with short-term Treasury rates, which tend to respond to monetary policy. Social Security benefits receive cost-of-living adjustments. Many parts of the tax code and federal spending are directly or indirectly influenced by inflation.

The federal balance sheet is not one giant fixed-rate bond waiting to be diluted.

It contains claims that adapt.

The private economy adapts faster still.

Workers seek higher wages.

Businesses raise prices.

Banks raise loan rates.

Landlords increase rents where markets permit.

Investors shift portfolios.

The Federal Reserve tightens monetary policy if inflation threatens price stability.

The more credible these responses, the less fiscal benefit the government receives from attempting to use inflation deliberately.

There is an ethical problem too.

Inflation does not distribute pain according to a budget vote.

It changes purchasing power across people with different assets, debts, contracts, wages, bargaining power, and consumption patterns.

A homeowner with a fixed three-percent mortgage can benefit because the real value of the payment falls while nominal income may rise. A renter gets no equivalent debt relief and may face higher rent. A worker with strong bargaining power can negotiate higher wages. A pensioner with a poorly indexed private benefit may lose. A holder of TIPS is protected more than a holder of an ordinary nominal bond. A business with pricing power responds differently from one locked into fixed contracts.

Inflation is not a uniform tax.

It is a revaluation of nominal relationships.

That makes it politically seductive because no legislature has to assign the burden openly.

It also makes it economically corrosive when it becomes unpredictable because every contract begins carrying a larger inflation risk premium.

The post-pandemic inflation episode offers a useful modern case precisely because it did not begin as an explicit fiscal strategy.

Prices rose rapidly as the world emerged from the pandemic. Supply chains were disrupted. Consumers shifted spending patterns. Labor markets tightened. Energy and commodity shocks arrived. Fiscal transfers supported household demand. Monetary policy remained highly accommodative for a time. Researchers continue debating the relative contribution of each factor and how the interaction changed over time.

The result for the federal debt was mixed.

Higher nominal GDP increased the denominator against which debt is often measured. Tax revenues rose in nominal terms. The real value of some outstanding fixed-rate debt fell.

Then the Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply to restore price stability.

Treasury refinancing became more expensive.

Net interest rose.

The inflation relief on old debt helped create the rate environment that made new debt costlier.

This is the fiscal boomerang.

Inflation can improve yesterday's balance sheet while worsening tomorrow's refinancing.

A country with very long fixed-rate debt can enjoy the first effect for longer. A country with large short-term issuance feels the second quickly.

The United States sits in between, with a diversified maturity structure but enough bills and maturing notes that higher rates pass through meaningfully over several years.

This is why monetary credibility becomes more valuable as debt grows.

At first glance, the opposite seems true. If interest expense is high, wouldn't the government prefer the Federal Reserve to cut rates and tolerate more inflation?

In the short run, perhaps.

In the long run, investors holding ten-, twenty-, and thirty-year nominal Treasuries care about the central bank's willingness to protect purchasing power. If they believe fiscal pressure will cause the Fed to accept higher inflation, they can demand a higher term premium.

The effort to reduce short rates politically can raise long rates through credibility loss.

A heavily indebted country therefore has more, not less, reason to protect central-bank independence.

This is the part of fiscal dominance that matters before any dramatic policy break.

Fiscal dominance occurs when the government's financing needs begin constraining monetary policy. The central bank may hesitate to raise rates because doing so increases debt-service costs or threatens financial stability. Markets may anticipate that hesitation and adjust inflation expectations.

The process can be gradual.

A politician criticizes a rate increase because it adds billions to the federal interest bill.

Another argues that the Fed should cap long yields.

A third proposes changing the central bank's mandate or leadership to secure cheaper financing.

None alone creates fiscal dominance.

Together, repeated pressure can change beliefs about the institution.

Beliefs are part of the yield.

This is why the Treasury-Federal Reserve Accord of 1951 still matters. During and after World War II, the Fed had supported low government borrowing rates, including a ceiling on long-term Treasury yields. As inflation pressures grew, conflict between debt-financing objectives and monetary control became increasingly difficult. The Accord restored greater Federal Reserve independence from Treasury financing needs.

The historical details belong to a different monetary and fiscal regime.

The institutional lesson is durable.

A central bank asked to guarantee cheap government finance can eventually lose control of the price level that makes the finance cheap in real terms.

There is a more subtle form of inflationary finance that does not require high headline inflation.

If nominal interest rates are held below inflation for sustained periods, government debt can carry negative real yields. Savers receive less purchasing power than they lent, while nominal GDP can grow faster than the debt's effective real cost. Combined with financial regulation that encourages institutions to hold government debt, this can help reduce debt ratios over time.

Economists often discuss this under the broad heading of financial repression, especially in accounts of the postwar period.

The phrase can be abused.

A bank liquidity rule is not automatically fiscal repression. Pensions own bonds for legitimate liability reasons. Central banks hold government securities as part of monetary operations. Negative real rates can emerge naturally during recessions or periods of high saving.

The key question is whether policy is designed primarily to channel private savings toward government at below-market real returns in order to ease fiscal pressure.

If so, the burden still exists.

It is being paid by savers through lower returns and constrained alternatives.

Inflation is the visible cousin of that mechanism.

Both remind us that the government has more ways to adjust a debt burden than explicit taxes and spending cuts.

That is exactly why a democratic society should make the alternatives visible.

Suppose Congress refuses to raise taxes or slow spending. The deficit remains large. Treasury issues more debt. If investors demand higher yields, interest expense rises. Political pressure builds for easier monetary policy. If the Fed accommodates and inflation rises, real debt burdens can decline but households lose purchasing power and new nominal yields adjust.

No line in the budget will say *inflation tax*.

The transfer is real.

A cleaner adjustment may involve explicit fiscal measures because voters can debate who pays and why. Explicit measures are politically harder precisely because they are visible.

This creates the central temptation of the fiscal wall: choose the adjustment mechanism that does not require naming the loser.

Inflation names the loser later.

The retiree whose grocery bill rises faster than a private pension.

The young household whose wages lag rent.

The bondholder receiving dollars that buy less.

The business facing unstable input costs.

The saver whose nominal interest looks high but real return is low.

The winners are equally dispersed: fixed-rate borrowers, owners of real assets, firms with pricing power, and the federal government on the portion of its debt not immediately repriced or indexed.

There is no tidy coalition.

This is why inflation can corrode political trust even when economists can explain every relative-price mechanism.

People experience money as a unit of account. When the unit becomes unstable, ordinary contracts feel less reliable.

The dollar's reserve role magnifies that concern. Foreign investors can accept exchange-rate risk, but persistent unexpected inflation makes nominal Treasury promises less attractive in real terms. A reserve currency does not need zero inflation; the Federal Reserve explicitly aims for two percent over time. It needs confidence that inflation will remain low and predictable enough for long-term contracts.

The fiscal wall therefore turns price stability into a financing asset.

A credible central bank can lower the inflation-risk component of long yields even if fiscal deficits remain large. That does not solve the primary deficit. It makes the cost of carrying it lower than it would be under an unanchored regime.

Trust reduces the term premium.

This is one of the rare places where institutional virtue has a measurable interest rate, even if economists cannot isolate it perfectly.

There is also a deflationary mirror image.

If inflation falls below expectations or prices decline, the real burden of nominal debt rises. Tax revenues may weaken while fixed nominal obligations remain. Severe deflation can be devastating for leveraged economies because debt becomes heavier in real terms.

This was part of the nightmare of the Great Depression and a concern during the slow post-2008 recovery.

The choice is not between inflation and a neutral zero-inflation world.

A stable low positive inflation rate can help an economy adjust wages and prices while preserving the real meaning of money better than either high inflation or deflation.

For fiscal sustainability, what matters is the combination of real growth, nominal growth, primary balances, and real borrowing costs.

This is why “inflate it away” and “rates must be zero” are mirror-image shortcuts.

Both try to solve a real-resource problem by manipulating the unit in which the claims are written.

Money matters enormously.

It cannot create an unlimited claim on goods, services, labor, energy, housing, medical care, or productive capacity.

The federal government can always make a nominal payment in dollars under ordinary institutional conditions if the legal and political machinery permits it.

Whether those dollars command the expected real resources is the deeper question.

Creditors know that.

They price it.

The fiscal wall is therefore not only a negotiation between Treasury and buyers over yield.

It is a negotiation over what a dollar will mean when the bond matures.

A thirty-year investor is making a political, monetary, and economic forecast whether the investor admits it or not.

Will the central bank remain credible?

Will fiscal policy force accommodation?

Will productivity rise?

Will the currency remain central?

Will the legal promise remain boring?

The coupon is compensation for living with those unknowns.

Inflation is the argument that settles later.

A wise fiscal strategy tries not to win it by surprise.

## The Difference Between Inflation and a Higher Price Level

One reason debt debates become confused is that people use *inflation* to describe two different events.

The first is a one-time rise in the price level.

The second is an ongoing rate at which prices continue rising.

For outstanding fixed nominal debt, the distinction matters enormously.

A surprise jump in the price level can reduce the real value of old debt once. If inflation then returns to a stable low rate, creditors absorb the surprise but new long-term pricing can normalize.

Persistently high inflation is different. It teaches every lender to demand compensation repeatedly. The government refinances into that expectation. Workers negotiate around it. Businesses price around it. Long-term contracts shorten. The nominal system becomes more expensive to use.

A state interested only in reducing the real burden of *old* debt would prefer the impossible combination of one surprise and perfect credibility afterward.

Markets know this.

That is why deliberate inflation is such an unreliable fiscal strategy. The announcement destroys the surprise.

This also helps explain why the post-pandemic price-level increase cannot be translated mechanically into “debt reduction.” The real value of some nominal liabilities fell, but nominal GDP, federal spending, indexed benefits, wages, tax brackets, interest rates, and future coupons all moved too.

The system repriced.

Debt dynamics live in the interaction.

A higher nominal GDP denominator can improve debt-to-GDP even while households feel poorer because real wages lag prices. A lower real value of outstanding fixed-rate debt can help Treasury while higher policy rates raise bill and floating-rate costs. Strong nominal tax receipts can coexist with higher nominal program spending.

The federal government does not consume ratios.

Citizens consume real goods and services.

This is why a fiscal strategy that looks successful only because nominal GDP rose through inflation deserves skepticism. The country may have improved one ratio by making the unit smaller.

Real growth is a cleaner denominator because it creates more output rather than merely more dollars attached to the same output.

The distinction becomes critical when policymakers search for an exit from the wall.

A period of modest inflation within a credible framework can help nominal debt dynamics as part of normal economic growth.

A strategy of tolerating persistently higher inflation to reduce debt is different. It risks raising nominal yields, shortening maturities, weakening the currency's real purchasing power, and creating distributional fights that are harder to legislate honestly.

The temptation grows because inflation can appear politically easier than a tax increase or benefit reduction.

The cost is dispersed.

That is exactly why it should not be treated as painless.

A transparent fiscal adjustment says which claims will change.

Inflation changes them through the unit of account.

The creditor notices.

The worker notices.

The retiree notices.

The borrower notices.

Eventually the voter notices.

By then, the market has already moved the argument into the next coupon.
