# The Hidden Tax of Duration

A bond can be safe enough to use as collateral for the financial system and still lose a quarter of its market value.

That is not a paradox.

It is duration.

Credit risk asks whether the borrower will pay.

Duration asks what a fixed future payment is worth when the market rate changes.

The distinction became painfully public in 2023 when several U.S. regional banks disclosed large unrealized losses on long-dated securities purchased during the low-rate era. The securities included Treasuries and agency mortgage-backed bonds—the kind of assets people describe casually as safe.

They were safe from one danger.

They were not safe from time.

If you buy a ten-year bond paying two percent and new ten-year bonds later pay five percent, nobody will pay you full price for your old two-percent bond unless another feature compensates for the missing income. The market price falls until the expected return becomes competitive.

If you can hold the bond to maturity and you do not need the cash, the price decline may be mostly an accounting experience. You keep receiving the promised coupon and principal.

If you need to sell, the loss becomes real.

If your depositors need their money while you are holding the bond, the distinction between unrealized and realized can disappear very quickly.

This is why duration is a hidden tax on promises made in one rate environment and carried into another.

The tax is not paid to the government.

It is paid to time through repricing.

The federal government's fiscal wall is usually discussed from the borrower's side: higher rates make newly issued debt more expensive. The investor side deserves equal attention because the market can absorb large Treasury supply only if institutions are willing and able to hold duration.

The supply of debt and the supply of balance sheet meet in the yield.

That sentence contains much of modern fixed-income finance.

A pension fund with liabilities thirty years away may welcome long duration. A bank funded with deposits that can leave tomorrow may have to be more careful. An insurer with predictable claims can hold long bonds for matching purposes. A hedge fund may hold Treasuries only as one leg of a leveraged relative-value trade. A household may buy a bill because it wants cash back in three months.

All are Treasury buyers.

They are not buying the same time.

This difference matters as the United States decides how much of its borrowing to issue short and how much long.

Short debt transfers duration risk away from investors and toward the Treasury.

A three-month bill barely exposes the holder to long-term rate changes because principal returns quickly. Treasury, however, has to refinance that funding again soon. If rates rise, the government's interest cost adjusts almost immediately.

Long debt does the opposite.

A thirty-year bond fixes the government's nominal coupon for decades. The investor takes the duration risk. If rates rise after issuance, Treasury keeps paying the old coupon while the bondholder absorbs the market loss.

The government has effectively purchased insurance against future rates.

The price of that insurance is the yield investors demand at issuance.

This is the hidden negotiation behind the maturity mix.

Borrowers like long fixed rates when they fear rates will rise.

Lenders want compensation for taking that risk.

There is no escape from duration.

There is only a decision about where it lives.

During the low-rate era, many private borrowers made an extraordinary trade: they locked in cheap long-duration liabilities. American homeowners with thirty-year fixed mortgages near three percent became the clearest example. When market mortgage rates later doubled, the old mortgage became economically valuable to the borrower.

The bank or investor funding that loan had the other side of the position.

Agency mortgage-backed securities added another complication because homeowners can prepay. When rates fall, borrowers refinance and return principal to investors just when the investor would prefer to keep the high coupon. When rates rise, borrowers stop refinancing, extending the life of the low-coupon bond just when the investor would prefer the opposite.

This negative convexity is one reason mortgage markets can amplify rate moves through hedging.

The federal government does not issue callable mortgages to itself, but Treasury securities sit at the center of the same duration ecosystem. Mortgages are priced relative to Treasury and swap rates. Corporate bonds are benchmarked against Treasuries. Municipal debt responds to the risk-free curve. Derivatives hedge Treasury duration. Pension portfolios use Treasury yields to value liabilities. The federal maturity decision therefore influences the amount and location of duration risk throughout finance.

When Treasury issues more long bonds, it is not simply choosing a payment schedule for taxpayers.

It is asking the market to warehouse more long-term interest-rate risk.

The market will do so at a price.

That price can appear as the term premium.

The term premium is the additional compensation investors require, beyond the expected path of short-term rates, for holding a longer-term bond. Economists cannot observe it directly. They estimate it using models, and models disagree. The concept matters even when the exact number is uncertain.

A thirty-year investor is exposed to things nobody can forecast precisely: inflation, productivity, fiscal policy, central-bank behavior, wars, technological change, demographics, regulation, and the future supply of bonds.

If uncertainty about those variables rises, investors may demand more yield to hold duration even when expected short-term rates are stable or falling.

That is the fiscal-wall scenario in which the Federal Reserve cuts rates but mortgage and long Treasury yields remain stubbornly high.

The front end of the curve can say monetary policy is easing.

The long end can say time is still expensive.

This is why the phrase *the Fed controls interest rates* is too broad.

The Federal Reserve has powerful influence over overnight rates and expectations of future policy. Long-term yields also incorporate inflation expectations, real growth expectations, risk premiums, supply, demand, and market structure.

A government with a large financing need can therefore encounter a long-rate problem the central bank cannot responsibly solve by decree.

It can buy long bonds.

Large-scale asset purchases can compress term premiums and support market functioning. The Fed has done this for macroeconomic and emergency reasons. But if purchases become a permanent tool for keeping Treasury financing cheap regardless of inflation or economic conditions, monetary policy becomes subordinate to fiscal needs.

The hidden tax of duration then moves again.

Instead of taxpayers paying higher nominal interest, holders of money can pay through inflation or a weaker currency if policy loses credibility.

Duration is stubborn that way.

Risk pushed out one door tends to return through another.

Banks offer a vivid institutional example.

A bank takes short-duration liabilities—deposits that can often be withdrawn quickly—and invests some of the money in longer-duration loans and securities. This maturity transformation is part of what banking does. It earns a spread and supports credit creation.

The danger arises when the duration of assets becomes too long relative to the stability and pricing of liabilities.

During the low-rate years, deposits were abundant and cheap. Some banks invested excess cash in long-duration securities to earn more yield. When the Federal Reserve raised rates, the market value of those securities fell. At the same time, depositors could earn much higher rates in money-market funds and Treasury bills.

The liability side suddenly became less sticky.

Silicon Valley Bank's 2023 failure was not a simple story of bad Treasury credit. It was a story of interest-rate risk, deposit concentration, liquidity, management, supervision, and the speed of modern bank runs. The securities did what bonds do when rates rise.

The bank's balance sheet was not prepared for how quickly the other side could move.

The lesson for the fiscal wall is not that Treasury debt endangers banks by existing.

Banks need safe liquid assets. Treasuries are central to that role.

The lesson is that the capacity of financial institutions to absorb more Treasury supply depends on their own liability structures and capital constraints.

A trillion dollars of demand is not interchangeable across buyers.

A money-market fund may want bills.

A pension may want thirty-year bonds.

A bank may prefer intermediate duration but face capital or liquidity rules that affect holdings.

A hedge fund may want exactly the bond cheap enough relative to a futures contract to support a leveraged basis trade.

Foreign reserve managers may care about liquidity, currency policy, and maturity.

The federal government cannot simply say “the world has plenty of savings.”

It has to issue the right claims at prices the actual holders will accept.

Duration is the segmentation inside aggregate demand.

This is also why the twenty-year Treasury bond is instructive.

Treasury reintroduced the maturity in 2020 as financing needs surged. The security expanded the menu of long-duration issuance, but investor demand at twenty years has at times been less natural than at ten or thirty years. The reasons include benchmark familiarity, hedging instruments, pension demand, liquidity, and relative-value relationships.

The issuer is the same.

The time is different.

Market depth is not created simply by printing a maturity date.

For a government facing a fiscal wall, the temptation to extend debt dramatically is obvious.

Why not issue fifty-year bonds?

Why not one-hundred-year bonds?

Lock the rate. Move refinancing risk beyond the horizon of current politics.

Some countries have issued ultra-long debt successfully. The United States has periodically examined whether investor demand would justify it.

The trade is not absurd.

It is not free.

An ultra-long bond asks investors to absorb enormous duration and inflation uncertainty. The yield might include a novelty premium. Liquidity could be weaker. A small dedicated buyer base could make regular issuance difficult. Treasury could reduce rollover risk while paying more expected interest.

Insurance always looks obvious after the loss.

Debt management has to buy it before knowing whether it will be needed.

The hidden tax also appears in public accounting.

Treasury interest expense is recorded as coupons and other financing costs are paid. The market value of outstanding federal fixed-rate debt can fall when rates rise, but the government generally does not record that decline as a gain in the way a trading desk marks a liability to market.

Economically, higher rates reduce the market value of old fixed obligations from the perspective of investors. Fiscally, the benefit to Treasury is temporary because those securities eventually mature and must be refinanced unless the debt is reduced.

The country has an enormous portfolio of old low-rate liabilities whose market value fell when rates rose.

That is good for the issuer in one narrow sense.

The issuer does not get to keep the benefit forever.

Maturity ends it.

This is why the federal interest bill rises with a lag.

Duration delays the tax.

The low-rate coupon keeps protecting the budget until the security reaches the end of its life. Then the protection expires.

The phrase *refinancing wall* makes that expiration sound simultaneous.

It is spread across a ladder.

That is good news.

A ladder gives time to adjust.

It also means the repricing can continue for years after the initial rate shock.

A homeowner sees the whole new payment on the reset date.

Treasury sees thousands of reset dates blended into an average cost.

The smoother line can disguise the same economics.

Duration has a political consequence too.

Long-term debt pushes financing decisions beyond election cycles. A thirty-year bond issued today will still be paying coupons under presidents and Congresses not yet elected. Short bills bring the cost of monetary policy into the current budget more quickly.

The maturity mix therefore allocates not just financial risk but political responsibility.

An administration that borrows short can enjoy a lower current cost if the curve favors it and leave rate risk to successors.

An administration that borrows long may pay more now to protect future taxpayers.

This is one reason Treasury debt management has historically emphasized regularity, predictability, and long-run cost-and-risk objectives rather than election-cycle optimization.

The institutional norm is a guardrail against using maturity to make current budgets look artificially cheap.

That norm becomes more valuable as the debt stock grows because the dollar stakes grow with it.

A small maturity decision on a giant portfolio is not small.

The hidden tax of duration, then, has two payers.

Investors pay when rates rise before maturity.

Borrowers pay when low-rate debt matures into higher rates.

Between them sits time.

The low-rate era favored borrowers who locked terms and punished savers who needed income.

The higher-rate era rewards new lenders and punishes borrowers who must reset.

The Treasury market transfers that balance every day through price.

No speech is required.

No law changes.

The coupon on the new note simply comes out higher.

This is why a country can cross the fiscal wall without a visible collision.

The hidden tax becomes visible only when the budget is asked what it can no longer afford.

By then, duration has already done the work.

## Duration Is a Choice About Who Waits

The language of duration can sound technical enough to hide its political content.

Strip away the formulas and the question is simple.

Who is being asked to wait, and who is being protected from waiting?

When Treasury issues a thirty-year fixed-rate bond, the investor agrees to wait decades for final principal while accepting a coupon that may become unattractive if inflation or market rates rise. Taxpayers receive the protection: the government's nominal rate on that security is fixed.

When Treasury issues a three-month bill, the investor waits almost no time. Taxpayers receive almost no protection from the next rate environment.

The maturity mix allocates patience.

Patience has value because the future is uncertain.

This suggests a way to evaluate debt-management choices that is more useful than asking whether Treasury “should have locked in rates” in hindsight. Ask instead whether the portfolio distributes uncertainty across enough investors and enough dates that no single future rate path becomes intolerable.

That is a resilience problem.

A heavily short-funded sovereign can enjoy low expected costs and still be fragile to persistent inflation.

A heavily long-funded sovereign can enjoy stable nominal coupons and still pay a large term premium for insurance or overwhelm the natural buyer base for duration.

A diversified portfolio accepts that forecasting skill is limited.

This is exactly why private institutions use asset-liability management rather than one heroic interest-rate bet.

The federal government's version is more complicated because Treasury is not the only public balance sheet that matters.

The Federal Reserve can hold long Treasury securities while financing them economically with reserve balances whose administered rate resets quickly. From the perspective of consolidated government finance, some duration that Treasury thought it had issued long can be shortened by central-bank asset purchases.

That does not make quantitative easing a debt-management error. The Fed buys assets for monetary and market-functioning reasons. It does mean that public-sector rate exposure cannot be understood from Treasury maturities alone.

The state has multiple clocks.

This matters if fiscal pressure grows.

Politicians can look at a low coupon on a bond held by the Fed and assume the government locked in cheap funding. The consolidated cash flow can tell a different story once the Fed pays a higher rate on reserves and remittances to Treasury fall or stop.

Duration migrated inside the public balance sheet.

Risk migration is the recurring theme.

Extend Treasury maturity and private investors absorb more duration.

Shorten it and taxpayers absorb more reset risk.

Use inflation-linked debt and taxpayers retain more inflation exposure.

Use central-bank purchases and duration can move from private investors into reserve-rate sensitivity.

Encourage leveraged nonbank buyers and duration can become liquidity risk through repo and margin.

There is no policy that destroys the risk.

There are only policies that choose its location and price.

That observation is useful because political arguments often treat risk transfer as risk reduction.

A regulation that pushes long bonds out of one balance sheet may make that institution safer while concentrating them elsewhere.

A Treasury strategy that protects today's budget can expose tomorrow's.

A Federal Reserve intervention that restores market functioning can protect liquidity while creating political pressure over the size of its balance sheet.

The correct unit of analysis is the system.

The fiscal wall is systemic precisely because Treasury debt is not an isolated liability. It is the collateral, benchmark, reserve asset, duration instrument, and safe-income source around which the rest of finance is organized.

The hidden tax of duration therefore appears whenever an institution promises one timescale and funds itself on another.

The closer those timescales are matched, the less violently a rate shock tends to redistribute wealth or force behavior.

The farther apart they are, the more the system depends on stability.

That is the lesson the low-rate era allowed many balance sheets to postpone.

The refinancing era collects it one maturity at a time.
