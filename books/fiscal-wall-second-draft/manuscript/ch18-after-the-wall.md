# After the Wall

A wall is usually a thing you cross once.

Fiscal pressure is not.

If the United States navigates the coming refinancing years well, there will be no morning after in the cinematic sense. No all-clear. No final Treasury auction followed by a ribbon-cutting ceremony for solvency. The debt will still exist. Securities will still mature. Congress will still argue about taxes and spending. The Federal Reserve will still set rates without knowing the next decade. Recessions, wars, disasters, elections, technologies, and demographic surprises will keep changing the arithmetic.

After the wall is not a debt-free country.

It is a country that has recovered the ability to choose.

That distinction matters because debt itself is useful.

Treasury securities are savings instruments for households, duration for pensions and insurers, reserves for central banks, collateral for markets, liquidity for banks, reference rates for private finance, and an emergency financing mechanism for the state. A federal government with zero debt would not simply be a more virtuous version of the current one. It would remove an asset the rest of the financial system actively uses.

The goal is not zero.

The goal is room.

Room means a recession can arrive and Washington can borrow aggressively without every conversation beginning with whether the bond market will tolerate it.

Room means a war can require mobilization without forcing the government to choose immediately between military necessity and domestic collapse.

Room means a pandemic, financial panic, hurricane, earthquake, cyberattack, energy shock, or technology race can be answered while there are still options.

Room means the Federal Reserve can raise rates when inflation requires it without politicians treating monetary restraint as an attack on the federal budget.

Room means Congress can debate a new investment on its merits rather than discovering that interest has already spent the money.

This is fiscal capacity.

It is easier to recognize after it has been lost.

A family experiences capacity as savings and manageable fixed expenses. A company experiences it as cash, unused credit lines, low leverage, and the ability to issue capital when an opportunity appears. A country experiences it through tax capacity, market access, monetary credibility, productive growth, institutional trust, and the relationship between its debt and the economy supporting it.

The United States possesses all of these in unusual quantities.

That is why the fiscal wall is a choice problem rather than an immediate solvency problem.

The country has time because the world continues to value its liabilities.

The question is whether time is treated as an asset or an excuse.

Imagine the good version of 2036.

Debt held by the public remains large, perhaps larger in nominal dollars than anyone in 2026 would have found comfortable. But the ratio to GDP has stabilized or begun to decline. Productivity growth has been stronger than the cautious baseline because investment in software, artificial intelligence, energy, manufacturing, research, and infrastructure produced real gains. Housing supply has improved in enough high-demand places that workers can move toward opportunity. Immigration and labor-force participation have softened demographic pressure.

Congress enacted a fiscal package years earlier that nobody called elegant.

Some tax preferences disappeared.

Some rates or bases changed.

Social Security financing improved through a combination of revenue and slowly phased benefit adjustments, with stronger protections near the bottom of the income distribution. Health policy reduced some prices and administrative waste rather than merely shifting costs. Discretionary spending was reviewed instead of cut by formula. High-return public investment survived.

The package did not balance the budget.

It changed the slope.

That was enough to change expectations.

Treasury continued its regular issuance. Bills remained abundant because cash investors wanted them. Notes and bonds continued providing benchmarks and duration. Central clearing and other market reforms increased netting and transparency. Repo experienced episodes of volatility without becoming a recurring emergency. The investor base broadened as households, pensions, insurers, funds, banks, foreign institutions, and trading firms adapted to the larger market.

The Federal Reserve cut rates when inflation allowed and raised them when inflation required. It did not become the financing desk of the Treasury. Its independence occasionally annoyed elected officials, which was evidence that the arrangement still worked.

Net interest stopped growing faster than everything else.

That was the key change.

Not because interest disappeared, but because it stopped taking another bite out of the budget each year.

The fiscal wall in that world becomes a period historians name afterward: the great repricing after the zero-rate era, when the United States discovered that a much larger debt stock had to be carried in a world where money once again had a meaningful price.

The country adapted.

Nothing about this scenario requires heroic austerity or miraculous growth.

It requires enough of several ordinary things.

That is why it is plausible.

Now imagine the less good version.

There is still no default.

Treasury still auctions debt. The dollar remains the largest reserve currency. The Federal Reserve still functions. The economy still grows.

But Congress never reaches a durable settlement. Every package is temporary. Tax cuts are extended without offsets. Spending programs are protected or expanded. Entitlement changes are postponed. Health costs continue outpacing the tax base. Productivity improves, but not enough to outrun the primary deficit and interest.

Debt rises faster than GDP.

Long yields contain a somewhat larger term premium because investors expect heavier supply and more policy uncertainty. The Fed sometimes cuts short rates, but mortgage rates do not return to the levels households remember from the 2010s. Treasury uses bills flexibly, which keeps financing smooth but leaves the budget sensitive to the policy rate.

Interest becomes one of the central facts of every appropriation.

No politician campaigns on paying bondholders, but every politician governs around the line.

Federal aid grows more slowly. Public investment is squeezed because it is easier to postpone than current benefits. Defense procurement becomes more contentious. Tax increases happen in pieces. Retirement ages and benefit formulas change later and more abruptly than they would have under an earlier settlement.

The country remains rich.

It becomes less agile.

This is not catastrophe.

It is the loss of option value.

A future emergency reveals it. Congress can still borrow, but the political argument is uglier and the market price more sensitive. The Fed must balance recession support against inflation credibility with a much larger fiscal stock in the background. States and cities absorb more responsibility. Younger taxpayers inherit a government capable of doing many things but free to begin fewer new ones.

The wall in that world was never crossed.

It became the landscape.

There is a third version, the one crisis books prefer.

Inflation returns persistently. Fiscal policy remains loose. Political attacks on the Federal Reserve intensify. Investors demand more compensation for long nominal debt. The dollar weakens while long yields rise. A leveraged Treasury trade unwinds during heavy issuance and repo markets become strained. A debt-limit confrontation creates payment uncertainty at exactly the wrong moment.

The Fed intervenes to preserve market functioning, and political leaders interpret the intervention as proof that the central bank can always finance the government. The distinction between liquidity support and fiscal accommodation becomes harder to defend.

Long-term inflation expectations begin to move.

Treasury shortens issuance because long bonds are expensive, which increases future refinancing sensitivity. Banks and pensions face political pressure to absorb more government debt. What began as market stabilization drifts toward financial repression.

The government never runs out of dollars.

The dollars begin to lose some of the institutional premium that made financing so easy.

This is the version in which the fiscal wall becomes monetary.

It is possible.

It is not inevitable.

The entire value of studying the wall before that point is to keep the tail scenario a tail.

What would success look like to an ordinary person?

Probably nothing like a fiscal victory.

A thirty-five-year-old worker would receive a Social Security statement reflecting rules that are credible enough to plan around. The benefit might be somewhat different from what an older generation received. The worker would have decades of notice.

A retiree could buy Treasury securities with a positive real yield without fearing that inflation was being used deliberately to reduce the government's obligations.

A homeowner would pay a mortgage rate determined by economic conditions rather than an added premium for fiscal and monetary instability.

A city could finance a water system at a rate reflecting the project and the market, not a national crisis premium.

A business could evaluate a factory against a risk-free rate that contained honest information about capital scarcity rather than political manipulation.

A taxpayer would still complain about taxes.

That is not a failure condition.

A citizen would still complain about spending.

Also normal.

The difference would be that federal interest no longer forced every complaint into a shrinking room.

Success would be boring enough that the phrase *fiscal wall* eventually sounded dated.

That is how institutional victories often look.

Airplanes do not crash.

Banks do not run.

Bridges remain standing.

Auctions clear.

A crisis arrives and the government has capacity.

The benefit is an event that did not happen.

This makes fiscal reform politically difficult because the reward is invisible. A tax change is visible. A benefit adjustment is visible. A canceled subsidy is visible. The future crisis that becomes easier to manage cannot vote in the present.

Democracies have always struggled with this asymmetry.

They are not uniquely bad at it.

Companies underinvest in resilience because spare capacity lowers current returns. Households underinsure because the premium is certain and the disaster is not. Cities postpone maintenance because the pothole repaired before it forms earns no ribbon-cutting.

Fiscal capacity is national redundancy.

It looks inefficient until needed.

The debt debate becomes healthier when framed this way.

The question is not whether debt is good or bad.

It is what quantity and structure preserve enough redundancy for a country facing an uncertain future.

This framing also changes the politics of surplus.

A budget surplus is not morally superior in every year. Running a surplus during a depression can be destructive. Refusing to borrow for a high-return investment can make future citizens poorer. A government should use its balance sheet when the social return exceeds the cost and when emergencies require collective risk bearing.

The purpose of restraint in ordinary years is to make generosity in extraordinary years possible.

That is a more defensible fiscal ethic than balanced budgets as virtue.

It is also harder because “ordinary” is contested.

One coalition sees climate change as an emergency requiring immediate investment.

Another sees geopolitical competition and defense capacity as the emergency.

Another sees housing, health care, inequality, immigration, infrastructure, or technological competition as urgent enough to justify borrowing.

Many of these problems are genuinely urgent.

Urgency cannot be the only financing test because a modern country will always have more urgent claims than free fiscal capacity.

The harder question is which expenses should be paid now by current taxpayers, which should be financed because benefits extend into the future, and which promises should be redesigned before borrowing becomes automatic.

This is political judgment, not bond mathematics.

Bond mathematics sets the price of postponing the judgment.

After the wall, the United States will still need a rule for that price.

A practical rule might begin with the primary balance rather than the headline deficit. Interest is largely inherited from past choices and current rates. The primary balance shows whether current taxes and non-interest spending are adding new pressure. Stabilizing the debt ratio does not require eliminating interest; it requires aligning the primary path with growth and financing costs over time.

Another rule is to distinguish recurring commitments from temporary emergencies and long-lived investments. Borrowing for a one-time recession response is different from borrowing for a permanent benefit with no dedicated revenue. Borrowing for infrastructure that raises future capacity is different from borrowing because current voters refuse to pay for current consumption.

The categories are not always clean.

The discipline comes from making the argument explicitly.

A third rule is to preserve maturity diversity. Treasury should not gamble the national balance sheet on a forecast that short rates will fall or long rates will never rise. A resilient debt portfolio accepts that forecasts fail.

A fourth is to preserve the institutions that make Treasury debt unusually valuable: an independent central bank, predictable issuance, reliable payment, open capital markets, strong legal institutions, and transparent fiscal data.

These are forms of capital.

They do not appear in the debt-to-GDP ratio.

They determine how much debt the ratio can carry.

A fifth rule is to act before the political pain needs to be concentrated.

Every year of delay changes who can adjust. A twenty-year phase-in becomes a ten-year phase-in. A modest tax-base change becomes a larger rate increase. A manageable primary adjustment becomes a crisis package. Interest compounds while the options narrow.

Delay is not neutral.

It is a decision to make future policy more abrupt.

This is why the title *Fiscal Wall* should not be read as prophecy.

Walls can be moved.

Roads can turn.

Drivers can slow.

The image matters because the current path eventually requires a change, not because the location or impact is predetermined.

There will be people who say the wall is invented because the United States can always issue dollars.

They are right that nominal solvency is different for a monetary sovereign.

There will be people who say the wall means the United States is already bankrupt.

They are wrong because a sovereign with enormous tax capacity, productive assets, its own currency, deep markets, and global demand for its liabilities is not a household whose liabilities exceed a checking account.

The useful position lies between slogans.

Real resources are finite.

Political promises compete for them.

Debt moves claims through time.

Interest prices the movement.

Inflation can change the real value of the claims.

Growth can make them easier to carry.

Institutions determine how much trust the claims receive.

That is the whole machine.

There is no mystery left after that.

Only choices.

A generation ago, the United States entered a world where the price of time kept falling. That world made a great deal possible. It also allowed difficult fiscal disagreements to be financed rather than resolved.

The next generation inherits a different price.

Its task is not to pay for every mistake of the past in one heroic act.

It is to stop using the future as the easiest constituency to bill.

If it succeeds, Treasury will continue doing what Treasury does.

A note will mature.

Another will be sold.

A pension fund will buy some.

A money-market fund will buy bills.

A household will build a bond ladder.

A foreign central bank will hold reserves.

A dealer will make a market.

The Fed will watch conditions.

Congress will argue.

The machinery will remain almost boring.

That is after the wall.

Not an ending.

A country that recovered enough room to have a future worth financing.
