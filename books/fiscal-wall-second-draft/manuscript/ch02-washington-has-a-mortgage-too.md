# Washington Has a Mortgage Too

Washington does not have a mortgage.

That sentence needs to come first because almost every popular discussion of federal debt eventually becomes a household story and then forgets to return.

A family cannot issue the currency in which its debts are denominated. It cannot tax its neighbors. It cannot require banks to hold assets that resemble its IOUs. It cannot create a central bank, regulate capital markets, alter the legal terms of public programs, or count on global institutions to use its liabilities as reserves and collateral. A family has a finite working life. The United States, as a political entity, has no scheduled retirement date.

Federal debt is therefore not a giant credit-card balance.

It is also not free.

The challenge is to hold both ideas at once.

The household metaphor survives only where it describes a real financial relationship: borrowing today creates claims on future resources, and the price of carrying those claims can change as debt matures and refinances.

That limited analogy is enough to explain why the current period matters.

The federal government's balance sheet contains many kinds of obligations, but Treasury securities are the part the market prices every day. Bills, notes, bonds, inflation-protected securities, and floating-rate notes are issued to investors in exchange for dollars. Those dollars finance deficits, replace maturing securities, and manage the government's cash position.

When a ten-year note matures, Treasury does not normally save ten years of tax revenue and hand the noteholder a pile of accumulated cash. It runs a continuing financing program. New securities are sold. The maturing principal is paid. The debt stock rolls forward.

This is normal sovereign finance.

The word *mortgage* becomes useful only if we imagine the entire federal debt not as one loan, but as millions of loans with different reset dates.

Some reset next week.

Some next month.

Some in two years.

Some in thirty.

A portion is indexed to inflation. A portion floats with short-term rates. Each maturity determines when a past borrowing decision meets a future market price.

The result is a national reset schedule.

That schedule explains why the Federal Reserve's rate increases after 2022 did not instantly make every dollar of federal debt expensive. Old securities kept their coupons. The government's average interest rate moved gradually as the portfolio turned over.

It also explains why the fiscal effect persists after the central bank stops tightening.

A policy rate can peak and fall while the average cost of Treasury debt continues rising for a time. If a low-coupon five-year note matures after market rates have already begun declining, the replacement can still carry a higher yield than the security being retired.

The government can therefore be refinancing into a cheaper market than last year and a more expensive market than the debt it is replacing.

That is the kind of sentence that makes public finance sound unnecessarily complicated.

A mortgage table makes it obvious.

Suppose you borrowed at two percent, rates rose to seven, and then fell to five before renewal. The refinancing environment is improving. Your payment is still going up.

Washington is living inside that lag.

CBO's 2026 baseline captured it in the average interest rate on debt held by the public. The agency estimated that average at about 3.4 percent in 2026 and projected it to rise toward 3.9 percent in the later years of the projection, reflecting both its interest-rate assumptions and the maturity structure of outstanding debt.

A few tenths of a percentage point sound small until they are applied to tens of trillions of dollars.

One percentage point on $30 trillion is $300 billion a year if the entire stock were repriced, which it is not. That simple multiplication is not a budget forecast because the debt mix, inflation-indexed securities, Federal Reserve remittances, maturity schedule, and new borrowing all matter.

It is useful for scale.

The federal balance sheet has become large enough that ordinary changes in the price of money produce extraordinary dollar effects.

This is the first way in which the present differs from many earlier episodes of high rates.

In the early 1980s, nominal Treasury yields were far higher than they are today. The United States endured double-digit interest rates as the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker fought inflation. Anyone who looks only at today's yield and calls it unprecedented has forgotten history.

The debt stock was different.

Federal debt held by the public was much smaller relative to the economy. The same rate could not generate the same fiscal burden because the base to which it applied was smaller.

Later, the debt stock became much larger, but rates fell.

Those trends offset one another for years.

The strange comfort of the post-2008 period was that the United States could add debt while the cost of carrying it remained unexpectedly low. Economists debated whether secular stagnation, demographics, global savings, central-bank policy, low inflation, regulatory demand for safe assets, and reserve-currency effects would keep rates depressed indefinitely.

For a while, the debate seemed almost academic.

Then inflation returned.

The Federal Reserve raised rates aggressively. Long-term yields rose. Money-market yields moved from near zero to levels savers could actually see. The cost of time reappeared.

The United States entered that change with a debt stock shaped by years of low-rate borrowing and several enormous shocks.

This combination—the larger stock and higher marginal rate—is what makes the refinancing era distinctive.

It does not make crisis inevitable.

It makes sensitivity larger.

A household with a $100,000 mortgage and one with a $1 million mortgage can face the same rate reset. The second household experiences a much larger dollar change. It may also have ten times the income and be perfectly capable of paying. The ratio matters as much as the amount.

For a country, GDP and federal revenue play versions of that denominator role.

Debt measured only in dollars is a weak guide across decades because the economy grows and prices rise. A trillion dollars meant something different in 1980 than it does in 2026. Debt relative to GDP provides a better sense of national carrying capacity, though it is still not a complete measure of fiscal risk.

CBO projected debt held by the public at about 101 percent of GDP in 2026 and 120 percent in 2036 under current law and its economic assumptions. That path matters because the denominator is already included. The debt is projected to grow faster than the economy.

A mortgage borrower whose debt grows faster than income for decades should at least ask why.

The federal answer begins with the primary deficit.

The primary balance is the budget excluding net interest. It tells us whether current taxes and non-interest spending are adding to debt before the cost of past borrowing is counted.

This distinction is essential.

If a government runs a primary surplus large enough to cover its interest burden, debt can fall. If it runs a small primary deficit while growth exceeds the effective interest rate, the debt ratio can sometimes stabilize or decline. If it runs a persistent primary deficit while interest costs rise, the debt dynamic becomes more demanding.

CBO's 2026 baseline projected a primary deficit of about 2.6 percent of GDP that year and continued primary deficits through the decade.

That means the United States is not merely borrowing to pay interest on old debt.

It is also borrowing to finance a gap between current non-interest spending and current revenue.

This is the second difference from a simple refinancing story.

Washington is resetting the mortgage while adding rooms to the house on credit.

Again, the metaphor will fail if pushed too far. Some federal spending is investment that can raise future productivity. Some tax changes can affect growth. The government does not have a fixed house whose value can be appraised. Public spending can alter the size of the future economy in both directions.

The underlying timing problem remains.

A borrower can handle a rate reset more easily when new borrowing stops.

Persistent primary deficits mean the financing program must absorb both rollover and expansion.

This is why headlines about “ten trillion dollars to refinance” can mislead in opposite directions.

A dramatic headline can exaggerate by treating gross maturities as if they were all new borrowing. Short-term Treasury bills can mature and be reissued several times in a year, causing the gross rollover number to become enormous without adding an equal amount to the debt stock.

A dismissive response can understate the issue by saying that rollover is routine and therefore irrelevant. Rollover is routine, but the replacement security receives a new yield. A very large routine operation can still have a large budget consequence when the price changes.

The correct question is not “Does ten trillion dollars really come due?”

The correct questions are: What exactly is included in that number? How much is gross rollover? How much is net new borrowing? Which maturities are resetting? At what rates? Who is buying? What is happening to the average interest cost? How much fiscal room is being consumed?

These are mortgage questions stripped of the metaphor.

The federal government has another advantage that households do not: it can change its maturity mix.

Treasury can issue more bills, more notes, or more bonds within the constraints of regular and predictable debt management and market demand. It can use floating-rate notes. It can issue TIPS. It can conduct buybacks to improve liquidity and cash management. It can adjust auction sizes gradually.

A homeowner choosing between a fixed and adjustable mortgage faces a primitive version of this decision.

Borrow short and the current rate may be cheaper, but the payment resets sooner.

Borrow long and the rate may be higher, but uncertainty is pushed away.

Treasury makes the same trade at national scale.

There is no perfect maturity.

Short bills are deeply demanded by money funds and cash investors. They are flexible. They also transmit Federal Reserve policy into the budget quickly.

Long bonds reduce rollover risk. They can be expensive when investors demand a term premium for holding decades of inflation and duration risk.

Treasury's task is not to predict rates better than the market.

It is to finance the government at the lowest expected cost over time subject to prudent risk.

That last phrase matters because a political system will always be tempted by today's cheaper payment.

If short-term rates are expected to fall, why not issue more bills and wait?

If long rates appear low, why not lock them for a generation?

Because both choices are forecasts, and the sovereign debt manager has to live with being wrong on a scale no private borrower can match.

This is one reason the regularity of Treasury issuance is an institutional asset. Predictable auctions reduce the suspicion that debt management is being used to manipulate short-term political optics.

A mortgage borrower can make a tactical rate bet.

The country should be more cautious.

The federal government also has a central bank.

This is where household metaphors become especially dangerous.

The Federal Reserve can buy Treasury securities and create reserves. During severe market stress, it can support Treasury market functioning. Through monetary policy, it can affect short-term rates and financial conditions. These powers make a classic liquidity crisis in dollar-denominated federal debt very different from a private refinancing crisis.

But the existence of the Fed does not make interest cost irrelevant.

If the central bank holds rates artificially low to help Treasury despite inflation, it can weaken price stability and credibility. If it purchases unlimited government debt to avoid market pricing, the problem can migrate from interest expense into inflation, exchange rates, and institutional trust.

The United States can create dollars.

It cannot create purchasing power without limit.

That is the resource constraint hidden beneath every monetary shortcut.

A household runs out of money.

A sovereign can run out of things worth buying at the old price.

Inflation is one way the constraint announces itself.

The reserve-currency role creates another crucial difference.

The world needs dollar safe assets for reasons that have little to do with whether Congress is behaving like a prudent family. Central banks hold Treasuries as reserves. Banks use them for liquidity. Dealers finance them in repo. Derivatives markets use them as collateral. Global investors need a deep place to park dollars.

Treasury debt is therefore both a liability of the U.S. government and an input into private financial infrastructure.

The mortgage lender wants to be repaid.

The Treasury investor may actively need the mortgage to exist.

This demand is part of America's fiscal privilege.

It can also encourage complacency.

A borrower surrounded by eager lenders can mistake access for affordability.

The fact that investors want Treasuries does not determine the yield they will demand. Higher supply can be absorbed at a higher price of time. A successful auction at five percent is still successful. The budget still pays five percent on that security.

This is the central paradox of the fiscal wall.

Market strength can hide fiscal weakness.

The Treasury market can remain the deepest and most liquid sovereign market in the world while higher rates steadily consume budget room. There is no requirement that a market crisis arrive first.

Washington's mortgage, then, is not a mortgage in legal or monetary terms.

It is a maturity schedule attached to future claims.

The government can refinance.

It can alter the mix.

It can tax.

It can grow.

It can inflate.

It can cut spending.

It can borrow more.

It can rely on a unique global demand for its liabilities.

Those options make the United States enormously resilient.

They also mean there is no single moment when somebody forces a decision.

A homeowner receives the reset letter.

Congress can postpone opening it.

That is the real danger of the analogy.

Not that Washington is like a household.

That it has far more ways than a household to avoid an immediate reckoning.

The fiscal wall is what accumulates while those ways are used.
