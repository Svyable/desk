# Cities, States, and the Federal Shadow

A city can have a balanced budget and still live in Washington's interest-rate shadow.

So can a state.

The United States is a federation, which means fiscal arguments occur on several balance sheets at once. The federal government can run deficits routinely and issue debt in a currency it controls. Most state governments operate under balanced-budget requirements of one kind or another. Cities borrow for capital projects, manage pensions, collect property and sales taxes, and sometimes enter bankruptcy or state-supervised restructuring when finances fail.

These governments are not miniature Treasuries.

Their borrowing costs still begin with the Treasury curve.

A municipal bond investor compares tax-exempt yields with taxable alternatives. A state issuing debt competes for capital against corporate bonds, Treasuries, mortgages, and other assets. When the risk-free rate rises, the required yield on new municipal debt usually rises too, even if the city's own credit has not changed.

That is the federal shadow.

A Treasury refinancing cycle can therefore tighten local public investment without Congress cutting a single grant.

The mechanism is ordinary.

Suppose a city wants to build a water-treatment plant expected to last forty years. At a three-percent borrowing cost, the debt service may fit comfortably inside projected utility revenue. At six percent, the same plant can require higher water bills, a smaller project, more upfront tax revenue, or a delay.

The plant did not become less necessary.

Time became more expensive.

Infrastructure is full of this problem because its benefits and costs live on different clocks. Roads, schools, transit systems, electrical grids, airports, sewers, flood defenses, and public buildings provide services for decades. Debt is often an appropriate way to make future users share the cost.

Higher rates do not make borrowing irresponsible.

They raise the hurdle for choosing what is worth financing.

That is healthy when the hurdle eliminates weak projects.

It is dangerous when essential maintenance is deferred because budgets were built around cheap money.

Deferred maintenance is debt without a coupon.

The bill arrives as deterioration.

American local government has long demonstrated how financing conditions can shape physical reality. A booming municipality with a growing property-tax base can borrow to extend roads and utilities to new neighborhoods. A shrinking city may struggle to maintain infrastructure built for a larger population. Pension obligations can compete with current services. A recession can reduce sales and income taxes just when social needs rise.

The federal government often acts as shock absorber.

During recessions and emergencies, Washington can send aid to states and localities. It can fund transportation, disaster recovery, health programs, schools, housing, and infrastructure. Federal tax rules can subsidize municipal borrowing by exempting certain bond interest from federal income tax. Federal agencies can guarantee or lend for specific purposes.

This creates another way the fiscal wall reaches local government.

A federal budget squeezed by rising interest has less room to transfer resources downward.

The pressure may not look like a direct cut.

Congress can reduce the growth of grants. It can require states to share more costs. It can change matching formulas. It can defer infrastructure authorizations. It can preserve headline programs while narrowing eligibility or reimbursement.

Federal fiscal stress can be exported through the intergovernmental system.

States then face choices the federal government can often postpone.

Raise taxes.

Cut services.

Delay projects.

Draw reserves.

Borrow within legal limits.

Shift costs to cities.

The balanced-budget rule can make state policy procyclical: tax revenues fall in a recession and spending pressure rises, yet the state has less freedom to borrow for operating expenses. Federal support can prevent those cuts from deepening the downturn.

A more indebted federal government does not automatically lose that capacity.

It can still borrow in a recession, and safe-haven demand may even lower Treasury yields. The question is how much political and fiscal room exists when the emergency arrives.

This is why public debt is partly insurance.

A strong sovereign balance sheet allows the national government to absorb shocks smaller jurisdictions cannot.

Running large structural deficits in ordinary times uses some of that insurance premium in advance.

Municipal markets also provide a useful comparison because actual subnational default exists in the United States.

Cities and local authorities can fail financially. Detroit filed for Chapter 9 bankruptcy in 2013 after decades of population loss, economic decline, high debt, pension and benefit obligations, and eroding tax capacity. Puerto Rico entered a sweeping restructuring under the federal PROMESA framework after years of debt accumulation, economic weakness, and fiscal crisis. Orange County, California, filed for bankruptcy in 1994 after leveraged investment losses tied to rising interest rates.

These cases are not analogies for federal default.

They are demonstrations of how different fiscal institutions matter.

Detroit could not create dollars.

Puerto Rico does not control the U.S. currency.

Orange County's investment portfolio could not ask the Federal Reserve to stabilize its funding.

The federal government stands above these entities in the monetary hierarchy.

That hierarchy creates responsibility as well as privilege.

When a state or city experiences crisis, the federal government may be the only balance sheet large enough to respond. Disaster aid, financial assistance, infrastructure funding, unemployment support, health transfers, and emergency lending can all depend on federal capacity.

The fiscal wall therefore affects national resilience through subsidiarity.

A heavily indebted sovereign can still help its parts.

It has to be willing to borrow more to do so.

This becomes more politically contentious when interest is already a dominant line in the federal budget.

The municipal market also illuminates a common misconception about government borrowing: the idea that all debt finances consumption.

Much local borrowing is tied directly to assets. A city issues bonds for a school or utility project because the asset will serve future taxpayers. This matches the duration of payment to the duration of benefit.

Federal accounting is less clean because the unified budget mixes investment and consumption. Borrowing can finance a bridge, a scientific program, a tax cut, a benefit payment, a war, or payroll. The Treasury security does not remember which one.

This makes federal debt management harder to judge morally.

A dollar borrowed for a productive investment can raise future capacity.

A dollar borrowed to avoid a politically inconvenient current tax may not.

The market prices both as Treasury debt.

Cities often face the discipline more directly because bond covenants, dedicated revenues, project economics, voter approval, and credit ratings can tie borrowing to purpose.

That does not make municipal finance inherently prudent. Local governments can make terrible investments, use optimistic pension assumptions, hide liabilities, subsidize private projects, or rely on speculative tax-base growth.

It simply makes the connection between asset and debt easier to see.

The federal shadow also falls through pensions.

State and local pension systems hold vast portfolios designed to meet long-term obligations to teachers, police officers, firefighters, civil servants, and other workers. Their funding status depends on contributions, benefit formulas, demographics, investment returns, and discount assumptions.

Low interest rates complicated those systems by reducing safe yields and increasing the appeal of higher-return assets. Some plans remained well funded through strong contributions and governance. Others entered the post-zero era with large unfunded liabilities.

Higher bond yields can improve future expected fixed-income returns, but mark-to-market losses and changes in asset values can affect funding ratios. More importantly, higher municipal borrowing costs and pension contributions compete for the same tax base.

A state can face a version of the household reset from both sides at once.

New debt costs more.

Old retirement promises still need funding.

Federal aid may become harder to expand.

Taxpayers feel the combined claim.

This is where the phrase *crowding out* becomes local and concrete.

At the federal level, economists often use crowding out to describe government borrowing absorbing savings that might otherwise finance private investment, potentially raising interest rates and reducing capital formation.

At the city level, crowding out can mean the pension contribution and debt service consume the budget before the parks department opens the gate.

The mechanism is visible enough that nobody needs a model.

Fixed claims arrive first.

Discretionary services receive what remains.

The federal government can avoid that sequence by borrowing more.

States and cities often cannot.

That difference is one reason federal fiscal problems can look benign for longer. The national government has a larger capacity to shift the adjustment through time.

The adjustment still exists.

It can later arrive through taxes, inflation, benefit changes, lower federal investment, or higher interest.

The municipal market also provides a counterexample to the idea that higher rates always produce fiscal disaster.

Many states entered the 2020s with stronger rainy-day funds than in prior cycles. Pandemic-era federal transfers and strong revenue in some years helped rebuild reserves. Municipal credit quality across much of the market remained strong even as yields rose. Investors benefited from higher tax-exempt income. Projects continued to be financed.

A rate shock can be absorbed when balance sheets have room.

That is the lesson worth carrying back to Washington.

Resilience comes from entering the shock with options.

Cash reserves.

Manageable debt.

Flexible budgets.

A broad tax base.

Pension discipline.

Access to markets.

The federal equivalents are not identical, but the principle is.

A sovereign with fiscal room can choose how to respond.

A sovereign without room discovers that markets, inflation, and politics choose more of the response for it.

The federal shadow reaches housing too.

Mortgage rates are closely linked to longer-term Treasury yields and mortgage-market spreads. If long Treasury yields remain elevated because of strong growth, inflation uncertainty, or a higher term premium, households can face expensive mortgages even after the Fed begins easing short rates.

Local governments then confront a feedback loop.

High mortgage rates reduce housing turnover and can slow new construction. Housing scarcity keeps rents and prices high in supply-constrained areas. Property-tax assessments may behave differently from transaction volume. Developers require higher returns. Affordable-housing projects need more subsidy. Infrastructure districts become more expensive to finance.

A federal refinancing environment can therefore show up in a zoning-board meeting without anyone mentioning the national debt.

The same is true for utilities.

Electricity demand is rising in parts of the country because of data centers, manufacturing, electrification, and population growth. Building generation, transmission, substations, and local distribution networks requires enormous capital. Utilities recover financing costs through rates approved by regulators or market structures.

Higher Treasury yields raise the baseline cost of capital across these investments.

If the country needs more physical capacity at the same moment federal borrowing is large, the competition is not merely accounting.

It occurs in the price of capital, construction labor, equipment, and political attention.

This does not mean federal debt crowds out every grid project dollar-for-dollar. Global capital markets are deep, and higher rates can attract more savings. Productive investment can raise growth. The interaction is not a fixed pool.

It means the federal balance sheet is never isolated from the capital costs facing the rest of the country.

Treasuries are the reference price.

The federal government casts a shadow because everything else is priced in relation to the safest borrower.

There is a final way the wall reaches states and cities: political blame.

When Washington tightens grants, governors blame Congress.

When states reduce aid, mayors blame governors.

When cities raise property taxes, residents blame city hall.

The original fiscal pressure can travel several layers before the voter experiences it.

This makes national adjustment difficult to see.

A federal spending cut may reappear as a local tax increase.

A federal tax change may affect municipal-bond demand.

A higher Treasury yield may raise infrastructure financing costs.

A change in Medicaid financing may force state budget decisions.

The fiscal wall does not stay in the Treasury Department.

It diffuses.

That diffusion can be politically convenient because no single actor owns the final pain.

It can also be economically rational because different levels of government should make different decisions. Localities know local needs. States can experiment. Federalism disperses power.

The danger is using the layers to hide the constraint.

A country cannot solve a national resource problem by moving the invoice between governments.

The taxpayer, ratepayer, worker, retiree, and investor eventually meet it somewhere.

Cities understand this because their streets are physical.

A bond issue becomes a school.

A deferred bond issue becomes an old school for another year.

A pension contribution becomes fewer current dollars for something else.

A higher interest rate becomes a smaller project or a larger bill.

Federal finance feels abstract only because the scale is larger and the feedback is slower.

The shadow makes it visible.

If the fiscal wall narrows Washington's choices, the first evidence may not be a failed Treasury auction.

It may be a governor told there is less federal matching money.

A transit authority paying more to refinance.

A utility commission approving higher rates.

A city postponing a bridge.

A school district asking voters for another bond.

The national balance sheet will still look national.

Its consequences will have addresses.

## The Capital Budget America Does Not Have

State and local finance also reveals a structural weakness in the way the federal debate talks about borrowing.

Many governments distinguish operating expenses from capital investment more explicitly than Washington does.

A city may borrow for a treatment plant expected to serve residents for forty years while paying current salaries from current revenue. The distinction is imperfect and can be gamed, but it forces a question: does the debt finance an asset whose benefits persist with the payments?

The federal unified budget does not create that same bright line.

A Treasury bond finances the government's aggregate cash need. The security does not carry a label saying *interstate bridge*, *fighter aircraft*, *tax cut*, *retirement check*, or *laboratory*.

This accounting neutrality is convenient for financing.

It can be dangerous for politics.

If every deficit dollar looks the same, policymakers can debate total borrowing without distinguishing whether the borrowing expands future productive capacity or merely shifts current consumption into the future.

That distinction becomes more important as rates rise.

A project with a durable social return may still justify debt at a higher yield.

Recurring spending without a financing source becomes more expensive for every year it persists.

The federal wall therefore creates a case for better fiscal classification even if no formal capital budget is adopted.

Policymakers should be able to ask which deficits are temporary stabilization, which finance long-lived assets, and which are structural gaps between recurring commitments and recurring revenue.

The municipal world cannot answer that question perfectly either. Stadium subsidies can be called investment. Pension borrowing can disguise operating stress. Infrastructure can be overbuilt. Special districts can obscure liabilities.

The discipline comes from asking, not from assuming the label is honest.

A federal government under interest pressure faces a particular risk: cutting capital because it is easier than cutting promises.

A bridge can be postponed.

A maintenance program can be deferred.

A research grant can be canceled.

A transmission line can wait another appropriations cycle.

Benefits already reaching households are politically harder to change.

The path of least resistance can therefore improve the near-term deficit while weakening the future denominator.

Cities know the cost of that strategy because deferred maintenance becomes visible in potholes, water breaks, school roofs, and unreliable transit.

At the national level, the damage can be slower and larger: weaker logistics, constrained energy, reduced scientific capacity, or lower resilience to disaster.

The fiscal wall should not become an excuse to eat the seed corn.

That principle also changes how federal aid to states should be judged.

A transfer that finances current consumption has one fiscal profile.

A transfer that enables high-return infrastructure has another.

A grant that replaces local revenue during a severe recession can prevent procyclical cuts and preserve productive capacity.

A permanent transfer formula can create a recurring federal claim.

The category *aid to states* is too broad for serious fiscal analysis.

So is the category *spending*.

The shadow of federal debt will fall on local governments regardless.

The policy question is whether it darkens investment or forces better prioritization.

That choice will determine whether the next generation inherits only the federal coupon or also the roads, grids, schools, ports, and water systems capable of earning around it.
