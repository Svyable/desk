# THE CONTAMINATION PROBLEM

*We went looking for alien life after bringing our own*

Sven Hardy Benson

## A note about evidence

This book is about a problem that becomes harder precisely when the evidence gets exciting.

There are four bins. They do not get to leak into one another.

**Documented.** Mission requirements, measured spacecraft bioburden, archived isolates, experimental survival data, meteorites with established planetary provenance, peer-reviewed transfer models, official sample-return and planetary-protection protocols.

**Credible but incomplete.** Results that establish one leg of a transfer pathway without establishing the whole trip; organisms surviving one simulated stress while other stresses remain untested; contamination histories reconstructed from incomplete mission archives.

**Disputed.** Claims of extraterrestrial organisms in meteorites, stratospheric samples, returned hardware, or other material where terrestrial contamination, morphology, provenance, or independent replication remains unresolved.

**Speculative.** The synthesis developed here: that planetary exploration has become a forensic problem in which Earth life is simultaneously the thing doing the searching, a contaminant capable of confusing the search, and perhaps a natural interplanetary traveler. Panspermia is treated as a hypothesis family, not a fact. No meteorite establishes living transfer merely by having moved between planets.

The governing rule is simple: **survival is not transfer; transfer is not colonization; resemblance is not provenance.**

## The thesis

We usually tell the search-for-life story as a problem of detection. Build a better instrument. Drill deeper. Bring back a cleaner sample.

But the stranger problem is prior to detection. We already know that material naturally moves between planets. We already know that spacecraft carry terrestrial microorganisms despite serious contamination controls. We already know that some organisms selected from spacecraft environments tolerate desiccation, ultraviolet radiation, salt and other stresses unusually well. And we want to recognize a second genesis using chemistry built from the same periodic table and perhaps the same molecular toolkit.

The collision produces an uncomfortable question: **when we eventually find life elsewhere, how will we know whose life it is?**

The book does not answer that question with panspermia. It uses panspermia to make the chain-of-custody problem unavoidable.
