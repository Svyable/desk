# What Secrecy Cannot Hide

Secrecy can hide a great deal.

CORONA hid its actual mission behind Discoverer. GAMBIT and HEXAGON protected astonishing imaging capabilities for decades. Code words, compartmented access, special contracting and need-to-know controls worked well enough that the strategic picture changed while the public knew little of the machinery producing it.

So any simple claim that a major program could not be concealed because too many people would know is historically weak.

The stronger limits come from physics and institutional metabolism.

Secrecy cannot eliminate heat. It cannot make a heavy object weightless for transport. It cannot stop skilled workers from retiring. It cannot prevent a test article from failing. It cannot make a contaminated laboratory clean without procedures. It cannot make a runway shorter by classification. It cannot recover a capsule from 16,400 feet without something capable of diving there.

What secrecy can do is redistribute the visibility of those burdens.

Power can be drawn inside a large facility where the increment is hard to distinguish. Specialists can work under a conventional contract title. Test flights can use restricted ranges already full of classified activity. Waste can enter controlled streams. Documentation can be segmented.

The question therefore becomes one of absorption. How much anomalous burden can an existing institution absorb without producing a distinguishable signature?

This is why military-industrial scale cuts both ways. A vast aerospace sector can hide small exceptional programs more easily because many relevant activities are already normal. Yet a revolutionary technology that requires a new industrial base may stand out more sharply precisely because it cannot borrow enough.

One can imagine three concealment regimes. The first is object concealment: hide a thing. The second is prototype concealment: hide repeated experimentation around a thing. The third is capability concealment: hide a reliable operational system with training, maintenance, deployment and succession.

Each step is harder because the logistics shadow broadens.

This framework does not tell us that extraordinary hidden technology is absent. It tells us that claims should specify which regime they imply.

A single recovered fragment and a fleet of operational vehicles are not neighboring claims. They are different planets of evidence.
