# Naming

The first time a team gives a recurring problem a good name, the room changes.

Before the name, everybody carries a private version of the pattern. One person remembers the customer who hit it last month. Another remembers the deployment that failed for the same reason. A third has a vague sense that this is “that thing that keeps happening.” Each person has evidence. Nobody has a handle.

Then somebody says, “We keep creating orphan decisions.”

The phrase sticks.

Suddenly the team can ask how many orphan decisions exist, where they come from, who owns them, and what would prevent the next one. A fuzzy collection of incidents has become an object of thought.

Naming is compression with a handle attached.

A good name does not merely describe. It permits manipulation.

This is why vocabulary matters so much in fields that solve complicated problems. Once engineers had a shared idea of technical debt, they could discuss a family of short-term choices that create future costs without replaying every example. Once economists could speak about externalities, they had a compact way to point at costs or benefits falling outside the immediate transaction. Once computer scientists could talk about race conditions, a strange class of timing-dependent failures became easier to recognize across different systems.

The name does not create the phenomenon.

It changes the cost of noticing it.

Without a name, each encounter may feel new. With a name, encounters accumulate.

This can become a serious competitive advantage. Organizations differ not only in what they know but in what they can talk about precisely. A team with language for its recurring failure modes can coordinate around them. A team without that language repeatedly rediscovers the same lesson in local anecdotes.

The effect is especially visible in craft.

A novice sees a chair.

A furniture maker sees joinery, grain direction, load paths, finish, tolerances, and the particular ways wood can move over time. A novice hears a song. A musician hears modulation, syncopation, voicing, cadence, and arrangement. A novice sees a paragraph. An editor sees rhythm, point of view, compression, repetition, and where the sentence is spending emphasis.

Expertise changes perception partly by supplying names.

Once you can distinguish two things linguistically, you can more easily compare them mentally.

This is not magic. There are limits to the old idea that language determines thought. People can perceive distinctions they cannot neatly name. But naming lowers the transaction cost of returning to a distinction, teaching it, testing it, and coordinating with other people about it.

Names become cognitive infrastructure.

That makes naming a high-leverage act and a dangerous one.

A bad name can distort as efficiently as a good name can clarify.

Suppose a company labels customers who ask for unusual accommodations “difficult.” The category may compress several genuinely costly cases. It may also collapse together customers with accessibility needs, customers exposing product defects, customers caught in broken internal processes, and customers making unreasonable demands.

The label saves time.

It may save the wrong time.

Once a category exists, new evidence is often interpreted through it. People stop asking what kind of case this is because the label appears to have answered the question already.

Naming therefore has two jobs that pull against each other.

A name must be broad enough to gather repeated instances.

It must be narrow enough not to erase distinctions that affect action.

That balance is hard because the best names feel inevitable after they are adopted. Their success hides the choices embedded inside them.

Consider the word “bug.” It compresses an enormous range of software failures into a familiar category. The term is useful precisely because teams do not need a new ontology for every defect. But the category can conceal causal differences that matter enormously: incorrect requirements, implementation mistakes, unsafe defaults, data-quality failures, infrastructure outages, security vulnerabilities, and misunderstandings of intended behavior can all arrive in the same bug tracker.

A useful top-level name does not eliminate the need for deeper distinctions.

It creates a place from which to make them.

The strongest naming systems are often hierarchical. They let people move between coarse and fine descriptions depending on the decision at hand.

Medicine works this way. So does biology. So does software architecture. So does a well-run kitchen. “Knife” is useful. “Chef’s knife” is more specific. Steel composition matters when buying one and may be irrelevant when asking somebody to hand it to you.

The correct level of naming depends on what the next action requires.

This is where organizations frequently overcomplicate language.

Someone notices that shared vocabulary is powerful and immediately creates a taxonomy project. Committees assemble. Glossaries grow. Every ordinary behavior receives an official term. The organization now possesses names nobody uses naturally and categories nobody remembers without consulting the document.

Naming has crossed from compression into bureaucracy.

A name earns its place when people reach for it because it makes conversation easier.

You cannot force that entirely from above.

Some of the best organizational vocabulary emerges from repeated use. A team invents a phrase because it needs one. The phrase survives because it compresses something real. Eventually it may deserve formal definition.

This suggests a better order: observe, name provisionally, test in use, refine.

Do not begin with the dictionary.

Begin with the recurring confusion.

What do people keep explaining from scratch?

Which failure appears under several different descriptions?

What kind of work becomes easier once everyone can point at the pattern in two or three words?

Those are naming opportunities.

A useful name often carries a small amount of theory.

“Attention debt” suggests that unresolved inputs are not merely clutter but obligations that create future costs. “Orphan decision” suggests a choice detached from an owner or rationale. “Single point of failure” suggests not just that a component is important but that the surrounding system lacks redundancy.

This theoretical content is why a good name can change behavior.

The phrase tells you what to look for.

It can also tell you what to do.

If something is technical debt, you may track repayment. If something is a bottleneck, you investigate flow around a constrained resource. If something is an externality, you ask who is bearing the unpriced consequence.

Names smuggle verbs inside nouns.

That is useful when the implied action is sound.

It is dangerous when the metaphor takes over.

Organizations become trapped by language when a metaphor is treated as literal architecture. People talk about pipelines and begin assuming work should flow linearly. They talk about human capital and begin flattening people into inventory. They talk about war rooms and import a style of command that may be appropriate for a crisis and corrosive as a permanent operating model.

Language does not merely report culture. It can steer it.

AI systems amplify both sides of this.

Models are excellent at generating labels. Give them a corpus and they can propose clusters, taxonomies, themes, personas, failure classes, and canonical phrases in seconds. This can accelerate consolidation dramatically.

But the speed should make us more skeptical, not less.

A generated taxonomy may be internally coherent and externally meaningless. A model can invent categories because categories are statistically plausible, not because the distinctions change anything in the world.

The test is action.

If two items have different names, should we treat them differently?

If several items share a name, what useful inference becomes possible?

If the answer to both is “nothing,” the taxonomy may be decorative.

Machine-assisted naming is most valuable when it proposes candidates and humans test them against consequence.

Ask the system to show examples that do not fit cleanly.

Ask which items would move categories under a different purpose.

Ask what operational decision each label supports.

Ask whether the names reproduce the assumptions already present in the corpus.

Naming should sharpen observation, not finish it.

There is another virtue to a good name: it can make a hidden problem discussable without making it personal.

A team that says “we have a handoff failure” can investigate a recurring interface between roles. A team that says “Alice keeps dropping things” has already compressed the problem around a person.

Sometimes Alice is the problem.

Often the label determines whether anyone looks for the system around her.

The language of failure changes the search space for causes.

This is why careful naming is one of the deepest forms of consolidation. You are deciding which experiences belong together and which differences deserve to survive.

Do it casually and you manufacture confusion at scale.

Do it well and you give a group the ability to think together.

A useful practice is to keep a small lexicon of terms that actually earn repeated use. Not every phrase. Not every clever label. Only the names that save explanation, preserve a consequential distinction, or expose a recurring mechanism.

Review them occasionally.

Some names will have outlived their usefulness. Some will have become too broad. Some will have changed meaning as the organization changed. Some will need to split into more precise categories.

Language accumulates debt too.

The goal is not to possess a perfect vocabulary.

It is to have enough good handles that the important parts of reality do not keep slipping through your fingers.
