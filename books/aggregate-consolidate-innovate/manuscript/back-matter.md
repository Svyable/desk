# Field Card

If the book has done its job, you do not need to remember twenty-five chapters.

You need three verbs and a few questions sharp enough to interrupt habit.

**Aggregate.**

What am I missing?

What is happening at the edge rather than the center?

Which source would disagree with the current story?

What changed?

What is being repeated under another name?

What did we expect to happen that did not happen?

What are we storing without ever retrieving?

Stop aggregating when another input is less valuable than making sense of what you already have.

**Consolidate.**

What is the shortest useful description of this problem?

Which distinctions matter and which are historical debris?

What should be named?

What can become a primitive?

Which recurring negotiation deserves a standard?

What can be merged?

What can be deleted without losing the valuable function underneath?

What provenance must survive the compression?

A summary is not neutral. Decide what must not disappear.

**Innovate.**

Where else does this problem exist?

Which mechanisms can travel even if the nouns cannot?

What constraint would make the hidden structure confess?

What is the smallest prototype that can attack the most dangerous assumption?

What would count as failure before the result exists?

Which candidate deserves more resources, and which should die?

What old door will let people enter the new thing?

What will the world teach us once the thing is real?

Then feed the answer back into the warehouse.

A few operating rules survive almost every domain.

Do not confuse a pile with a corpus.

Do not confuse storage with memory.

Do not confuse a metric with the thing it measures.

Do not confuse centralization with consolidation.

Do not confuse a polished prototype with evidence.

Do not confuse a working project with the best use of the resources it consumes.

Do not confuse compatibility with surrender.

Do not confuse AI fluency with provenance.

Do not let every important signal compete on frequency.

Do not make irreversible commitments when a cheap reversible question is still available.

Do not preserve a complexity merely because nobody remembers why it exists.

Do not keep an experiment alive after it stops changing the model.

When generation becomes cheap, selection becomes expensive.

When retrieval becomes cheap, judgment about relevance becomes expensive.

When compression becomes cheap, deciding what must survive becomes expensive.

When action becomes cheap, authority and consequence become expensive.

Spend attention accordingly.

A useful weekly version of the whole book can fit on one page.

Look at what actually happened.

Write three observations.

Compress them into two principles.

Make one decision.

Kill one zombie.

Choose one cheap experiment that can make reality answer a question you still care about.

Then close the notebook.

The purpose of a system is not to create a larger system for maintaining the system.

The purpose is to make better contact with reality and turn that contact into useful change.

Collect widely.

Compress ruthlessly, but not blindly.

Combine boldly.

Test cheaply where you can.

Commit deeply when the evidence earns it.

Preserve the lesson.

Delete the debris.

Begin again.
