# AI as Aggregator

The warehouse door has been torn off its hinges.

For most of history, aggregation was expensive because attention was attached to a body.

A person had to travel to the archive. Read the report. Copy the passage. Interview the witness. Translate the document. Sort the records. Compare the versions. Remember enough of yesterday to recognize the contradiction in today.

Institutions solved this partly by hiring more bodies.

Libraries, intelligence agencies, research departments, newsrooms, bureaucracies, laboratories, consultancies, and universities are all, among other things, machines for putting more human attention onto a larger world.

Now a new kind of machine can ingest, retrieve, translate, classify, compare, summarize, and generate across quantities of material that would exhaust a human team before lunch.

This does not mean the machine knows everything.

It means the old scarcity has moved.

That move changes the economics of curiosity.

A question that once required a week of preliminary research can now produce a first map in minutes. A founder can ask for examples of a pricing mechanism across industries. An engineer can compare several technical approaches. A lawyer can inspect a pile of contracts for recurring clauses. A scientist can traverse adjacent literatures. A writer can search for analogies far outside the original domain.

The first gift is breadth.

The second is speed.

The third is that breadth and speed together alter what questions are worth asking.

When retrieval is expensive, people ration curiosity.

You hesitate before asking, “Where else has this problem appeared?” because the answer may require several days and a library card.

When retrieval becomes cheap, the question itself can become routine.

That is a profound change.

The frontier of thought expands partly because more weak hypotheses become affordable to inspect.

This is aggregation as option creation.

But abundance arrives with a trick.

The machine can return ten relevant things.

It can also return a hundred things that look relevant.

It can produce a map whose confidence exceeds its provenance.

It can collapse disagreements into a smooth paragraph.

It can retrieve the wrong document elegantly.

It can generate plausible connective tissue between facts that never actually touched.

The warehouse is larger.

So are the opportunities to get lost in it.

This is why AI does not eliminate the collector’s fallacy.

It industrializes it.

The old researcher could procrastinate by reading one more paper.

The AI-assisted researcher can ask for fifty more papers, twelve more frameworks, nine more edge cases, three competing syntheses, and an annotated comparison table before the coffee cools.

Research can now become infinite at interactive speed.

The stopping rule matters more, not less.

What decision could the next input change?

What uncertainty remains dangerous?

What evidence would reverse the current model?

Without these questions, AI becomes a slot machine for context.

Pull the lever.

More information falls out.

The experience feels productive because the screen changes.

This is attention debt with excellent typography.

The strongest use of AI as an aggregator therefore begins before the query.

Define the hunt.

What are we looking for?

A contradiction?

A precedent?

A boundary condition?

A mechanism from another field?

A failure mode?

A source that disagrees with the current consensus?

A decision made under similar constraints?

A good retrieval system should widen the field without dissolving the question.

This sounds like a minor distinction.

It is the difference between research and grazing.

AI also changes the value of provenance.

When a human researcher manually gathers ten sources, they often retain some memory of where each came from. The friction of collection creates accidental context.

Automated retrieval strips much of that friction away.

The result arrives separated from the journey.

This is useful.

It is also why source identity has to be deliberately restored.

Where did this claim come from?

Was it primary or derivative?

When was it true?

Was the document current?

Did three summaries ultimately trace back to one original source?

Did the retrieved example actually support the conclusion, or merely contain similar vocabulary?

Aggregation without provenance creates persuasive ghosts.

The more fluent the system, the harder the ghosts can be to see.

This is not a reason to retreat to manual research as a moral virtue.

It is a reason to redesign the research stack.

Machines are excellent at surfacing candidates.

They are excellent at scanning for patterns.

They are excellent at translating formats, extracting repeated structures, comparing large corpora, and remembering that a detail occurred in a document nobody has opened in nine months.

Humans remain responsible for deciding what deserves epistemic weight.

That responsibility can also be partly systematized.

Separate discovery from verification.

Label source type.

Preserve dates.

Track confidence.

Ask the system to surface contradictions rather than smoothing them.

Store links between conclusions and the material that produced them.

Make unsupported synthesis visibly different from retrieved fact.

These are not bureaucratic niceties.

They are the new shelving system.

The need becomes especially acute inside organizations because corporate information is messy in ways public information is not.

Policies exist in old versions.

Teams use local acronyms.

A slide deck says one thing and the contract says another.

A customer name changed twice.

A project codename became a product name.

A decision was announced in chat but never updated in the documentation.

The company does not possess one corpus.

It possesses sediment.

AI can make sediment searchable.

This is extraordinary.

It can also surface sediment as if it were current law.

An employee asks, “What is our refund policy?”

The system finds four policies.

One is current.

One applied to a discontinued product.

One was a proposal.

One is a support-team cheat sheet written during a crisis.

The model can summarize all four beautifully.

The institution needs more than search.

It needs status.

This is where the previous chapters return.

Naming.

Standards.

Provenance.

Authority.

Editing.

AI is not a substitute for these.

It makes their absence impossible to ignore.

A messy organization connected to a powerful retrieval system does not become coherent.

It becomes queryable chaos.

There is still enormous value in that transition because queryable chaos is easier to diagnose than invisible chaos.

Ask the system where policies disagree.

Ask which definitions of “active customer” appear across teams.

Ask which projects depend on the same vendor.

Ask which incidents mention a particular failure mode.

Ask where a decision is referenced without an identifiable source.

Ask which documents appear authoritative but have not been updated in years.

Now aggregation becomes institutional archaeology.

The machine is not merely retrieving answers.

It is helping reveal the shape of the organization’s memory.

This is one of the most interesting uses of AI because it shifts the question from “What can the model tell us?” to “What can the model reveal about the structure of what we have told ourselves?”

That second question is more powerful.

It turns the machine into an instrument for finding contradictions, duplication, stale assumptions, and hidden dependency.

In other words, the aggregator can help prepare consolidation.

There is also a personal version.

For years, people have built private archives: notes, saved links, email, messages, documents, photographs, bookmarks, highlights, voice memos, code, calendars.

Most of this material has been write-heavy and read-light.

We store because storage is cheap.

Retrieval has been expensive enough that the archive becomes largely ceremonial.

AI changes that.

A person can ask their archive a question.

What themes have I repeatedly returned to?

Which predictions did I make last year?

Where have I changed my mind?

What projects did I abandon for reasons that may no longer apply?

Which people have I promised to follow up with?

What ideas from one project resemble a current problem?

The archive begins acting less like a basement and more like a conversational memory.

This can be genuinely transformative.

It can also turn personal history into a hall of mirrors if the system lacks temporal awareness.

A note captures what you believed then.

The model may present it as what you believe now.

A draft captures a possibility.

The model may promote it to intention.

A complaint captures a bad afternoon.

The model may infer a worldview.

Context is not optional merely because retrieval is semantic.

Memory should remember that memory changes.

This leads to a deeper insight about aggregation in the AI era.

The important unit is no longer the document.

It is the relationship among document, source, time, status, user, question, and action.

A fact can be useful in one context and dangerous in another.

An old strategy can be valuable history and terrible instruction.

A customer complaint can be weak evidence about the market and strong evidence about a specific workflow.

An AI aggregator needs more than a big bucket.

It needs a theory of relevance.

Relevance is not fixed.

The same archive is different when the question changes.

This is where retrieval becomes an active form of compression.

The system selects a small subset of a much larger world and places it into working context.

That selection shapes thought.

Which sources were retrieved?

Which were not?

Which examples became salient?

Which disagreement was omitted?

Every context window is an editorial act.

This is true whether a human assembles it or a machine does.

AI simply makes the act faster and easier to overlook.

The responsible user therefore learns to interrogate the retrieval itself.

What might be missing?

What source would disagree?

What edge case does this summary suppress?

What changed recently?

Which result is repeated because it is popular rather than because it is true?

Where did the machine search, and where could it not search?

These are collector’s questions for a machine-scale warehouse.

The promise is enormous.

A person can stand at the mouth of a corpus larger than any private library in history and ask for a path through it.

An organization can interrogate its own accumulated experience.

A scientist can traverse disciplinary boundaries.

A builder can find mechanisms hiding under unfamiliar nouns.

The old bottleneck of access is weakening.

But weakening one bottleneck exposes the next.

The world has become easier to gather.

Now we have to decide what it means.
