# Maintenance Cannot Sprint

A new system enters the world clean.

That condition does not last.

Users discover edge cases. Parts wear. Dependencies change. Data accumulates. People leave. Vendors revise contracts. Attackers learn. Documentation falls behind. An operating environment that looked simple during design becomes full of exceptions because real life is unusually talented at producing them.

The system becomes itself through maintenance.

This is easy to forget because maintenance does not have the drama of invention. A new bridge is architecture. The inspection program is administration. A new software product is innovation. Dependency upgrades are housekeeping. A new hospital wing has donors' names on it. The work of keeping sterilization, ventilation, power, elevators, and medical equipment dependable happens in the background.

The background is where the future lives.

Maintenance has a different clock from creation.

Creation can happen in a burst. A team works nights, resolves a thousand decisions, and produces a launch. Maintenance begins after the burst and continues for as long as anyone expects the thing to remain useful.

This is why maintenance cannot sprint.

A sprint can reduce a backlog. It cannot replace a durable maintenance system.

The same is true for recovery. An organization can mobilize extraordinary effort after a failure. People work around the clock. Vendors join emergency calls. Executives remove ordinary approvals. The system returns.

Then attention moves on.

If recovery depends on heroics every time, the organization has not built resilience. It has rented it from exhausted people.

Software engineering has unusually rich language for this because software changes continuously. Meir Lehman's work on software evolution observed that systems used in a real environment must continue changing or become progressively less satisfactory. The exact “laws” he proposed have been debated and refined, but the practical experience is familiar to anyone who has maintained a long-lived system.

Software does not remain finished.

The world around it moves.

Browsers change. Operating systems change. security assumptions change. APIs disappear. Users behave differently. Scale increases. regulations change. libraries age. Hardware changes. The organization itself changes.

A system can become obsolete without anyone modifying the system.

That is maintenance as adaptation.

Physical infrastructure has the same property at a slower cadence. A road designed for one traffic pattern encounters another. Climate conditions alter the distribution of heat, rain, flood, or fire that an asset experiences. A school built for one population serves another. A factory changes products. A hospital treats different patients with different technologies.

Maintenance is not merely preserving a static object.

It is keeping a relationship viable while both sides change.

This makes maintenance central to the pace of change argument. If creation accelerates while maintenance capacity remains fixed, the installed base grows faster than the institution's ability to care for it.

The system becomes wealthier in assets and poorer in attention per asset.

The problem can remain invisible for a long time.

A new application adds a few dependencies. A new product adds a few support cases. A new policy adds a few reporting requirements. A new building adds one more roof, elevator, and mechanical system. Each addition is manageable.

The cumulative system is not merely the sum of the additions.

Interactions grow. Knowledge fragments. Standards overlap. Old versions remain in use. Edge cases migrate across boundaries. The cost of understanding rises.

Organizations respond by adding tools to manage the complexity.

The tools themselves require maintenance.

This is how maintenance systems acquire their own installed base.

A company buys a ticketing system to manage service work. It adds monitoring to detect failures. It adds an asset database to know what exists. It adds identity management to control access. It adds dashboards to summarize dashboards. Each layer may be rational. Together they create another system whose reliability depends on people understanding the relationships among them.

Modern organizations can therefore experience maintenance inflation even when individual technologies become easier to use.

The unit cost of a feature falls.

The number of features rises faster.

This is a version of Jevons-like rebound that does not need to be formalized to be visible. When something becomes cheaper to create, people create more of it. If the cost of maintaining each object does not fall as fast as creation cost, the maintenance burden can still grow.

Generative software may intensify this.

If code becomes cheaper to produce, organizations may produce more applications, automations, scripts, integrations, agents, and internal tools. Some will be valuable. Some will be temporary. Some will quietly become critical because the temporary thing solved a real problem and nobody replaced it.

The future maintenance burden is created at the moment of successful convenience.

That is the paradox.

Bad prototypes die.

Useful prototypes become infrastructure.

The organization then discovers that the person who built the tool left, the model underneath it changed, the API now costs more, the prompt contains assumptions nobody documented, and a workflow that began as a shortcut now moves financially or legally important information.

Creation speed can turn experiments into dependencies before governance notices the promotion.

This is why fast systems need a retirement process as much as a launch process.

Every new thing should eventually face a question: is this still an experiment, a maintained product, or a thing that should disappear?

Organizations are much better at starting than stopping.

Starting has a sponsor.

Stopping has users.

A tool survives because someone depends on it. A report survives because one executive once asked for it. A meeting survives because nobody knows whether removing it will break coordination. A policy survives because the event that created it has been forgotten but removing it requires someone to accept responsibility.

The installed base grows through temporal cowardice.

No single addition looks expensive enough to fight.

The bill appears as a permanent reduction in organizational flexibility.

A company with hundreds of half-owned systems cannot change quickly because every change encounters unknown dependencies. It may respond by creating a transformation program intended to simplify the environment.

The transformation adds another layer before removing the old one.

This is how attempts to accelerate change can temporarily increase the pace gap.

Migration is maintenance at its most difficult because two states coexist. The old system must keep working while the new one is built. Data must be reconciled. Users need support in both worlds. Exceptions surface. The organization is paying the cost of history and the cost of the future simultaneously.

The rational temptation is to shorten the overlap.

Sometimes that is exactly right. Running duplicate systems too long creates confusion and expense. A decisive cutover can eliminate ambiguity.

But shortening overlap without understanding recovery can turn migration into a bet that nothing important was missed.

The correct cutover pace depends on observability, reversibility, dependency knowledge, and the cost of outage.

Again, not one speed.

Maintenance is also where organizational memory becomes technical reality.

A strange-looking check may exist because of an incident ten years ago. A backup path may look redundant because it has not been needed recently. A configuration may contain an exception for a customer contract whose authors are gone. A physical component may have an inspection interval based on failure history that newer staff never personally experienced.

When maintainers disappear, context disappears with them.

Documentation can preserve some of it.

Documentation also has a clock.

If a system changes faster than documentation is updated, written knowledge becomes less trustworthy. Once people stop trusting documentation, they stop consulting it. Once they stop consulting it, the incentive to maintain it falls further. The organization shifts toward oral transmission and individual memory.

Then turnover becomes a technical event.

A person leaves and the system loses capability.

This is why rapid organizational churn can damage maintenance even when hiring keeps headcount stable. Ten people arriving do not replace ten people leaving if the people leaving carried years of causal history about why the system behaves as it does.

Headcount is a stock.

Institutional memory is an accumulation.

The clocks differ.

High-reliability organizations take this seriously because rare failures can occur on intervals longer than employee tenure. They preserve procedures, training, incident reviews, and operational attention precisely because recent experience is not enough. They assume that apparent normality can contain developing risk.

That stance is difficult to sustain in fast-growth environments.

Growth rewards capacity added. Maintenance consumes capacity without always adding visible output. The organization hires builders because there is demand for more. Maintainers are asked to automate themselves.

Automation helps enormously.

Predictive maintenance can reveal failure before it becomes catastrophic. Infrastructure-as-code can make configurations reproducible. Automated tests can detect regression. Monitoring can reduce mean time to detection. Remote diagnostics can avoid physical travel. Robotics can inspect environments dangerous to people.

Maintenance itself can become faster.

But automation does not eliminate the maintenance clock. It changes what needs maintenance.

The predictive model needs data and validation. The monitoring system needs thresholds. The automated test suite needs revision as the product changes. The robot needs repair. The remote management channel becomes a security dependency.

Every reduction in one maintenance burden can create another layer that needs care.

This is not an argument against automation.

It is an argument against the fantasy of maintenance-free complexity.

A living system always pays somebody to remember reality.

The payment may be money, staff, redundancy, spare parts, training, downtime, inspection, documentation, or design simplicity.

If an organization refuses all of these costs, reality collects through failure.

The timing of that collection is uncertain, which makes neglect politically attractive.

Preventive maintenance competes against visible needs now. A machine is working. Why stop it? A road is passable. Why close a lane? A security control has not failed. Why fund another test? A backup has never been used. Why replace it?

The answer is rarely that failure is certain tomorrow.

The answer is that the system's recovery time and consequence make waiting for visible failure too expensive.

Maintenance is a decision made before the proof is available.

That makes it a trust exercise in engineering judgment.

Poor maintenance organizations lose that trust through overmaintenance as well as neglect. If every risk is described as catastrophic, leaders learn to discount warnings. If maintenance schedules ignore actual condition and technological improvement, the program becomes ritual. If every old component is protected because replacement carries risk, the installed base becomes unchangeable.

Good maintenance is not maximal caution.

It is informed attention to degradation, criticality, and recovery.

This returns us to pace.

How often does the asset meaningfully change?

How quickly can degradation be detected?

How long does repair take?

How much spare capacity exists during repair?

How often does the surrounding environment change the asset's operating conditions?

How much undocumented knowledge is required to recover?

A fast change cadence is sustainable only when these answers support it.

The CrowdStrike incident is instructive because the company responded by changing not only the specific defective mechanism but aspects of validation, testing, and staged deployment. The lesson was not simply “be slower.” A cybersecurity product still needs rapid response. The safer path is to make the rapid path more observable, more bounded, and more recoverable.

Maintenance design is what allows urgency without recklessness.

The same principle could transform how organizations think about transformation.

Before adding another system, ask who will own it in year three.

Before automating a workflow, ask how failure will be diagnosed after the creator leaves.

Before expanding an infrastructure asset, ask what replacement cycle the expansion creates.

Before creating a new policy, ask what administrative capacity will keep it current.

Before speeding releases, ask whether support, documentation, security, and recovery are speeding with them.

These questions sound less exciting than strategy.

They are strategy after the launch party.

Maintenance also offers a different definition of innovation.

Sometimes the innovation is not a new feature.

It is a system that can be repaired without shutting down.

A component that can be replaced without rebuilding the whole.

A standard connector instead of a custom one.

A clear diagnostic instead of an opaque failure.

A design that lets a technician reach the part that will eventually break.

A data format that can survive the vendor.

A policy with a review mechanism instead of an assumption of permanence.

These choices can reduce the apparent sophistication of the new thing while increasing the pace at which the system can safely evolve later.

Maintainability is stored future speed.

That may be the most important temporal inversion in this book.

The organization that appears to move slightly slower during creation can become much faster over the life of the system because it is not repeatedly paying for hidden coupling and forgotten context.

The organization that sprints through creation may spend years walking through repair.

Maintenance cannot sprint.

But good maintenance can keep the rest of the system from having to stop.
