# The Permit and the Product

A product can change before the meeting about the product begins.

This is the regulator's temporal problem.

A company submits information about a system. Staff review it. Experts ask questions. Lawyers interpret authority. Public comments arrive. A proposed rule is drafted. The rule moves through procedure. Months later, perhaps years later, the final text describes a technology that has acquired new features, new business models, new users, and new risks since the process began.

The obvious conclusion is that regulation is too slow.

Sometimes it is.

The less obvious conclusion is that writing faster rules is not enough.

A faster rule can still be obsolete on arrival if it is written at the wrong layer.

David Collingridge described a dilemma that has become more important as technical systems become easier to deploy. Early in a technology's life, society has more freedom to shape it but less information about its consequences. Later, the consequences are easier to observe but the technology may be entrenched in infrastructure, organizations, markets, and daily behavior, making intervention more expensive.

Knowledge arrives after leverage begins to disappear.

That is a pace problem.

It does not yield a simple answer because the two clocks contain real value. Early action preserves optionality. Later action has better evidence.

Regulation sits between them.

The traditional caricatures are unhelpful.

One caricature sees the regulator as an old institution standing in front of a young technology with a clipboard, slowing down people who understand the future.

Another sees the innovator as a reckless company externalizing risk while demanding permission to scale before anyone can understand the consequences.

Both characters exist.

Neither is a theory of governance.

The more useful question is how to create an institutional loop that learns on a cadence appropriate to the technology and the harm.

Some domains already do this better than others because they were forced to confront temporal mismatch early.

Financial regulators distinguish among statutes, rules, guidance, supervision, reporting, stress tests, examinations, and enforcement. The system is imperfect and often slow, but not every response requires Congress to write a new law. Aviation combines long-lived legal authority with continuously updated technical standards, incident reporting, operating rules, manufacturer obligations, inspections, and investigation. Drug regulation separates discovery, trials, review, manufacturing controls, labeling, post-market surveillance, and adverse-event reporting because not all evidence exists before approval.

These systems are not templates to copy blindly.

They demonstrate a principle: governance can have layers with different clocks.

The law can be slow while sensing is fast.

A standard can change faster than a statute.

A reporting requirement can reveal new risk before a new prohibition is written.

A provisional approval can permit use while preserving the ability to revise conditions.

A post-market obligation can continue learning after launch.

The permit and the product do not need to move at the same speed if the interface between them is designed for change.

This distinction matters because many regulatory failures begin with an attempt to freeze implementation detail into durable law.

A legislature encounters a new technology and writes a definition based on the current architecture. Companies change the architecture. A threshold is set based on a current scale. Costs fall. The threshold catches too much or too little. A rule names a particular technical method. A new method arrives that produces the same consequence through a different mechanism.

The law remains stable.

The target moves around it.

This is one reason outcome-based regulation is attractive. Instead of specifying the exact implementation, the rule specifies a property the system must preserve: safety, capital adequacy, privacy, non-discrimination, auditability, interoperability, disclosure, or another public objective.

The attraction is real.

So is the difficulty.

Outcomes can be hard to measure. A vague outcome standard can become discretionary enforcement. Firms may not know what compliance requires until after a dispute. Regulators may lack technical capacity to distinguish real compliance from clever presentation.

Stable principles need operational interfaces.

The answer is often a layered regime: durable statutory goals, more adaptable standards, reporting that keeps the regulator informed, and procedural rights for the people affected.

This is temporal architecture in public law.

It is also a way to reduce the false choice between speed and legitimacy.

Sheila Jasanoff's idea of “technologies of humility” is useful because it rejects the fantasy that governance can predict every consequence before action. Institutions can instead organize themselves around what is unknown, whose experience counts, how vulnerability is distributed, and how learning can continue.

Humility is not passivity.

It is a design response to incomplete knowledge.

A regulator facing a fast-changing technology has at least five temporal tools even before deciding what the substantive policy should be.

It can improve sensing.

It can stage permission.

It can preserve reversibility.

It can separate stable obligations from changing technical standards.

It can create faster routes for appeal and correction when automated or scaled systems make many decisions quickly.

These are not substitutes for democratic judgment. They change the interval in which democratic judgment operates.

Sensing is the least glamorous and often the most valuable.

A regulator cannot govern what it cannot see until a newspaper story or catastrophe makes the problem legible. Reporting requirements, incident disclosure, audit access, technical expertise, market monitoring, and relationships with researchers can shorten the detection clock.

This allows a slow institution to become temporally competent without pretending it can legislate every week.

A lighthouse does not move as fast as a ship.

It does not need to.

It needs to see the ship in time.

Staged permission addresses a different problem. A new system may be promising enough that requiring complete certainty before use would destroy beneficial experimentation. Full unrestricted deployment may still create more exposure than the evidence justifies.

A staged regime creates intermediate states.

A limited population. A defined environment. A narrower set of use cases. Additional supervision. A cap that increases with evidence. A license that changes as operational history accumulates.

The design is common in engineering and medicine because risk cannot always be understood in a laboratory alone.

The political danger is that “pilot” becomes a rhetorical loophole. A company labels mass deployment an experiment after the scale has already made exit difficult. An agency runs a pilot whose affected population bears real consequences without meaningful recourse. A temporary measure becomes permanent through inertia.

Staging only creates information if the stage is actually bounded.

The boundary has to mean something.

Reversibility is closely related.

Dixit and Pindyck's work on investment under uncertainty formalizes a powerful idea: waiting can have value when a decision is irreversible and future information may change the choice. The intuition extends beyond investment, though the mathematics should not be dragged casually into every social problem.

If a decision is cheap to reverse, learning by doing can be attractive.

If a decision is expensive or impossible to reverse, waiting for information can be rational.

Regulation should therefore care about the reversibility of both innovation and intervention.

A rule that can be revised administratively may justifiably move faster than a constitutional change. A product feature that can be disabled with little harm may deserve more experimental freedom than a system that creates permanent records, physical exposure, or irreversible biological effects.

This is why “permissionless innovation” is too broad to be a useful temporal principle.

Some experiments occur inside the experimenter's domain.

Others rewrite the conditions for people who did not choose to participate.

A programmer trying a new editor needs little public oversight. A company changing the ranking of entertainment recommendations creates a different exposure. A company deploying a system that determines access to employment, credit, housing, medical care, or public benefits operates on a different clock because the decision can create consequences that the affected person cannot reverse quickly.

The relevant unit is not novelty.

It is consequence plus reversibility.

Appeal matters for the same reason.

If the first decision becomes cheaper and faster through automation, the volume of consequential decisions can increase. A system can deny, rank, flag, suspend, price, or route at machine speed. The institution handling challenges may still operate through forms, queues, office hours, and manual review.

The decision clock accelerates.

The correction clock does not.

A person can be wronged in a second and wait months for the institution to discover it.

This is one of the clearest pace gaps in digital governance.

The right response is not necessarily to slow every first decision to human speed. That would discard useful automation. It is to make the correction system proportional to the decision system.

Fast power needs fast recourse.

This principle applies inside companies as much as governments. An automated fraud system can block a legitimate customer immediately. If the appeal goes into a generic queue, the company has designed fast enforcement and slow justice. A content system can remove material automatically while review takes days. A workplace system can flag performance or compliance issues faster than an employee can correct underlying data.

The imbalance becomes a source of institutional distrust.

Products experience a similar problem with permits in the literal sense.

A factory developer can make a financing decision in weeks and spend years resolving land, environmental, utility, and construction approvals. An advocate for faster permitting can point to real duplication and delay. An advocate for review can point to real externalities and irreversible changes.

The useful question is again what information the time produces.

Does the process surface environmental risk that would otherwise be missed?

Does it coordinate infrastructure?

Does it give affected communities a meaningful voice?

Does it resolve conflicting property claims?

Or does it ask for the same document five times, create serial review where parallel review would work, and allow agencies to leave applications idle because no one owns the clock?

Process can be both legitimate and badly timed.

A serious reform agenda should preserve the information-producing parts while compressing dead time.

Dead time is different from deliberation.

A permit waiting three months in an inbox is not becoming wiser.

This distinction has enormous practical value because debates about institutional speed often become moralized. One side defends process as though every day of delay protects democracy. The other attacks process as though every day is sabotage.

Time should have to earn its place.

That is a demanding standard for both innovation and regulation.

A product team asking to move quickly should explain why the change is observable, bounded, and reversible.

A regulator asking for more time should explain what evidence, participation, or protection the time produces.

The same discipline could improve many slow institutions.

Courts could distinguish adjudicative time from administrative waiting. Universities could distinguish faculty deliberation from calendar inertia. corporate compliance could distinguish thoughtful review from sequential handoffs whose only function is tradition.

Slowness becomes more defensible when its output is visible.

Speed becomes more defensible when its recovery path is visible.

This is temporal legitimacy.

It matters increasingly because technological systems are not merely faster; they can change continuously after deployment.

The old regulatory imagination often assumed a product had a meaningful pre-market state and a meaningful post-market state. Software weakens that boundary. A system can receive new code, models, data, policies, interfaces, and capabilities after approval or adoption. The thing governed on Monday may not be exactly the thing operating on Friday.

Static permission has trouble with dynamic objects.

The answer is not continuous repermission of every change. That would make the regulator part of the deployment pipeline and likely fail.

The more promising approach is to govern the change process itself.

What classes of changes require notice?

What testing must occur before broader exposure?

What logs and records must be preserved?

Which changes can be rolled back?

What incidents trigger reporting?

When does a modification cross a threshold that deserves renewed review?

This is already familiar in regulated domains. The broader lesson is that governing a fast system often means governing its update mechanism rather than freezing its current state.

The institution regulates the clock.

This can also reduce the regulator's dependence on predicting technical direction. Instead of betting on which architecture will dominate, it can require the organization to demonstrate how it manages change, evidence, risk, correction, and accountability.

That does not eliminate substantive rules. Some outcomes should simply be prohibited. Some rights should not depend on a company's experimentation protocol.

The point is to match the legal instrument to the temporal structure of the problem.

A durable right belongs on a durable clock.

A technical implementation detail may belong on a faster one.

An incident report belongs on a very fast one.

A precedent with wide consequences may deserve a slow one.

Regulation becomes more competent when it stops treating time as a single queue.

The same is true for the innovator.

A company that wants freedom to change should invest in the properties that make freedom safer: observability, auditability, containment, interoperability, portability, reversibility, and recourse.

Those properties are not merely compliance costs.

They are the architecture of permission.

The faster the product moves, the more valuable they become.

The permit and the product will never share one clock.

They should not.

The goal is a relationship in which the product can learn before the law freezes it, the law can see before the product entrenches itself, and people affected by both have a path to correction that does not arrive after the consequence has become permanent.
