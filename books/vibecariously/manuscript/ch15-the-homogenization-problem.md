# The Homogenization Problem

The most important question about creative AI may not be whether it makes an individual more creative.

It may be what happens when everyone becomes more creative in the same direction.

This is difficult to see from inside a single session. A person begins with an idea, asks for help, receives alternatives, and ends with something they prefer. The process feels expansive because their personal option set grew. They saw things they would not have produced alone. If an evaluator likes the result more, the evidence seems straightforward: the tool improved creativity.

Now zoom out.

Thousands or millions of people are using related models trained on overlapping cultural material. They ask similar systems for headlines, characters, logos, photographs, product names, architectural concepts, songs, essays, and jokes. Each person gets customized output. The outputs are not copies. Yet customization can coexist with convergence.

Several early studies have found versions of this pattern.

In a 2024 *Science Advances* paper, researchers Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser studied short-story writing with access to generative AI ideas. Outside evaluators rated AI-assisted stories as more creative and better written on average, with the largest gains going to writers who began with lower baseline creativity. This is the individual story that attracts attention: the system helped people make better work.

The researchers also compared the stories with one another. AI-assisted stories were more similar.

Individual improvement came with a collective cost.

A 2024 CHI study on design fixation reached a related result through a different task. Participants used generative AI images while solving divergent design problems. The generated examples could help produce visually developed ideas, but exposure also increased fixation and reduced several measures of exploratory diversity compared with a baseline condition. Seeing a concrete suggestion made certain features harder to escape.

Later research has extended the concern into larger corpora. A study comparing thousands of college-admissions essays written by humans and by ChatGPT found that human essays introduced new ideas at a higher rate across the corpus, while AI-written essays became collectively more redundant as the sample grew. The individual essay could be coherent and distinctive enough for a reader. The population told a different story.

This is the homogenization problem.

It does not mean models always produce the same thing. Modern generative systems have enormous output spaces. Change the prompt, seed, context, reference images, fine-tuning, sampling settings, or model and the result changes. At a pixel level, variation is practically limitless.

Homogenization occurs at a higher level.

Which compositions recur? Which metaphors? Which narrative arcs? Which kinds of faces? Which visual symbols stand for intelligence, wealth, sustainability, romance, community, rebellion, childhood, futurity? Which sentence rhythms feel “clear”? Which app interfaces look “modern”? Which color palettes look “premium”? Which jokes sound like internet jokes?

A culture can be infinitely varied in surface detail and narrow in grammar.

This is not a new digital problem. Recommendation systems already showed how personalization can create convergence. Millions of listeners receive individually tailored music suggestions, yet promotion can still concentrate attention around certain tracks. Fashion can offer enormous product variety while silhouettes converge. Social media lets everyone post anything while platform incentives produce recognizable formats: the same hooks, cuts, captions, poses, thumbnails, and emotional rhythms.

Generative AI changes the mechanism because it sits inside production, not only distribution.

A recommendation system decides which existing things you see. A generative system can influence the thing you make before anyone else sees it.

This creates a feedback loop. Models learn from culture. People create with models. Model-assisted culture becomes part of the future training environment. Even without dramatic “model collapse,” the ecology can become self-referential. The statistical center of past output helps shape new output, which strengthens the visibility of the center.

Researchers have begun testing this dynamic directly. Experiments in recursive language-image generation, where one model captions an image and another generates a new image from the caption over repeated cycles, can converge toward a limited set of generic motifs. The exact behavior depends on models and setup, but the metaphor is powerful: information can drift toward stable attractors. A complex image passes through several rounds of synthetic interpretation and becomes something more conventional.

Culture has attractors too.

Genres are attractors. Markets reward familiar forms. Institutions teach templates. Algorithms select for legibility. Human audiences like some amount of predictability because entirely unfamiliar work is hard to parse. The homogenization problem is therefore not an alien force entering a diverse paradise. Culture has always balanced repetition and mutation.

The concern is that generative systems may make the repeating side unusually efficient.

Imagine a company asking a model for brand names. The system has learned what startup names look like from existing language and market examples. It can generate hundreds. Most will be unusable because of trademarks, domains, meaning, or taste. The shortlist will likely contain names that feel name-like. That is useful.

Now imagine tens of thousands of companies using similar systems. The average naming process becomes less dependent on the eccentric references of a founder or agency and more dependent on a broad statistical sense of what names work. The system may reduce very bad names while also producing an eerie competence band around the middle.

The same applies to writing. A language model can remove awkwardness, clarify structure, and improve grammar. If everyone uses the same model to make prose “more professional,” professional prose may converge toward the model's preferred rhythms. The system does not need to insert identical phrases. It can normalize cadence: balanced clauses, explicit transitions, moderate sentence length, careful caveats, smooth endings.

Good writing can become too consistently good in the same way.

The resistance must come from somewhere.

One source is personal data—not private data in the surveillance sense, but personal material in the creative sense. Specific memories, local observations, unusual references, proprietary research, physical experiments, field notes, conversations, drawings, mistakes, dialects, constraints, and experiences that the broad model cannot predict easily. The stranger and more situated the input, the harder it is for the output to collapse entirely into average culture.

This is why “write from your life” may become practical anti-homogenization advice rather than literary sentimentality.

The model has read broadly. It has not stood in your uncle's garage in February or watched the particular way commuters run when the 6:12 doors begin to close. It can generate garages and commuters. The difference between the generic scene and the observed one lives in detail that was earned by presence.

Presence creates data with a sample size of one.

Another source of resistance is specialized expertise. Domain knowledge creates constraints the model's generic solution may miss. A furniture maker knows which joint makes the form possible. A nurse notices a workflow detail in a hospital concept. A local historian sees that the “period” façade belongs three regions away. A musician hears a harmonic cliché the casual listener accepts. Expertise pushes generation through a narrower, more consequential filter.

A third source is deliberate diversity of tools and collaborators.

If every member of a team asks the same model first, the team may become less diverse than its human composition. Different people carry different priors. Independent ideation preserves them. Different models carry different priors too. Hand sketches, physical prototypes, interviews, walks, archives, old media, specialized software, and unassisted drafts keep the creative search from flowing entirely through one statistical bottleneck.

This suggests a simple organizational practice: separate before combining.

Before using generative AI in a group, let people produce first ideas independently. Then use the model to challenge, expand, or recombine. The sequence protects human variance long enough to become visible.

The same principle can apply to individuals. Write down your first three associations before prompting. Make an ugly sketch. Take a photograph. Find one physical reference. Ask the model for directions deliberately unlike your own. The goal is not to prove purity. It is to seed the process with material that did not come from the same prior.

The homogenization problem also complicates the idea of democratization.

Suppose AI helps a less experienced writer produce a story judged more creative than their unaided story. That is a real benefit. It can expand participation, confidence, and opportunity. It would be perverse to reject those gains because elite creators fear sameness.

The collective cost is not an argument for restoring old barriers.

It is an argument for designing systems and practices that give more people access without turning access into normalization.

This may require product changes. Generative interfaces tend to optimize for satisfaction: the user asks, the system gives something likely to be accepted. A diversity-oriented system might sometimes optimize for distance. It could show how similar a concept is to common patterns, deliberately sample from less likely regions, surface underrepresented references, or offer “nearest cliché” and “furthest plausible alternative” side by side.

Recommendation systems already expose diversity controls poorly, if at all. Generative systems could do better because the user is explicitly in a creative mode.

Imagine asking not “give me five ideas” but “give me five ideas that share no major metaphor, structure, or reference class.” The model can attempt this. It may not always succeed, but the instruction makes diversity an object of the process rather than an accidental property.

The harder problem is that users often prefer convergence.

Clichés are clichés because they work. A website that looks like other websites is easier to use. A romantic comedy that resembles the genre meets expectations. A résumé that follows convention reduces risk. A political message that uses familiar symbols is legible. A brand may say it wants originality while punishing anything that feels unfamiliar.

Generative AI can therefore become the perfect corporate compromise: enough variation to avoid literal copying, enough familiarity to avoid frightening anyone.

This may be economically rational.

Most cultural production is not trying to become timeless art. It is trying to sell a product, explain a policy, entertain for an evening, fill a schedule, communicate a feature, decorate a room, produce a training video, or make a child laugh. Generic competence is valuable.

The danger is environmental. An individual rational choice can create a collectively boring world.

If every restaurant uses the same tools to make the most broadly appealing interior, if every city uses the same visualization grammar, if every company adopts the same AI-polished tone, if every song is optimized toward familiar structure, nobody has made a catastrophic decision. The streets simply begin to rhyme.

Culture loses variance one good decision at a time.

This is why diversity is not merely a moral or representational issue. It is an adaptive resource.

Biological systems need variation because environments change. Markets benefit from competing strategies. Scientific fields progress when assumptions are challenged. Culture needs weirdness because nobody knows which weirdness will become the next language everyone else learns.

A system optimized around past preference has difficulty valuing what current preference cannot yet recognize.

Humans have the same difficulty. Critics reject new genres. Audiences mock unfamiliar styles. Institutions select conventional work. The defense has never been perfect. It has been the existence of many independent scenes, subcultures, local traditions, eccentric patrons, stubborn artists, cheap venues, obscure publications, and people willing to make things nobody asked for.

Generative abundance can strengthen those people by lowering production cost.

A tiny subculture can make its own media. A person can visualize an aesthetic with no institutional support. A local language community can create material at lower cost. An outsider can prototype a world far enough to attract collaborators.

This is the counterforce the homogenization literature cannot capture fully in controlled tasks. Models may pull toward the center, but cheaper tools can create more centers.

The cultural outcome depends on whether generative infrastructure is used to standardize output for mass distribution or empower groups with genuinely different intentions.

Diversity of *users* is not enough if the users all ask the system to make them legible to the same market.

Diversity of goals matters.

So does the willingness to keep an output that the model, evaluator, or audience would not rank first.

The most important act in a generative workflow may sometimes be choosing the lower-probability thing on purpose.

Not because rarity is virtue.

Because culture needs enough wrong turns to discover new roads.

## Collective diversity is infrastructure

We usually talk about cultural diversity as a property of the finished catalog. Are different voices represented? Are many genres available? Can audiences find work from different places, communities, languages, and traditions?

Generative AI adds an upstream question.

Are the processes that produce culture independent enough from one another?

A forest can contain millions of trees and still be vulnerable if they are genetically too similar. A financial system can contain thousands of trades and still share one crowded assumption. A media ecosystem can contain endless output and still become brittle if the output is produced through a small number of models, platforms, optimization targets, and feedback signals.

Independence is a form of resilience.

This does not require every creator to avoid common tools. Cameras standardized photography in some ways while photographers remained different. Word processors standardized interfaces while writers remained different. The concern grows when the tool participates directly in proposing the content and when many users accept its proposals before supplying much independent material.

The first suggestion matters.

An idea shown early can anchor search. A phrase can define the problem. A generated mood board can establish the visual neighborhood. Once the group begins discussing a concrete option, alternatives have to fight not only for quality but for attention against something that already exists.

That makes pre-model diversity unusually valuable.

Organizations could treat it the way robust systems treat redundancy: not as inefficiency but as protection against common-mode failure. Two people brainstorm without seeing one another's output. One person begins from field observation, another from archival research, another from generation. Teams intentionally use different tools for the first round. A creative director withholds the model-generated reference until human directions are visible.

The point is not ritual purity.

It is to preserve genuinely independent starting conditions.

Once those conditions exist, models can be excellent combiners. They can compare, challenge, mutate, and expose similarities nobody noticed. AI becomes more valuable when it has diverse material to work with than when it supplies the diversity itself.

## Anti-homogenization is a practice

The problem sounds systemic, but creators can act locally.

Keep a private reference library that is not ranked by a platform.

Read old things and local things.

Photograph your own textures.

Record phrases people actually say.

Use primary sources before summaries.

Go somewhere inconvenient enough that the details have not already been optimized for a feed.

Make one version before asking for alternatives.

When a generated solution arrives too quickly, ask what convention made it feel immediately correct.

Ask for the anti-reference: not another example of the same aesthetic, but an object from a different field that solves a related problem.

Preserve errors that have energy.

Do not automatically choose the option that receives the strongest instant approval from everyone in the room.

Give strange work enough time to become legible.

These habits are small defenses against the statistical gravity of familiar culture.

They are also good creative habits without AI.

That may be the broader lesson. Generative systems do not create the need for taste, independent experience, eccentricity, or local knowledge. They make the cost of neglecting those things more visible.

When average competence is expensive, mediocrity can hide behind effort.

When average competence becomes cheap, difference has to come from somewhere else.
