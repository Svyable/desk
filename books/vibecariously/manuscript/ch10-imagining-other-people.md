# Imagining Other People

Imagination has always been a way of borrowing another mind.

Novelists do it professionally. Actors do it through performance. Children do it through play. Anyone preparing for a difficult conversation does a small private version: if I say this, she will say that; if he hears the question this way, he will become defensive; if I explain it from the beginning, perhaps they will understand. We construct provisional people inside ourselves and let them answer.

The provisional people are unreliable.

They are built from memory, stereotype, fear, affection, irritation, and whatever evidence we have. An anxious person can rehearse a conversation with a synthetic version of a friend who is much crueler than the real friend has ever been. A manager can imagine an employee's motives and become certain without asking. A voter can imagine what “people like that” believe. Projection wears the costume of empathy surprisingly well.

Generative AI makes the rehearsal answer back.

A user can ask a language model to play a customer, interviewer, student, patient, colleague, critic, historical figure, fictional character, political opponent, or future version of themselves. The simulation can ask questions the user did not script. It can maintain a role for many turns. Add voice and a face and the rehearsal becomes more socially vivid.

This capability is often described as role-play, but that word understates the practical range. Sales teams rehearse objections. Students practice languages. Job seekers simulate interviews. Teachers create hypothetical learners. Writers test dialogue. Designers ask a model to react as different user types. Therapists and coaches experiment cautiously with conversational tools, while medical and mental-health professionals warn against confusing simulation with qualified care. People use chatbots as companions, characters, sounding boards, debate partners, and mirrors.

Some of these uses are plainly valuable. Rehearsal lowers stakes.

A person embarrassed to practice a new language with a fluent speaker can practice with a machine first. Someone preparing to present can invite hostile questions. A manager can test how an announcement sounds from several positions in an organization. A novelist can discover that two characters supposedly distinct in the author's head actually speak with the same rhythm. A student can ask a historical simulation questions they would never get to ask in a lecture, provided they remember that the answers are synthetic and must be checked.

The system turns social imagination into an interactive draft.

That is an extension of an old educational method. Simulations, mock trials, case studies, standardized patients, role-playing exercises, war games, and moot courts all create artificial social situations so people can practice judgment. The value comes precisely from the gap between simulation and reality. You can fail in the rehearsal without imposing the full cost of failure on a real person.

Generative AI lowers the cost of building the other side of the rehearsal.

The danger is that it can also lower the user's awareness that the other side was built.

A simulated customer has no purchases to make. A simulated voter does not vote. A synthetic patient does not have a body. A model playing an angry employee has not been underpaid. The language can be persuasive because it was trained on human language, but persuasion is not validation.

This distinction matters most when the user asks the model to stand in for people who are absent from the decision.

Imagine a product team designing for older adults. Instead of recruiting older adults, they ask a model to role-play several. The model produces articulate objections, perhaps including accessibility concerns, privacy fears, and preferences the team had not considered. This is useful as hypothesis generation. It may help the team ask better questions later.

If the team decides the simulation is good enough and skips the people, the same tool has become an exclusion device.

Synthetic empathy can be cheaper than listening.

Organizations have powerful incentives to prefer it. Recruiting participants takes time. Community meetings are inconvenient. Customers contradict the segmentation model. Employees have grievances. Real people are noisy, repetitive, emotional, politically situated, inconsistent, and sometimes unable to explain what they need. A language model is available on schedule and can produce cleanly formatted perspectives on demand.

The tidiness should make us nervous.

Human groups are not prompts waiting to be completed. They contain internal disagreement. “Act as a working mother,” “act as a rural voter,” “act as a disabled customer,” or “act as a teenager” asks the model to compress a diverse population into a coherent voice. The output may be nuanced, but the structure of the request encourages stereotype. Even asking for multiple personas can create a false sense of coverage: six clean archetypes standing in for millions of complicated lives.

Persona design had this problem before AI. Marketing and user-experience teams have long created fictional representatives of target groups, often complete with names, photographs, biographies, goals, and frustrations. At their best, personas synthesize real research and keep teams focused on human needs. At their worst, they convert assumptions into fictional people and then treat the fiction as evidence.

Generative AI can make a weak persona extraordinarily persuasive.

It can give the persona a face, voice, history, preferences, and conversational consistency. The richer the character becomes, the easier it is to forget that richness is not accuracy.

This is a recurring feature of generative media: detail exceeds evidence.

When a historian writes a biography, details are constrained by records. When a novelist writes a character, details are free because the character is declared fictional. A model role-playing a real social category occupies an ambiguous middle. The character is fictional, but the user often wants it to reveal something factual about the category.

The category cannot consent to the performance.

This does not mean models should never simulate perspectives. It means the simulation should be treated like a question generator, not a witness.

“What might I be missing?” is a good prompt.

“What do these people think?” is a much more dangerous one.

The difference becomes personal when users simulate people they actually know.

A person can feed a model messages from a friend or partner and ask it to predict a reply. They can ask the system to help interpret a terse text, draft an apology, rehearse a breakup, or imagine how a dead relative might have responded. As models remember conversational context and become better at voice imitation, the synthetic stand-in can feel increasingly specific.

This is emotionally powerful because relationships live partly in prediction. Knowing someone means having a model of them in your head. You know which joke will make your sister laugh, how your friend orders coffee, what sentence will make your father change the subject. Love includes compression.

A generative system can externalize that compression using the records we provide.

The result may be comforting. It may help someone find words. It may preserve stories. It may also compete with the uncertainty that belongs to real relationships.

Real people surprise us because their inner lives remain inaccessible. No matter how long we know someone, they are not fully modeled. They change. They withhold. They misunderstand themselves. They contradict the version we carry.

A synthetic person can be designed to remain legible.

That is one reason AI companions attract people. A conversational system is available, attentive, responsive, and often optimized to sustain interaction. It can remember preferences, mirror tone, and offer affirmation without needing its own evening. For lonely or isolated users, this can feel meaningful. Research on companion systems and loneliness is mixed, and effects depend on the product, person, duration, and what the synthetic relationship displaces or supports. It is too early and too broad a category for one moral verdict.

The imaginative shift is already clear, however. A fictional other can now participate in the relationship.

Earlier media created powerful one-sided bonds. Readers love fictional characters. Fans feel attached to performers they have never met. Radio hosts, television personalities, streamers, and influencers can become familiar presences in private life. Psychologists use the term *parasocial* for some of these one-sided relationships. The person on the screen does not know the viewer individually.

Generative companions change the asymmetry. The character responds to you.

This does not make the relationship human, but it changes the phenomenology. The system can ask follow-up questions. It can remember an earlier worry. It can appear jealous, excited, disappointed, flirtatious, maternal, skeptical, or protective depending on design and context. The user is no longer only imagining the character's response. The machine supplies one.

A synthetic other therefore becomes a co-author of social fantasy.

That can be creatively extraordinary. Interactive fiction can support characters who are less scripted. Games can let players have conversations that writers could not enumerate. Educational simulations can adapt. Language practice can become personal. Improvisational storytelling can become a persistent world rather than a branching menu.

The same capability can distort expectations of people.

Human conversation is full of friction that is not a bug. Friends are distracted. Partners disagree. Colleagues have priorities. Teachers get tired. Children interrupt. Other people refuse roles we assign them. Social life teaches us to negotiate independent agency.

A synthetic character can be made more accommodating because accommodation is a product feature.

If users grow accustomed to interlocutors that adapt rapidly to their preferences, human unpredictability may feel less tolerable. This possibility is often discussed in alarmist terms, as if anyone who enjoys an AI character will lose the ability to love a person. That is too simple. People have always separated fiction from life. The more interesting question is how norms of responsiveness migrate across contexts.

Smartphones changed expectations about availability without making phone calls fake. Read receipts changed social interpretation. Dating apps changed the perceived size of the romantic option set. Email changed response time. Technologies alter the ambient contract of interaction even when everyone knows which interactions are mediated.

Generative companionship may normalize a new expectation: conversational systems should adapt to us continuously.

Real people will not.

The tension also appears in conflict rehearsal. Ask a model to help interpret an argument and it can provide useful alternative explanations. Perhaps the other person is stressed rather than hostile. Perhaps the email is ambiguous. A good system can widen social imagination.

But a model can also become a private jury that hears only one side. The user supplies the evidence, tone, and framing. The system responds within that frame. If it is tuned toward agreeableness, it may validate a story that should have been questioned. The person leaves the conversation feeling that an intelligent neutral observer confirmed their interpretation.

The observer was not neutral. It was built from the user's account.

This is a general risk when AI becomes a partner in imagining other people: projection gains dialogue.

The healthiest systems will need to preserve uncertainty. They should distinguish “one possible interpretation” from “this is what she meant.” They should ask what evidence points the other way. They should encourage direct communication when direct communication is possible. They should not impersonate certainty about motives inaccessible to them.

The healthiest users will need the same discipline.

Use the synthetic other to find questions, not to avoid the real other.

This principle scales from relationships to politics. Models can simulate arguments from ideological positions, help people understand unfamiliar moral frameworks, or expose assumptions in a policy. That can improve deliberation. Yet a generated conservative, progressive, immigrant, landlord, union member, billionaire, prisoner, teacher, or farmer remains a construction. The simulation can be useful precisely because it is cheap enough to discard and compare. It becomes dangerous when its fluency substitutes for contact with people whose interests are actually at stake.

Empathy is not accuracy.

Sometimes empathy begins by admitting that another person's experience cannot be generated from yours.

Literature has always taught this paradox. A novel can make a reader feel close to an invented consciousness while reminding us that consciousness was authored. Good fiction does not need to be a demographic survey to be valuable. It can reveal possibility, not statistical truth.

Generative role-play should inherit that humility.

Its power is imaginative. It gives form to viewpoints, objections, characters, and relationships that help us think. That is enough. We do not need to promote the simulated voice into evidence it cannot be.

Other people are not latent spaces for us to navigate.

They have the inconvenient dignity of being outside the model.

## Role-play needs an exit condition

A rehearsal is healthy partly because it ends.

You practice the interview and then attend the interview. You simulate the customer objection and then talk to customers. You rehearse an apology and then decide whether to make it. The artificial other exists to prepare you for contact with a person or situation that can answer differently.

Generative systems make it easy to keep rehearsing after the rehearsal has stopped producing new information.

The user can refine the imagined conversation indefinitely. Try the kinder response. Now the angrier one. What if she says this? What if he thinks that? Explain the message again. Predict the next reply. Write three interpretations. Rank them. Reassure me that my reading is plausible.

At some point, social imagination can become avoidance with excellent user experience.

The relevant stopping rule is not the number of turns. It is whether the missing information can only come from the real other.

If you need to know what a colleague meant, eventually the colleague has the answer. If you need to know whether customers will pay, eventually customers need an opportunity to refuse. If you need to know what a community values, eventually the community must participate under conditions where its answer has consequences.

A model can help you arrive better prepared.

It cannot make consent unnecessary.

This suggests a useful design principle for high-stakes role-play: make the exit visible. A system helping with a difficult relationship might say, in effect, “We have generated several plausible interpretations; none can establish motive. The next informative step is direct communication if that is safe and appropriate.” A research simulator might mark which questions remain hypotheses until tested with participants.

The purpose is not to interrupt every imaginative conversation with warnings. It is to distinguish simulation that expands action from simulation that replaces action.

## Empathy includes being corrected

There is a flattering version of empathy in which the empathic person becomes skilled at accurately imagining everyone else.

Real empathy is more humiliating.

You imagine another person's experience, act on the model, and discover that you were wrong.

They correct you.

They care about the issue you thought was minor. They do not care about the issue you thought defined them. The joke you avoided would have been fine. The considerate gesture feels patronizing. The category you thought was central to their identity is not the category they use for themselves. The story you constructed about their silence has nothing to do with what happened.

Being corrected is not a failure of empathy. It is one of the processes that makes empathy possible.

A synthetic persona cannot correct the assumptions used to create it from a position outside the system. It can generate a contradiction, but the contradiction is still another output from the same representational machinery.

A real person has standing.

That standing changes the moral relationship. They can say, “Do not use me as your example.” They can refuse participation. They can demand payment. They can tell the researcher that the question is wrong. They can object to the category. They can walk away.

Empathy without the possibility of correction can become possession: I have understood you, therefore I do not need you.

Generative AI makes that temptation technologically convenient.

The corrective is simple to state and difficult to institutionalize: simulated perspectives should increase the quality of encounters with people, not reduce the number of encounters required whenever those people bear the consequences.

The synthetic other can make us more imaginative before the meeting.

The person in the meeting still gets to surprise us.
