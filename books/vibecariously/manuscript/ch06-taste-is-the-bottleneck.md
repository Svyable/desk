# Taste Is the Bottleneck

Before cheap cameras, making a technically competent photograph was difficult. Exposure had to be understood. Film cost money. Focus could fail invisibly until development. A darkroom introduced another layer of skill and expense. The smartphone did not make photography trivial, but it removed enough technical friction that billions of people became habitual image-makers.

The result was not a world full of equally good photographs.

It was a world in which technical adequacy stopped being the most interesting separator.

Generative AI pushes many creative domains toward the same condition. It can provide baseline competence on demand: a plausible composition, grammatical prose, an acceptable icon, a conventional melody, a clean presentation, a coherent illustration, a functional block of code. The floor rises quickly. What happens to value when the floor rises is one of the most important questions in the creative economy.

The answer often begins with taste.

Taste is a dangerous word because people use it as if it were a private aristocracy of vibes. “She has taste” can mean that she knows which expensive chair to buy, which obscure record to mention, which restaurant will become difficult to reserve, which shade of beige currently signals sophistication. Taste can be class performance disguised as perception.

But taste in creative work is more practical than that. It is the ability to discriminate among plausible choices before the market, client, audience, or algorithm has fully validated one. It is noticing that the third version has life and the fourth is merely polished. It is knowing when a familiar solution is appropriate and when it is dead. It is feeling that a sentence is technically fine and rhythmically false. It is recognizing that an image is beautiful but belongs to the wrong project.

Taste is judgment under uncertainty.

Craft historically trained this judgment because making and seeing developed together. A painter mixed colors and learned relationships that became perceptual. A designer set type and began to notice spacing. A writer revised sentences and built an ear. A musician practiced scales until tension and release became bodily. The labor was not separate from taste; it educated taste.

When generative systems reduce the labor, we have to ask whether judgment can develop by another route.

The optimistic answer is yes. Exposure can educate. Someone who generates, compares, edits, studies, and reflects may develop strong discrimination without reproducing every traditional technique. Film critics can understand cinema without directing films. Great editors need not be great novelists. Art directors often work through other specialists. Human culture already contains expert judges whose execution abilities differ from the people they guide.

The pessimistic answer is that cheap generation can simulate progress so effectively that the user stops before perception deepens.

This risk appears in the way people react to early AI-assisted work. The first competent output can feel miraculous because it exceeds the user's own execution baseline by a large margin. If you cannot draw, an image with coherent light and anatomy feels exceptional. If you fear writing, a grammatical page feels professional. If you have never composed music, a track with structure and instrumentation feels finished. The model clears a threshold that used to require craft, and the user may mistake crossing that threshold for arriving.

Professionals have the opposite problem. Because the baseline is already below their standard, they see the failures more quickly. A generated design feels generic. The prose has no pressure. The music resolves too obviously. The product image cannot survive manufacturing. This is sometimes dismissed as guild defensiveness, and sometimes it is. People whose status rests partly on scarce technique have an economic reason to emphasize what the new tool cannot do.

But expertise also creates genuine perceptual resolution. The professional may be seeing defects the novice has not learned to see.

This asymmetry helps explain a recurring finding in research on AI and creativity: generative tools can produce large gains for people who begin with weaker creative performance, while gains for stronger performers are smaller, mixed, or concentrated in particular stages. In some design experiments, AI helps ideation but can slow experts during implementation because the expert must spend time correcting outputs that are superficially plausible but wrong in ways their own practiced workflow would have avoided.

The tool is not uniformly an amplifier. It changes the shape of the work.

For a novice, it can supply missing execution. For an expert, it can supply breadth, speed, provocation, or repetitive production—but it can also create cleanup. This means the fashionable phrase “AI won't replace you; someone using AI will” is too crude. Sometimes the person using AI becomes better. Sometimes they become faster. Sometimes they produce more options and worse final decisions. Sometimes an expert without AI beats a novice with AI because the scarce resource was never the first draft.

Taste becomes a bottleneck when generation stops being the bottleneck.

The word *bottleneck* matters because abundance upstream creates pressure downstream. If a model can make a hundred concepts, somebody has to review a hundred concepts. If it can write ten headlines, somebody has to know which headline is honest. If it can create endless product variations, somebody has to choose what can be manufactured, differentiated, supported, and sold. If it can generate film shots, somebody has to know whether they belong in the same film.

More supply increases the value of selection until selection itself becomes overwhelmed.

This has happened elsewhere in digital culture. Streaming services gave listeners access to more music than a record store could hold, increasing the importance of playlists, recommendations, critics, friends, and algorithmic discovery. Digital photography made taking pictures almost free, then people accumulated libraries too large to revisit. Social media made publishing easy, then attention became scarce. Search made information abundant, then ranking became power.

Generative AI adds a new layer: it makes *candidate realities* abundant.

When the world can be rendered ten ways before breakfast, the valuable person may be the one who can explain why option seven is wrong despite looking expensive.

This is a less glamorous description of creativity than genius. It makes creative excellence sound like quality control. Yet much mature creative work already looks like this from the inside. Professionals spend enormous amounts of time rejecting. The novelist cuts scenes. The photographer takes hundreds of frames and publishes one. The architect studies schemes that never become buildings. The scientist discards hypotheses. The chef tests dishes. The animator removes movement. The producer leaves songs off the album.

Taste is often visible in the waste stream.

Generative systems make that stream bigger and cheaper. The challenge is that cheap waste can encourage shallow judgment. When an option cost nothing, the user can reject it without understanding why. That sounds efficient, but reasons matter because reasons improve the next search. “I don't like this” keeps the model guessing. “The image makes the product feel fragile because the highlights turn every edge into glass” teaches both user and system something more precise.

The ability to articulate taste may therefore become as important as taste itself.

This is where language-based interfaces have a productive effect. People are forced to name preferences they previously acted on implicitly. A designer can point to a reference and say “more like this,” but a conversational model invites explanation. Why more like this? Is it the proportion, material, mood, historical association, or restraint? The system can ask follow-up questions. It can offer vocabulary. It can turn tacit preference into explicit criteria.

Done well, this strengthens judgment.

Done poorly, it produces pseudo-theory: elaborate explanations pasted onto preferences after the fact. Humans are very good at rationalizing taste. A person likes the blue one, then discovers a paragraph of strategic reasons why blue was inevitable. Models are even better at providing such paragraphs. The presence of an explanation is therefore weak evidence that a choice was well grounded.

One defense is consequence.

Taste improves when choices meet a world that can object. A chair gets sat on. A book gets read past page forty. A song gets played twice or forgotten. A building leaks. A joke fails in a room. A garment pulls at the shoulder. A business discovers that customers will not pay. Reality generates feedback that an aesthetic simulation cannot fully predict.

This is why physical practice remains valuable even when imagination becomes synthetic. The generated object can teach you what you think you want; the made object can teach you what wanting missed.

The gap between those lessons may become a major source of expertise.

Consider architecture. Image models can produce compelling buildings with extraordinary ease. They are good enough that architectural visualization has become one of the obvious uses of generative tools. Research reviews of AI in architecture show a heavy concentration in early and schematic design, where fast variation is most valuable and physical constraints are not yet fully binding. This makes sense. AI excels when the question is “What could this feel like?” rather than “What connection detail carries this load while satisfying code, waterproofing, maintenance, procurement, and budget?”

The architect's taste therefore has to include invisible things.

A beautiful render can be bad architecture. A professional eye does not see only form; it sees likely consequences. Where does water go? What happens at noon in August? How expensive is that span? Who cleans the surface? Does the entrance make sense from the street? What does the building look like after five winters? Which detail is pretending gravity does not exist?

The same distinction applies to writing. A generated paragraph can sound good locally while weakening a book globally. It may repeat a metaphor used three chapters earlier, smooth away productive uncertainty, introduce an unsupported fact, or explain what the reader already understands. Taste at book scale includes memory.

Generative tools tend to impress at the scale of the sample. Professional judgment often operates at the scale of the system.

This gives experts a role that is less about guarding technique and more about preserving coherence across abundance. The opportunity is significant. An architect who can generate dozens of early schemes may spend more time on tradeoffs. A filmmaker can visualize ambitious shots before deciding which are worth shooting. A small business can test packaging directions that once required an agency budget. A writer can generate counterarguments and find weaknesses before publication.

But these gains depend on the quality of evaluation.

The cultural danger is not that everyone will make bad work. It is that everyone will make work competent enough to be hard to dismiss and similar enough to be hard to remember.

Competence has its own aesthetic. Smoothness. Correctness. Balanced composition. Appropriate tone. Familiar surprise. The absence of obvious mistakes. These qualities matter, especially in commercial work, but they can accumulate into a landscape where nothing is bad enough to offend and little is strange enough to persist.

Taste in that environment may require a tolerance for roughness.

The most distinctive human work often contains decisions that look wrong before context teaches us how to see them. New music can sound incompetent relative to the rules of old music. Fashion can look ugly before it creates a new proportion. A sentence can feel awkward because it refuses a familiar cadence. A visual identity can seem unfinished because it does not perform the conventional signs of professionalism.

Generative models trained to produce plausible continuations have a structural reason to be good at the already legible. Users chasing approval have another. Together they can produce a feedback loop in which taste becomes the art of selecting from what the machine already knows how to make look finished.

Breaking that loop requires stronger taste, not less.

Strong taste is not the ability to recognize luxury signals. It is the willingness to make a choice before consensus has guaranteed it. It includes knowledge but also nerve. An experienced creator sometimes keeps a weird element because they can feel a relationship the audience has not yet learned. The model can generate weirdness on request, but randomness is not conviction.

Conviction shows up in what survives revision.

This may become one of the clearest differences between generated abundance and authored work. A model can propose a thousand eccentricities. A person has to decide which eccentricity belongs and defend it against the pressure to normalize.

The economic implications are uncomfortable. If baseline execution becomes cheaper, some paid creative labor will become less valuable. Not every displaced task will be elevated into more interesting direction. Companies will use AI to reduce costs. Some clients will accept generic work because generic work was what they needed. A local business may not require a bespoke visual language; a plausible logo at one-tenth the price can be rational. The romantic defense of human craft will not reverse basic incentives everywhere.

This makes taste a market advantage but not a universal shelter.

There will be enormous demand for good-enough generation. There will also be places where sameness becomes expensive: brands that need distinction, entertainment that needs attention, products that must create desire, institutions whose credibility depends on voice, artists whose value is inseparable from identity, physical experiences that have to survive contact with bodies.

In those places, the ability to see beyond competent output will matter more.

A tool can raise the floor and still raise the value of the ceiling.

The difficulty is getting enough people to notice the distance between them.

## Taste needs a training set too

If taste is judgment, judgment needs examples.

People develop taste by encountering work, comparing it, living with consequences, learning histories, arguing, copying, failing, and revising their opinions. The person who seems to “just know” that one chair, sentence, cut, color, or chord is better usually carries an internal archive too large to narrate in the moment.

That archive can become narrow.

A designer raised entirely on contemporary interface galleries develops a different eye from one who also studies signage, industrial controls, books, vernacular architecture, toys, public transit, and old operating systems. A writer who reads only current bestsellers learns a different range of sentence possibilities than one who reads letters, manuals, diaries, translations, court opinions, advertising, and poetry.

Generative AI can widen the archive by making unfamiliar material easier to find and understand.

It can also narrow it by becoming the archive.

If every question about style is answered through the same model, the model's summaries and examples become a filter between the creator and culture. Even when the answers are accurate, the route becomes standardized.

Strong taste therefore still benefits from direct encounter.

Read the book, not only the explanation of its style.

Visit the building when possible.

Listen to the whole album.

Handle the object.

Watch the film without reducing it to screenshots.

Look at mediocre work from the same period, not only masterpieces, so excellence has a background against which to appear.

Taste grows partly by learning what survives duration.

Generative output arrives too quickly to have survived anything yet.

## The taste audit

When choices multiply, teams need ways to distinguish preference from principle.

One useful practice is a taste audit after selection.

Choose the version first. Then ask why.

Which qualities actually drove the decision? Which were merely easy to verbalize? Would the same rule produce a good choice in another context? Are we rewarding familiarity? Are we responding to polish? Does the choice survive in black and white, without the expensive rendering, without the model's persuasive copy around it? What would a person with different status incentives choose?

Then test the chosen rationale against an alternative.

If the team says the selected design feels “more trustworthy,” strip away the cues associated with institutional seriousness and see whether the underlying information architecture still earns trust. If a paragraph feels “clearer,” ask whether it became clearer by deleting an important uncertainty. If a concept feels “more original,” compare it against actual precedents rather than the team's memory of them.

This practice turns taste from an oracle into a discipline.

It does not eliminate subjectivity. Creative judgment cannot be reduced to a checklist without destroying what makes judgment useful. The goal is to make the pressure behind a choice visible enough that people can learn from it.

Generative systems can assist here too. They can compare versions against stated criteria, identify recurring preferences across a project, or produce adversarial readings of the favored option.

But the final act remains human in a very ordinary sense.

Somebody has to care which consequence matters.

Taste is not only recognizing quality.

It is deciding what kind of quality this work is for.
