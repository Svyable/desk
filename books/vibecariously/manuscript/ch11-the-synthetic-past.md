# The Synthetic Past

When photography was young, its evidentiary power came partly from the difficulty of making one.

A photograph required a camera in front of something. Manipulation arrived almost immediately—compositing, retouching, staged scenes, altered negatives, selective framing—but the medium still carried a physical trace of light. The picture had an indexical relationship to a moment in front of a lens, even when the meaning attached to that moment was deceptive.

Digital imaging weakened the simplicity of that relationship without destroying it. A digital photograph still usually began with a sensor exposed to a scene. Editing could change pixels afterward. Everyone learned, gradually, that photographs could lie while continuing to use photographs as evidence.

Generative AI complicates the arrangement because an image can now look photographic without a photographed event anywhere in its history.

The public debate has focused, reasonably, on fraud: synthetic political images, non-consensual sexual imagery, fake evidence, impersonation. Those uses matter. But one of the quieter consequences may be more intimate. We can now manufacture pictures of our own past.

Not only restore them. Manufacture them.

The distinction is slippery because restoration already requires invention. A damaged photograph contains missing information. Any system that repairs a torn face or removes a stain must infer what should be there. Traditional conservators do this cautiously and document intervention. Consumer software has long automated some of the same work. Colorization adds information a black-and-white image did not preserve. Sharpening estimates edges. Upscaling invents detail between pixels.

Generative tools make the intervention conversational and potentially unlimited.

Fix the scratches. Open his eyes. Remove the person at the edge. Change the background. Put the family closer together. Make it summer. Extend the frame. Add the dog. Add the relative who was absent. Recreate the room from another angle. Animate the still. Give the person a voice.

The first instruction may feel like repair. The last is clearly invention. There is no natural line between them that the interface has to respect.

This matters because memory is not a video archive stored in the brain. Decades of cognitive research show that human remembering is reconstructive. We retain pieces, meanings, impressions, and associations; retrieval rebuilds an event. Later information can influence that reconstruction. Suggestive questions, misleading details, repeated stories, and images can alter what people remember or how confident they feel about remembering it.

Generative media adds a powerful new source of later information.

Researchers at the MIT Media Lab and collaborators demonstrated the mechanism directly in a preregistered study reported at CHI in 2025. Participants viewed original images or AI-edited versions and, in some conditions, AI-generated videos derived from those edited images. The more synthetic the presentation became, the more false recollections participants reported. The most manipulated condition—AI-generated video based on an AI-edited image—produced substantially more false memories than the unaltered control, while also increasing confidence in those false recollections.

The finding should not be inflated into “AI rewrites everyone's memory.” Human memory is variable. Laboratory tasks are not family life. The study tested a controlled exposure, not decades of living with generated archives. But the direction is important because it confirms something we should have expected: a realistic image can become material for memory even when the underlying event did not occur as shown.

Images do not need to deceive us at the moment of viewing to affect later recall.

This is a crucial distinction. People sometimes treat media literacy as a binary fact-checking task: label the image synthetic and the problem is solved. If the viewer knows, while looking, that the picture was edited, they have not necessarily neutralized every cognitive effect. The image can still become familiar. Details can be retained while source information fades. A later recollection may preserve the scene more strongly than the disclaimer attached to it.

Human memory already suffers from this separation. People remember a claim and forget where they heard it. They remember a face but not the context. A family story gets retold until the retelling becomes indistinguishable from first-person memory. Photographs can create the feeling of remembering events that were actually learned through the photograph.

Synthetic family media enters a system already built for confusion.

The emotional incentives favor use. Consider the many ordinary losses a family archive contains. Someone was behind the camera and therefore missing from every picture. A child looked away. A wedding photograph includes a relative who later became estranged. The only picture of a grandparent is scratched. A parent died before meeting a grandchild. Two branches of a family never occupied the same room. Generative tools can repair, combine, and reanimate these absences.

The desire is not fraudulent. It is grief, love, play, and the wish for continuity.

That makes the archival question harder, not easier.

A generated photograph of a dead parent holding a grandchild can be clearly understood by the adult who made it as a symbolic image. The child who encounters a printed copy twenty years later may not know. File metadata may be gone. The caption may be missing. A relative may remember the image but forget its origin. The object can outlive the context that made its meaning safe.

Families are not archival institutions. They are bad at provenance.

We inherit boxes of unlabeled photographs and spend afternoons asking who the people are. We forward images through messaging apps that strip metadata. We screenshot. Crop. Print. Compress. Rename. Move files between devices. The idea that consumer synthetic media will remain permanently attached to reliable machine-readable provenance is optimistic even if technical standards improve.

This suggests a need for cultural practices, not only technical ones.

A synthetic memorial image may deserve a visible caption. A restored photograph may deserve an original preserved beside it. A family archive could keep “as captured” and “reconstructed” versions distinct. Schools could teach children that a photograph can be meaningful without being a record of an event.

That last lesson is already necessary because photography itself was never pure evidence. The difference is one of degree. Generative tools make the production of plausible non-events cheap enough to become domestic.

The synthetic past also extends beyond family photographs.

Museums and educators can reconstruct historical environments. A student can ask to visualize a street in ancient Rome, a nineteenth-century factory, a vanished neighborhood, an extinct animal, a historical garment, or the interior of a ship. Used carefully, such images can make research legible. Archaeologists and historians have long built reconstructions; digital modeling is not new. The benefit is obvious when abstraction prevents people from understanding scale and environment.

But historical visualization has always carried a problem: uncertainty disappears in the render.

A scholarly reconstruction may be based on fragments, competing interpretations, and educated guesses. The finished image has to pick a roof shape, color, texture, arrangement, posture, weather condition, and thousand other details. Unless uncertainty is deliberately visualized, every choice looks equally established.

Generative systems amplify this because they can fill gaps effortlessly. Ask for a scene from a historical period and the model produces detail faster than a historian can verify it. The image feels information-rich. Much of that information may be generic plausibility rather than historical evidence.

This is not only a risk of factual error. It changes the imaginative relationship to history.

History is strange. People in the past did not know they were in an aesthetic period. They lived among old objects, cheap objects, repairs, imports, anomalies, fashions, leftovers, and local variations. Popular imagery tends to clean eras into coherent looks: “the 1950s,” “Victorian London,” “ancient Egypt,” “medieval village.” Generative systems trained on popular representations can intensify the coherence, producing a past that looks more like the genre of itself than the lived mess.

The same thing happens to personal nostalgia.

Ask for childhood in the 1980s or 1990s and the system can return a concentrated atmosphere of period objects. The cassette player, television, wallpaper, toy, clothing, light, and color all agree about the decade. Real childhood did not. Houses contained furniture from earlier decades, new electronics beside old carpet, regional products, hand-me-down clothes, and objects that had no interest in becoming nostalgic.

Generated nostalgia can be more period-correct emotionally than reality ever was materially.

That is why it feels good.

It gives memory a production designer.

The danger is not that nostalgia becomes fake. Nostalgia was always editorial. We remember emotionally salient fragments and reconstruct eras around them. The danger is that synthetic imagery can standardize the fragments. Millions of people may come to picture a decade through the same model's compressed iconography, making private memory converge toward public aesthetic.

This is another version of the homogenization problem. The past becomes a style prompt.

There is also a political use. Nations imagine themselves through selected history. Monuments, textbooks, films, museums, ceremonies, and propaganda turn complex pasts into scenes that justify present identities. Cheap generative reconstruction gives political actors a new supply of historical-looking imagery that does not have to claim documentary status to be persuasive.

A campaign can visualize an idealized industrial past, a heroic founding, a victimized community, a lost city, a border before change. The image may be labeled illustration. It can still activate memory structures and identity.

This is why the phrase “fake image” is too blunt. An explicitly fictional historical visualization can be politically potent without containing a deception about its production. The relevant questions are: what historical claim does the image smuggle through atmosphere? Which uncertainties have been resolved in one direction? Which people are shown as central? Which are missing? What does the scene make emotionally inevitable?

The same questions apply to historical entertainment, but entertainment can declare itself fiction. Problems arise when the aesthetics of reconstruction migrate into education and public memory without the uncertainty traveling with them.

We may need visual conventions for doubt.

Maps use dotted lines. Scientific charts use error bars. Archaeological drawings sometimes distinguish surviving material from reconstruction. Generated historical imagery could develop its own grammar: visibly schematic regions, alternative overlays, confidence labels, side-by-side variants based on different interpretations. The goal would not be to make images ugly. It would be to prevent beauty from pretending every detail has the same evidentiary status.

Personal synthetic memory needs a related grammar.

One possibility is to treat generated memorial images more like paintings than photographs. The image can be realistic, but its social framing says: this represents a wish, relationship, or interpretation. It is not a recovered camera event. The distinction respects the emotional value without pretending the past changed.

This becomes particularly important as video and voice generation improve. A still image is easier to hold at aesthetic distance. Motion creates behavior. Voice creates timing. A synthetic relative who turns, smiles, and says a familiar phrase can trigger a stronger sense of presence than a photograph.

For grief, that presence may comfort some people and disturb others. There will be no universal answer about whether it is healthy, respectful, uncanny, or exploitative. Families differ. Cultures differ. Individuals differ over time. The ethical baseline should be consent where consent is possible, transparency, and respect for the fact that the dead cannot update their preferences.

The larger imaginative change is undeniable. The past used to resist revision through the brute fact that it was over. We could retell it, repaint it, dramatize it, dream it, and misremember it, but producing a convincing audiovisual alternate past required significant labor.

That labor is collapsing.

The past can now be treated like another editable canvas.

This is both moving and dangerous because memory depends on a productive tension between what we wish had happened and what did.

A mature person learns, painfully, that the two are not always reconcilable. The missed photograph remains missed. The person was absent. The conversation never occurred. The house is gone. The child did not know the grandparent. Grief is partly the shape of those missing events.

Generative AI can give the absence an image.

The image may help us carry it.

It should not be allowed to erase the fact that it was absent.

## The family archive needs two clocks

A useful archive records at least two kinds of time.

The first is the time represented by the image: Christmas 1998, the summer after graduation, the day the house was sold, a grandparent at twenty-three.

The second is the time the image itself was made or changed.

Photography usually allowed us to collapse those clocks because the exposure and the represented event were nearly simultaneous. A scanned photograph might be digitized decades later, but the image still pointed back to one captured moment. Synthetic reconstruction separates the clocks radically. A scene depicting 1987 may have been generated in 2026 from three photographs, a family story, and an instruction to make the room feel warmer.

Both dates matter.

Without the second clock, later viewers can mistake a contemporary interpretation for historical evidence. With it, the image becomes more interesting rather than less. It tells us not only something about the past being represented but something about what a family in 2026 wanted to recover, repair, or imagine about that past.

The reconstruction acquires its own history.

This suggests a simple archival norm: preserve the original, preserve the derivative, and preserve the relationship between them.

The norm sounds obvious to conservators and excessive to ordinary people. But consumer technology has already made family archives technically complex. A phone photograph may include computational processing no one sees. Cloud services create edited versions. Messaging apps recompress. Social platforms strip context. Generative tools add another layer, one whose changes can be semantic rather than merely technical.

A visible caption can do more work than invisible metadata.

“Reconstructed in 2026 from two photographs and family description. The original is preserved here.”

That sentence does not drain the image of emotion. It protects the emotion from becoming accidental evidence.

## Restoration should expose its confidence

The word *restore* deserves special care because it implies return.

Some restoration really does recover information. A high-resolution scan can reveal details hidden in a poor copy. Color correction can reverse fading. Multiple damaged prints can be compared. Known geometry can constrain repair.

Other restoration estimates what probably occupied a missing region.

The visual result may be seamless even when the underlying confidence is not.

A responsible generative restoration tool could make that distinction visible. It might let users toggle between observed pixels and synthesized regions. It might show alternate plausible reconstructions. It might warn when a face, inscription, uniform detail, architectural element, or other semantically important feature has been substantially invented rather than recovered.

This is especially important when an image is used beyond private enjoyment. Historians, journalists, museums, genealogists, courts, and public archives need different standards from a person making a cleaned-up anniversary print.

The interface should help users know which activity they are performing.

Repair for viewing.

Reconstruction for interpretation.

Illustration for imagination.

Evidence for claims.

They can all be valuable. They are not interchangeable.

The synthetic past becomes manageable when we stop asking every image to carry the same burden of truth.
