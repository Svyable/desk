# Vibecariously

Before booking a hotel, people inspect photographs of the room. Before ordering dinner, they look at pictures posted by strangers. Before buying an apartment, they study a listing until they can mentally walk from the kitchen to the bedroom. Before traveling, they watch videos made by people who are already there. Before a concert, they may have seen the stage on a phone from six other cities.

Modern life was already becoming vicarious before generative AI arrived.

The internet made previews abundant. It let people consume approximations of experience before experience itself: reviews, walkthroughs, unboxings, reaction videos, travel diaries, virtual tours, before-and-after images, outfit posts, home tours, restaurant feeds. We learned to make decisions through other people's representations of what it might be like to have, visit, wear, eat, become, or endure something.

Generative AI changes the source of the preview.

The old preview usually came after reality. Someone had to visit the hotel. The jacket had to exist. The room had to be decorated. The meal had to be cooked. The photograph pointed, however selectively, toward an event or object in the world.

A generated preview can come first.

Now the apartment can be redecorated in twelve styles before a chair is moved. The trip can be visualized before tickets are bought. The product can appear in advertising before manufacturing is finalized. A person can see approximate versions of themselves with different hair, clothing, ages, bodies, careers, bedrooms, partners, cities, and lives. A public agency can show a park that has not been funded. A couple can see wedding photographs from a wedding that has not happened. A game studio can publish the feeling of a world whose mechanics barely exist.

We begin to experience possibilities through their vibes.

The word *vibe* is easy to mock because it is imprecise. That is why it is useful. People reach for it when a judgment arrives before a clean explanation. A room has a vibe. A person does. A neighborhood, website, restaurant, jacket, company, meeting, train station, political campaign, or decade can have one. The word compresses many weak signals—color, posture, rhythm, language, material, status, sound, memory—into a felt whole.

A vibe is perception before accounting.

Generative systems are unusually good at producing this level of coherence. Ask for a spreadsheet model and exactness becomes the test. Ask for a legal citation and verification matters. Ask for “the feeling of a beach town in winter if everybody there worked in robotics,” and the looseness becomes an advantage. The model does not need to prove a world. It needs to make the world legible enough that you can react.

That reaction is the core of what I mean by *vibecariously*.

To imagine vibecariously is to inhabit a possibility through a synthetic approximation before—or sometimes instead of—encountering the costly, resistant thing itself. It is not virtual reality in the technical sense. The preview can be a paragraph, image, video, voice, simulation, or interactive character. The defining feature is that the generated representation gives emotional shape to a branch of reality cheaply enough that the branch can influence a decision.

The practice is not inherently shallow. Scientists use models. Architects render. Engineers simulate. Militaries rehearse. Writers imagine. Children pretend. Human beings have always externalized possible worlds because reality is too expensive to use as scratch paper.

What changes is the fidelity-to-cost ratio.

A crude sketch is cheap but requires interpretation. A physical prototype is informative but costly. A professional rendering can be persuasive but takes skill and time. Generative AI collapses these categories. It can produce something visually rich at the cost of a sketch, sometimes in the hands of a person without specialist training. High apparent fidelity no longer implies high investment.

This means the emotional force of a preview can exceed the epistemic force behind it.

The picture feels detailed, so the possibility feels considered.

This is where vibecarious living starts to alter desire.

Desire has always responded to representation. Advertising exists because seeing an object in the right world changes what the object means. Real-estate staging exists because an empty room asks the buyer to imagine too much. Fashion photography does not merely document clothing; it teaches viewers a life around the clothing. Political campaigns do not present policy as spreadsheets because a policy must also be felt.

Generative AI personalizes this machinery.

Traditional advertising imagines a desirable life for a market segment. Generative systems can imagine one for you. Your face. Your room. Your city. Your preferences. Your body type. Your budget. The representation becomes harder to dismiss as somebody else's aspiration because it is built from material you recognize as yours.

Retailers already experiment with virtual try-on and synthetic product visualization. Home-design tools let people reimagine rooms from photographs. Image models make it trivial to produce informal versions of the same behavior without waiting for a specialized application. As multimodal systems become more persistent and personalized, the preview can become conversational: show me the coat with my usual shoes; now make it rain; now put me somewhere I would actually wear this; now compare it with the black one.

The commercial incentive is obvious. Reduce uncertainty, increase desire, close the sale.

The personal effect is less obvious because the preview can also help people avoid purchases. A person may discover that the dramatic wall color they loved in theory becomes exhausting when rendered across the whole room. Someone can test a tattoo concept cheaply enough to abandon it. A traveler can visualize the difference between two itineraries. A founder can see that the product concept looks indistinguishable from competitors before spending money building it.

Vibecarious imagination can be a machine for disillusionment.

That is healthy. Good imagination does not only intensify desire. It lets desire encounter consequences before reality has to pay for them.

The danger arrives when the preview optimizes the wrong consequences.

An image generator is rewarded, in practical use, for producing a result the user finds satisfying enough to continue. It has no built-in obligation to include inconvenience. A generated dream kitchen does not wake the person who cleans it. A generated cabin in the wilderness does not show the two-hour drive to groceries. A future body does not include the discipline, genetics, illness, aging, cost, or time implied by the transformation. A rendered business does not contain payroll. A beautiful family photograph contains no argument that happened five minutes earlier.

The image can make an outcome feel like a state rather than a process.

Social media already did this by showing selected moments from other people's lives. Generative media removes the requirement that anyone live the moment first.

This may create a new kind of comparison pressure. Earlier generations compared themselves with neighbors, celebrities, magazine covers, then influencers and algorithmically selected strangers. A person using generative AI can compare themselves with counterfactual versions of themselves. The rival is no longer another person. It is the branch in which you made every aesthetically satisfying choice.

Your apartment, but cleaner.

Your face, but more symmetrical.

Your career, but represented by the most cinematic image of success.

Your relationship, but without boredom.

Your city, but with the street trees fully grown.

These images can motivate. They can also make lived reality feel like a low-resolution draft of its generated concept.

The mechanism resembles an old problem in architectural visualization: the render becomes a promise. Buyers see the sunlight, landscaping, polished surfaces, and active public space. Years later, the building may be physically faithful to the plan and emotionally unlike the image because weather, maintenance, traffic, human behavior, immature trees, and ordinary clutter have entered the frame.

When anyone can make renders of their own future, each person becomes both developer and buyer.

We market possible selves to ourselves.

This is not entirely new. Vision boards, daydreams, fantasy, and aspirational consumption all precede AI. People have long pictured the house, wedding, body, office, or vacation they wanted. The difference is that internal fantasy is flexible enough to conceal contradiction. A generated version selects details, and those details can become sticky.

Once you have seen the blue kitchen, other kitchens compete with a memory.

Once you have watched a synthetic trailer for a project, the actual project has a tonal benchmark.

Once you have seen your face in a particular aesthetic, a mirror can feel like an alternative interpretation.

The visualized possibility acquires what economists might call an option value and psychologists might describe more simply as salience. It becomes easier to retrieve, easier to discuss, easier to desire, and easier to compare against the world.

This can improve collective decisions when possibilities have historically been hard to communicate. Imagine a neighborhood meeting about a redesign. Residents who struggle with abstract plans can inspect multiple scenarios. A disabled resident can ask to visualize the route from curb to entrance. A shop owner can ask where deliveries go. A parent can ask what the crossing looks like at school dismissal. Instead of one heroic rendering supplied by the project team, the meeting can generate alternatives in response to objections.

The power dynamic changes if the tool is genuinely available to everyone in the room.

It changes in the opposite direction if only the most powerful participant controls the image machine.

A developer with beautiful generative renderings can flood the visual field faster than critics can explain invisible costs. A campaign can generate emotionally precise futures that policy cannot support. A corporation can show workers an idealized automated workplace while leaving job loss as a sentence in the appendix. Cheap imagination is politically neutral only in the sense that a printing press is politically neutral. Access, literacy, distribution, and credibility determine who gets to make possibility look real.

Vibecarious politics may therefore become a contest over previews.

This is uncomfortable because people often assume visual manipulation matters only when an image pretends to document the past. Deepfakes and synthetic evidence deserve concern, but prospective imagery can influence decisions without deceiving anyone about provenance. Everyone can know the park is imaginary and still prefer the policy that comes with the greener picture.

The future has no documentary standard.

There is no “real” photograph of 2040 against which a generated one can be fact-checked. A future image can only be judged by the assumptions it contains. This makes prospective visual literacy different from forensic media literacy. The question is not “Is this fake?” It is “What would have to be true for this to become real?”

That question is the bridge out of vibes.

What would it cost? Who would maintain it? Who would lose? What happens in bad weather? What regulations apply? What materials exist? What is the timeline? Which behavior is being assumed? What evidence suggests people would respond this way? Which values were optimized? Which tradeoffs remain outside the frame?

The same discipline can be personal.

What would have to be true for this generated life to become real? Not “manifest it.” Not “believe hard enough.” Translate the image back into causes. If you like the room, is it because of the furniture or because there is less clutter? If you like the imagined body, is the desire about health, status, gender expression, clothing, age, or lighting? If the synthetic version of your career looks attractive, which part of the image represents actual work and which part represents social recognition?

The generated preview can become a probe for desire rather than an instruction to obey it.

This is where the term *vibecariously* earns more than novelty. It describes a new step in the decision process: feel the possible before paying for it. The step can make people wiser if they use the feeling as evidence about themselves and then test the world separately.

It can make them more manipulable if feeling becomes evidence about the world.

The distinction is simple and hard to maintain.

“This image makes me want that” is a fact.

“This image shows that this will be good” is a claim.

Generative AI makes the first statement cheaper to produce at enormous scale. Our institutions, markets, schools, and private habits will spend years learning not to confuse it with the second.

The future may be full of synthetic rehearsals. We will try on homes, jobs, identities, policies, trips, products, relationships, and aesthetic selves before committing. Many of those rehearsals will save time, money, regret, and argument. Some will create desires that no lived experience can satisfy because the generated world had no obligation to contain the boring parts.

The central skill will not be resisting the preview.

It will be remembering that a vibe is the beginning of a question, not the answer to one.

## The preview becomes a product

There is another possibility hiding inside vibecarious life: sometimes the preview is no longer merely a step toward consumption. It becomes the thing consumed.

A person may spend an evening generating dream homes without intending to move. They may design vacations they will never book, wardrobes they will never buy, gardens they will never plant, alternate careers they will never pursue, or fictional weddings that are enjoyable precisely because no wedding has to follow.

This is not failed planning.

It is entertainment built from counterfactual possession.

The internet already trained us to browse beyond intention. Real-estate sites are full of people who are not buying houses. Travel videos are watched by people who are not traveling. Product reviews entertain viewers who have no purchase in mind. Generative systems make this behavior interactive. Instead of touring somebody else's fantasy, you can manufacture one fitted to your own taste.

That creates a new media category somewhere between game, shopping, daydream, and design tool.

The person is not asking, “Which couch should I buy?” They are asking, “What would my life feel like if everything around me followed this aesthetic?”

The answer can be satisfying even if no transaction follows.

Companies will notice. A commerce platform does not need every generated fantasy to convert immediately. Repeated rehearsal can clarify desire, produce preference data, keep a person inside an ecosystem, and make a later purchase easier to target. The imagination session itself becomes commercially valuable.

This is why the boundary between creative tool and shopping interface may become difficult to see. A room-design system can help you think. It can also place purchasable objects into the room. A style assistant can help you explore identity. It can also learn which version of yourself makes you spend more. A travel simulator can help compare itineraries. It can also discover which imagined sunset moves you toward a booking.

The generated future becomes a sales environment before it becomes a future.

That does not make the tools corrupt by definition. People benefit when products fit better and uncertainty falls. The question is whether the system is helping the user understand desire or optimizing desire toward monetizable outcomes.

Those goals overlap until they do not.

A useful consumer norm may be to distinguish exploratory mode from transactional mode. Let people imagine without every object becoming a recommendation. Let the system show what a cheaper version of the desired quality would look like. Let it suggest subtraction, repair, borrowing, or doing nothing alongside purchase.

If the system understands the vibe, it may discover that the thing for sale was never the important part.

## Desire has an opportunity cost

Every vivid possibility competes with other possibilities for attention.

This matters because generated previews are cheap enough to proliferate beyond any person's ability to act on them. A person can see ten careers, fifty interiors, twenty possible cities, six versions of a relationship, and a hundred personal aesthetics in a week.

The practical world still offers one body and twenty-four hours in a day.

Abundant imagination can therefore create a new scarcity: commitment without resentment.

The more compelling the alternatives look, the easier it is to experience the chosen life as a foreclosure of better-looking branches. A real apartment contains maintenance and compromise. The other apartments remain immaculate images. A real career includes repetitive work. The alternate careers remain highlight reels. A real partner becomes a whole person. The imagined partner remains responsive to the premise.

This asymmetry can make reality lose a contest it was never designed to win.

Reality includes duration.

The preview does not.

One response is to ask the generative system to model opportunity costs rather than merely outcomes. If I choose this path, what becomes harder? What must I stop doing? Which benefits arrive only after repetition? What does year three look like after the novelty disappears? Which parts of the alternate path am I romanticizing because I never have to inhabit them?

These questions turn synthetic imagination from a branching machine into a choosing tool.

A mature vibecarious practice does not maximize the number of lives you can preview.

It helps you return to one life with better reasons.
