# What Would Survive Seventy Years

Seventy years is long enough for a secret to become a genealogy.

If a recovery program began in 1947 and remains active, the original participants are mostly gone. Buildings have been renovated. contractors merged. classification systems changed. analytical instruments transformed. Congressional oversight structures changed. Digital records replaced paper and then acquired their own obsolescence.

What survives such transitions?

Hardware can, if deliberately curated. Sample archives can. Photographs can. accession registers can. classification guides can. technical reports can. Contractor corporate memory can. Pension records and biographies can preserve personnel links. Facility environmental histories can survive because contamination laws create external obligations. Patents and publications may reveal adjacent technical work, though they should never be treated as proof of hidden provenance.

Most important, technical questions survive. If a material resisted characterization in 1952, someone would eventually try electron microscopy, then better microscopy, then atom-probe methods, synchrotron techniques, high-resolution mass spectrometry, or whatever the relevant tool became. A seventy-year exploitation effort should contain repeated attacks on the same unknowns.

This predicts stratigraphy in the archive: generations of measurement.

A hoax or rumor chain has a different stratigraphy. It accumulates retellings, authority claims, and reinterpretations but little increase in instrument resolution.

The difference is not absolute. Secret technical data may remain hidden. But it suggests what declassification should eventually reveal if the program is real: not merely memos about secrecy, but the sediment of engineering.
