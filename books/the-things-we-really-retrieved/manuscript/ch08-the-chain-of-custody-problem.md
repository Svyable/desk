# The Chain of Custody Problem

The phrase “chain of custody” sounds legal because courts made its importance obvious. Science needs the same discipline whenever origin is part of the claim.

If I hand a laboratory a magnesium alloy and say it came from a crashed vehicle, the lab can characterize the alloy. It cannot infer the crash from my sentence. Provenance is a separate dataset.

This separation should reorganize the entire UAP materials debate.

There are two hypotheses hidden inside most specimen stories. H1: the material has unusual or technologically significant properties. H2: the material originated in the reported anomalous event. Evidence for H1 does not automatically support H2. A strange sample with no custody history is a materials problem. A well-documented crash fragment with ordinary composition is an event artifact. The strongest case needs both.

Intelligence exploitation understands this intuitively because provenance creates value. A turbine blade known to come from a particular adversary engine constrains that engine. The identical blade bought at a flea market is a curiosity until its origin is established.

The UAP field has often reversed the burden. Provenance becomes an anecdote attached to a specimen, then technical unusualness is treated as retroactive proof of the anecdote.

That is backwards.

A better protocol begins at recovery and assumes future controversy. Record every handoff. Keep split samples. Preserve packaging. Retain soil, dust, water, paint, and ordinary nearby materials as controls. Photograph cut surfaces. Document cleaning. Archive raw analytical files in durable formats. If classification prevents publication, preserve the internal chain anyway so later declassification can reconstruct it.

This matters even under the strongest secrecy hypothesis. A hidden program serious about reverse engineering would need better internal provenance than the public does, not worse. Otherwise its scientists could not distinguish contamination, substitution, sabotage, and actual artifact properties.

This creates a useful prediction: if a historical exotic-material program is ever declassified, one should expect internal sample-control practices proportional to the scientific stakes. Absence of such practices would suggest either profound incompetence or a program more interested in belief than exploitation.

KONA BLUE, discussed later, is illuminating precisely because AARO’s public account describes a proposed program intended to receive and reverse-engineer off-world material that its advocates hoped might become available. The proposal existed; the material did not arrive. A named program can therefore be real while the thing it was meant to handle remains hypothetical.

That fact should haunt archival research. Organizational documents prove organizational intentions. They do not prove the existence of the target object.

The chain has to reach the object.
