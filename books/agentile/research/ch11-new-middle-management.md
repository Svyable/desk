# Chapter 11 — The New Middle Management

## Claim under test

Chapter 11 argues that agents can compress routine middle-management work—status collection, routing, scheduling, information transfer, basic approvals—without eliminating the organizational need to allocate judgment, resolve exceptions, coach people, design decision rights, and take responsibility. The predicted change is therefore not simply fewer managers. It is a shift from managing information flow toward governing a mixed human-machine production system.

## Sources checked

- Tania Babina, Anastassia Fedyk, Alex He, and James Hodson, “Firm Investments in Artificial Intelligence Technologies and Changes in Workforce Composition,” published in *Technology, Productivity, and Economic Growth* (University of Chicago Press/NBER, 2025; working-paper version NBER 31325). The authors find AI investment associated with more educated and specialized workforces and flatter hierarchical structures, including lower shares in middle-management and senior roles. The chapter treats this as suggestive association, not a causal forecast for agentic AI. https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/technology-productivity-and-economic-growth/firm-investments-artificial-intelligence-technologies-and-changes-workforce-composition
- Nicholas Bloom, Luis Garicano, Raffaella Sadun, and John Van Reenen, “The Distinct Effects of Information Technology and Communication Technology on Firm Organization,” NBER Working Paper 14975, later *Management Science* (2014). The study distinguishes technologies that lower information-acquisition costs, which were associated with greater autonomy and wider spans, from communication technologies that can support greater centralization. This is useful counterevidence to any one-direction “technology flattens firms” claim. https://www.nber.org/papers/w14975
- Ina M. Sebastian, Peter Weill, Mareike Haskamp, and Jan vom Brocke, MIT CISR, “Designing Decision Rights for AI” (June 18, 2026). Based on executive interviews, the briefing frames human/AI decision allocation around ambiguity and risk and separates framing a decision, acting on it, and learning from outcomes. It is used as current management-practice evidence, not a general productivity estimate. https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/2026_0601_AIDecisionMatrix_SebastianWeillHaskampVomBrocke
- Jonas Hjort, Hannes Malmberg, and Todd Schoellman, “The Missing Middle Managers: Labor Costs, Firm Structure, and Development,” NBER Working Paper 30592 (2022), published in *American Economic Review: Insights* 8(3), 2026. The paper shows that middle-management costs can materially affect which organizational structures are viable. Its development setting is not AI; the chapter uses only the general organizational-economics mechanism. https://www.nber.org/papers/w30592
- Tania Babina, “Understanding Firms’ AI Efforts and Their Economic Impact,” NBER Working Paper 35123, revised August 2026. The review emphasizes measurement differences across invention, adoption, outsourcing, activity, and investor perceptions, supporting caution about treating any single AI-adoption measure as definitive evidence of organizational effect. https://www.nber.org/papers/w35123

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

Cheaper coordination can flatten a hierarchy, centralize it, or do both in different dimensions. AI-investing firms are selected and differ from non-adopters; observed workforce changes may not be caused solely by AI. Some managerial tasks are relational, developmental, political, or accountability-bearing rather than information-processing tasks.

The chapter also preserves a human-capital countereffect: automating routine work can remove the apprenticeship through which future experts and managers learn. A firm can improve current throughput while weakening its future skill pipeline.

## What would weaken the chapter

The thesis would weaken if agent adoption does not materially change spans of control, escalation patterns, decision rights, management workload, or hierarchical structure; if routine coordination remains too unreliable to automate; or if mixed human-agent teams prove no harder to govern than conventional software workflows. It would also narrow if firms that flatten management consistently retain equivalent training, exception-handling, and accountability functions elsewhere without significant new governance work.
