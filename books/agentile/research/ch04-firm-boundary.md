# Chapter 4 — The Firm Has a New Boundary research note

## Why this note exists

Chapter 4 uses Coase and Williamson to argue that agentic software can lower coordination costs on both sides of the firm boundary. That is a strong organizing idea, but it should not slide into the simpler claim that cheaper market transactions automatically make firms smaller. The underlying economics is comparative: markets, hierarchies, and hybrids each carry different contracting, adaptation, control, and governance costs.

## Primary and authoritative grounding

Ronald Coase's 1991 Nobel lecture describes the practical costs of using the price mechanism: discovering prices, negotiating, drawing up contracts, inspecting performance, and arranging dispute settlement. He also explicitly says that his 1937 argument made the market-versus-firm choice central without fully specifying all the factors that determine that choice. The Royal Swedish Academy's 1991 explanation states the boundary condition cleanly: organization inside a firm is favored when total production, contract, and administrative costs are lower internally than through market exchange, and expansion stops when the next internal allocation costs more than contracting for it externally.

Sources:

- Ronald H. Coase, Nobel Prize lecture, 9 December 1991: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1991/coase/lecture/
- Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 1991 prize press release: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1991/press-release/1000/

Oliver Williamson's work makes the chapter's second move more precise. His 2009 Nobel lecture frames transaction-cost economics as a comparative-governance problem centered on when a firm should make rather than buy. The Nobel citation specifically recognizes his analysis of economic governance and the boundaries of the firm. Williamson's framework is useful here because it resists reducing every transaction to search price: adaptation, conflict resolution, contractual hazards, and transaction-specific investment can make hierarchy or hybrid governance attractive even when an outside supplier exists.

Sources:

- Oliver E. Williamson, Nobel Prize lecture, 8 December 2009: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/williamson/lecture/
- Nobel Prize, 2009 economics prize summary: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/summary/

## A current agent-era stress test: switching costs can rise while capability gets cheaper

The chapter's strongest modern claim is not that agents reduce transaction costs in the abstract. They may reduce some transaction costs while increasing others.

The FTC's January 17, 2025 staff report on large cloud-provider investments and partnerships with generative-AI developers identified potential increases in switching costs among the competitive implications it examined. The report is not evidence about ordinary agent deployments generally, and it does not establish that any particular partnership is anticompetitive. It is useful because it documents a live mechanism the chapter already anticipates: a capability can be externally rented while contractual, technical, infrastructure, or ecosystem dependence makes exit more costly.

Source:

- Federal Trade Commission, “FTC Issues Staff Report on AI Partnerships & Investments Study,” 17 January 2025: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-issues-staff-report-ai-partnerships-investments-study

## Strongest counterargument

The chapter risks making “transaction costs” so elastic that the theory explains every possible organizational outcome after the fact. If agents can make firms smaller because markets get cheaper, and larger because management gets cheaper, the thesis can become unfalsifiable.

The manuscript should therefore keep the mechanism specific. For any example, ask which cost actually changes: search, specification, monitoring, adaptation, dispute resolution, internal information routing, managerial attention, switching, asset specificity, security, or regulatory accountability. Then ask what alternative governance arrangement is being compared. “Agents lower coordination costs” is a hypothesis generator, not an empirical conclusion.

A second counterargument is that firm boundaries are not chosen solely to minimize a scalar called transaction cost. Property rights, incomplete contracts, regulation, capital requirements, tax, labor law, strategic control, learning, culture, and bargaining power can matter. Coase himself emphasized the complexity of the market/hierarchy mix. Chapter 4 is strongest when it uses transaction-cost economics as a lens rather than as a universal one-variable law.

## Editorial consequence

The chapter's sentence that a firm “expands when it can coordinate another activity internally at lower cost than arranging the same activity through the market” is a fair high-level summary of the Coasean boundary, but the surrounding prose should preserve **total comparative costs**, not imply that contracting cost alone decides the issue.

The most defensible agent-era proposition is narrower and more interesting:

> Agentic software can change several costs of governing a transaction at once. Because it may cheapen both external contracting and internal administration—and may also create new switching, security, and dependency costs—the organizational effect depends on which margin moves most for the transaction in question.

That formulation keeps the chapter's useful ambiguity while making it testable. It also strengthens the later discussion of portability and lock-in: rented capability is not synonymous with a competitive market if switching becomes expensive.

No manuscript change is required in this research pass. A later prose pass can use this note to replace generic “coordination gets cheaper” language with named mechanisms where the chapter makes concrete predictions.
