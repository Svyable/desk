# Chapter 18 — A New Force

## Claim under test

Chapter 18 synthesizes the book around one claim: agentic software is economically important because it lowers the cost of delegated action. That mechanism can alter firm boundaries, job bundles, managerial spans, consumer and seller behavior, ownership returns, verification demand, protocol infrastructure, and public administrative capacity without requiring claims about machine consciousness or inevitable mass unemployment. The chapter's main prediction is a migration of scarcity toward goals, permissions, verification, trust, ownership, physical bottlenecks, judgment, legitimacy, and responsibility as some forms of cognitive execution become cheaper.

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The “new force” framing would be overstated if agent systems remain predominantly advisory, require near-complete human supervision, or fail to reduce the full cost of delegation after verification, integration, security, and error costs are included. Existing automation theory may explain most observed outcomes without requiring a durable new descriptive category.

The chapter also preserves several two-sided effects rather than forecasting one direction: cheaper coordination can help small firms or enlarge incumbents; cheaper search can empower buyers or strengthen intermediaries; automation can displace tasks or expand demand; public agents can improve service or increase coercive capacity; open protocols can reduce lock-in while trust layers re-concentrate power.

No claim is made that human economic value depends on retaining comparative advantage over machines. Economic contribution, income distribution, social status, and human dignity are analytically distinct.

## What would falsify or materially narrow the book

The book's thesis would require major revision if delegated action does not become materially cheaper in real organizations; if agent adoption remains shallow because verification and integration costs consistently offset capability gains; if firm boundaries, spans of control, task allocation, machine commerce, ownership patterns, or public administration show little response to agent adoption; or if agents remain unable to operate across tools and institutions without continuous human sequencing.

A milder result—agents becoming ubiquitous but disappearing into ordinary software—would not itself falsify the thesis. General-purpose technologies often become economically important by becoming boring infrastructure. In that case, the strongest confirmation would be that organizations stop talking about “agents” while continuing to reorganize around cheap delegated action.
