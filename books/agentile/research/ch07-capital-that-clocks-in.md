# Chapter 7 — Capital That Clocks In

## Claim under test

The chapter argues that agentic software creates an economically unusual form of productive capacity: it can be purchased or rented through capital-like contracts while behaving in some workflows like directed labor. The claim is not that software agents are legally employees or that national accounts need a new factor category. The narrower claim is that agents can move tasks from labor to capital while preserving enough flexibility, responsiveness, and divisibility to make the operational distinction feel less obvious inside the firm.

## Sources checked

- Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, “Artificial Intelligence, Automation and Work,” NBER Working Paper 24196 (2018), later published in *The Economics of Artificial Intelligence: An Agenda*. Their task framework distinguishes the displacement effect of automation from productivity effects, capital accumulation, and the creation of new tasks. https://www.nber.org/papers/w24196
- Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, “Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor,” NBER Working Paper 25684 (2019). The paper treats automation as capital taking over tasks previously allocated to labor, while new tasks can reinstate labor and raise labor demand. https://www.nber.org/papers/w25684
- Daron Acemoglu, Fredric Kong, and Pascual Restrepo, “Tasks At Work: Comparative Advantage, Technology and Labor Demand,” NBER Working Paper 32872, revised March 2025. The review separates labor augmentation, capital augmentation, automation, and new-task creation rather than treating all technological change as one effect. https://www.nber.org/papers/w32872
- Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, “Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets,” NBER Working Paper 23285, published in *Journal of Political Economy* (2020). The paper finds negative local employment and wage effects from industrial robot exposure in the studied period and is useful counterevidence against assuming productivity gains automatically compensate displaced workers locally. https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285
- Daron Acemoglu, Andrea Manera, and Pascual Restrepo, “Does the US Tax Code Favor Automation?” NBER Working Paper 27052 (2020). The paper models tax treatment of labor and capital as a factor that can alter automation incentives; its numerical estimates are model- and period-specific and are not generalized in the chapter. https://www.nber.org/papers/w27052
- NIST, “Announcing the AI Agent Standards Initiative” (February 17, 2026; updated February 18, 2026), which describes current agents as capable of autonomous actions including code work, email/calendar management, and shopping. This supports treating agent capability as a real deployment category rather than a speculative metaphor. https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/02/announcing-ai-agent-standards-initiative-interoperable-and-secure
- Visa, “Agentic Payments: What Onchain Data Reveals Commerce” (July 14, 2026), describing agents beginning to book travel, reorder inventory, query data providers, and buy compute. This is an interested industry source and is used only as evidence that payment-capable agent systems are being deployed, not as evidence of their economy-wide scale or welfare effects. https://www.visa.com/en-us/thought-leadership/innovation/agentic-payments-from-the-ground-up

## Counterevidence and limits

The chapter deliberately avoids equating a software agent with a human worker. Agents do not possess employment rights, human scarcity, or independent legal personhood merely because their work resembles a role. The chapter also does not claim that every task shifted to software reduces employment. Productivity effects, complementary labor, new tasks, implementation work, and rising demand can increase labor demand elsewhere.

The “capital that clocks in” phrase is therefore behavioral, not an accounting assertion. A hosted agent may be economically closer to a purchased service than owned capital for a particular adopter, and firms can externalize human work through contractors in ways that already complicate headcount measures. The stronger empirical prediction is that organizations will increasingly need measures of productive capacity that supplement payroll headcount, because meaningful work can be performed by rented machine systems whose scale does not map cleanly to a number of employees.

## What would weaken the chapter

The thesis would need to narrow if agent systems remain unable to perform useful work without near-complete human supervision, if verification cost prevents elastic scaling, or if most apparent machine work turns out to be hidden human labor rather than model/tool execution. It would also weaken if firms do not change staffing, capital intensity, vendor dependence, or organizational metrics as agent adoption rises.
