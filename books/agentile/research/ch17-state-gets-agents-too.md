# Chapter 17 — The State Gets Agents Too

## Claim under test

Chapter 17 argues that agentic systems can increase public administrative capacity by reducing the cost of gathering records, interpreting rules, routing cases, monitoring programs, and serving citizens. Because government also possesses coercive authority and privileged data, the same productivity gain can make enforcement, denial, surveillance, and other exercises of state power cheaper. The chapter therefore treats notice, appeal, records, data minimization, human accountability, procurement independence, fallback capacity, and citizen-side agent access as institutional complements to public-sector automation.

## Sources checked

- U.S. Office of Management and Budget, Memorandum M-25-21, “Accelerating Federal Use of AI through Innovation, Governance, and Public Trust” (April 3, 2025). The memorandum encourages wider federal AI adoption while assigning agency governance roles and requiring compliance with applicable law and government-wide guidance. The chapter uses it as evidence that federal policy simultaneously pursues adoption and governance, not as a timeless or bipartisan settlement. https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-21-Accelerating-Federal-Use-of-AI-through-Innovation-Governance-and-Public-Trust.pdf
- U.S. GAO, “Artificial Intelligence: Federal Efforts Guided by Requirements and Advisory Groups” (GAO-25-107933, September 9, 2025). GAO identified numerous government-wide requirements and oversight bodies governing federal AI use, demonstrating that public-sector adoption already sits inside a dense institutional framework. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107933
- U.S. GAO, “Artificial Intelligence: An Accountability Framework for Federal Agencies and Other Entities” (GAO-21-519SP, June 30, 2021) and GAO's current AI issue page. The framework organizes accountability around governance, data, performance, and monitoring and remains relevant as systems become more agentic. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-519sp and https://www.gao.gov/artificial-intelligence
- U.S. GAO, “VA Disability Benefits: Opportunities and Challenges to Modernizing Technology and Adopting AI” (GAO-26-109137, July 2026). The testimony links AI adoption to a high-volume, high-consequence benefits system with longstanding modernization and program-management challenges, illustrating why administrative AI cannot be evaluated separately from the institution it enters. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-109137
- U.S. GAO, “Fraud and Improper Payments: Data Quality and a Skilled Workforce Are Essential for Realizing Artificial Intelligence's Benefits” (GAO-26-108850, January 13, 2026). This supports the chapter's two-sided public-administration mechanism: AI can help detect fraud and improper payments, but reliable data and skilled staff remain prerequisites. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108850
- NIST, AI Risk Management Framework and “Concept Note: AI RMF Profile on Trustworthy AI in Critical Infrastructure” (April 2026; updated July 17, 2026). NIST emphasizes lifecycle risk management for AI-enabled capabilities in high-stakes infrastructure. The chapter uses this to support operational resilience and fallback-capacity arguments. https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework and https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/concept-note-ai-rmf-profile-trustworthy-ai-critical-infrastructure
- U.S. GAO and CIGIE, Federal Information System Controls Audit Manual, 2026 revision (GAO-26-108633, June 29, 2026). The revision reinforces the continuing importance of controls, auditability, and operating effectiveness in federal information systems as AI becomes another component of public infrastructure. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108633

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The chapter does not treat every government AI use as an exercise of coercive power. Many uses are internal, advisory, clerical, or citizen-serving. Automation can improve consistency and reduce arbitrary human variation. It can also lower the cost of appeals and compliance for citizens, not merely increase state capacity.

Conversely, requiring a human signature on every decision does not guarantee fairness or accuracy. Humans can rubber-stamp machine recommendations, exhibit bias, or create longer queues with no meaningful review. The relevant control is substantive responsibility and contestability, not ceremonial human presence.

Federal AI policy is politically contingent and can change across administrations. The chapter therefore relies on durable mechanisms—authority, records, appeal, procurement, data access, and institutional accountability—rather than treating any one executive policy as permanent.

## What would weaken the chapter

The thesis would weaken if public-sector agents remain confined to low-consequence internal assistance and do not materially affect service delivery, enforcement, adjudication, or citizen interaction. It would also narrow if automated government decisions prove consistently easier to contest and audit than human bureaucracy without requiring new records, appeal, or governance infrastructure. The citizen-side agency argument would need revision if high-quality personal representation becomes universally available at negligible cost without public intervention.
