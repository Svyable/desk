# Chapter 8 — Machine Customers

## Claim under test

Chapter 8 argues that the economically important change in agentic commerce is not simply conversational shopping. It is that parts of demand can become delegable: search, comparison, monitoring, timing, switching, negotiation, and payment can be carried out under a principal's preferences, budget, and authority. This can lower consumer search costs and make some demand more elastic, while also creating new power for the platforms that represent, rank, and route that demand.

## Sources checked

- OpenAI, “Buy it in ChatGPT: Instant Checkout and the Agentic Commerce Protocol” (September 29, 2025). The launch joined product discovery to checkout while retaining explicit user confirmation and merchant control of payment, fulfillment, and support. It is used as evidence of an early delegated-purchase architecture, not as proof of autonomous mass adoption. https://openai.com/index/buy-it-in-chatgpt/
- OpenAI, “Powering Product Discovery in ChatGPT” (March 24, 2026). The company expanded the Agentic Commerce Protocol into product discovery, structured merchant feeds, comparison, and richer shopping. This supports the chapter’s claim that merchant legibility to agents is becoming a distribution surface. https://openai.com/index/powering-product-discovery-in-chatgpt/
- Visa, “The Top Payments Predictions That Will Reshape 2026.” Visa describes a prospective “Buy for Me” agent architecture built around payment credentials, preference data, category restrictions, and spend limits. This is an interested industry forecast and is used to illustrate the permission problem rather than establish adoption rates. https://corporate.visa.com/en/sites/visa-perspectives/trends-insights/2026-predictions.html
- Visa, “Agentic Payments: What Onchain Data Reveals Commerce” (July 14, 2026). Visa and Artemis report live activity in which agents book travel, reorder inventory, query data providers, and buy compute. The report is useful evidence that payment-capable agent transactions exist, but it does not establish their share of total commerce. https://www.visa.com/en-us/thought-leadership/innovation/agentic-payments-from-the-ground-up
- Visa, “New Visa Research Finds Consumer Trust is Accelerating the Path to Agentic Commerce” (September 9, 2026). Harris Poll fielded the underlying U.S. survey May 26–28, 2026, with 2,065 adults matched to the U.S. general-adult population. Only 23% said they trusted generative AI to handle payment transactions on their behalf, while 61% said they would trust Visa to handle agentic transactions. This is Visa-commissioned survey evidence, not an independent adoption forecast, but it directly complicates any inference from technical availability to consumer willingness to delegate payment authority. https://investor.visa.com/news/news-details/2026/New-Visa-Research-Finds-Consumer-Trust-is-Accelerating-the-Path-to-Agentic-Commerce/default.aspx
- Google Cloud, “Powering AI commerce with the new Agent Payments Protocol (AP2)” (September 16, 2025). AP2 separates user intent, cart approval, and payment into cryptographically signed mandates. For delegated tasks, a user can pre-authorize constraints such as price and timing in an Intent Mandate. This provides a concrete mechanism for the chapter’s “authority envelope” idea rather than leaving it as abstract governance language. https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-agents-to-payments-ap2-protocol
- Aviv, Gandh, Bitton & Shabtai, “Beyond the Mandate: A Systematic Security Analysis of the Agent Payments Protocol (AP2)” (arXiv:2608.23858, August 24, 2026). The paper analyzes AP2 v0.2 and argues that signed Checkout/Payment Mandates protect transaction data after signing but do not, by themselves, guarantee that the pre-authorization context was not manipulated. The authors build a testbed spanning five deployment architectures and demonstrate proof-of-concept attacks for the high-risk threats they identify. This is a recent preprint, not a peer-reviewed field deployment, so the manuscript uses it only for the narrower technical boundary: cryptographic proof of authorization is not equivalent to proof that the agent faithfully reconstructed the principal’s intent. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23858
- Mastercard, “Mastercard launches Agent Pay for Machines” (June 10, 2026). Mastercard describes infrastructure for high-velocity machine-driven transactions and microtransactions across several payment forms. This is evidence of payment-network preparation, not neutral evidence of future volume. https://www.mastercard.com/us/en/news-and-trends/press/2026/june/mastercard-launches-agent-pay-for-machines.html
- Stripe, “Agentic commerce: How AI agents are changing the way businesses buy and sell” (updated April 22, 2026). Stripe describes goal-directed, multistep commerce and advises merchants to expose structured product data and checkout flows usable without a human. Provider material is used for implementation evidence rather than welfare claims. https://stripe.com/resources/more/agentic-commerce

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The chapter does not assume that shopping becomes fully autonomous or that buyer agents make markets perfectly rational. Early systems often retain explicit user confirmation. Product data can be incomplete, models can be influenced by presentation and defaults, agents can misunderstand preferences, and switching costs can remain high even when search costs collapse. Human consumption also includes taste, identity, surprise, ritual, and social meaning that many users may prefer not to delegate.

The 2026 Visa/Harris result adds a different constraint: technical capability can arrive before social permission. A protocol may support delegated payment while most surveyed consumers remain unwilling to trust generative AI with the payment itself. Because Visa commissioned the survey and benefits from positioning established payment networks as the trust layer, the 61% brand-specific figure should not be treated as neutral evidence that network-backed agentic commerce will win. The useful finding is the gap between broad AI use and willingness to surrender transaction authority.

The AP2 security work adds a second distinction. Authorization evidence and intent fidelity are separate properties. A signed mandate can establish what was approved and still fail to establish that the information presented for approval was complete, truthful, or free from upstream manipulation. The chapter should therefore resist treating credentials, tokens, or signed mandates as a complete answer to delegated commerce. They narrow one failure surface while leaving retrieval, tool, merchant, model, and interface manipulation in scope.

The chapter’s stronger market-power claim is conditional. Buyer agents empower consumers only when they can compare broadly, preserve the principal’s private information, switch providers at reasonable cost, and avoid being captured by a dominant intermediary’s ranking or monetization incentives. The same agent layer that reduces search cost can become a powerful gatekeeper over demand.

## What would weaken the chapter

The thesis would weaken if agent-mediated shopping remains mostly a conversational front end with little delegated search, monitoring, or transaction authority; if consumers consistently reject preference memory and payment delegation; or if merchants do not alter product data, checkout, pricing, and distribution strategies to accommodate machine buyers. It would also need revision if agent platforms prove unable to represent user intent without leaking commercially sensitive preference or willingness-to-pay information.

A stronger disconfirmation would be sustained evidence that high-assurance authorization plus ordinary fraud controls are enough to make delegated purchasing reliable in practice, with low dispute rates and little evidence of upstream context manipulation. That would reduce the importance of the chapter’s distinction between permission and faithful representation. The current public evidence does not establish that outcome either way.
