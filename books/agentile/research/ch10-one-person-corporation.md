# Chapter 10 — The One-Person Corporation

## Claim under test

Chapter 10 argues that agents may lower the minimum staffing needed to operate a sophisticated small business, allowing a single principal to rent or delegate more organizational functions before hiring employees. The chapter is careful to distinguish synthetic capacity from institutional durability: lower staffing does not eliminate the need for governance, professional review, capital, physical infrastructure, or independent human judgment.

## Sources checked

- U.S. Census Bureau, Nonemployer Statistics program, current program description (page revised July 23, 2026). Census defines nonemployer businesses as businesses with no paid employees that are subject to federal income tax and meet the program’s receipts threshold. It states that the majority of U.S. business establishments are nonemployers and that most nonemployers are self-employed individuals operating unincorporated businesses. This establishes that very small, no-payroll enterprises are already a large and measurable part of the business population; it does not establish that AI agents caused their prevalence or growth. https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nonemployer-statistics/about.html
- U.S. Census Bureau, “Number of U.S. Nonemployers Grew Faster Than Employer Businesses Nearly Every Year From 2012 to 2023” (July 30, 2025), based on Nonemployer Statistics. Census reports that nonemployer establishments grew an average 2.7% annually from 2012 through 2023 versus 1.1% for employer establishments, and that nonemployers represented the larger share of U.S. businesses throughout that period. The article also reports roughly $1.8 trillion in 2023 nonemployer receipts. These data provide a pre-agentic baseline for the chapter’s argument that organizational scale and payroll headcount are already imperfectly coupled. https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2025/07/nonemployer-business-growth.html
- U.S. Census Bureau QuickFacts, United States, business statistics (accessed September 2, 2026). QuickFacts reports 30,427,808 nonemployer establishments for reference year 2023, compared with 8,361,342 employer establishments. The figures should be used only as a scale baseline, not as evidence that a nonemployer establishment is equivalent to the chapter’s proposed “one-person corporation”; many are side businesses, gig work, sole proprietorships, partnerships, or other arrangements with limited organizational complexity. https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/US/PST045225

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The Census category is broader and less glamorous than the chapter’s concept. “No paid employees” does not mean “one full-time founder,” much less a durable synthetic corporation. A nonemployer business may be a side hustle, a sole proprietor with modest receipts, a partnership, or a corporation that purchases substantial outside labor. Census explicitly notes that the business may not be the owner’s principal source of income. The statistics therefore support only a baseline claim: businesses can exist and generate substantial aggregate receipts without payroll employees, and this form is already common.

The chapter’s stronger prediction would be weakened if agent adoption fails to raise the revenue, functional complexity, survival, or capital efficiency of such firms relative to comparable nonemployers without agents; if outsourced model and platform costs simply replace payroll costs; or if legal, trust, physical, verification, or financing bottlenecks force successful firms to add humans at roughly the same thresholds as before. Historical growth in nonemployer businesses predates modern agents, so it cannot be attributed to AI. The evidence should therefore discipline the chapter away from “AI invented the one-person firm” and toward its narrower claim that agents may change what a very small firm can reliably do.
