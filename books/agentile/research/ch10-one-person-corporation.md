# Chapter 10 — The One-Person Corporation

## Claim under test

Chapter 10 argues that agents may lower the minimum staffing needed to operate a sophisticated small business, allowing a single principal to rent or delegate more organizational functions before hiring employees. The chapter is careful to distinguish synthetic capacity from institutional durability: lower staffing does not eliminate the need for governance, professional review, capital, physical infrastructure, or independent human judgment.

## Sources checked

- U.S. Census Bureau, “Census Bureau Releases 2023 Nonemployer Statistics” (May 15, 2025). Census defines the program as covering businesses with no paid employees that are subject to federal income tax and generally have at least $1,000 in receipts ($1 or more for Construction). It notes that most nonemployers are self-employed individuals operating unincorporated businesses, though the category also includes some partnerships and corporations. https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/2023-nonemployer-statistics.html
- U.S. Census Bureau, table `NONEMP2023.NS2300NONEMP`, “All Sectors: Nonemployer Statistics by Legal Form of Organization and Receipts Size Class … 2023.” The national table reports **30,427,808 nonemployer establishments** and **$1,752,978,019,000 in receipts**. This directly supports the manuscript’s “nearly $1.8 trillion” wording without turning the rounded prose into false precision. https://data.census.gov/table/NONEMP2023.NS2300NONEMP
- U.S. Census Bureau, “Number of U.S. Nonemployers Grew Faster Than Employer Businesses Nearly Every Year From 2012 to 2023” (July 30, 2025), based on Nonemployer Statistics. Census reports that nonemployer establishments grew an average 2.7% annually from 2012 through 2023 versus 1.1% for employer establishments, and that nonemployers represented the larger share of U.S. businesses from 1997 through 2023. These data provide a pre-agentic baseline for the chapter’s argument that organizational scale and payroll headcount are already imperfectly coupled. https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2025/07/nonemployer-business-growth.html
- U.S. Census Bureau QuickFacts, United States, business statistics (accessed September 2, 2026). QuickFacts reports 30,427,808 nonemployer establishments for reference year 2023, compared with 8,361,342 employer establishments. The figures should be used only as a scale baseline, not as evidence that a nonemployer establishment is equivalent to the chapter’s proposed “one-person corporation.” https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/US/PST045225

## Comparability warning

Census warns that the **2022 Nonemployer Statistics release introduced significant methodological improvements that affect historical comparability**. The chapter can safely use Census’s own published conclusion that nonemployers were the larger share of U.S. businesses from 1997 through 2023. It should not, however, infer a precise uninterrupted long-run growth rate from the raw series across the methodological break. https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nonemployer-statistics/data/datasets.html

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The Census category is broader and less glamorous than the chapter’s concept. “No paid employees” does not mean “one full-time founder,” much less a durable synthetic corporation. A nonemployer business may be a side hustle, a sole proprietor with modest receipts, a partnership, or a corporation that purchases substantial outside labor. Census explicitly notes that the business may not be the owner’s principal source of income. The statistics therefore support only a baseline claim: businesses can exist and generate substantial aggregate receipts without payroll employees, and this form is already common.

The chapter’s stronger prediction would be weakened if agent adoption fails to raise the revenue, functional complexity, survival, transferability, or capital efficiency of such firms relative to comparable nonemployers without agents; if outsourced model and platform costs simply replace payroll costs; or if legal, trust, physical, verification, or financing bottlenecks force successful firms to add humans at roughly the same thresholds as before. Historical growth in nonemployer businesses predates modern agents, so it cannot be attributed to AI. The evidence should therefore discipline the chapter away from “AI invented the one-person firm” and toward its narrower claim that agents may change what a very small firm can reliably do.

## Editorial implication

The manuscript’s Census passage is already stronger than the usual “one-person unicorn” story because it begins with the old form rather than claiming novelty from a handful of startup anecdotes. Preserve that move. Future evidence of an agentile transformation should be sought in outcomes such as revenue per nonemployer, organizational complexity, survival, transferability, purchased professional services, and founder dependence, ideally with direct measures of agent use. Company counts alone cannot carry the claim.
