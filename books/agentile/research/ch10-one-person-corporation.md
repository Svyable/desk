# Chapter 10 — The One-Person Corporation

## Claim under test

Chapter 10 argues that agents may lower the minimum staffing needed to operate a sophisticated small business, allowing a single principal to rent or delegate more organizational functions before hiring employees. The chapter is careful to distinguish synthetic capacity from institutional durability: lower staffing does not eliminate the need for governance, professional review, capital, physical infrastructure, or independent human judgment.

## Sources checked

- U.S. Census Bureau, 2023 Nonemployer Statistics, table NS2300NONEMP. The table reports 30,427,808 U.S. nonemployer establishments and $1,752,978,019,000 in receipts for 2023. Census defines nonemployer businesses as businesses with no paid employees that are subject to federal income tax and meet the program’s receipts threshold. https://data.census.gov/table/NONEMP2023.NS2300NONEMP
- U.S. Census Bureau, “Census Bureau Releases 2023 Nonemployer Statistics” (May 15, 2025). Census says the majority of U.S. business establishments are nonemployers and that most nonemployers are self-employed individuals operating unincorporated businesses, while noting that the category also includes some partnerships and corporations and may or may not represent the owner’s principal source of income. https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/2023-nonemployer-statistics.html
- U.S. Census Bureau, “Number of U.S. Nonemployers Grew Faster Than Employer Businesses Nearly Every Year From 2012 to 2023” (July 30, 2025). Census reports that nonemployers represented a larger share of U.S. businesses in every year from 1997 through 2023. This is useful as a pre-agentic baseline, not as evidence that agents caused the form or its growth. https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2025/07/nonemployer-business-growth.html
- U.S. Census Bureau, 2023 County Business Patterns, table CB2300CBP. The table reports 8,361,342 employer establishments in 2023. The comparison with NES is useful for scale, but the two statistical products should not be presented as legally identical business units. https://data.census.gov/table/CBP2023.CB2300CBP
- U.S. Census Bureau, Nonemployer Statistics datasets notice. Census warns that the 2022 NES release incorporated significant methodological improvements that affect historical comparability. The Bureau itself still publishes the larger-share result across 1997–2023, but future prose should not imply that the full time series is methodologically seamless. https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nonemployer-statistics/data/datasets.html

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The Census category is broader and less glamorous than the chapter’s concept. “No paid employees” does not mean “one full-time founder,” much less a durable synthetic corporation. A nonemployer business may be a side business, a sole proprietor with modest receipts, a partnership, or a corporation purchasing substantial outside labor. The statistics therefore support only a baseline claim: businesses can exist and generate substantial aggregate receipts without payroll employees, and this form was common long before modern agents.

That pre-AI prevalence is counterevidence against the strongest version of the chapter’s thesis. It may indicate that administrative labor is not the main constraint on many tiny firms. Demand, capital, regulation, physical work, customer trust, risk tolerance, verification, and founder attention can remain binding even when cognitive execution gets cheaper.

The chapter’s stronger prediction should therefore be longitudinal rather than rhetorical. If agentile capacity matters, evidence should eventually appear in what no-payroll firms can actually sustain: revenue distribution, functional breadth, survival, export reach, capital use, and operational continuity. The thesis would be weakened if agent adoption fails to improve those outcomes relative to comparable firms, if model and platform costs merely replace payroll costs, or if legal, trust, physical, verification, and financing bottlenecks force successful firms to add humans at roughly the same thresholds as before.
