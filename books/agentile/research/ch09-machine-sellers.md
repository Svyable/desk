# Chapter 9 — Machine Sellers

## Claim under test

Chapter 9 argues that agentic commerce changes supply as well as demand. A seller agent can interpret an open-ended request and make bounded decisions about price, quantity, delivery, configuration, payment terms, follow-up, and escalation. That lowers the minimum transaction size at which individualized selling and negotiation can be economical. The chapter does not claim that human sales disappears; it treats selling as a bundle of tasks and asks which parts become machine-executable.

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

Individualized offers can improve matching and expand trade; they are not synonymous with exploitation. Dynamic pricing and negotiated terms long predate AI agents. Machine sellers may also increase competition by allowing small firms to respond to more buyers and operate continuously. Human sales remains important where trust, politics, product ambiguity, relationship history, regulation, or high-consequence contracting dominate.

The chapter also separates fast reaction from collusion. Independent seller systems may lawfully react to public market information. Competition concerns become different when competitors share sensitive data or use common mechanisms that reduce independent pricing. The exact legal boundary is jurisdiction- and fact-specific.

## What would weaken the chapter

The thesis would weaken if machine seller systems remain limited to scripted customer service rather than discretionary quoting, negotiation, configuration, or follow-up; if individualized selling remains too costly to justify below today’s transaction thresholds; or if buyers systematically reject machine-generated offers. It would also require revision if merchants do not begin treating structured agent access, auditability, and machine-readable commercial terms as meaningful distribution infrastructure.
