# Chapter 14 — Who Owns the Agentile Force

## Claim under test

Chapter 14 argues that the distributional consequences of agentic production depend on a layered ownership and dependency stack: models, compute, chips, cloud, energy, data, protocols, distribution, memory, and the adopting firm's own workflow. The chapter does not claim that concentration at any layer is automatically anticompetitive. It asks whether scarce inputs, switching costs, vertical integration, or non-portable institutional memory allow one layer to capture a disproportionate share of the value created above it.

## Sources checked

- Federal Trade Commission, “FTC Issues Staff Report on AI Partnerships & Investments Study” (January 17, 2025). The report describes partnerships between major cloud service providers and prominent AI developers involving equity and revenue-sharing rights, cloud-spending commitments, access to compute, certain consultation/control/exclusivity rights, and sensitive information. FTC staff identified potential implications for access to inputs and contractual or technical switching costs; these are potential competition mechanisms, not findings that the partnerships are unlawful. https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-issues-staff-report-ai-partnerships-investments-study
- OECD, “Competition in artificial intelligence infrastructure” (2025). The report maps the multi-layer AI-compute supply chain across chip design and manufacturing, specialized inputs, server racks, data centers, energy, networking, cloud access, and AI development. The chapter uses it to support the bottleneck/stack framework rather than to assert a fixed market structure. https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/11/competition-in-artificial-intelligence-infrastructure_69319aee/623d1874-en.pdf
- Benjamin Moll, Lukasz Rachel, and Pascual Restrepo, “Uneven Growth: Automation's Impact on Income and Wealth Inequality,” NBER Working Paper 28440 (2021). The model develops a mechanism by which automation can increase inequality through returns to wealth even when technology raises output. https://www.nber.org/papers/w28440
- Douglas Kruse, Richard Freeman, Joseph Blasi, and collaborators, *Shared Capitalism at Work* (University of Chicago Press/NBER, 2010). The volume studies employee ownership, profit sharing, gain sharing, and broad-based stock options, including worker outcomes, firm performance, distribution, and diversification risks. The chapter treats shared ownership as one institutional option, not a universal prescription. https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/shared-capitalism-work-employee-ownership-profit-and-gain-sharing-and-broad-based-stock-options
- Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, “Tasks, Automation, and the Rise in US Wage Inequality,” NBER Working Paper 28920 (2021). The paper documents a strong relationship between task displacement and changes in the U.S. wage structure over the studied decades, reinforcing the chapter's distinction between productivity growth and who receives the resulting income. https://www.nber.org/papers/w28920

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

Infrastructure concentration is not static. Open-weight models, smaller models, new chip designs, multi-cloud strategies, standards, and local inference can reduce dependence. Vertical integration can improve performance and lower coordination cost. Large capital requirements can reflect genuine scale economies rather than exclusionary conduct.

Broader employee ownership is also not a free distributional fix. Concentrating both employment income and savings in one firm creates diversification risk, and firm-level ownership cannot by itself address people outside the firm or regions that lose activity. Competition, portability, diversified asset ownership, taxation, public investment, and standards solve different problems and should not be collapsed into one policy.

## What would weaken the chapter

The thesis would weaken if switching costs among models and infrastructure providers remain trivial in production, if open alternatives reliably eliminate dependency at the relevant scale, or if AI-linked capital income becomes broadly distributed enough that ownership concentration has little effect on household outcomes. It would also need revision if the main rents in agentic systems consistently accrue to abundant application-layer suppliers rather than scarce infrastructure, data, distribution, or trust bottlenecks.
