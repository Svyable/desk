# Who Owns the Agentile Force

A worker can quit.

A machine can be unplugged.

A share certificate can cross the world without either one moving.

Ownership is the quiet variable beneath almost every argument about automation.

We notice the task because the task is visible. A person used to reconcile the account; now software does. A person used to research the market; now an agent does. The organization reports fewer hours, faster service, or higher output.

Then the economic value flows somewhere.

To the customer through a lower price.

To the firm through a higher margin.

To the worker through a higher wage because the agent makes that worker more productive.

To the model provider.

To the cloud company.

To the chip supplier.

To the electricity producer.

To a lender who financed the data center.

To a shareholder whose name never appears in the workflow.

The agentile economy is not only a story about what machines can do.

It is a story about who owns the machinery of delegated action.

This matters because the machinery is unusually layered.

A textile mill could be seen.

The building stood in a town. The looms belonged to a firm. Workers entered through a door. The owners might live elsewhere, but the productive asset had an obvious physical boundary.

An agent can arrive through a subscription.

The company using it may own almost none of the infrastructure that makes the work possible.

The model may belong to one company.

The model may run on a second company's cloud.

The accelerators may be designed by a third company, manufactured using equipment from several others, assembled into servers by another chain of suppliers, housed in a data center financed by infrastructure investors, powered by a utility, connected through telecom networks, wrapped in software from an agent platform, authenticated by an identity provider, and paid for through a payment network.

A clerk sees an assistant on a screen.

The economy sees a stack of property rights.

The stack can be competitive at one layer and concentrated at another.

Thousands of companies can build agent applications while depending on a small number of upstream suppliers.

Millions of users can choose among interfaces that ultimately rent capacity from the same infrastructure.

A market can feel diverse at the surface while remaining narrow at the bottleneck.

This is not unique to AI.

The internet produced millions of websites on top of a smaller number of operating systems, browsers, cloud providers, app stores, advertising systems, and payment networks. Electricity allowed countless businesses to flourish on top of regulated or concentrated grids. Railroads created local commerce while concentrating transport through rights of way that were difficult to duplicate.

General-purpose technologies often democratize one thing by concentrating another.

The important question is not whether concentration exists.

It is whether the concentrated layer can extract an increasing share of the value created above it, restrict entry, raise switching costs, or shape the direction of innovation.

The Federal Trade Commission's 2025 study of large cloud-provider partnerships with prominent AI developers made this question unusually concrete. Staff described arrangements involving equity, revenue-sharing, cloud-spending commitments, access to compute, consultation rights, and exchanges of sensitive technical and business information. The report did not conclude that such partnerships are inherently anticompetitive. It identified mechanisms through which they could affect access to inputs and increase switching costs.

That is the right level of analysis.

Partnership is not monopoly.

Investment is not capture.

Integration is not abuse.

But dependency has economics even before it becomes a legal violation.

Imagine an agent company that has built its product around one model provider.

The prompts are tuned to that model.

The evaluations are calibrated to its behavior.

The tool calls use its interface.

The customer expects its style and latency.

The security review covers its data practices.

The legal agreement names it.

The team has learned its quirks.

Technically, another model exists.

Economically, switching is not a dropdown.

The company may have to retest every important workflow.

It may have to rebuild features.

Customers may notice changed behavior.

The replacement may be cheaper and still be expensive to adopt.

This is switching cost as organizational memory.

It does not require a proprietary file format.

The lock-in can live in everything the firm learned while making the system reliable.

That is one reason model competition can remain meaningful even when several capable models exist.

The relevant price is not the token price.

It is the cost of changing the production system.

The same applies one layer lower.

An AI developer can theoretically use several clouds. Training and inference at scale require chips, networking, storage, power, deployment tooling, security, and engineering expertise. The OECD's work on competition in AI infrastructure describes a multi-layer supply chain whose critical inputs include advanced chips, manufacturing equipment, data centers, energy, networks, and cloud access.

Each layer has different economics.

Chip fabrication requires enormous capital and technical capability.

A data center needs land, grid interconnection, equipment, permits, financing, and customers.

Cloud software benefits from scale and accumulated services.

Models benefit from compute, talent, data, distribution, and feedback.

The stack contains bottlenecks that cannot be copied with a weekend hackathon.

This does not mean the agentile economy belongs permanently to today's incumbents.

Technological markets can shift quickly.

New chip architectures can emerge.

Open models can reduce dependence on proprietary APIs.

Cloud customers can multi-home.

Inference can move to smaller machines.

Specialized models can outperform larger general systems in narrow domains.

Governments can invest in infrastructure.

Standards can reduce switching costs.

Competition can arrive from another layer entirely.

The purpose of mapping the stack is not to declare the winners.

It is to understand what kind of power a winner would possess.

The difference between owning an agent and renting an agent is easy to blur because software feels local.

A company may say, “Our agent handles procurement.”

The possessive pronoun can be doing too much work.

The company may own the instructions, customer data, workflow, and integration while renting the model and compute under terms that can change.

This is normal commerce.

Firms rent office space, lease vehicles, license databases, buy electricity, hire contractors, and depend on suppliers they do not own.

Ownership is not required for productive use.

What matters is bargaining power.

Can the firm switch?

Can the supplier raise price without losing the customer?

Can the customer bring its data and evaluations elsewhere?

Does the customer know how dependent it has become?

Is there a credible substitute during an outage?

Can the provider compete with the customer using information learned from the relationship?

These are ordinary questions of industrial organization applied to a new input.

They become more consequential when the input performs more of the firm's cognition.

A company can tolerate dependence on a vendor for office furniture.

Dependence on the system that writes code, serves customers, reconciles money, researches markets, and coordinates operations is different.

The vendor relationship begins to resemble a labor market, a utility relationship, and a software license at the same time.

This changes procurement.

The chief financial officer wants price stability.

The chief technology officer wants performance.

The security team wants control.

Legal wants predictable rights.

Operations wants reliability.

Employees want tools that work.

Management wants exit options.

The cheapest provider can be the most expensive if switching later becomes impossible.

This is why open models matter economically even for companies that never use one.

An open alternative can create an outside option.

It can reduce the credibility of extreme price increases.

It can let a firm test whether its application truly depends on proprietary behavior.

It can support local deployment where privacy or latency matters.

It can create suppliers that compete on hosting rather than model ownership.

Open weights do not magically decentralize the whole stack. The largest models can still require expensive hardware. Training may remain concentrated. Expertise matters. Serving millions of users is an infrastructure business.

But ownership has degrees.

A company running an open model on rented cloud compute is dependent differently from a company renting both model and compute from one vertically integrated supplier.

A company running a smaller model on hardware it controls has another dependency again.

The question is not open versus closed as a moral binary.

It is how many independent ways the firm can obtain the capability it needs.

Redundancy has a price.

So does captivity.

This brings the ownership problem back inside the adopting firm.

Suppose an agent increases a worker's output by fifty percent.

Who owns the gain?

There is no technological answer.

The employer can capture it as margin.

Competition can pass it to customers.

The worker can bargain for a raise.

The firm can reduce staffing.

The firm can expand and hire more people.

The worker can leave for an employer willing to pay more for someone who knows how to use agents well.

The outcome is governed by markets and institutions.

Technology changes the surplus available for bargaining.

It does not determine the bargaining rule.

This is why Chapter 13 ended with wages rather than unemployment.

A labor market can remain fully employed and still undergo a major redistribution of value.

If agents make certain forms of execution abundant, the price of those skills can fall relative to scarce judgment, relationships, physical presence, credentials, or capital ownership.

If the productive system becomes more capital-intensive, returns to ownership can matter more even while real wages rise.

Research on automation and wealth inequality makes this distinction explicit. Benjamin Moll, Lukasz Rachel, and Pascual Restrepo model a channel through which automation raises returns to wealth and can widen inequality even when technological progress increases aggregate output.

A person who owns productive assets can receive the gains through a route unavailable to a person living only on wages.

This does not imply a simple class map.

Workers own capital through pensions, retirement accounts, mutual funds, homes, businesses, and direct shares.

Founders are workers and owners.

Employees receive stock.

Public pension funds own large companies on behalf of beneficiaries.

The categories overlap.

They overlap unevenly.

A household with substantial financial assets experiences an AI-driven increase in corporate profits differently from a household with no such assets.

The relevant distribution is not only labor share versus capital share.

It is who owns the capital share.

That can change policy without changing the technology.

One response is broader ownership.

Employee stock ownership, profit sharing, gain sharing, broad-based equity, retirement savings, public funds, cooperative structures, and other forms of shared capitalism all attempt, in different ways, to let people receive some income linked to capital or firm performance in addition to wages.

The evidence is mixed enough to deserve humility rather than slogans.

Research collected in the NBER Shared Capitalism Project found associations between broad-based sharing arrangements and several positive worker and firm outcomes in the studied companies. The same research tradition also emphasizes risks: workers can become dangerously undiversified when both their job and savings depend on one employer, and benefits can be distributed unevenly inside firms.

Ownership is not free money.

It carries risk.

A worker whose pension is diversified across the economy is in a different position from a worker whose entire retirement depends on the same startup that pays the salary.

The question is not whether everyone should own the company they work for.

It is whether a society in which capital performs more cognition also needs broader access to diversified claims on productive capital.

That question becomes less theoretical as agents become ordinary infrastructure.

Another response is competition.

If no supplier can hold customers captive, more of the productivity gain can flow downstream through lower prices and better services.

A competitive market is itself a distribution mechanism.

It distributes surplus to buyers.

This is easy to overlook because competition policy is often discussed as a legal specialty rather than social policy.

But the difference between a ten-dollar and a one-dollar unit of synthetic cognition determines who can build with it.

A large firm can absorb expensive tools.

A school, small business, clinic, municipality, or individual may not.

Price and access decide who gets leverage.

Competition at the agent layer can therefore affect inequality even before any tax system responds.

So can interoperability.

A standard that lets a company switch models without rebuilding every integration can reduce vendor power.

A portable identity framework can let an agent move between platforms.

A common tool protocol can reduce the cost of plugging different models into the same systems.

Exportable evaluations can preserve institutional knowledge when providers change.

These sound like engineering details.

They are market structure.

The railroad gauge was market structure.

The electrical standard was market structure.

The web protocol was market structure.

A seam determines who can connect.

The owner of a seam can become more powerful than the owner of a product.

This gives large buyers a strategic choice.

They can optimize for today's performance or preserve tomorrow's optionality.

The choices can conflict.

A vertically integrated vendor may offer the best product because the layers are tuned together.

Avoiding integration for ideological purity can be expensive.

Accepting integration without measuring exit cost can be more expensive later.

The rational strategy is not permanent independence.

It is conscious dependence.

Know what you rent.

Know what you own.

Know what would have to change if the supplier disappeared.

This is business continuity for cognition.

The same discipline applies to governments.

A state that depends on one private agent provider for tax administration, benefits processing, defense analysis, or regulatory work has created a form of public dependency that deserves different scrutiny from an office-software contract.

Critical infrastructure has always raised ownership questions because failure affects people who never signed the contract.

If agentic systems become part of public administration, procurement decisions can shape sovereign capability.

Does the government retain records and models of its own policies?

Can it audit the system?

Can another supplier take over?

Can officials continue operating if a service is withdrawn?

Who has access to sensitive citizen data?

The more the system acts, the more these questions resemble institutional design rather than IT purchasing.

There is also the physical ownership underneath the digital story.

AI appears weightless because the interface is text.

The infrastructure is not.

Compute requires chips.

Chips require fabs, tools, materials, water, and energy.

Data centers require land, transformers, transmission, generators, cooling, financing, construction, and grid capacity.

The OECD's infrastructure report emphasizes the interdependence of these layers for competition. The implication for the agentile economy is simple: abundance at the cognitive surface can depend on scarcity deep below it.

If electricity becomes the bottleneck, electricity owners gain leverage.

If advanced accelerators are the bottleneck, chip suppliers gain leverage.

If data-center interconnection is the bottleneck, suitable land and grid access gain leverage.

The rent migrates toward the constraint.

This book has returned to that idea repeatedly.

Queues migrate toward scarcity.

Firm boundaries move around transaction cost.

Management moves toward judgment.

Wages move around task substitution and complementarity.

Ownership rents move toward bottlenecks.

The agentile force does not abolish economics.

It makes the old mechanics operate through new assets.

That should temper both utopian and dystopian claims.

A person with twenty dollars can already rent forms of cognitive capability that would once have required access to a large organization. That is real democratization.

The same twenty-dollar service can be supplied by an infrastructure stack requiring billions of dollars. That is real concentration.

Both can be true.

The surface can decentralize while the substrate centralizes.

The outcome depends partly on whether the surface can switch substrates.

This is why the healthiest agentile economy may not be the one in which every person owns a data center.

It may be the one in which people and firms have credible alternatives, portable data, interoperable tools, transparent terms, competitive providers, and broad ways of sharing in the capital gains produced by the system.

Ownership is not only possession.

It is exit.

The ability to say no to the current provider because another path exists.

A worker's power partly comes from the ability to leave one employer for another.

A buyer's power partly comes from the ability to leave one seller for another.

A firm renting synthetic labor needs the same thing.

Without exit, the agent that looked like an employee can become the landlord.

There is a final ownership question closer to the individual.

Who owns the agent's memory of you?

A truly useful personal agent can accumulate extraordinary context.

Your preferences.

Your routines.

Your professional history.

Your contacts.

Your financial constraints.

Your writing style.

Your medical scheduling preferences.

Your unfinished projects.

The reasons you rejected prior recommendations.

This memory can make the agent more valuable than a stateless competitor even if the underlying models are equivalent.

If the memory cannot move, the user is locked in by autobiography.

That may become one of the strongest switching costs in consumer technology.

The person does not merely lose an app.

They lose a trained representation of themselves.

Portability therefore has a human meaning.

Can I take my preferences, records, permissions, evaluations, and agent history somewhere else?

Can I revoke the old system?

Can I know what remains?

Can the new system understand the context without starting from zero?

The answer will determine whether personal agents become tools people command or relationships platforms own.

No single ownership structure resolves all of this.

Private investment can build infrastructure quickly.

Open systems can preserve optionality.

Public investment can supply capabilities markets underprovide.

Competition can discipline price.

Regulation can constrain abuse.

Shared ownership can distribute returns.

Diversified savings can spread capital income.

Standards can reduce switching cost.

Procurement can preserve public autonomy.

Each tool solves a different problem.

The mistake is to ask whether AI should be owned by companies or people as if intelligence were one machine in one room.

The agentile force is a stack.

The model is one layer.

Compute is another.

Energy is another.

Data is another.

Protocols are another.

Distribution is another.

Memory is another.

Authority is another.

The person or institution with power over the scarce layer can collect rent from everything built above it.

So follow the rent.

When an agent saves an hour, ask where the value goes.

When a firm becomes leaner, ask what it now rents.

When a tool becomes cheap, ask what scarce input made the abundance possible.

When a service feels free, ask what relationship becomes hard to leave.

And when someone says the agent belongs to you, ask the oldest property question in a new form.

What, exactly, can you take with you when you walk away?
