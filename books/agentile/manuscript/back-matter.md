# Back Matter

The argument of *Agentile* is deliberately narrower than the largest claims now made about artificial intelligence. It does not require a machine to be conscious, a single moment called AGI, every office job to disappear, or every firm to shrink to one person. It requires something more ordinary: software becoming good enough at receiving an intention, carrying work across several steps, using tools, making bounded decisions, and returning with an outcome that a person once had to pursue directly.

If that capability becomes cheap enough, reliable enough, and sufficiently embedded in institutions, the price of delegated action falls. The book has treated that falling price as a force because the effect does not stop at the task. It changes what is worth delegating, how many things can be attempted in parallel, where queues form, which permissions matter, how firms draw their boundaries, what managers supervise, how buyers and sellers meet, where verification becomes necessary, and who remains responsible after action has been distributed across people and machines.

That framing should remain falsifiable. If capable agents remain too unreliable to hold useful work for more than short stretches without constant human reconstruction, the force is weaker than this book suggests. If verification costs rise almost one-for-one with machine execution, much of the apparent gain disappears into supervision. If organizations discover that the tacit knowledge required to act safely cannot be encoded, learned, or escalated well enough for software delegates, then the human minimum size of many institutions will remain higher than the chapters here imply.

The argument would also weaken if agentic capability remains unusually concentrated in a few narrow technical domains. Coding, research, scheduling, procurement, customer service, public administration, and market participation do not need to move at the same speed. A technology can be transformative without becoming universal, but the broader economic thesis depends on delegated action crossing enough institutional boundaries to change coordination itself.

The first thing to watch is the cost of delegation, not the model price. Tokens can get cheaper while memory, tools, monitoring, security, retries, human review, insurance, and integration make a reliable agent system more expensive. The economically relevant price is the cost of a completed, verified, attributable outcome. That same distinction applies to management: one person supervising a hundred machine processes may become extraordinarily productive, or may simply gain the ability to propagate one bad objective faster. A wider span of control matters only when useful, verified work survives the decision boundary the manager owns.

The one-person institution is another test. A founder may gain the operating capacity of a larger team without gaining its relationships, capital, reputation, licenses, physical assets, or accumulated judgment. Software can lower the minimum apparatus needed to try something without abolishing the advantages of durable organizations. Labor is similarly resistant to a clean verdict. Task substitution does not map directly to employment, wages, dignity, or bargaining power. Demand can expand, new work can appear, entry-level pathways can erode, and ownership can matter more. A society can remain fully occupied while income and control move substantially.

Ownership therefore belongs beside employment in any account of the gains. If machine agency behaves partly like labor and partly like capital, the distribution depends heavily on who owns the infrastructure, applications, firms, protocols, and complementary physical assets. Competition can push benefits toward customers. Broad capital ownership can spread gains. Concentrated infrastructure can do the opposite. None of those outcomes follows automatically from the capability itself.

Verification may prove to be the decisive bottleneck. Cheap action creates more things that need to be trusted, and the winning systems may not be the ones that generate the most activity but the ones that can prove what happened, under whose authority, against which evidence, with what opportunity for appeal or reversal. If verification remains stubbornly expensive, the agentile economy will grow around that scarcity rather than eliminate it.

Then there is the physical world. Software can search, schedule, negotiate, plan, diagnose, design, and coordinate faster than factories can be built, nurses trained, transformers manufactured, court hearings held, houses permitted, or grids expanded. A faster cognitive economy can make physical bottlenecks more valuable. Any account of abundance that ignores those constraints will overstate what cheap agency can accomplish by itself.

Government adds a different constraint. Administrative capacity is not synonymous with good government. Agents can make a useful rule easier to execute and a bad rule easier to execute. Lower implementation cost increases the value of due process, appeal, audit, and clear authority because error and coercion can scale along with service.

Human judgment remains in the loop for a less fashionable reason: objectives are never complete descriptions of what people value. A model can help discover inconsistency, compare options, and expose assumptions. It cannot make responsibility disappear. The more execution is delegated, the more important it becomes to know who chose the objective, who granted authority, who could stop the process, and who must repair the consequence.

These are empirical questions, not articles of faith. The useful way to read this book later will be to ask where it was too early, where it was too confident, and where the bottleneck moved somewhere the manuscript did not expect. The language may age. “Agent” may become as ordinary as “app.” The specific model architectures and products will certainly change. The economic structure is the part worth testing: whether intention can be delegated cheaply enough to change the minimum scale of action; whether organizations respond by delegating more; whether coordination gets cheaper without verification becoming equally expensive; whether firm boundaries, managerial spans, market interfaces, public capacity, and ownership structures move in response; and whether responsibility becomes clearer as delegation grows or easier to evade.

Those questions are more durable than a benchmark score.

The book began with the claim that an old category was becoming too small. It does not need to end by installing a new one permanently. *Agentile* is useful only while it distinguishes an economic capability that familiar words blur together. If the word stops helping, discard it. The underlying test is whether delegated action keeps getting cheaper, and what becomes scarce when it does.
