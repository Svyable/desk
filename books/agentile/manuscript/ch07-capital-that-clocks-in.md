# Capital That Clocks In

A machine on a factory floor has always made economic classification look easy.

It is capital.

The company buys it, depreciates it, maintains it, and expects it to produce something. The machine does not negotiate a salary, resign for a competitor, ask for parental leave, or become offended by the foreman. It can replace work people once did, but the balance sheet still knows where to put it.

A software agent is less cooperative.

You may rent it by the month.

You may pay by the token, task, call, minute, or unit of compute.

You can give it a name, a role, an inbox, a budget, a set of credentials, a queue of work, a memory, and instructions about when to escalate.

It can read the morning’s messages and begin doing things before anyone else logs in.

Then you turn it off.

Was that labor?

The legal answer will depend on the jurisdiction and the purpose of the question. The accounting answer will depend on contracts and standards. The philosophical answer can keep a seminar busy.

The economic answer is more useful.

Something important happens when capital begins to behave like a worker.

Industrial machinery has been doing parts of this for two centuries. A loom embodies the ability to weave. A forklift embodies the ability to lift. A robot arm embodies a sequence of motions that would otherwise require a person. Automation has always allowed an owner to buy a durable claim on productive capability.

Agentic software changes the texture of the claim.

The capability is no longer tied to one narrow motion.

It can be directed.

That makes it easier to treat the same productive asset as if it were labor available on demand.

Consider the ordinary language of a company adopting agents.

“We gave it the reconciliation work.”

“It handles first-line support overnight.”

“It watches the inbox.”

“It prepares the brief.”

“It follows up with leads.”

“It checks the contracts.”

“It opens a ticket when something looks wrong.”

None of these sentences sound like descriptions of a database.

They sound like descriptions of employees.

Yet the company may own no employee corresponding to the role.

It may simply have access to a service running on somebody else’s machines.

This is one of the peculiarities of the agentile economy: the organization can acquire labor-like capacity through a capital-like purchase.

That distinction matters because labor and capital live under different rules.

A worker receives wages. Capital receives returns.

A worker can accumulate experience and bargaining power. Capital can be copied, financed, depreciated, pledged, leased, and concentrated.

A worker is a legal person with rights outside the firm. A machine is property or service.

A worker bears some of the cost of being ready to work. A cloud service can spread readiness across millions of customers.

A worker is scarce in a deeply physical sense. There is one body, one life, and only so much time.

Software is scarce too, but differently. The model can be copied. Compute can be added. Instances can be started in parallel. The constraint migrates toward chips, power, network capacity, data, permissions, verification, and whatever cannot be duplicated as easily.

The result is not that capital becomes human.

The result is that the old economic boundary becomes harder to see from the work itself.

Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo’s task framework is useful here because it avoids arguing about labels. Production can be understood as a collection of tasks. Some are performed by labor. Some are performed by capital. Automation occurs when capital becomes capable of tasks that labor previously performed.

The language is deliberately plain.

It asks who or what does the task.

Agents add an unusual case: capital can now receive instructions at a level of abstraction that used to require another worker.

The machine does not merely perform a prewritten motion. It can sometimes decide which motion comes next.

That is why an agent can feel like a new factor of production even though, in orthodox economic terms, it can still be analyzed as a form of capital performing a widening set of tasks.

The feeling is not entirely mistaken.

Categories are useful until the behavior they summarize changes.

A factory owner buying a lathe purchases a narrow productive possibility. A company renting a capable agent may be purchasing access to a broad decision-making substrate whose daily assignment has not yet been chosen. One morning it researches vendors. The next it drafts customer replies. In the afternoon it tests software. Overnight it reconciles records.

The flexibility resembles labor.

The ownership resembles capital.

That combination changes several things at once.

The first is utilization.

A human role often arrives in a large block.

Suppose a small business needs forty minutes of accounts-payable work, twenty minutes of customer follow-up, an hour of market research, and fifteen minutes of spreadsheet cleanup.

These needs do not automatically add up to a job.

The tasks may belong to different professions. They may occur at different times. They may be too small to justify finding, contracting, briefing, and managing separate people.

So they wait.

Chapter 6 called this the shadow backlog.

An agent can make that backlog economically legible because the productive capacity is divisible.

The business does not need a person called “miscellaneous cognitive work.”

It needs access to capability when a task crosses the threshold of being worth doing.

Cloud computing already familiarized organizations with this pattern. A company no longer had to purchase enough servers for its annual peak and leave much of them idle. It could rent capacity as demand changed. Compute became elastic.

Agents bring elasticity closer to work.

That does not mean every task becomes instantaneous or cheap. The model may need context. The agent may need a tool. A human may need to verify the result. Some jobs remain indivisible because responsibility cannot be rented in thirty-second slices.

But the direction is important.

Productive cognition begins to look less like a scheduled shift and more like a callable service.

This is why “AI as software” can understate the economic change.

Most software waits.

The spreadsheet does nothing until someone opens it.

The payroll system executes a workflow someone already designed.

The database stores.

The search engine returns.

Agents can be arranged to notice, initiate, pursue, retry, and escalate.

Capital that waits for a button is familiar.

Capital that clocks in is different.

The phrase should not be taken literally. The agent does not experience a morning. It does not have a work ethic. It does not deserve praise for being punctual.

That is precisely the point.

The organization receives some of the operational properties of staffing without staffing a person.

The second change is parallelism.

A human manager cannot make five copies of a strong analyst before lunch.

A company can sometimes launch five agent instances.

The copies are not free. Compute costs money. Tool use costs money. More agents create more outputs to review. They may duplicate mistakes. They may collide in shared systems if coordination is poor.

Still, the elasticity changes planning.

Traditional management asks whether enough people can be hired, trained, scheduled, and retained.

Agentile management can also ask how much parallel cognition should be rented for the next hour.

That sounds like infrastructure planning.

It also sounds like workforce planning.

The two begin to merge.

Imagine a tax-preparation firm in March.

Historically the peak creates a staffing problem. Temporary workers can be hired, contractors engaged, overtime authorized, and queues lengthened. The firm has to forecast demand far enough ahead to acquire human capacity.

Now imagine that agents can perform bounded parts of intake, document checking, classification, reconciliation, and follow-up.

The firm still needs accountants. It still needs professional responsibility. It still needs humans for ambiguous and consequential cases.

But some of the peak can be met by increasing machine capacity rather than recruiting more people.

The balance between the two will depend on cost, error, law, customer preference, and the quality of the system.

The important shift is that workforce elasticity starts to inherit the economics of computing.

The third change is ownership.

A machine can improve productivity while widening the distance between the person doing the work and the person receiving the gain.

This is not new.

Automation has always raised distributional questions because the owner of productive capital receives a claim on the output. If automation replaces a task that used to pay wages, some income can move from labor toward the owners of machines, intellectual property, infrastructure, or firms.

Acemoglu and Restrepo’s work makes the mechanism explicit. Automation can create a displacement effect by assigning tasks from labor to capital. Productivity gains can raise demand elsewhere, and new tasks can reinstate labor. The final labor-market effect is not mechanically determined.

But factor shares can move even when output rises.

Agentic systems make this question harder to ignore because the substituted task may look intensely human.

Writing an email.

Reading a contract.

Researching a market.

Explaining a policy.

Updating a record.

Scheduling a meeting.

Comparing bids.

These activities do not resemble the industrial robot in the popular imagination. They resemble the office.

When the capability enters through a software contract rather than a payroll account, the distributional route becomes visible.

Money that might once have gone to salaries may instead go to model providers, cloud companies, software vendors, integrators, data suppliers, security providers, and the shareholders behind them.

Some of it may remain with the adopting firm as higher profit.

Some may be competed away into lower prices.

Some may fund new employment.

Some may disappear into verification and implementation costs.

The outcome is empirical.

The ownership question is structural.

Who owns the callable capacity?

A business that builds and runs its own open model has a different dependency from a business whose entire synthetic workforce depends on one external API.

A worker can leave.

A vendor can change the price.

A worker can be poached.

A platform can change the model.

A worker can take institutional memory with them.

A hosted agent can lose behavior after an update.

A worker has bargaining power constrained by the labor market.

A firm renting agents has bargaining power constrained by the vendor market.

These are not equivalent risks, but they belong in the same management conversation because both determine whether productive capacity remains available on acceptable terms.

The fourth change is depreciation.

Human skill and machine capability age differently.

An employee may become more valuable after learning the organization.

Software can become obsolete because a new model arrives.

The company may spend months building an agent workflow around one system only to discover that the underlying provider changed a model, removed a feature, altered a price, or introduced a better version that behaves differently enough to require revalidation.

Capital usually depreciates.

Agentile capital may depreciate culturally as well as technically.

A machine tool becomes old because a better machine exists or because it physically wears.

An agentic workflow can become old because the surrounding digital world changes.

Websites change.

APIs change.

Policies change.

The organization changes.

The model changes.

The data changes.

The benchmark changes.

A system that worked last quarter can still run perfectly while no longer matching the work.

This makes maintenance a larger share of the economics than a demonstration suggests.

A vendor can show an agent completing a task once.

A business needs the task completed reliably for months, including the days when conditions are strange.

The useful unit is not capability.

It is maintained capability.

That brings us back to labor in another way.

Employees are maintained too.

They need training.

They need context.

They need management.

They need updated policies.

They need tools.

They need feedback.

An organization that treats agents as magical capital may rediscover these costs in software form.

The fifth change is measurement.

A person appears in headcount.

An agent may not.

This sounds administrative until executives begin comparing firms.

Suppose two companies each generate one hundred million dollars in revenue.

One employs a thousand people.

The other employs one hundred and rents substantial agentic capacity.

Revenue per employee makes the second company look extraordinarily productive.

It may be.

It may also be measuring the denominator oddly.

The second firm has externalized productive capacity into software and compute.

The same problem already appears when companies outsource labor. Headcount falls while contractors remain economically essential. Productivity measures can improve because work moved outside the organizational boundary.

Agents intensify the ambiguity because the outsourced capacity may not correspond neatly to any number of people.

How many “employees” is a million agent calls?

The question may be meaningless.

The firm needs different measures.

Cost per completed outcome.

Verification hours per completed outcome.

Exception rate.

Compute spend per unit of revenue.

Human attention consumed per thousand transactions.

Capital dependence.

Vendor concentration.

Recovery time when the agent system is unavailable.

These measures begin to reveal the actual production function.

They also make comparisons messier.

An agentile firm can look lean because it truly eliminated work.

It can look lean because it moved work into software.

It can look lean because customers are now doing the work themselves.

It can look lean because quality quietly deteriorated.

A low headcount is not a business model.

The sixth change is bargaining.

Labor and capital have historically bargained through different institutions.

Workers organize.

Investors vote.

Lenders impose covenants.

Vendors negotiate contracts.

Regulators define rights.

Tax systems treat wages and capital differently.

When labor-like output arrives through capital-like contracts, institutions built around the old distinction can create incentives.

Acemoglu, Manera, and Restrepo have argued that the U.S. tax system has at times favored capital relative to labor in ways that can encourage excessive automation. Their particular estimates belong to a specific model and period, not a universal law. The broader point is durable: rules around taxes, depreciation, payroll, benefits, liability, and finance influence whether firms prefer to buy a machine or hire a person.

Agentic capacity enters this landscape with unusual flexibility.

If an agent can substitute for a set of office tasks without creating an employment relationship, the economic comparison between hiring and renting software includes far more than raw productivity.

Benefits.

Payroll taxes.

Compliance.

Recruiting.

Severance.

Licensing.

Insurance.

Procurement.

Compute.

Integration.

Security.

Verification.

Vendor margin.

Switching cost.

A manager choosing between labor and agents is not comparing one hourly wage with one token price.

The comparison spans two institutional systems.

That should make us cautious about any clean prediction that firms will simply choose the cheaper one.

The inputs are complements as often as substitutes.

A strong employee with agents may outperform both a larger unassisted team and a fully automated workflow.

A professional may become the scarce supervisor of a much larger amount of machine-produced work.

An agent may make a human role more valuable because the person can now act across a wider surface.

Or the agent may remove enough of the role that the job disappears.

Different tasks will go in different directions.

This is why the task lens matters.

Jobs are bundles.

Capital does not have to replace the whole bundle to change bargaining power.

If software takes the easiest half of a job, the remaining half may become more valuable because it concentrates judgment.

It may also become worse because all the pleasant routine disappears and only exceptions remain.

The person becomes the error department.

There is no theorem guaranteeing that augmentation feels good.

The seventh change is formation.

Capital usually needs financing before it can be acquired.

Labor usually needs hiring before it can be deployed.

Agents can lower the minimum commitment required for both.

A founder with a credit card can rent machine capability that would once have required either employees or a larger software build.

This does not erase the need for money.

It changes the staircase.

The first step becomes shorter.

A company can reach a higher level of operational complexity before payroll becomes necessary.

Chapter 10 will return to this in the one-person corporation.

But the implication begins here.

If the first unit of organizational capability can be rented cheaply and in tiny increments, more people can attempt organizations that previously died in the gap between idea and staffing.

Some will become real firms.

Many will become expensive hobbies with excellent automated email.

Cheap agency does not create demand.

It does not create judgment.

It does not create trust.

It does not create a product people want.

It removes one class of friction.

That is enough to alter who gets to try.

The final change is political.

A society in which productive intelligence is mainly embodied in people distributes some economic power through human scarcity.

People have to be persuaded to work.

They can quit.

They can withhold labor.

They can carry knowledge elsewhere.

They vote.

They age.

They form families.

They need housing.

They have interests the firm cannot own.

Capital has owners.

If a growing share of economically useful cognition becomes capital, questions about capital ownership become questions about access to agency.

That can be liberating.

A small organization can rent capabilities once reserved for a large corporation.

A person in a remote place can summon expertise without moving to a commercial center.

A nonprofit can automate administrative work that once consumed donations.

A local company can operate globally.

It can also concentrate power.

If the most capable agents depend on a handful of infrastructures, the apparent democratization of labor-like capacity can coexist with deeper ownership concentration underneath it.

The user sees a tireless assistant.

The economy sees a capital stack.

Chips.

Data centers.

Energy.

Models.

Platforms.

Payment rails.

Identity systems.

Proprietary data.

Distribution.

The agent’s friendliness is an interface to that stack.

This book calls the resulting capability agentile because the word needs to name something broader than a model and narrower than intelligence itself.

It is the capacity to turn intention into delegated action.

Chapter 3 described its falling cost.

Chapter 4 showed how that can move the firm boundary.

Chapter 5 showed why authority has to be designed.

Chapter 6 showed how cheap action can empty one queue and fill another.

Now the factor question comes into focus.

The agentile force is economically strange because it can be purchased like software, scaled like infrastructure, directed like labor, and deployed with some of the discretion we associate with management.

That does not require inventing a new category in the national accounts tomorrow.

It requires noticing that behavior can change before language does.

The factory machine stayed where it was bolted.

The cloud server stayed in the data center.

The agent can appear wherever the credentials permit it to act.

A company may wake up with more productive capacity than it had when everyone went home.

No one was hired.

No one came through the door.

Still, something clocked in.
