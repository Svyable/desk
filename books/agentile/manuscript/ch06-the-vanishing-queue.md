# The Vanishing Queue

Every economy has a second economy made of waiting.

A customer waits on hold. A permit waits in a municipal inbox. A damaged shipment waits for somebody to compare photographs with an insurance clause. A scientist waits for a data-cleaning job that is too tedious to interrupt the experiment for and too small to justify another hire. A founder has a folder called later. A physician has notes to finish after dinner. A manager has fourteen messages marked unread because none is important enough to ruin the morning and all of them are important enough to remain.

The queue is where economically valuable actions go when the cost of attention exceeds their apparent value.

We usually notice queues when they become physical. A line outside a passport office is visible. A traffic jam can be photographed from a helicopter. Containers stacked at a port become a news story. Cognitive queues are quieter. They live in email, ticket systems, browser tabs, notebooks, draft folders, CRM records, exception reports, calendars, voicemail, and memory. Many disappear without resolution because nobody ever reaches the bottom.

This hidden backlog matters to the agentile economy because the first effect of cheap delegated cognition may not be the replacement of work already being done. It may be the activation of work that currently dies in the queue.

Consider the difference between a call center and the reason the call center exists. The visible institution is a room, or increasingly a distributed workforce, where people receive one customer problem after another. The deeper institution is a buffer. Demand arrives unevenly. Some problems take seconds, some take an hour, some need a supervisor, some need another department, and some cannot be solved at all. Staffing is an attempt to keep the buffer from becoming intolerable without paying for a workforce that sits idle during quiet periods.

Human labor is expensive partly because a person must be scheduled as a person. You cannot usually buy eleven seconds of an accountant at 2:13 in the morning, then thirty-seven seconds at 2:16, then nothing for a week. Employment and contracting bundle time into administratively meaningful blocks. Even gig platforms, which fragment labor more aggressively, still coordinate whole humans with sleep, commutes, preferences, minimum earnings, legal status, and finite attention.

Software already handles enormous volumes of the predictable work. The remaining queue is often what escaped prediction.

That is why the arrival of agents is not simply another round of automation. Traditional automation attacks the common path. It is good at the standardized transaction that occurs a million times. The awkward residue survives because the residue is too diverse to encode cheaply. Each exception may require reading a different document, looking up a different account, comparing several systems, inferring what happened, and deciding whether the case belongs to someone else.

For decades, the economics favored automation for the center and people for the edge.

Agents move the boundary toward the edge.

Imagine an insurance company with ten thousand minor claims in an exception queue. None is identical. One photograph is missing metadata. One claimant used a maiden name on an older policy. One repair estimate contains a duplicate line. One police report refers to the wrong vehicle color. A rigid program needs explicit branches for each class of irregularity. A human adjuster can usually read the mess and understand the shape of the problem.

An agent can begin to occupy that middle ground. It can inspect documents, query systems, compare dates, draft a conclusion, request missing evidence, or escalate the case. It may be wrong. The question is whether it can reduce the amount of scarce human attention consumed by the ordinary weirdness of the world.

If it can, the queue changes character.

The obvious prediction is that it shrinks.

The more interesting possibility is that it first grows.

Cheap roads create traffic. Cheap communication creates messages. Cheap storage creates files nobody would once have saved. When an action becomes inexpensive, people discover more reasons to perform it. The same rebound can happen with cognitive administration.

Suppose a compliance team can now review every low-value vendor contract rather than sampling the largest. That sounds like the elimination of a backlog. It may instead create a larger stream of findings. Each finding can produce a clarification request, a remediation ticket, an exception decision, and a record. The original queue disappears only to reveal several downstream queues that were previously suppressed by ignorance.

An agent that reads every customer comment may discover thousands of product complaints no one had categorized. An agent that examines every dormant sales lead may identify hundreds worth revisiting. An agent that checks every invoice against every contract may uncover years of tiny discrepancies. An agent that follows up every unanswered inquiry may generate more replies than the organization can absorb.

Abundance at one stage exposes scarcity at the next.

This is a general rule of production, but agents make it unusually vivid because their output is often another claim on somebody's attention.

A factory can increase production and fill a warehouse. A cognitive system can increase production and fill your inbox.

The difference sounds comic until the consequences become institutional. If every department can cheaply create analysis, requests, reports, proposals, experiments, and escalations, the organization may become more informed and less governable at the same time. Information ceases to be the scarce good. The scarce good is deciding which information deserves to change anything.

Queues are therefore not merely failures of capacity. They are also crude systems of prioritization.

This is uncomfortable because queues feel morally neutral. First in, first out. Take a number. Wait your turn. But any institution with insufficient capacity is rationing something. It can ration by price, urgency, status, persistence, relationships, lottery, geography, paperwork skill, or waiting time. A queue converts shortage into delay.

When agents supply cheap administrative capacity, some delay-based rationing can genuinely disappear. A person should not need to spend an afternoon on hold merely because the organization finds human service expensive. A small business should not wait weeks for an ordinary reconciliation if a system can safely perform it in minutes. A citizen should not lose access to a benefit because nobody opened a routine document before a deadline.

Those gains are not trivial. Administrative burden falls unevenly on people. A form that costs a professional twenty minutes can cost someone with limited time, language fluency, disability accommodation, transportation, or institutional knowledge much more. Agents could reduce some of that burden by performing the navigation rather than merely explaining it.

They can also make burden worse.

The same institution can put an agent on the other side.

Now every application can be checked more thoroughly. Every inconsistency can be questioned. Every deadline can be enforced. Every account can receive individualized follow-up. The cost of saying no politely and at scale falls along with the cost of saying yes.

A bureaucracy has always contained a strange mercy: it forgets.

Files get lost. Small rules go unenforced. A human clerk looks at a technically imperfect application and understands the substance. A low-value debt is not worth chasing. An appeal succeeds because someone notices the letter behind the form. Friction wastes enormous amounts of human life, but some friction also limits institutional reach.

An agentile bureaucracy can be both more responsive and more relentless.

That means the disappearance of the queue should not be treated as an unqualified measure of progress. We need to ask what the queue was doing.

Was it merely evidence that capacity was scarce? Then remove it.

Was it protecting people from arbitrary first-come rationing? Replace it with a better rule.

Was it quietly limiting the enforcement of a bad policy? Fix the policy before making enforcement perfect.

Was it the only place where a human being exercised discretion? Decide whether the agent has comparable authority to recognize exceptions or is merely accelerating literalism.

The economic temptation will be to measure agents by throughput. Cases closed per hour. Tickets resolved. Claims processed. Leads contacted. Documents reviewed. These are easy numbers and sometimes useful ones. They are also invitations to move the queue rather than solve the problem.

A support agent can close a ticket by sending an answer that causes the customer to open another ticket. A collections agent can increase contact attempts while reducing the probability anyone wants to remain a customer. A procurement agent can find more policy violations by making policy more intrusive than the savings justify. A research agent can produce fifty memos when the decision needed one fact.

Throughput is not flow if the work returns.

The old operations lesson applies: optimize the system, not the workstation. Agentile organizations will need to learn it in a domain where the work product can create more work almost instantly.

There is a deeper complication. Human queues impose a natural rate limit.

A lawyer can send only so many letters in a day. A salesperson can make only so many calls. A citizen can file only so many requests. An activist can submit only so many public-records applications. A fraudster can write only so many convincing appeals. A recruiter can contact only so many candidates. These limits have never been fair or designed, but institutions evolved in their presence.

Agents remove some of them.

The recipient does not receive the sender's productivity improvement as a gift. It receives volume.

This is why the agentile economy will invent machine etiquette and eventually machine law. Rate limits, deposits, authenticated identity, reputation, proof of stake, fees for excessive requests, priority classes, and reciprocal access rules are not peripheral technical details. They are ways of deciding who is allowed to put work into someone else's queue.

Email offers an early warning. The marginal cost of sending a message fell almost to zero, so the recipient inherited the filtering problem. Spam filters became a necessary institution because cheap speech at scale creates an attention externality. Agents can extend the pattern from messages to actions.

A machine can request a refund, appeal a decision, negotiate a price, open a support case, submit a bid, schedule a meeting, ask for a quote, file a form, dispute a charge, or make an offer. Each action can be legitimate. At sufficient volume, legitimacy is not enough.

The receiving institution needs a market design for attention.

Some organizations will respond by deploying agents to face agents. The customer agent files the claim; the insurer agent reviews it. The buyer agent requests bids; seller agents respond. The applicant agent completes paperwork; the agency agent checks it. A human may appear only when the two systems cannot settle the matter.

This can look like efficiency approaching its logical conclusion. It can also become an arms race in procedural persistence.

Suppose a consumer agent learns that disputing every ambiguous fee has positive expected value. The merchant agent learns that denying the first dispute reduces payouts because some consumers stop. The consumer agent never gets tired, so it appeals. The merchant agent never gets annoyed, so it resists. The cost of persistence falls toward compute and policy limits. A behavior that was once moderated by human inconvenience can become a stable machine strategy.

We will need explicit settlement rules for interactions that used to terminate because one party ran out of patience.

This is a recurring feature of agentile economics: human fatigue has been doing hidden institutional work.

It limits fraud and justice, harassment and advocacy, exploitation and accountability indiscriminately. Removing fatigue does not tell us which activities deserve to expand. It simply exposes the choice.

The same is true inside companies.

A chief executive might imagine a future with no unread reports because agents summarize everything. But summaries themselves create a new queue. Which five summaries should the chief executive read? An agent can rank them. Then the important decision becomes how the ranking agent is instructed and what it has learned to consider important.

The queue moves upward until it reaches an objective that cannot be delegated without changing who is governing.

This is where the vanishing queue meets authority.

There is a fantasy of the perfectly fluid organization in which no request waits, no information gets lost, and every task finds the right actor immediately. The fantasy is attractive because much waiting is pure waste. But an organization without queues is an organization making continuous decisions about priority. Someone or something must decide that this claim precedes that claim, this experiment deserves compute, this customer deserves an exception, this alert deserves a human, and this request can die.

Those decisions are the organization.

Agents can make them faster. They cannot make them value-free.

One of the first serious jobs in the agentile economy may therefore be queue design. Not call-center scheduling in the old sense, but the architecture of machine attention: which requests agents are allowed to generate, how much resource each can consume, what evidence earns priority, when persistence becomes abuse, what gets sampled, what expires, and where a person must be able to interrupt the flow.

This work will feel annoyingly procedural. That is usually how institutions are born.

The nineteenth-century office invented filing systems because paperwork multiplied. The twentieth-century corporation invented management information systems because transactions multiplied. The internet invented search and spam filtering because pages and messages multiplied. An agentile economy will need machinery for a world in which competent requests multiply.

The most valuable agents may not be the ones that do the most work.

They may be the ones that know what should not become work at all.

That distinction matters because the shadow backlog is enormous. Every person and organization can name dozens of useful things they would do if asking were free: reconcile old records, compare every supplier, check every renewal, test every assumption, translate every document, contact every lead, revisit every dormant account, inspect every exception, follow every citation, personalize every explanation.

Once agents make many of these actions affordable, economic activity expands into the backlog. Measured productivity may rise. Service quality may improve. Errors may be found. Opportunities may be created.

And then the new work collides with the finite world.

Customers still have twenty-four hours in a day. Courts still have judges. Hospitals still have beds. Companies still have budgets. Roads still have lanes. Managers still have reputations to risk. Physical goods still require atoms. Political institutions still need legitimacy. There is no agentic shortcut through every constraint.

The queue does not disappear.

It migrates toward whatever remains scarce.

That is a useful way to understand the entire economic transition. When cognition, persistence, and administrative action become cheaper, the economy does not become frictionless. It discovers its next friction. Perhaps that is trust. Perhaps it is permission. Perhaps verification, electricity, capital, land, human judgment, or simple willingness to be bothered.

The important question is not whether agents eliminate waiting.

It is what we will be waiting for next.

The migration can already be seen in miniature wherever software makes an administrative step cheap. A calendar link eliminates the exchange of messages required to find a meeting time and promptly makes meetings easier to create. Electronic filing removes the trip to a counter and makes filing more attractive. A dashboard makes measurement cheaper and invites more measurement. Each improvement is real. Each also changes the equilibrium around it.

The economist William Baumol spent much of his career thinking about activities in which productivity rises slowly because the work itself seems to require human time. A string quartet still needs musicians for roughly the duration of the piece. A haircut cannot be compressed into a millisecond merely because computers become faster. Agents complicate the boundary of this old problem. They may pull pieces of many services out of the slow domain without eliminating the embodied or relational core.

A teacher can delegate preparation of practice questions while remaining with students. A clinician can delegate parts of documentation while retaining responsibility for care. A lawyer can delegate document comparison while remaining responsible for advice. A building inspector can arrive with a synthesized history of prior permits without delegating the physical inspection. The queue shrinks around the human bottleneck while making the bottleneck more visible.

That visibility will be politically important. If a hospital uses agents to eliminate clerical delay but still cannot provide an appointment for six months, patients will understand more clearly that the scarce resource is not paperwork. If a planning department processes applications instantly but housing still takes years to approve, the institution can no longer blame the inbox. Cheap cognition can remove excuses as well as costs.

It can expose that some queues are policy.

A scarce license may be scarce because the government chose a cap. A specialist appointment may be scarce because training capacity is limited. A court date may be scarce because adjudication is intentionally careful and judges are finite. A board decision may wait because the directors want time to consider it. Speed is not a universal objective.

One danger of an agentile culture is that everything slow begins to look defective. Machines will be able to prepare, route, remind, compare, and chase at a pace that makes ordinary human deliberation appear like latency. The pressure to accelerate can then move into places where delay has value.

A credit committee may need an afternoon because the downside belongs to real people. A hiring manager may need several conversations because employment changes lives and organizations. A jury must deliberate rather than merely aggregate predictions. A regulator may need public comment because legitimacy is not the same thing as computational throughput.

The distinction is between waiting caused by neglect and waiting caused by judgment.

Agents are very good reasons to become more explicit about which is which.

There is also a class of queues built from distrust. Expense reports wait because someone may cheat. Payments wait because fraud is possible. Changes wait for code review because a mistake can break production. Capital waits for due diligence because a persuasive story is cheaper than a sound investment. These queues cannot be eliminated merely by giving the submitter a better agent. If anything, synthetic fluency makes verification more important because a weak claim can arrive beautifully packaged.

So the agentile economy may produce a paradoxical combination: less waiting for clerical work and more deliberate waiting at consequential gates. Routine cases flow continuously. Exceptional cases slow down on purpose. The art is detecting the boundary.

That suggests a different picture of efficiency from the one implied by an empty inbox. A healthy organization does not process everything at maximum speed. It spends almost no time on matters that are safe and reversible, and disproportionate time on matters that are consequential, ambiguous, or difficult to undo.

Agents can make this discrimination economical. They can gather the evidence that lets a routine payment pass automatically while flagging the one whose details genuinely deserve attention. They can prepare the ordinary contract for signature while surfacing the unusual indemnity clause. They can reconcile the predictable transaction and isolate the unexplained balance.

The best queue is not always no queue.

It is a queue whose delay corresponds to the cost of being wrong.
