# Agentile

*The Birth of a New Force in the Economy*

Sven Hardy Benson

There are moments when an old category stops being large enough for what has entered it.

A steam engine was called a machine, which was true, but not very informative. A railway was transportation, which was also true, though a railway reorganized where cities could grow, what factories could hold in inventory, how quickly prices converged, and how far a person could imagine a market extending. Electricity was energy, but the electric motor did not merely provide another way to turn a shaft. It broke the factory’s dependence on a central drive train and eventually helped rearrange the building around the work.

Artificial intelligence has spent most of its short commercial life inside similarly inadequate nouns. It has been software. A tool. A chatbot. Automation. A copilot. A feature.

All of those names still describe something real.

They increasingly fail to describe the interesting part.

A conventional program waits. A spreadsheet does not wake up because quarterly close is approaching. A database does not decide that two records probably refer to the same customer and then negotiate with another system to settle the ambiguity. A search box does not spend the afternoon comparing suppliers, draft three purchase orders, notice a compliance problem, ask another program for help, retry a failed workflow, and return at dinner with a folder of completed work.

An agent can begin to do those things.

The economic change is not that software has become conscious. This book does not require that claim and has no use for it. The change is more ordinary and, for that reason, easier to underestimate. Software is acquiring enough perception, judgment, persistence, tool use, and delegated authority to become an actor inside economic processes rather than only an instrument used by an actor.

Something that used to sit in the capital stock is learning to behave, in limited ways, like labor.

Something that used to be purchased as labor is becoming copyable, schedulable, and callable like software.

The border between the two is becoming crowded.

I use the word *agentile* for the capacity of a system to receive an intention, work toward it across multiple steps, make bounded decisions along the way, use tools, recover from some failures, and return with an outcome rather than merely an answer. The word is meant to name an economic property, not a species. A person can act agentically. So can a company, a bureaucracy, a market intermediary, and increasingly a software system. What is new is the industrial production of nonhuman agency.

That production creates a force.

Labor has always multiplied human intention by putting more people behind it. Capital has multiplied human intention by giving those people better tools. Organizations have multiplied it by coordinating specialized people and capital across time and distance.

Agentile systems add another route: intention can now be duplicated into parallel, semi-autonomous action.

The distinction becomes visible when one person can start ten tasks before breakfast and discover at lunch that seven are finished, two require judgment, and one failed loudly enough to be useful. It becomes visible when a small firm can maintain processes that once demanded a department. It becomes visible when customers send software to shop on their behalf, vendors send software to answer, and the negotiation occurs partly between machines that neither sleep nor become bored by fine print.

The phrase “AI productivity” is too small for this.

Productivity usually asks how much more output a worker produces with a better tool. That is an important question. It is not the only question once the tool itself can hold a queue of work.

The deeper questions concern the shape of the economy around it.

What happens to the firm when searching, monitoring, drafting, reconciling, scheduling, testing, filing, and following up become radically cheaper? What happens to management when a manager can delegate to hundreds of machine workers but still owns the mistakes? What happens to wages when some useful forms of cognitive labor are available by the token, second, or completed task? What happens to entrepreneurship when the minimum staff needed to try an idea falls toward one? What happens to markets when buyers and sellers arrive with tireless representatives? What happens to regulation when the regulated party can generate a thousand compliant-looking artifacts faster than an inspector can read ten?

And where does scarcity move?

It does not disappear.

The history of economic abundance is mostly the history of bottlenecks moving somewhere else.

When arithmetic became cheap, deciding what to calculate mattered more. When copying became cheap, attention became more valuable. When communication became cheap, filtering and trust became harder. When software reproduction approached zero marginal cost, distribution, ecosystems, data, and switching costs became strategic terrain.

If agentile action becomes cheap, authority, verification, objectives, access, capital, reputation, liability, and the right to make consequential decisions may become more valuable.

This book is an attempt to follow that movement before the vocabulary settles.

It begins with a small change in the unit of work. The chatbot answered. The agent is asked to return when the errand is done.

From there, the consequences become less small.
