# The Firm Has a New Boundary

A company is a strange answer to a simple question.

Why not buy everything from somebody else?

If markets are so good at coordinating strangers through prices, why does a corporation hire people, own equipment, maintain departments, issue policies, hold meetings, and spend enormous sums telling insiders what to do?

The question sounds theoretical until you try to run even a small business.

You can hire an accountant or retain a firm.

Employ a designer or commission a freelancer.

Build software or subscribe to it.

Own a warehouse or contract with one.

Keep customer support in-house or send it to a specialist.

Recruit employees or hire through an agency.

Maintain servers or rent cloud infrastructure.

Make the component or buy it.

Every firm is a moving border between commands and contracts.

Inside the border, work is coordinated partly by authority. A manager can tell an employee that priorities changed this morning. The employment contract does not need to specify every future task. Assets can be reassigned. People can be moved between projects. Information can circulate under standing permissions.

Outside the border, coordination happens through agreements between separate parties. Scope, price, service level, liability, access, ownership, and exit may need to be negotiated. The vendor has its own interests and its own hierarchy. The buyer cannot simply order the vendor’s employees around as if they were colleagues.

Ronald Coase made this border into an economic question.

His answer was not that firms are inherently superior to markets. It was that both methods of coordination have costs.

Markets require search, negotiation, contracts, monitoring, and enforcement.

Firms require management.

A firm expands when it can coordinate another activity internally at lower cost than arranging the same activity through the market. It stops expanding when internal organization becomes more expensive than the alternative.

The boundary is therefore not sacred.

It moves when coordination costs move.

This is the chapter in which agents become a theory of the firm.

The first temptation is to predict that firms will shrink.

If an entrepreneur can summon software specialists on demand, why hire a large permanent staff? If an agent can search vendors, negotiate routine purchases, monitor service levels, reconcile invoices, and switch providers, then using the market becomes easier. Functions that once required internal employees may be rented as services.

This is plausible.

It is also only half the story.

Agents can lower the cost of internal coordination too.

They can brief employees.

Track projects.

Reconcile decisions.

Retrieve institutional memory.

Prepare management information.

Monitor exceptions.

Translate across functions.

Write first drafts of policy.

Compare performance.

Route questions.

Maintain records.

Reduce the number of small coordination failures that make large organizations cumbersome.

If both market coordination and managerial coordination become cheaper, the direction of the boundary depends on which cost falls faster.

That ambiguity is not a weakness in the theory.

It is the theory.

Oliver Williamson spent much of his career making Coase’s insight more operational. He emphasized that transactions differ. Some are simple and easy to specify. Others involve uncertainty, repeated interaction, specialized investments, and conflicts that cannot be fully resolved in advance by contract. Markets work beautifully for many exchanges. Hierarchies can work better when adaptation and dispute resolution become central.

Agents alter the terms on both sides.

Consider a company buying a routine commodity.

The market is already good at this. There are many suppliers. Specifications are clear. Prices are visible. Switching is easy. An agent that searches quotes and places orders may reduce costs a little, but the basic institution does not change.

Now consider a service contract that was previously painful to outsource.

The vendor needs access to several internal systems. Requirements change every week. Performance is hard to measure. Employees must explain context repeatedly. Disputes arise over whether a task was inside scope. The buyer spends so much time managing the vendor that someone eventually says, “We should just hire a person.”

The frustration is not primarily the price of the service.

It is the interface.

An agent can make the interface cheaper.

It can package context.

Translate internal requests into structured work orders.

Track what was asked.

Compare delivery against the agreement.

Maintain a history.

Escalate ambiguous cases.

Rebid routine components.

The vendor can deploy its own agent on the other side.

Suddenly the cost of coordinating across the legal boundary falls.

That should favor outsourcing.

But now change the scene.

A large company has twenty internal teams whose work overlaps. Information is fragmented across documents, chat, tickets, email, databases, and people’s memories. A project stalls because everyone holds a different piece of the state.

An agent that can read across these systems, prepare a common picture, chase missing inputs, maintain decision logs, and remind the right people can make internal coordination cheaper.

That should favor scale.

The same technology pushes in opposite directions.

The mistake is to ask whether agents make companies bigger or smaller in general.

A better question is which boundaries become cheaper to cross.

Some functions may leave the firm while the firm itself grows.

A retailer might outsource more logistics, software, marketing production, and customer-service operations while expanding into more countries and product categories because the orchestration cost fell.

A tiny company might remain tiny in headcount while coordinating hundreds of external services.

A large firm might internalize work it once outsourced because agents make it easier to manage specialized internal capabilities.

A professional-services partnership might become smaller at the bottom and larger at the top, using agentic capacity to support more clients per partner.

The result can be a company that is simultaneously more virtual and more powerful.

Headcount is an increasingly poor measure of organizational reach.

This becomes obvious if we look at the corporation as a bundle of capabilities rather than a census of employees.

A company needs some way to sell.

Some way to collect money.

Some way to account for money.

Some way to satisfy law.

Some way to produce its good.

Some way to serve customers.

Some way to decide.

Historically, many of these capabilities had to be embodied in employees because the alternative was expensive or unreliable. Even outsourced functions required internal people to manage the outsourcing.

Agents can place another layer between capability and employment.

A company can possess a function without possessing a full department.

This is already true because of software-as-a-service and business-process outsourcing. Agents deepen it by making services easier to coordinate and by making software flexible enough to handle more of the irregular work around the service.

The corporation begins to resemble an operating system for contracts.

That sounds like the death of the firm.

It is not.

The operating system still needs a kernel.

Someone decides the objective.

Someone owns the assets.

Someone bears residual risk.

Someone signs.

Someone chooses which data can leave.

Someone decides what must remain proprietary.

Someone answers when the system harms a customer.

Someone controls the credentials.

Someone can fire the vendor.

The firm may become thinner in execution while remaining dense in authority.

This distinction matters because the economic boundary and the legal boundary do not have to move together.

An agent may perform tasks that resemble those of an employee while legally belonging to no workforce at all. It may be software licensed from one provider, running on infrastructure owned by another, connected to tools supplied by several others, acting under credentials ultimately controlled by the customer company.

Where is the activity?

Inside the firm because the agent acts under the firm’s authority?

Outside the firm because the capability is rented?

Both descriptions capture part of the arrangement.

The old make-or-buy question becomes make, buy, rent, invoke, orchestrate, or combine.

This new granularity can produce organizations that are difficult to describe with twentieth-century categories.

A founder uses a model provider for reasoning, a cloud platform for execution, a payment company for transactions, a payroll service for one employee, a fulfillment company for physical goods, an agent for support, another for research, and contractors for exceptional work.

The founder owns almost none of the infrastructure.

Yet the founder coordinates the whole.

Is this a small firm?

By headcount, yes.

By dependency graph, no.

It may sit on top of more external organizational capacity than a factory employing hundreds once could access.

The concept that changes is not size.

It is span.

Agentile firms can reach farther with fewer internal layers.

That creates opportunity and fragility together.

The opportunity is obvious.

A small organization can buy capabilities that once required scale.

It can maintain twenty-four-hour support.

Operate in several languages.

Analyze large datasets.

Monitor compliance.

Run experiments.

Produce marketing variants.

Maintain software.

Search global suppliers.

Prepare legal and financial material for professional review.

The minimum efficient scale for many organizational functions falls.

This is one reason Chapter 10 will consider the one-person corporation.

The fragility is less romantic.

A thin firm can become a tower of dependencies.

The model provider changes a policy.

The cloud service fails.

The payment processor freezes an account.

A vendor API changes.

A platform raises prices.

A critical tool loses access to data.

A security incident forces credentials to be revoked.

The organization that looked wonderfully light discovers that it was not small.

It was externally assembled.

This is an old problem in a new costume.

Outsourcing removes activities from the payroll but not necessarily from the risk surface.

A company that outsources manufacturing still cares whether the factory burns down.

A bank that uses a cloud provider still cares whether systems are available.

A retailer that uses a logistics partner still cares whether packages arrive.

Agentile firms will learn the same lesson about cognitive infrastructure.

If your customer service, analysis, coding, procurement, and scheduling all depend on a small number of external model and cloud providers, the company has not eliminated operational concentration.

It has moved it.

This gives us one counterweight to the idea that low transaction costs automatically produce a paradise of tiny firms buying everything on open markets.

Markets themselves depend on infrastructure.

Search has to work.

Identity has to work.

Contracts have to be enforceable.

Payments have to settle.

Data has to move.

Interfaces have to remain compatible.

Suppliers must be replaceable enough for competition to matter.

When those conditions fail, dependency can become stronger than employment ever was.

Williamson cared about relationship-specific investment partly because it changes bargaining power. If one party makes an investment valuable only within a particular relationship, walking away becomes costly. The threat of opportunistic renegotiation grows.

Agentile systems can create digital versions of specificity.

A company may spend years encoding its processes into one provider’s agent framework.

Its evaluations may be written for one model family.

Its knowledge base may depend on one retrieval architecture.

Its permissions and logs may live in one platform.

Its employees may learn how to supervise one system.

Its customers may interact through one proprietary interface.

Technically, the service is rented.

Economically, switching can be painful.

The market contract begins to feel like an internal dependency without the internal control.

This is why portability and interoperability matter economically, not just technically.

If an agent can move between providers while keeping identity, tools, memory structures, permissions, and evaluation criteria, the market becomes more competitive.

If every agent relationship creates expensive lock-in, firms may respond by internalizing more of the stack.

A falling price in one layer can therefore provoke vertical integration in another.

Companies may rent foundation models but build their own agent harnesses.

Rent infrastructure but own their data layer.

Use external agents for low-risk tasks but internal agents for proprietary workflows.

Buy commodity capability and make the governance layer.

The boundary moves piece by piece.

This is how real organizational change usually happens.

Not one dramatic choice between “employees” and “AI.”

Thousands of make-or-buy decisions are revisited.

Should this queue be internal?

Should this model call be external?

Should this workflow be standardized?

Should this decision require an employee?

Should this exception go to a specialist?

Should this capability be shared across the company?

Should this vendor be replaced?

Should the agent have direct authority or only recommend?

Each answer redraws a small segment of the firm.

The aggregate becomes the new organization.

There is another force pushing toward larger firms.

Data.

An agent becomes more useful when it has relevant context. Large organizations often possess enormous stores of proprietary information: customer histories, operational data, contracts, process records, research, internal decisions, and domain-specific feedback.

If agents can turn those stores into useful action, incumbents may gain an advantage from information accumulated over decades.

The same technology that lets a startup rent capabilities cheaply may let a giant enterprise activate knowledge that was previously trapped in silos.

Scale can become more manageable and more valuable at the same time.

A conglomerate with poor coordination is merely large.

A conglomerate whose agents can connect knowledge across divisions may discover economies of scope that were previously theoretical.

This possibility should make us cautious about predictions of universal organizational atomization.

Digital technologies have produced both tiny firms and giant platforms.

The internet lowered the cost of reaching customers, yet some of the largest companies in history grew on top of it.

Cloud computing lowered the cost of starting software companies, yet cloud infrastructure itself became concentrated.

Search lowered the cost of finding information, while concentrating enormous value in the systems that organized attention.

Lower coordination cost can democratize participation and reward scale simultaneously.

Agents may do the same.

The smallest firm becomes more capable.

The largest firm becomes more governable.

Which effect dominates will vary by industry.

Industries built around local physical service may see networks of small operators gain administrative capacity without becoming giant employers.

Industries built around data and software may see scale advantages intensify because agentic systems can exploit large datasets and distribute decisions globally.

Highly regulated industries may retain large legal entities because accountability, capital requirements, and licensing do not fragment as easily as tasks.

Creative and professional work may see more fluid constellations of small principals, external specialists, and synthetic execution.

The outcome is not “the end of the corporation.”

It is more organizational variety.

That is economically consequential because firms are not passive containers for work. They determine how risks and rewards are distributed.

An employee receives wages and protections under an employment relationship.

A contractor bears more of her own risk.

A software provider captures subscription or usage revenue.

A platform can take a toll.

An owner receives residual profit.

A lender receives interest.

When a task moves across the boundary of the firm, the legal and economic claims attached to that task can change.

Replacing an internal function with agentic services therefore changes more than productivity.

It can move income from payroll to software spend.

Move risk from employer to vendor.

Move bargaining power from worker to platform.

Move intellectual property across contractual boundaries.

Move accountability without moving public expectations.

These transitions will be fought over because the boundary of the firm is also a boundary of rights.

A company may prefer a synthetic service partly because it is flexible.

Society may ask what happens to the protections that were bundled with employment.

A worker may use personal agents to regain leverage.

A regulator may decide that some forms of delegated decision remain the firm’s responsibility regardless of who or what performed them.

The legal boundary can resist the operational boundary.

That resistance can be healthy.

A bank should not be able to outsource its duty to customers by saying an agent made the decision.

A hospital should not escape responsibility for care by pointing to a vendor.

A company should not gain a magical liability shield because a probabilistic system occupied the middle of a workflow.

The principal will remain important.

So will the chain of delegation.

This is where the corporate map becomes a graph.

Traditional organization charts show people and reporting lines.

Agentile organization maps will need to show humans, agents, vendors, models, tools, data stores, credentials, approval gates, and external counterparties.

The relevant question will not simply be, “Who reports to whom?”

It will be, “Who can cause what to happen?”

That graph may describe the real firm better than the payroll does.

It will also reveal something Coase would recognize.

The firm is an answer to coordination costs.

Change those costs and the answer changes.

Agents are changing them on both sides of the border.

They make markets easier to use because searching, specifying, monitoring, and switching can become cheaper.

They make hierarchies easier to run because information, supervision, memory, and routing can become cheaper.

They make new hybrid arrangements possible because a capability can be partly internal, partly rented, and partly delegated to software.

The future firm is therefore unlikely to have one new shape.

It will have a more mobile boundary.

Some companies will become remarkably small in human terms.

Some will become enormous and strangely thin.

Some will pull critical agent infrastructure inside.

Some will assemble themselves almost entirely from services.

Some will discover that the cheapest-looking arrangement is brittle and rebuild internal capacity.

The experimentation will be continuous because the underlying prices will keep changing.

One way to see the editability is to follow a single function through time.

Take payroll.

A century ago, payroll inside a large company could mean clerks, time books, pay envelopes, arithmetic, supervisors, and a physical chain of records. Later it meant specialized internal systems and payroll departments. Then software vendors and service bureaus absorbed more of the function. Cloud payroll platforms let small companies purchase capabilities that once demanded internal administration.

Yet even modern payroll leaves an edge of irregular work.

A worker changes country.

A bonus has unusual tax treatment.

A manager enters the wrong effective date.

A contractor becomes an employee.

A benefit deduction does not reconcile.

A jurisdiction changes a rule.

The commodity center of the function is outsourced; the messy edge returns to people.

This is exactly where agents can alter the boundary again.

An agent can collect context, inspect documentation, compare records, identify which issue is routine, and prepare the exception for a human or vendor specialist. The external service becomes easier to use because the buyer’s coordination burden falls.

Now imagine the same mechanism across finance, HR, legal operations, sales operations, procurement, IT, and support.

The corporation does not outsource itself in one decision.

Its edge becomes perforated.

Work passes through the boundary more frequently and in smaller pieces.

That increases the importance of interface quality.

A bad interface is a tax on every crossing.

If the agent must translate between incompatible systems, repeatedly rebuild context, or ask humans for missing permissions, the supposed market advantage evaporates. If providers expose clean, stable, machine-readable interfaces with clear rights and predictable semantics, outside capability becomes much easier to compose.

Standards can therefore influence firm structure.

This is not new. Shipping containers changed which organizations could participate in global trade by standardizing a physical interface. Internet protocols changed which networks could connect. Accounting standards make financial statements easier to compare. Barcodes reduced the cost of coordinating retail supply chains.

Agent protocols, identity standards, tool schemas, and permission systems may become a similar institutional layer.

They lower the cost of crossing organizational borders.

But standards also create strategic contests.

Who defines the schema?

Who controls the identity?

Who certifies the agent?

Who can revoke it?

Which provider sees the transaction data?

Which logs are portable?

What happens when two agents disagree about the meaning of a contract or permission?

These questions sound technical until enough money moves through them.

Then they become industrial policy.

The company that owns a dominant agent interface may influence where the firm ends for everyone using it.

This makes one final point important.

Firm boundaries are not determined by efficiency alone.

Power matters.

History matters.

Law matters.

Managers can prefer control even when outsourcing looks cheaper. Vendors can price below cost to create dependency. Regulators can require functions to remain accountable to licensed entities. Workers can resist changes that strip employment protections. Customers can prefer dealing with a company that visibly owns the service it promises.

Coase gave us a way to ask why an activity sits on one side of the corporate boundary.

Williamson gave us richer reasons to care about adaptation, conflict, and specific investment.

Agents add a new set of changing prices to that old choice.

But they do not turn the choice into arithmetic.

Organizations are political and legal objects as well as production systems.

The editable firm will still be negotiated by humans who care about more than efficiency.

That may be fortunate.

A world in which every relationship instantly dissolved into the cheapest available contract would be efficient only under an impossibly thin definition of human life.

Firms also create continuity.

Memory.

Training.

Identity.

Mutual obligation.

Places where people learn each other well enough to coordinate without pricing every favor.

Agents can make formal transactions cheaper. They may also reveal how much value sits in relationships that were never fully contractual in the first place.

The boundary will move.

Some of what remains inside will remain because we finally learned what the boundary was protecting.

This is the durable prediction.

When the price of coordination changes, organizations renegotiate what they are.

The agentile economy does not abolish the firm.

It makes the firm editable.
