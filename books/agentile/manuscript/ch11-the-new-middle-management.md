# The New Middle Management

The middle manager has never had a good publicist.

In cartoons, the role is a layer.

In restructurings, it is an expense.

In corporate folklore, the middle manager schedules the meeting that could have been an email, forwards the email that could have been a decision, and asks for an update because someone above asked for an update.

This caricature survives because some organizations deserve it.

It also hides what hierarchy is for.

A large firm contains more information than any one person can hold. Customers disagree. Projects slip. Employees need decisions. Specialists speak different professional languages. Headquarters has objectives that arrive locally as ambiguous instructions. Local teams discover facts that headquarters does not know.

Somebody has to compress.

The middle manager has historically been one of the compression layers.

What happened?

What matters?

Who needs to know?

What can be decided here?

What has to go up?

Who should talk to whom?

Which exception is real?

Which complaint is merely loud?

Which deadline is movable?

Which promise has become dangerous?

That work can look like bureaucracy because much of it is communication.

It can also be the mechanism that lets a large organization behave like one organization.

Agents attack the communication cost.

They can summarize projects.

They can read tickets.

They can compile metrics.

They can draft status reports.

They can schedule.

They can route.

They can remind.

They can compare plans with actuals.

They can surface anomalies before the weekly meeting.

A manager whose job is mostly to carry information from one layer to another should be nervous.

A manager whose job is to decide what the information means may become more important.

This distinction is older than artificial intelligence.

Research on information technology and firm organization has long found that different technologies pull hierarchy in different directions. Nicholas Bloom, Luis Garicano, Raffaella Sadun, and John Van Reenen showed that technologies which make information easier for lower-level workers to acquire can be associated with more autonomy and wider spans of control, while technologies that make communication from the center easier can support centralization.

Technology does not simply flatten.

It changes the cost of knowing and the cost of directing.

Agents change both.

They can give a worker more information without waiting for a manager.

They can also give headquarters a cheaper way to direct, monitor, and evaluate work far below it.

The same system that empowers the edge can strengthen the center.

This is why the easy prediction that AI eliminates middle management is unsatisfying.

Some layers may shrink.

Some spans may widen.

Some decisions may move downward because local actors have better machine assistance.

Other decisions may move upward because executives can see and steer more of the organization directly.

And some middle managers will be replaced by a new kind of middle management that does not resemble the old job description very much.

The emerging role sits between intention and a population of human and machine actors.

It is less about passing messages.

It is more about allocating judgment.

Imagine a customer-support organization with ten thousand cases a day.

Before agents, frontline employees handle the cases, supervisors answer difficult questions, managers watch service levels, and directors adjust policy.

Now agents resolve the ordinary cases.

They read account history, interpret policy, draft or send responses, issue routine credits, and escalate when confidence is low or a rule prevents action.

The queue arriving at the supervisor changes.

There are fewer ordinary cases.

The remaining cases are stranger.

Fraud.

Threats.

Policy contradictions.

High-value customers.

Repeated failure.

Edge cases involving law or safety.

Situations where the customer is technically wrong and commercially important.

The supervisor may manage fewer people.

The supervisor may manage far more consequences.

This is a recurring pattern in automation.

The routine disappears first.

The residue becomes the job.

That can make the role more skilled and more exhausting at the same time.

A traditional manager can learn the business partly by observing a representative sample of ordinary work.

An agentile manager may see mostly the tails.

If every easy decision is automated, human experience becomes statistically weird.

The person lives in the exception distribution.

This creates a new management problem.

How do you understand a system when you personally see only what the system could not handle?

You need sampling.

You need audits.

You need aggregate measures.

You need random review of successful cases, not only failed ones.

You need to inspect the quiet center as well as the noisy edge.

Otherwise management develops a distorted picture of reality.

Every customer seems furious because only furious customers reach a person.

Every transaction seems suspicious because ordinary ones pass automatically.

Every agent seems unreliable because the manager only sees the cases it escalated.

Or the reverse: every dashboard looks healthy because failures are being quietly resolved elsewhere.

The new middle manager needs statistical humility.

That is not a traditional line on the management curriculum.

Neither is agent evaluation.

A human team is usually evaluated through some combination of outcomes, observation, peer judgment, customer feedback, and manager experience.

An agentic workflow adds new questions.

Did the model choose the right tool?

Did it retrieve the right policy?

Did it stay inside its authority?

Did it escalate the right cases?

Did it fail safely?

Did a new model version change behavior?

Did performance improve because the system became better or because people quietly took over more work?

Are the evaluations measuring the actual business or an artificial test?

Somebody owns these questions.

That somebody may sit in middle management even if the title sounds technical.

The role is part operations, part product management, part risk, part coaching, and part systems design.

It manages a mixed workforce.

Human.

Model.

Vendor.

Workflow.

Policy.

Credential.

The organization chart is no longer enough to describe the unit.

Chapter 5 called attention to the permission graph: who or what can act on whose behalf.

The new middle manager lives inside that graph.

The manager can no longer understand capacity merely by counting direct reports.

A team of six people may command fifty agents.

A team of fifty people may use one shared system.

A contractor may control an integration that determines whether the whole workflow functions.

A model provider may change a capability overnight.

A policy update may alter a thousand decisions without changing headcount at all.

Management becomes partly the stewardship of a production system.

Factories learned this long ago.

A plant manager does not manage only workers.

The plant manager manages workers, machines, maintenance, quality, throughput, inventory, safety, scheduling, suppliers, and failure modes.

White-collar management has often felt less like production engineering because much of the production system lived inside people's heads.

Agents make the system visible.

That can improve management.

It can also invite a terrible mistake.

Once work becomes measurable, organizations often begin managing the measure.

A manager sees agent-resolution rate and pushes it higher.

The system escalates fewer cases.

The metric improves.

Customer outcomes deteriorate.

A manager sees cost per transaction and reduces verification.

Errors arrive later, outside the measurement window.

A manager sees response time and removes a pause that was preventing fraud.

Speed rises.

So does loss.

Goodhart's law does not become obsolete because the dashboard was generated by AI.

The new middle manager needs to know where optimization should stop.

That is one reason decision rights matter.

MIT researchers studying how companies allocate decisions between humans and AI have recently emphasized two dimensions that sound almost embarrassingly simple: ambiguity and risk.

They matter because the cheapest decision-maker is not always the right decision-maker.

A low-risk, low-ambiguity decision is natural territory for automation.

A high-risk, high-ambiguity decision is natural territory for human judgment.

The interesting work lies between.

A decision can be unambiguous and dangerous.

Approve a payment above a threshold.

Delete an account.

Publish a legal filing.

The rules may be clear while the downside of a mistake is high.

Another decision can be ambiguous and low stakes.

Choose the order of internal agenda items.

Draft three names for a meeting room.

The agent can be allowed to improvise because being wrong is cheap.

The organization needs more than a binary human-in-the-loop policy.

It needs a portfolio of decision rights.

Frame.

Recommend.

Act.

Review.

Escalate.

Learn.

The manager's job becomes deciding how these rights are distributed.

This is why some middle management can become more valuable as the number of human reports falls.

The manager may have fewer people to instruct and more autonomous capacity to govern.

Management leverage rises.

So does the cost of a bad objective.

A mediocre instruction given to one employee wastes an afternoon.

A mediocre instruction deployed across one thousand agent runs can become policy.

The quality of management is multiplied by the execution system.

So is managerial error.

That changes promotion.

Traditional organizations often promote the best individual contributor into management.

The logic is understandable. The person knows the work and has demonstrated competence.

The result is famously mixed because doing work and designing work are different skills.

Agents widen the gap.

The strongest analyst may be excellent at solving cases and poor at specifying what makes a case good.

The best salesperson may close deals through intuition that is difficult to turn into rules.

The best lawyer may spot risk instantly and struggle to explain the pattern in a way a system can use.

The new manager must externalize judgment.

What do you notice?

What are the red flags?

When do you stop?

What evidence changes your mind?

Which rules are real and which are folklore?

How do you know when an exception deserves to become a policy?

This is apprenticeship turned inside out.

Historically the junior watches the senior and slowly internalizes tacit knowledge.

Now the organization also asks the senior to make parts of that tacit knowledge legible to machines.

Not all of it will become legible.

That matters.

The danger of operationalizing judgment is confusing what can be written down with what is important.

A policy can encode common cases beautifully and miss the moral intuition that causes a good manager to violate the policy once a year.

The system may then become consistent in exactly the wrong way.

Middle management remains valuable partly because institutions need sanctioned inconsistency.

Someone has to recognize when the rule and the purpose have separated.

This is the oldest bureaucratic problem.

Agents can make it more acute because machines are good at applying rules at scale.

A human clerk once let an imperfect form pass because the intent was obvious.

A manager waived a fee because the customer had been through something terrible.

A supervisor ignored a target because pursuing it would have created a larger failure.

Sometimes this is favoritism.

Sometimes it is judgment.

Organizations spend enormous effort trying to distinguish the two.

The new middle manager becomes the exception governor.

That title will not appear on many business cards.

The function will exist.

There is an economic reason.

Middle management is expensive.

Jonas Hjort, Hannes Malmberg, and Todd Schoellman have shown how the relative cost of middle managers can constrain the adoption of management-intensive modern firm structures in poorer countries. Their empirical setting is not AI. The larger lesson is that management cost affects what kinds of organizations are viable.

If agents lower some coordination costs, organizational forms can change.

A firm may become viable at a scale that previously required too much supervisory overhead.

A small operator may coordinate a geographically distributed business.

A nonprofit may run a more complex program without building a large administrative layer.

A founder may reach customers in many countries while maintaining a small human team.

This is a real democratizing possibility.

It has a mirror image.

Large firms can use the same technology to become even larger.

If coordination is cheaper, the diseconomies of scale arrive later.

An executive can receive synthesized information from more units.

Policies can propagate faster.

Central systems can monitor compliance continuously.

The organization can absorb acquisitions without adding proportional administrative headcount.

Cheap management does not imply small firms.

It implies that management cost is less of a brake.

What happens next depends on market power, capital, regulation, technology, and the other limits to scale.

The empirical evidence on AI and hierarchy is already suggestive but not final.

Work by Tania Babina, Anastassia Fedyk, Alex He, and James Hodson finds that firms investing more heavily in AI have been associated with more educated and specialized workforces and flatter hierarchies, including lower shares of workers in middle and senior management.

Association is not destiny.

The firms adopting AI are different in many ways.

The technologies in the data are broader than today's agents.

The observed reorganization occurred before much of the current agentic wave.

Still, the direction is plausible.

Information processing gets cheaper.

Some layers compress.

The mistake would be stopping the story there.

Flattening can remove a layer from the org chart while moving its work into software, standards, metrics, and governance teams.

The hierarchy becomes less visible, not necessarily less hierarchical.

A worker may have no human supervisor nearby and still operate inside tighter machine-defined constraints than before.

Every action can be logged.

Every metric can be compared.

Every deviation can trigger a message.

Every workflow can require a permission.

This is management without a manager standing in the room.

The organization can flatten socially and centralize technically.

That possibility deserves attention because autonomy is not the same as absence of supervision.

A delivery driver with no manager in the passenger seat can still be directed minute by minute by software.

A warehouse worker can receive tasks from a system rather than a foreman.

An office worker can be evaluated by machine-generated activity measures.

Agents can be used to empower people.

They can also be used to intensify control.

The design is not predetermined.

This makes the politics of middle management more interesting than a headcount forecast.

The middle manager has often served as a buffer.

Between executive ambition and operational reality.

Between policy and exception.

Between a customer and a rigid process.

Between an employee and an abstract metric.

A good manager absorbs contradiction.

A bad manager transmits pressure.

When the layer disappears, the contradiction goes somewhere.

It may move to the worker.

It may move to the customer.

It may move into software.

It may move upward to a smaller group of senior decision-makers.

Organizations should not celebrate removing a layer until they know what work the layer was doing.

This is the same principle that applied to queues.

Friction can be waste.

Friction can also contain function.

Management can be bureaucracy.

Management can also be institutional memory, interpretation, training, mercy, and accountability.

Agents make it possible to unbundle the role.

Status collection can be automated.

Scheduling can be automated.

Routine approvals can be automated.

Basic coaching can be augmented.

Exception detection can be automated.

The remaining human function becomes clearer.

Set direction.

Allocate decision rights.

Resolve ambiguity.

Coach judgment.

Design feedback.

Protect standards that metrics cannot see.

Take responsibility when the system fails.

There is also a change in the social contract of management.

People do not experience a manager only as a routing system.

A manager gives permission to care about one thing instead of another.

A manager notices that somebody is overloaded.

A manager tells a nervous employee that a mistake is survivable.

A manager explains why a strategy changed.

A manager can defend a team against a target that makes no sense.

A manager can also humiliate, hoard information, play favorites, and turn uncertainty into fear.

Agents will automate pieces of both good and bad management.

A system can notice workload imbalance earlier than a person.

It can remind a manager that one employee has received no feedback.

It can summarize a policy consistently.

It can surface promotion disparities.

It can also make surveillance cheap.

It can score every message.

It can infer sentiment.

It can turn normal variation in working style into a continuous stream of management signals.

The cost of observing work is falling along with the cost of doing work.

That creates temptation.

Once a company can measure something, someone will propose measuring it.

Once the measure exists, somebody will propose attaching a target.

The manager then has a new duty: defend the organization from its own observability.

Not every signal deserves action.

Not every deviation is a defect.

People need room to experiment, recover, complain, think slowly, and have an unproductive afternoon without generating a permanent risk score.

This is not softness.

It is capacity management for humans.

A machine can run continuously until cost or reliability says stop.

A person cannot.

The mixed workforce therefore contains two different kinds of resource.

Machine capacity is elastic but externally metered.

Human capacity is embodied, path-dependent, and damaged by chronic overload.

The manager who treats them identically will optimize the human team into failure.

This may become one of the most valuable distinctions in agentile management.

Give machines the volume.

Give people the ambiguity.

But do not give people only ambiguity.

A job made entirely of escalation, conflict, edge cases, and responsibility can become intolerable.

Routine work is not merely waste. It can provide rhythm, mastery, confidence, and recovery between difficult decisions.

A thoughtful manager may deliberately preserve some ordinary work for humans even when an agent could perform it cheaper.

That can look inefficient in a spreadsheet.

It may be efficient over the lifetime of a workforce.

Organizations already accept similar trade-offs in training. A junior doctor performs work a senior could do faster because practice creates future competence. An apprentice takes longer because the firm is producing skill as well as output.

Agentile firms will have to decide where humans still practice.

Otherwise the company can become excellent at consuming expert judgment and terrible at creating new experts.

That is a management responsibility no model benchmark will capture for them.

This is not the death of middle management.

It is a narrower and more consequential version of it.

The title may change.

Agent operations lead.

Workflow owner.

AI governance manager.

Decision architect.

Business systems director.

Exception manager.

Or simply manager.

The name matters less than the location in the economic system.

Someone stands between broad intention and distributed execution.

That position is ancient.

The factory had foremen.

The army had officers.

The railroad had dispatchers.

The corporation had managers.

The agentile firm will still need people and systems that turn goals into coordinated action.

What changes is the ratio.

One manager can govern more execution.

One bad manager can damage more execution.

One good manager can make a small team behave like a much larger institution.

The span of control widens.

The span of consequence widens with it.

The new middle manager is not the person in the middle of the org chart.

It is whoever owns the boundary between what the system can decide and what still deserves a human mind.
