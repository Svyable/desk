# A New Force

The first hire who was not hired did not look like a new factor of production. It looked like software, which was part of the disguise.

Economies are full of technologies that enter through an old category and then force the category to stretch. The electric motor first looked like another way to turn a shaft; factories eventually reorganized around distributed power rather than one central drive train. The computer looked like a faster calculator before organizations built databases, networks, software industries, and businesses that could not exist without computation. The internet looked like faster communication before it became a market, a publication system, a distribution network, and part of the operating system of institutions.

Agentic software is arriving through the same narrow door. At first it summarizes, drafts, answers, and helps. Then it receives a tool, an account, memory, permission, a budget, a queue, perhaps another agent. The behavior changes before the accounting category does.

That is the agentile force: intention made delegable to software. The system does not need consciousness or personhood to matter economically. It needs to be able to take a goal, continue across several steps, use tools, make bounded decisions, recover from some failures, and return with an outcome without requiring a human to narrate every move.

The useful question is therefore not whether this is “really” labor or “really” capital. The machinery of older economics still applies. What changes is the price and shape of delegated action. When that price falls, organizations can afford more delegation, and the consequences travel into firm boundaries, job bundles, management, queues, markets, ownership, verification, and public capacity.

The easiest way to follow the change is to follow scarcity. Technology rarely abolishes it. Cheap communication made filtering more valuable. Cheap storage made organization harder. Cheap computation increased the value of choosing what to compute. Agentic software makes some execution cheaper, so the pressure moves toward goals, permissions, verification, trust, ownership, physical bottlenecks, judgment, legitimacy, and responsibility.

That is a more useful map than a single employment forecast. A general technology does several things at once: one task becomes cheaper, demand may expand, a complementary occupation may become more valuable, an apprenticeship path may weaken, a small company may become viable, a large company may coordinate more cheaply, a local wage may become a payment to remote infrastructure, and a regulator or fraudster may both acquire more capacity. Contradictory effects are not evidence that nothing can be said. They are evidence that the mechanism is broader than replacement.

The first pressure appears at the minimum size of organization. Institutions historically required people because action required people. A founder needed somebody to answer customers, keep books, research the market, coordinate vendors, and maintain systems. A town needed clerks. A nonprofit needed administrators. Large organizations could afford specialized labor and managerial coordination that small ones could not.

Agents lower some of those thresholds. One person can rent fragments of an institution before hiring its staff. That does not provide the institution's capital, reputation, regulatory standing, relationships, or accumulated judgment, but it can provide pieces of operating capacity. This is why the one-person corporation matters even if no one-person corporation becomes the largest company on earth. The form reveals that the minimum apparatus required to attempt organized economic action is changing.

The boundary of the firm moves for the same reason. Companies exist partly because markets are expensive to use. Finding someone, explaining the task, negotiating terms, monitoring quality, protecting information, and resolving failure all create costs. Agents can lower some of those costs outside the firm while also lowering the cost of internal coordination. Small firms can buy more capability; large firms can coordinate more complexity. Outsourcing can increase in one layer while integration increases in another. The direction depends on what remains difficult to contract across: liability, unique data, infrastructure, trust, relationships, regulatory standing, or control.

Labor changes more awkwardly because a job is not a sack of tasks. It is a human institution that bundles work with income, benefits, training, status, relationships, bargaining, and responsibility. An agent can perform pieces of that bundle without inheriting any of those obligations. Enough task substitution can still change the job dramatically. The role may shrink, become more skilled, become more miserable because only exceptions remain, or become more productive because routine work disappears. Demand may expand enough to create more work elsewhere. None of those outcomes is encoded in the model itself.

This is why entry-level work deserves particular attention. Routine tasks often produce both today's output and tomorrow's expert. A junior accountant learns what normal looks like through reconciliations. A young lawyer learns which clauses matter by drafting and comparing ordinary language. A new engineer learns a system by fixing bugs that look trivial from above. If agents remove the cheapest practice, institutions have to decide whether they are also removing the training path. Preserving drudgery for its own sake would be foolish. Preserving the production of judgment is not.

Wages matter alongside employment because productive capacity has owners. If useful cognition arrives increasingly through capital-like infrastructure, then some gains can flow to model providers, cloud companies, chip suppliers, data centers, application firms, and the businesses that adopt them. Competition may push gains toward customers. Scarce complementary workers may capture some through wages. New firms, pensions, savings, and broad ownership can spread the gains. Concentrated infrastructure can do the opposite. The technology creates a surplus; institutions and bargaining decide who can claim it.

Management changes because part of management has always been communication technology. Information moves upward, instructions move downward, status is compressed, exceptions are interpreted, and work is allocated. Agents make some of that routing cheaper. What remains is more revealing: setting the objective, choosing the metric, defining the decision boundary, protecting the apprentice, deciding which friction buys safety, and taking responsibility when the system behaves exactly as instructed and the instruction turns out to be wrong.

A manager may therefore supervise far more execution without supervising far more people. That is real leverage. It is also a way to propagate one bad objective through a larger surface. The mixed workforce needs managers who know what they are delegating, what evidence counts as success, which actions are reversible, and where a human should still be able to interrupt the machine.

Markets change when both sides acquire software. A buyer can keep searching after a person would reasonably stop. A seller can quote and negotiate at a scale that once required human time. Search, comparison, monitoring, and parts of negotiation get cheaper. That can increase competition where switching is actually possible. It can also strengthen the platform that represents the buyer, ranks the offers, supplies the protocol, or sees both sides of the transaction.

The important market question becomes less cinematic than “agents trading with agents.” Who represents the principal well? Who controls the ranking? Who leaks information? Who can walk away? Which merchant is legible to the buyer agent, and which buyer is trusted by the seller? A protocol can lower transaction costs while the trust layer above it becomes a new gatekeeper. Open roads can still lead through tollbooths.

Verification may be the least glamorous part of the transition and one of the most important. Cheap generation creates more candidate actions than institutions can inspect naively. A machine can draft code, contracts, research, payments, claims, offers, and public decisions. The economically relevant question is not how cheaply it can produce the first result. It is how cheaply the whole system can produce a result that is reliable enough to use.

That includes tests, logs, permissions, provenance, monitoring, review, rollback, and evidence about what happened. Software agents already benefit from an unusually mature environment of version control, test suites, sandboxes, staged deployment, and telemetry. Other domains have weaker answer keys. A strategy memo does not compile. A legal interpretation can be fluent and wrong in a way that surfaces months later. A customer apology can be linguistically perfect and commercially disastrous.

So verification cost belongs in the productivity calculation. An agent that performs a task for ten cents and requires five dollars of expert review is not cheaper than one that costs thirty cents and needs fifty cents of review. A system that is slightly less accurate but fails visibly and reversibly can be safer than one that is impressive on average and occasionally catastrophic. The relevant unit is not generation. It is a completed, attributable, recoverable outcome.

This logic becomes more serious when the state acquires agents. Governments are full of queues, records, eligibility rules, inspections, appeals, filings, and administrative work that agents can help process. That can make public service substantially better. It can also make public power easier to exercise.

The distinction matters because some friction is a right. A document should not sit untouched for six weeks because no one opened it. A notice period before property is seized is different. An appeal from a benefits denial is not merely redundant review. Public-comment periods, evidentiary records, and the ability to know why the government acted can slow a process while protecting legitimacy. Agentic administration should remove accidental delay without confusing due process with latency.

Public systems also carry a special dependency problem. A company can become uncomfortably dependent on one model provider; a government can become operationally dependent on a private system that helps administer taxes, benefits, regulation, or other public authority. The state therefore needs to retain its policy, records, evaluations, authorization rules, and failure history in forms that survive a vendor change. Exit is not merely procurement hygiene when sovereign capability is at stake.

Then the software meets the material world. A transformer still takes time to manufacture. A patient has one body. A courtroom has finite hearing time. A semiconductor fab requires equipment, clean rooms, power, materials, and years of investment. A data center needs electricity and land. Agents can discover and schedule around those constraints faster; they cannot wish them away. As cognitive coordination becomes cheaper, the stubborn physical bottleneck can become more valuable.

Time belongs in the same category. Some waiting is neglect. Some waiting creates a second chance to notice an irreversible mistake. A cooling-off period, another signature, a staged rollout, a clinical trial, a judicial process, or a night's sleep before sending the angry message all impose delay for reasons that are not captured by throughput. An agentile institution should ask what the delay protects before optimizing it away.

None of this requires settling whether machines understand, feel, deserve rights, or count as minds. Those questions may matter enormously, but the economic transition does not wait for them. A corporation does not need a theory of machine consciousness before deciding whether an agent may access the bank account. Capability can become institutionally consequential while ontology remains disputed.

The harder scarcity is the objective itself. An agent can pursue a goal through many steps. A system of agents can divide the work. An organization can measure progress. None of that tells us which goal deserves pursuit. “Reduce fraud,” “increase engagement,” “accelerate approvals,” or “maximize growth” can each be sensible until the metric starts consuming the purpose it was supposed to serve.

Agents raise the cost of a bad objective because they can execute it efficiently. This makes judgment less mystical and more operational. What evidence would change the objective? Which side effects matter? What may the system trade away? Who is allowed to stop it? Those questions belong in the design of the work, not in an ethics appendix added after deployment.

Responsibility becomes more valuable for the same reason. A machine can draft an answer and a person can sign. The signature is no longer payment for typing. It is payment for bearing consequence. That can improve professional work when the signer can actually inspect what matters. It becomes dangerous when humans are asked to rubber-stamp volumes they cannot meaningfully review. Responsibility has to scale with consequence, not merely appear somewhere in the workflow.

Economic analysis also has a boundary. People do not exist to supply a factor of production. A child is not a future unit of labor. A friendship is not an unmonetized transaction. A citizen is not only a taxpayer. If machines become better at a task, humans do not need to prove their worth by locating another task machines cannot perform. Economic value and human value are different categories.

Work has supplied income, but also structure, status, social contact, mastery, and usefulness. If agents change work, the institutional question is therefore larger than how to replace a wage. It includes whether people can still gain skill, own productive assets, form businesses, move between institutions, participate in public life, and live with dignity while more machine capability surrounds them. Technology can increase the resources available for those things. It does not allocate them by itself.

The agentile force is not destiny. The automobile did not determine the city before planners, firms, governments, lenders, and households made choices around it. The internet did not predetermine today's platform economy. Agentic software opens another design space in which permissions, memory portability, open protocols, contestable markets, appeal, apprenticeship, surveillance limits, pacing, and data rights can look like implementation details until enough of them harden into an institution.

That is why the present matters. A personal agent's memory does not have to become captive to one provider. Delegated authority does not have to expand as it passes down a chain. Public agents can preserve evidence for consequential decisions. Companies can measure human verification time alongside compute. Professions can preserve the experiences that produce judgment even when machines perform more of the underlying task. None of this requires waiting for some distant superintelligence. These choices arrive in procurement forms, product settings, contracts, permissions, and checkboxes.

The word *agentile* is useful only if it helps us notice this change. AI names a technical field too broad to describe the economic capability. Automation suggests fixed procedures. Software names the substrate. Labor names a human institution. Capital names ownership. Agency names the missing property: the capacity to act on behalf of a principal. *Agentile* names the economy's growing ability to supply some of that property through machines.

The term should be used lightly. The economy does not need another ideology. It needs a way to notice when an old assumption has stopped holding.

The most important signal is not the model price but the all-in cost of delegation. Watch which tasks cross the threshold from not worth doing to worth assigning. Watch where the resulting queues move. Watch what companies rent instead of hire, which capabilities become divisible, and which bottlenecks become more valuable because everything around them became easier. Watch ownership, verification cost, switching costs, and who gets an agent versus who merely has to face one.

A modest system embedded in payroll, procurement, customer service, logistics, or public administration may have more economic consequence than a spectacular model no institution trusts enough to use. Deployment is the bridge from capability to history.

Human beings built economies by giving instructions to other human beings and creating institutions that could carry those instructions across time. Now a portion of that delegated action can be instantiated in software, copied, scaled, paused, restricted, given a budget, and asked to negotiate with another instance. The consequences begin before the machines become human.

They begin when the machines become useful delegates.
