# A New Force

The first hire who was not hired did not look like a new factor of production.

It looked like software.

That was the disguise.

Economies are full of technologies that do not initially resemble the thing they eventually become.

The early electric motor looked like a replacement for the steam engine. It took time for factories to reorganize around distributed power rather than one central shaft.

The early computer looked like a faster calculator. It took time for organizations to discover databases, networks, software industries, digital media, and businesses that could not exist without computation.

The early internet looked like faster communication. It became a market, a publication system, a distribution network, a social environment, a surveillance surface, and part of the operating system of modern institutions.

Agentic software is arriving through the same narrow door.

At first it is an assistant.

It summarizes.

It drafts.

It answers.

It helps.

Then it receives a tool.

Then an account.

Then a memory.

Then permission.

Then a budget.

Then a queue.

Then another agent.

The language has to change because the economic behavior changed.

A tool becomes an actor when it can take a goal and continue acting toward it without requiring a human to specify every step.

That is the agentile force.

Not intelligence in the abstract.

Not consciousness.

Not a claim about personhood.

Not a new species hiding inside the server rack.

It is a productive capability: intention made delegable to software.

That capability is sufficiently different from ordinary software to alter old economic boundaries.

It is sufficiently continuous with older automation that we do not need mystical economics to understand it.

Both statements matter.

The mistake on one side is to say nothing new is happening because machines have always automated tasks.

The mistake on the other is to say everything old has become irrelevant because the machines can reason.

History is usually less obliging.

New forces enter old institutions.

The institutions bend.

The force acquires shape through the bending.

We can now state the argument of this book without the excitement of the opening chapters.

When delegated action becomes cheaper, organizations can afford to delegate more.

When they delegate more, the cost structure of coordination changes.

When coordination changes, firm boundaries, job bundles, managerial spans, queues, markets, ownership, verification, and public capacity can change with it.

The agentile force is therefore not one labor-saving machine.

It is a reduction in the price of agency.

That reduction has consequences wherever the old price of agency mattered.

The consequences are easiest to see by following scarcity.

Economics is often the study of where scarcity goes after technology attacks it.

A faster machine makes production less scarce and distribution more important.

Abundant information makes attention scarce.

Cheap communication makes filtering scarce.

Cheap storage makes organization scarce.

Cheap computation makes good objectives more important.

Agentic software makes one class of execution cheaper.

The scarcity migrates.

Toward goals.

Toward permissions.

Toward verification.

Toward trust.

Toward ownership.

Toward physical bottlenecks.

Toward judgment.

Toward legitimacy.

Toward people willing to accept responsibility for consequential action.

This is the new map.

The map matters more than any forecast of how many jobs disappear.

Job forecasts are attractive because they produce a number.

A number feels like an answer.

But a general technology rearranges the economy through multiple channels at once.

A task becomes cheaper.

The price of a service changes.

Demand changes.

A complementary occupation expands.

A training pathway disappears.

A new company becomes viable.

A large company becomes larger.

A small company gains capabilities it never had.

A local wage becomes a payment to remote infrastructure.

A consumer receives a service that no human was ever going to provide.

A manager governs more work with fewer direct reports.

A regulator can inspect more records.

A fraudster can attempt more fraud.

The aggregate effect arrives through the interaction.

This is why the early data can look contradictory without anyone being dishonest.

AI can raise productivity in experiments and remain shallow in many workplaces.

It can reduce hiring in one task bundle while creating demand elsewhere.

It can flatten some hierarchies and strengthen centralized control.

It can democratize access to expertise while concentrating infrastructure ownership.

It can make markets more competitive for buyers and make the platforms representing buyers more powerful.

It can reduce bureaucracy and increase the state's capacity to enforce bureaucracy.

The contradictions are the subject.

A force is defined partly by what it pushes against.

The first thing the agentile force pushes against is the old minimum size of organization.

Institutions have historically required people because action required people.

A founder needed someone to answer customers.

Then someone to keep books.

Then someone to research the market.

Then someone to coordinate vendors.

Then someone to maintain systems.

A nonprofit needed administrators.

A political campaign needed organizers.

A laboratory needed people to monitor and document.

A town needed clerks.

A large organization enjoyed advantages partly because it could afford specialized labor and managerial coordination.

Agents lower some of these thresholds.

One person can borrow fragments of an institution.

Not the institution's capital.

Not its brand.

Not its regulatory standing.

Not its relationships.

But pieces of its operating capacity.

This is why the one-person corporation matters even if no one-person corporation becomes the world's largest company.

It reveals a change in the minimum apparatus needed to attempt economic action.

A founder can reach farther before hiring.

A professional can serve more clients.

A researcher can coordinate more tools.

A citizen can navigate more bureaucracy.

The effect is not only substitution.

It is entry.

More people can try things.

That increases competition and noise together.

The second thing the force pushes against is the old boundary of the firm.

Companies exist partly because markets are expensive to use.

Finding someone, explaining the task, negotiating terms, monitoring quality, protecting information, and resolving failure create transaction costs.

An internal employee is often easier to coordinate than a new contract every hour.

Agents reduce some market transaction costs and some internal coordination costs at the same time.

This creates no simple prediction about firm size.

Small firms can buy outside capabilities more easily.

Large firms can coordinate internal complexity more cheaply.

Outsourcing can increase.

Vertical integration can increase where data, trust, or infrastructure make integration valuable.

The boundary moves toward whatever remains expensive to contract across.

Trust.

Liability.

Unique data.

Physical assets.

Deep relationships.

Strategic control.

The firm survives.

Its membrane changes permeability.

The third pressure point is labor.

The word worker becomes harder to map onto work.

A job remains a human institution.

It bundles tasks, responsibility, identity, income, benefits, status, training, relationships, and legal protections.

Agentic software can perform pieces of the bundle without inheriting the institution.

This is why “AI replaces jobs” and “AI only replaces tasks” can both obscure the mechanism.

Tasks determine production.

Jobs determine how human beings participate in production.

When task allocation changes enough, the job bundle changes.

Maybe fewer people are needed.

Maybe the job becomes more skilled.

Maybe the job becomes worse because only exceptions remain.

Maybe the machine helps a beginner perform like an experienced worker.

Maybe the entry-level role disappears and the pipeline into expertise weakens.

Maybe demand expands enough to absorb everyone.

The agentile force does not prescribe the outcome.

It changes the feasible set.

Bargaining, demand, institutions, ownership, and time determine the distribution.

This is why wages matter as much as employment.

A society can remain busy while the ownership of productive capacity shifts.

If a growing share of useful cognition arrives through capital-like infrastructure, then claims on capital become more important.

The person who owns the model company, cloud company, chip company, data center, energy source, application firm, or adopting business can receive a different share of the gain from the person whose task was automated.

Competition can push gains toward customers.

Labor scarcity can push gains toward workers.

Taxes can redistribute.

Savings and pensions can broaden ownership.

Profit sharing can share firm-level gains.

New firms can create new fortunes and new employment.

There is no single pipeline from agent capability to inequality.

There is a political economy.

The fourth pressure point is management.

The traditional manager has been partly a communications technology.

Information travels up.

Instructions travel down.

Status gets compressed.

Exceptions are interpreted.

Work is allocated.

People are coached.

Agents make the communication portion cheaper.

This does not make management disappear.

It exposes the parts of management that were not mere communication.

Set the objective.

Define the boundary.

Choose the metric.

Resolve the exception.

Protect the apprentice.

Decide which friction buys safety.

Take responsibility when the system behaves exactly as instructed and the instruction turns out to be wrong.

The new middle manager owns a decision boundary more than an org-chart box.

This can widen spans dramatically.

One person can supervise far more execution.

The productivity gain sounds obvious.

The failure gain is symmetrical.

One bad manager can propagate a bad objective through far more execution.

Management quality becomes leverage.

The fifth pressure point is markets.

The customer acquires software.

The seller acquires software.

Search cost falls.

Quoting cost falls.

Negotiation cost falls.

Monitoring cost falls.

Switching can become easier where switching itself is not locked by contracts, data, hardware, or habit.

The market can become more competitive.

The intermediary representing the buyer can become more powerful.

The seller can personalize more efficiently.

The buyer can compare more efficiently.

The result is not perfect competition.

It is a new arms race in representation.

Who knows the principal better?

Who has more options?

Who leaks less information?

Who can walk away?

Who controls the ranking?

Who controls the protocol?

Who settles the dispute?

The price of agency falls on both sides.

The institutional structure decides who benefits.

The sixth pressure point is verification.

This may be the least glamorous and most important.

The agentile force produces more candidate actions than human institutions were designed to inspect.

Every cheap action creates a potential demand for proof.

Did the agent act under authority?

Did it use the right evidence?

Did it follow policy?

Did it produce the intended outcome?

Can the action be reversed?

Can another person reconstruct it?

The world does not become trustworthy because the model improves.

Trustworthy systems emerge when capability is surrounded by tests, logs, permissions, monitoring, provenance, insurance, reputational stakes, and accountable institutions.

The boring complement wins.

This is familiar from earlier technological revolutions.

Cheap transportation increased the value of logistics.

Mass production increased the value of quality control.

Complex finance increased the value of accounting and audit, even while repeatedly proving those institutions imperfect.

The verification economy grows because scalable action requires scalable trust.

The seventh pressure point is the state.

Governments have always sought administrative capacity.

Agents can supply it.

A government can answer questions, process files, detect anomalies, summarize cases, monitor compliance, and coordinate services more cheaply.

That can make public administration dramatically better.

It can also make public power easier to exercise.

The problem is not government AI in the abstract.

The problem is power with lower execution cost.

Good policy becomes easier to implement.

Bad policy becomes easier to implement.

The value of due process rises because the state can produce decisions faster.

The value of appeal rises because error can scale.

The value of records rises because the human who “made” the decision may have supervised a system rather than touched the case.

The value of citizen-side agents rises because procedural equality depends partly on whether ordinary people can match institutional complexity.

The agentile force raises the return to good governance.

That is not the same as creating good governance.

The eighth pressure point is physical reality.

The software can move at machine speed.

The world cannot.

A house still takes materials and labor.

A transformer takes time to manufacture.

A patient has one body.

A courtroom has finite hearing time.

A cargo ship occupies water.

A semiconductor fab requires equipment, clean rooms, power, materials, and years of investment.

A data center needs electricity and land.

A child requires years to become an adult.

The agentile force can optimize around physical constraints.

It cannot repeal them.

As cognitive coordination becomes cheaper, physical bottlenecks can become more valuable.

A brilliant procurement agent cannot buy a transformer that does not exist.

A perfect scheduling agent cannot create a nurse for tomorrow if training takes years.

A planning agent can discover the bottleneck faster.

The economy still has to build through it.

This is one reason predictions of purely digital abundance are incomplete.

Cheap agency makes the material world more visible as a constraint.

The ninth pressure point is time.

Agents are fast.

Institutions contain reasons to be slow.

Some delays are waste.

Some delays create reflection.

A cooling-off period.

A second signature.

A comment window.

An appeal deadline.

A staged rollout.

A clinical trial.

A judicial process.

A night's sleep before sending the angry message.

Acceleration ideology treats all waiting as failure.

Civilization has learned repeatedly that speed is a design variable.

An agentile institution should ask what the delay protects.

If it protects nothing, remove it.

If it provides a chance to detect irreversible error, preserve or redesign it.

Friction has a function before it has a cost.

The tenth pressure point is meaning.

This book has mostly avoided the grander question of whether machines understand, feel, deserve rights, or count as minds. Those questions may become important. They are not required for the economic transformation described here.

A corporation does not need to know whether the forklift suffers in order to reorganize a warehouse around it.

A customer does not need to know whether an agent enjoys negotiation in order to let it bargain.

A government does not need to decide whether a model is conscious before deciding what data it may access.

Economic institutions can be transformed by systems whose metaphysical status remains unresolved.

This is one reason the debate can become confused.

People ask the deepest question first.

Is it really intelligent?

The economy asks a shallower question with enormous consequence.

Can it do the task under acceptable conditions?

Capability arrives before consensus about ontology.

That pattern has happened before.

Airplanes flew before philosophy settled what flight meant for human identity.

Computers transformed work before society agreed whether machines could “think.”

The agentile force needs no final definition of mind.

It needs interfaces to reality.

That brings us to the deepest scarcity of all.

Ends.

An agent can optimize a goal.

A powerful agent can pursue the goal through many steps.

A system of agents can divide the work.

An organization can measure the result.

None of this tells us which goal deserves pursuit.

The temptation is to hide values inside metrics.

Maximize growth.

Minimize cost.

Increase engagement.

Reduce fraud.

Accelerate approvals.

Raise test scores.

Increase compliance.

Each objective can be sensible.

Each can become absurd if pursued without the surrounding purpose.

Human institutions have always struggled with this.

Agents increase the cost of getting it wrong because they can execute the mistake more efficiently.

The highest-value skill in the agentile economy may therefore be neither prompting nor coding nor even verification.

It may be the ability to choose worthy objectives under uncertainty and to revise them when the world refuses the model.

That is judgment in its fullest sense.

Judgment is not mystical.

It can be improved.

It can use evidence.

It can be taught.

It can be distributed through institutions.

Agents can support it.

Agents can critique it.

Agents can expose inconsistency.

But judgment remains tied to responsibility because someone must live with the consequences.

This is where the human role persists even in a highly agentile economy.

Not as the slow hand on every keyboard.

Not as the ceremonial approver who clicks yes.

As the bearer of purpose, authority, relationship, and consequence.

That is not guaranteed.

Institutions can try to automate responsibility too.

A company can blame the model.

A regulator can blame the vendor.

A manager can blame the workflow.

A citizen can blame the agent that followed the citizen's own instructions.

Responsibility can dissolve in a chain of delegation.

The healthier design moves the other direction.

The longer the chain, the clearer the accountability.

Who chose the objective?

Who granted the authority?

Who selected the system?

Who monitored it?

Who benefited?

Who had the power to stop it?

Who must repair the harm?

These are governance questions.

They are also economic questions because responsibility has a price.

Insurance prices it.

Law prices it.

Reputation prices it.

Employment contracts price it.

Professional licenses price it.

Markets pay more for people and institutions willing and able to stand behind consequential work.

The agentile force may make accountability one of the last expensive components in many cognitive services.

A machine can draft the answer.

A person signs.

The signature is no longer payment for typing.

It is payment for bearing the consequence.

This can create healthier professional work if institutions recognize the distinction.

It can also create unbearable risk if people are asked to rubber-stamp volumes they cannot meaningfully review.

Again, verification-adjusted productivity matters more than raw throughput.

The economy does not need a human name attached to every machine output.

It needs responsibility proportional to consequence.

What, then, should a person do in an agentile economy?

There is no single answer because people do not exist to supply one factor of production.

A child is not a future unit of labor.

A friendship is not an unmonetized transaction.

A citizen is not only a taxpayer.

A parent is not merely a provider of care services.

The obsession with human comparative advantage can become degrading if taken too far.

If machines become better at a task, humans do not need to prove their worth by finding a task machines cannot do.

Economic value and human value are different categories.

This matters because technological abundance can create a cultural crisis even when material outcomes improve.

Work has provided income.

It has also provided structure, status, identity, social contact, mastery, and a sense of usefulness.

If some of that work changes, the question is not only how income is replaced.

It is what institutions help people build meaningful lives with more machine capability around them.

The answer may involve work.

Probably a great deal of it.

Humans have a remarkable ability to invent new needs once old needs become cheap.

But the quality of the transition matters.

A society that responds to greater productive abundance by making everyone compete harder for fewer status goods has misunderstood abundance.

A society that treats displaced people as failed machines has misunderstood humanity.

The distributional project is therefore larger than transfers.

It includes access to capital.

Education.

Housing.

Health.

Mobility.

Business formation.

Public goods.

Institutions for participation and dignity.

Technology can increase the resources available for these things.

Politics determines whether it does.

The agentile force is not destiny.

This is perhaps the most important conclusion.

Forces shape possibilities.

Institutions shape outcomes.

Steam power did not determine the factory law.

Electricity did not determine the utility regime.

The automobile did not determine the city.

The internet did not determine the platform economy before firms, governments, engineers, investors, and users made millions of choices about architecture and rules.

Agentic software opens a design space.

Which permissions are default?

Which memories are portable?

Which protocols are open?

Which markets remain contestable?

Which actions require proof?

Which public decisions require appeal?

Which gains are shared?

Which human skills are deliberately preserved?

Which forms of surveillance are unacceptable even when cheap?

Which decisions should remain slow?

Which objectives should not be optimized at all?

These choices can look small when made.

A product setting.

A procurement clause.

A technical standard.

A court ruling.

A tax rule.

A management policy.

A professional norm.

A data-export format.

A right to appeal.

Together they become the political economy of agency.

The history of technology is full of moments when infrastructure looked inevitable only after the choices hardened.

It is useful to remember the softness now.

We are early enough that basic assumptions are still available for negotiation.

Should a personal agent's memory belong to the user in portable form?

Should an agent be able to pass delegated authority to another agent by default?

Should public agents preserve evidence packets for consequential decisions?

Should a machine-generated offer disclose when pricing is individualized?

Should firms measure human attention consumed by verification alongside compute?

Should companies preserve apprenticeship tasks even when automation is cheaper?

Should agent protocols make reputation portable?

Should the same platform be allowed to represent buyer and seller without clear conflict rules?

These are not questions for some distant superintelligence.

They are product decisions arriving now.

The future often enters as a checkbox.

The word agentile was coined here because existing words split the phenomenon incorrectly.

AI describes a technical field too broad to name the economic capability.

Automation suggests fixed processes and predetermined substitution.

Software describes the substrate but not the behavior.

Labor implies a human institution.

Capital describes ownership but not directed action.

Agency names the missing property: the capacity to act on behalf of a principal.

Agentile names the economy's growing ability to supply that property through machines.

Like mobile or digital, the term matters only if it helps us notice a change that would otherwise be hidden in familiar categories.

We should use it lightly.

The economy does not need another ideology.

It needs clearer observation.

Watch the cost of delegation.

Watch the shadow backlog.

Watch where queues migrate.

Watch which work becomes exceptions.

Watch what companies rent instead of hire.

Watch which capabilities become divisible.

Watch the new bottlenecks.

Watch ownership.

Watch verification cost.

Watch the protocols.

Watch who gets an agent and who faces one.

Watch where responsibility goes when the chain gets long.

These indicators will tell us more than theatrical demos.

The most advanced system is not necessarily the one with the greatest economic consequence.

A modest agent embedded in payroll, procurement, customer service, logistics, and public administration can matter more than a spectacular model no institution trusts enough to use.

Deployment is the bridge from capability to history.

Institutions are the bridge from deployment to consequence.

The agentile force is crossing both.

One day this period may look less like the invention of a magical intelligence and more like the moment civilization learned to mass-produce delegated action.

That description is smaller.

It may be more radical.

Human beings built economies by giving instructions to other human beings.

We created firms, armies, churches, hospitals, governments, laboratories, schools, ships, markets, and movements by coordinating agency across bodies.

Every large institution is a technology for getting other people to do things together.

Now a portion of that agency can be instantiated in software.

Copied.

Scaled.

Paused.

Measured.

Connected.

Rented.

Owned.

Restricted.

Given a budget.

Asked to negotiate with another copy.

The economic consequences do not wait for the machines to become human.

They begin when the machines become useful delegates.

We are already there.

The next question is no longer whether the force exists.

It is what we build around it.
