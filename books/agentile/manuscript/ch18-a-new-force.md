# A New Force

The first hire who was not hired did not look like a new factor of production. It looked like software, which was part of the disguise.

Economies are full of technologies that do not initially resemble the thing they eventually become. The early electric motor looked like a replacement for the steam engine. It took time for factories to reorganize around distributed power rather than one central shaft. The early computer looked like a faster calculator. It took time for organizations to discover databases, networks, software industries, digital media, and businesses that could not exist without computation. The early internet looked like faster communication. It became a market, a publication system, a distribution network, a social environment, a surveillance surface, and part of the operating system of modern institutions.

Agentic software is arriving through the same narrow door. At first it is an assistant. It summarizes, drafts, answers, helps. Then it receives a tool, an account, a memory, permission, a budget, a queue, another agent. The language has to change because the economic behavior changed.

A tool becomes an actor when it can take a goal and continue acting toward it without requiring a human to specify every step. That is the agentile force: not intelligence in the abstract, consciousness, personhood, or a new species hiding inside the server rack, but intention made delegable to software.

That capability is sufficiently different from ordinary software to alter old economic boundaries and sufficiently continuous with older automation that we do not need mystical economics to understand it. The mistake on one side is to say that nothing new is happening because machines have always automated tasks. The mistake on the other is to declare that everything old has become irrelevant because machines can reason. New forces enter old institutions. The institutions bend, and the force acquires shape through the bending.

The argument of this book can now be stated without the excitement of the opening chapters. When delegated action becomes cheaper, organizations can afford to delegate more. When they delegate more, the cost structure of coordination changes. Firm boundaries, job bundles, managerial spans, queues, markets, ownership, verification, and public capacity can move with it. The agentile force is not one labor-saving machine. It is a reduction in the price of agency, with consequences wherever the old price of agency mattered.

The consequences become easier to understand if we follow scarcity rather than prediction. Technology rarely eliminates scarcity. It moves it. Faster production makes distribution more important. Abundant information raises the price of attention. Cheap communication raises the price of filtering. Cheap storage makes organization harder. Cheap computation makes good objectives more valuable. Agentic software makes one class of execution cheaper, so scarcity moves toward goals, permissions, verification, trust, ownership, physical bottlenecks, judgment, legitimacy, and people willing to accept responsibility for consequential action.

That map is more useful than a single forecast of how many jobs disappear. Job forecasts are attractive because they produce a number, and a number feels like an answer. A general technology rearranges the economy through several channels at once. A task becomes cheaper, a service price changes, demand expands, a complementary occupation grows, a training pathway weakens, a new company becomes viable, a large company coordinates more cheaply, a small company rents capabilities it never could have staffed, a local wage becomes a payment to remote infrastructure, a regulator inspects more records, and a fraudster attempts more fraud. The aggregate effect arrives through the interaction.

This is why the early data can look contradictory without anyone being dishonest. AI can raise productivity in experiments and remain shallow in many workplaces. It can reduce hiring in one task bundle while creating demand elsewhere. It can flatten some hierarchies and strengthen centralized control. It can democratize access to expertise while concentrating infrastructure ownership. It can make markets more competitive for buyers while making the platform that represents those buyers more powerful. It can reduce bureaucracy and increase the state's capacity to enforce bureaucracy. The contradictions are not noise around the subject. They are the subject.

The first pressure appears at the old minimum size of organization. Institutions have historically required people because action required people. A founder needed someone to answer customers, someone to keep books, someone to research the market, someone to coordinate vendors, someone to maintain systems. A nonprofit needed administrators. A political campaign needed organizers. A laboratory needed people to monitor and document. A town needed clerks. Large organizations enjoyed advantages partly because they could afford specialized labor and managerial coordination.

Agents lower some of those thresholds. One person can borrow fragments of an institution before hiring the institution's staff. Not its capital, brand, regulatory standing, relationships, or accumulated judgment, but pieces of its operating capacity. This is why the one-person corporation matters even if no one-person corporation becomes the world's largest company. It reveals a change in the minimum apparatus required to attempt economic action. A founder can reach farther before hiring. A professional can serve more clients. A researcher can coordinate more tools. A citizen can navigate more bureaucracy. The effect is not only substitution. It is entry. More people can try things, which increases competition and noise together.

The boundary of the firm moves for the same reason. Companies exist partly because markets are expensive to use. Finding someone, explaining the task, negotiating terms, monitoring quality, protecting information, and resolving failure all create transaction costs. An internal employee is often easier to coordinate than a new contract every hour. Agents can reduce some of those market transaction costs while also reducing internal coordination costs, so there is no simple prediction that firms become larger or smaller.

Small firms can buy outside capabilities more easily. Large firms can coordinate internal complexity more cheaply. Outsourcing can increase. Vertical integration can increase where data, trust, physical assets, or strategic control make crossing the boundary expensive. The membrane moves toward whatever remains difficult to contract across: liability, unique data, deep relationships, infrastructure, reputation, regulatory standing, and control. The firm survives. Its permeability changes.

Labor changes more awkwardly because a job is not merely a packet of tasks. It is a human institution that bundles work with responsibility, income, benefits, training, relationships, status, bargaining, and legal protection. Agentic software can perform pieces of the bundle without inheriting any of that structure. This is why both "AI replaces jobs" and "AI only replaces tasks" can hide the mechanism. Tasks determine production. Jobs determine how human beings participate in production. Change task allocation enough and the job bundle changes.

Fewer people may be needed. A role may become more skilled because routine execution disappears. The job may become worse because only exceptions remain. A machine may help a beginner perform at a competent level while simultaneously weakening the entry-level work through which expertise used to form. Demand may expand enough to absorb more workers than the technology displaces. None of those outcomes is encoded in the model. The technology changes the feasible set; bargaining, demand, institutions, ownership, and time distribute the consequences.

That is why wages matter as much as employment. A society can remain busy while the ownership of productive capacity shifts. If a growing share of useful cognition arrives through capital-like infrastructure, claims on that infrastructure become more important. The owner of the model company, cloud company, chip company, data center, energy source, application firm, or adopting business may receive a different share of the gain from the person whose task was automated. Competition can push gains toward customers. Scarce complementary labor can capture some through wages. Taxes, pensions, savings, profit sharing, and new firm formation can spread ownership. Concentrated infrastructure can do the opposite. There is no single pipeline from agent capability to inequality. There is a political economy.

Management changes because part of management has always been communication technology. Information travels up. Instructions travel down. Status gets compressed. Exceptions are interpreted. Work gets allocated. People are coached. Agents make the communication portion cheaper and expose the parts that were not merely communication: setting the objective, defining the boundary, choosing the metric, resolving the exception, protecting the apprentice, deciding which friction buys safety, and taking responsibility when the system behaves exactly as instructed and the instruction turns out to be wrong.

The new middle manager owns a decision boundary more than an org-chart box. That can widen spans dramatically. One person may supervise far more execution. The productivity gain is obvious; the failure gain is symmetrical. One bad manager can propagate a bad objective through far more activity. Management quality becomes leverage.

Markets change when both sides acquire software. Search gets cheaper. Quoting gets cheaper. Negotiation and monitoring get cheaper. Switching becomes easier where switching itself is not locked by contracts, data, hardware, habit, or regulation. The market can become more competitive at the same time that the intermediary representing the buyer becomes more powerful. The seller can personalize more efficiently; the buyer can compare more efficiently. The result is not perfect competition. It is a new contest over representation.

Who knows the principal better? Who has more options? Who leaks less information? Who can walk away? Who controls the ranking, protocol, and settlement rules? Once the price of agency falls on both sides, the institutional structure matters as much as the intelligence inside the agents.

Verification may be the least glamorous part of the transition and one of the most important. Agentile systems produce more candidate actions than human institutions were designed to inspect. Every cheap action creates a potential demand for proof. Did the agent act under authority? Did it use the right evidence? Did it follow policy? Did it produce the intended outcome? Can the action be reversed? Can another person reconstruct what happened?

A more capable model does not make a system trustworthy by itself. Trustworthy systems emerge when capability is surrounded by tests, logs, permissions, monitoring, provenance, insurance, reputational stakes, and accountable institutions. This is familiar from earlier technologies. Cheap transportation increased the value of logistics. Mass production increased the value of quality control. Complex finance increased the value of accounting and audit, even while repeatedly proving those institutions imperfect. The verification economy grows because scalable action requires scalable trust.

This logic becomes more serious when the state acquires agents. Governments have always sought administrative capacity, and agents can supply some of it. A government can answer questions, process files, detect anomalies, summarize cases, monitor compliance, and coordinate services more cheaply. That can make public administration dramatically better. It can also make public power easier to exercise.

The problem is not government AI in the abstract. It is power with lower execution cost. Good policy becomes easier to implement. Bad policy becomes easier to implement. Due process becomes more valuable because the state can produce decisions faster. Appeal becomes more valuable because error can scale. Records matter more when the human who "made" the decision may have supervised a system rather than touched the case. Citizen-side agents matter because procedural equality depends partly on whether ordinary people can match institutional complexity. Agentile capacity raises the return to good governance. It does not create good governance.

Then the software meets the material world.

A house still requires materials and labor. A transformer takes time to manufacture. A patient has one body. A courtroom has finite hearing time. A cargo ship occupies water. A semiconductor fab requires equipment, clean rooms, power, materials, and years of investment. A data center needs electricity and land. A child requires years to become an adult.

Agents can optimize around physical constraints. They cannot repeal them. A brilliant procurement agent cannot buy a transformer that does not exist. A perfect scheduling agent cannot create a nurse for tomorrow if training takes years. A planning agent can discover the bottleneck faster; the economy still has to build through it. As cognitive coordination becomes cheaper, physical bottlenecks can become more valuable. Cheap agency makes the material world more visible as a constraint.

Time belongs in the same category. Agents are fast, but institutions contain reasons to be slow. Some delays are waste. Others create reflection: a cooling-off period, a second signature, a comment window, an appeal deadline, a staged rollout, a clinical trial, a judicial process, a night's sleep before sending the angry message. An acceleration culture can treat all waiting as failure. Mature institutions ask what the delay protects. If it protects nothing, remove it. If it creates a chance to detect irreversible error, preserve or redesign it. Friction can have a function before it has a cost.

The book has mostly avoided the grander question of whether machines understand, feel, deserve rights, or count as minds. Those questions may become important. They are not required for the economic transformation described here. A corporation does not need to know whether the forklift suffers before reorganizing a warehouse around it. A customer does not need to know whether an agent enjoys negotiation before letting it bargain. A government does not need to settle machine consciousness before deciding what data an agent may access.

Economic institutions can be transformed by systems whose metaphysical status remains unresolved. Capability arrives before consensus about ontology. The relevant question for an economy is often much shallower than the philosophical one and far more immediate: can the system do the task under acceptable conditions?

That leads to the scarcity that sits underneath all the others: ends.

An agent can optimize a goal. A capable agent can pursue it through many steps. A system of agents can divide the work. An organization can measure the result. None of this tells us which goal deserves pursuit.

The temptation is to hide values inside metrics. Maximize growth. Minimize cost. Increase engagement. Reduce fraud. Accelerate approvals. Raise test scores. Increase compliance. Each objective can be sensible and each can become absurd if pursued without the surrounding purpose. Human institutions have always struggled with this. Agents increase the cost of getting it wrong because they can execute the mistake more efficiently.

The highest-value skill in the agentile economy may therefore be neither prompting nor coding nor even verification. It may be the ability to choose worthy objectives under uncertainty and revise them when the world refuses the model.

That is judgment in its fullest sense, but judgment need not be mystical. It can use evidence. It can be taught. It can be distributed through institutions. Agents can support it, critique it, and expose inconsistency. What they cannot do is make consequence disappear. Someone chose the objective. Someone granted the authority. Someone selected the system. Someone benefited. Someone had the power to stop it. Someone has to repair the harm when the chain fails.

These are governance questions and economic questions because responsibility has a price. Insurance prices it. Law prices it. Reputation prices it. Professional licenses price it. Markets often pay more for people and institutions willing and able to stand behind consequential work. The agentile force may make accountability one of the last expensive components in many cognitive services.

A machine can draft the answer and a person can sign. The signature is no longer payment for typing. It is payment for bearing consequence. That can create better professional work if institutions recognize the distinction. It can also create unbearable risk if people are asked to rubber-stamp volumes they cannot meaningfully review. Verification-adjusted productivity matters more than raw throughput. The economy does not need a human name attached to every machine output. It needs responsibility proportional to consequence.

This is also where economic analysis reaches its limit. People do not exist to supply one factor of production. A child is not a future unit of labor. A friendship is not an unmonetized transaction. A citizen is not only a taxpayer. A parent is not merely a provider of care services. The obsession with human comparative advantage becomes degrading if taken too far. If machines become better at a task, humans do not need to prove their worth by discovering a task machines cannot do. Economic value and human value are different categories.

Work has supplied income, but it has also supplied structure, status, identity, social contact, mastery, and a sense of usefulness. If some of that work changes, the question is not only how income is replaced. It is what institutions help people build meaningful lives with more machine capability around them. The answer will probably involve a great deal of work because humans have a remarkable ability to invent new needs once old needs become cheap. Still, the quality of the transition matters. A society that responds to productive abundance by making everyone compete harder for fewer status goods has misunderstood abundance. A society that treats displaced people as failed machines has misunderstood humanity.

The distributional project is therefore larger than transfers. It includes access to capital, education, housing, health, mobility, business formation, public goods, and institutions for participation and dignity. Technology can increase the resources available for these things. Politics determines whether it does.

The agentile force is not destiny. Forces shape possibilities; institutions shape outcomes. Steam power did not determine factory law. Electricity did not determine the utility regime. The automobile did not determine the city. The internet did not determine the platform economy before firms, governments, engineers, investors, and users made millions of choices about architecture and rules.

Agentic software opens another design space. Permissions, memory portability, open protocols, contestable markets, requirements for proof and appeal, apprenticeship, surveillance limits, pacing, conflict rules, and the right to move one's data can look like implementation details when each is chosen. Together they become the political economy of agency.

The history of technology is full of moments when infrastructure looked inevitable only after the choices hardened. It is useful to remember the softness now. A personal agent's memory does not have to become permanently captive to one provider. Delegated authority does not have to be transferable without limit. Public agents can be required to preserve evidence for consequential decisions. Machine-generated offers can disclose individualized pricing. Companies can measure human verification time alongside compute. Professions can deliberately preserve the experiences that produce judgment even when a machine can perform the underlying task more cheaply. Agent protocols can carry portable reputation rather than reproduce the lock-in of earlier platforms.

These are not questions for some distant superintelligence. They are product, management, legal, and institutional decisions arriving now. The future often enters as a checkbox.

The word *agentile* was coined here because familiar words divide the phenomenon awkwardly. AI describes a technical field too broad to name the economic capability. Automation suggests fixed processes and predetermined substitution. Software describes the substrate but not the behavior. Labor implies a human institution. Capital describes ownership but not directed action. Agency names the missing property: the capacity to act on behalf of a principal. *Agentile* names the economy's growing ability to supply some of that property through machines.

Like *mobile* or *digital*, the term matters only if it helps us notice a change that would otherwise stay hidden inside familiar categories. We should use it lightly. The economy does not need another ideology. It needs clearer observation.

Watch the all-in cost of delegation rather than the model price. Watch where the shadow backlog begins to turn into actual work and where the resulting queues migrate. Watch what companies rent instead of hire, which capabilities become divisible, and which bottlenecks become more valuable because everything around them became easier. Watch ownership and verification cost. Watch protocols and switching costs. Watch who gets an agent and who merely faces one. Above all, watch where responsibility goes when the chain gets long.

Those signals will tell us more than theatrical demos. The most advanced system is not necessarily the one with the greatest economic consequence. A modest agent embedded in payroll, procurement, customer service, logistics, or public administration can matter more than a spectacular model no institution trusts enough to use. Deployment is the bridge from capability to history, and institutions are the bridge from deployment to consequence.

One day this period may look less like the invention of a magical intelligence and more like the moment civilization learned to mass-produce delegated action. That description sounds smaller. It may be more radical.

Human beings built economies by giving instructions to other human beings. We created firms, armies, churches, hospitals, governments, laboratories, schools, ships, markets, and movements by coordinating agency across bodies. Every large institution is, among other things, a technology for getting people to do things together.

Now a portion of that agency can be instantiated in software, copied, scaled, paused, measured, connected, rented, owned, restricted, given a budget, and asked to negotiate with another instance. The economic consequences do not wait for machines to become human. They begin when machines become useful delegates.

We are already there.

What matters next is what we build around them.
