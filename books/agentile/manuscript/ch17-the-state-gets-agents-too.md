# The State Gets Agents Too

The state is the largest organization most people cannot leave.

You can cancel a subscription.

You can change banks.

You can resign from a job.

You can move to another city, though even that can be difficult.

But taxes, licenses, courts, borders, benefits, criminal law, public schools, land records, safety rules, and citizenship do not behave like ordinary products.

Government has authority precisely where ordinary market exit is limited or impossible.

That makes agentic government a different category of problem.

The productive promise is enormous.

Governments are made of queues.

Permit queues.

Claims queues.

Inspection queues.

Benefits queues.

Tax queues.

Court queues.

Visa queues.

Procurement queues.

Public-records queues.

Appeal queues.

The citizen experiences the state as waiting because public administration is full of tasks that require gathering records, interpreting rules, checking eligibility, comparing precedents, routing cases, requesting missing information, and producing explanations.

These are exactly the kinds of activities agents can help perform.

A benefits agent can assemble a file before a caseworker sees it.

A tax agent can answer questions against current guidance.

A permitting agent can check whether an application is complete.

A procurement agent can compare bids against requirements.

A regulatory agent can monitor filings for anomalies.

A public-records agent can search large archives.

An inspector can arrive on site with the relevant history already summarized.

A disability examiner can receive a chronology instead of two thousand unstructured pages.

A citizen can ask a question in ordinary language rather than learning the internal vocabulary of an agency.

This is the hopeful case.

The state becomes easier to use.

Administrative capacity rises without requiring headcount to rise at the same rate.

Rules become easier to understand.

Routine cases move faster.

Skilled public servants spend more time on ambiguity and less on paperwork.

The government's own 2025 federal AI policy reflects part of this ambition. The Office of Management and Budget directed agencies toward wider AI adoption while preserving governance roles, legal compliance, privacy, civil-rights protections, monitoring, and accountability. Whatever administration writes the next memo will confront the same structural issue: public agencies have reasons to use AI and reasons to fear ungoverned use.

The problem is not whether the state adopts agents.

The problem is what kind of state they make easier to operate.

An agent that makes a benefit application easier can be humane.

An agent that makes benefit denial easier can also be efficient.

An agent that helps a regulator find fraud can protect public money.

An agent that produces false fraud signals can create misery at scale.

An agent that helps a tax agency answer questions can reduce compliance cost.

An agent that helps a tax agency generate thousands of erroneous enforcement letters can reduce the government's cost of creating disputes.

The state gets both sides of the productivity gain.

It can serve faster.

It can compel faster.

This is why administrative due process becomes a central technology question.

Due process is often described in constitutional language.

At operational level, it is an information system.

A person is told what the government intends to do.

They are told why.

They can see enough of the case to challenge it.

They can present evidence.

A decision-maker considers the dispute under an intelligible rule.

A record exists.

An appeal may be possible.

These steps create friction.

The friction is deliberate.

It prevents the state from converting every suspicion into an irreversible act at machine speed.

Agentic administration will create strong incentives to remove that friction.

Some should be removed.

A citizen should not wait six weeks because a document sat in an inbox.

A clerk should not manually copy the same data between systems.

A caseworker should not spend twenty minutes finding a rule a machine can retrieve in a second.

But a notice period before property is seized is not a queueing defect.

A right to appeal a denial is not redundant human review.

A requirement that a person understand the basis of a government action is not merely an explainability feature.

It is a limit on power.

The agentile state therefore needs a distinction the agentile firm also needed.

Friction can be waste.

Friction can be a right.

This is easy to lose when government modernization is measured through throughput.

Cases per hour.

Days to decision.

Cost per application.

Fraud dollars detected.

Backlog reduced.

These are legitimate measures.

They do not fully describe public value.

A system can clear a backlog by denying more claims.

It can reduce average handling time by discouraging complex applicants.

It can improve fraud detection by increasing false positives.

It can make permitting faster by becoming less careful about environmental or safety concerns.

The state does not have one objective function.

It has law.

That matters.

A company can change policy because management decides the old policy is bad.

An agency operates under statutes, regulations, court decisions, appropriations, procedural requirements, executive direction, and public obligations that do not collapse neatly into a prompt.

The law is full of words like reasonable.

Material.

Eligible.

Necessary.

Public interest.

Good cause.

Undue hardship.

Substantial evidence.

These are not bugs in legal drafting.

They are places where institutions preserve judgment.

An agent can help interpret them.

An agent cannot make their political and legal meaning disappear.

The danger is policy laundering.

A government official makes a contested choice.

The choice becomes a rule in software.

The software applies the rule thousands of times.

Later, everyone describes the outcome as what “the system” decided.

Responsibility evaporates behind implementation.

The more sophisticated the system, the easier this can become.

A rigid rule at least exposes itself.

An adaptive model can produce individualized decisions whose pattern is difficult for any one official to see.

This is why governance has to preserve authorship.

Who approved this use?

Which policy is it implementing?

Which official owns the outcome?

What data can it use?

What action can it take without a person?

How is performance monitored?

What kinds of error require suspension?

Who can receive a complaint?

Can the affected person obtain a meaningful explanation?

Can the system's recommendation be overridden?

Who is accountable when it is wrong?

These questions resemble the GAO's accountability framework, which organizes federal AI oversight around governance, data, performance, and monitoring. The framework predates the current agentic wave, but its basic architecture becomes more important as systems move from analysis toward action.

Government does not need philosophical certainty about machine intelligence.

It needs controls.

The data problem is particularly important because the state owns datasets markets cannot easily replicate.

Tax records.

Benefit records.

Criminal records.

Health records.

Property records.

Immigration records.

Education records.

Procurement histories.

Regulatory filings.

The data exists because citizens and firms are required to interact with the state.

That creates a special duty.

A commercial user can choose not to give an assistant certain information.

A taxpayer cannot always choose whether the government has income data.

A defendant cannot opt out of the court record.

A veteran seeking benefits may have to provide intimate medical evidence.

The agentile state therefore holds both coercive authority and privileged context.

That combination is productive.

It is dangerous.

An agency can answer a citizen's question more accurately because it knows the person's actual case.

It can also infer far more about the person than the original program required.

Data minimization becomes a governance principle for agency, not only privacy.

The agent should know what it needs to know to perform the mandate.

The fact that government possesses a record does not mean every government agent should be allowed to use it.

This is the public-sector version of least privilege.

The boundaries must survive organizational enthusiasm.

The same is true of enforcement.

Governments have always used technology to expand state capacity.

Census systems made populations legible.

Telegraphs accelerated military and administrative command.

Databases linked records.

Computers scaled tax and benefit administration.

Networked surveillance expanded investigatory reach.

Agents can reduce the cognitive cost of using all of these systems together.

The crucial change is not that the state gets more data.

It is that the state gets cheaper interpretation of data.

A human investigator can examine only so many cases.

An agent can screen millions.

A human regulator can compare only so many filings.

An agent can look for anomalies continuously.

A human intelligence analyst can read only so many documents.

An agent can synthesize huge streams.

This can improve enforcement against genuine wrongdoing.

It can also make weak laws easier to enforce.

Technological capacity is indifferent to the justice of the rule.

A brilliant tax-collection agent faithfully enforcing an absurd tax rule does not make the rule good.

A perfect surveillance system does not determine what should be surveilled.

The state remains a political institution.

Agency amplifies its objectives.

That is why democratic legitimacy matters more as execution gets cheaper.

A slow bureaucracy can be frustrating and still create accidental protection against bad policy because implementation is difficult.

A capable agent system can remove that protection.

The government can execute a policy closer to its formal design.

This is usually described as state capacity.

State capacity is valuable when the state is doing something valuable.

It is alarming when the state is doing something oppressive.

The agentile state raises the return to institutional quality.

Good institutions can deliver more.

Bad institutions can impose more.

This is not a reason to prefer incompetence.

It is a reason to avoid treating competence as legitimacy.

The procurement problem follows.

Chapter 14 asked what a firm truly owns when its cognitive infrastructure arrives through external vendors.

The same question is sharper for government.

Suppose an agency builds a critical benefits workflow around one proprietary agent platform.

The vendor changes its price.

The model changes behavior.

The contract expires.

The supplier is acquired.

A legal dispute interrupts service.

The agency wants to switch.

Can it?

Does it own the prompts, evaluations, policy translations, audit logs, workflow data, and performance history required to move?

Can a new vendor reproduce the behavior without retraining the whole bureaucracy?

Can the old vendor retain sensitive public data?

Can the government continue operating during transition?

This is not ordinary vendor management when the system is exercising public authority.

Exit becomes a sovereignty issue.

OMB's procurement guidance and subsequent federal oversight work reflect the importance of acquisition alongside use. A government can become dependent through contracts long before anyone calls the dependency strategic.

The healthiest public architecture therefore preserves institutional memory outside the model provider.

The agency should know its own policy.

Its own evaluations.

Its own authorization rules.

Its own records.

Its own failure history.

The vendor supplies capability.

The state should retain the ability to govern the capability.

There is a labor issue here too.

Public servants contain tacit institutional memory.

They know which rule causes trouble.

Which form confuses everyone.

Which exception is common despite appearing rare in the manual.

Which field office has a different reality from headquarters.

Which fraud pattern is obvious only after ten years of cases.

Which citizen problem looks suspicious on paper but is usually innocent.

Automation can capture some of this knowledge.

It can also destroy the workforce that produces it.

A government that uses agents to eliminate entry-level administrative roles may save money and weaken its future bench of experienced public servants.

This is Chapter 11's apprenticeship problem inside the state.

The consequences can be larger because public expertise is not always easy to buy from the private market.

A regulator who understands one obscure statutory regime may take years to develop.

A benefits adjudicator who understands how evidence arrives from real people may carry knowledge absent from any policy manual.

The agentile state must decide which human capabilities are strategic enough to preserve even when machines can perform much of the routine work.

This is particularly important in emergencies.

A resilient government cannot assume the agent works.

Networks fail.

Vendors fail.

Models fail.

Cyberattacks succeed.

Power goes out.

A system receives an input nobody anticipated.

NIST's 2026 work on AI in critical infrastructure emphasizes lifecycle risk management for exactly this reason. A capability that improves efficiency in normal operations can create fragility if no one remembers how to operate without it.

The paper process everyone hates can sometimes be a disaster-recovery system.

That does not mean preserving paper forever.

It means preserving fallback capacity.

A pilot still needs to know what to do when automation disconnects.

A public agency may need the equivalent.

The agentile state will also create a new political constituency: people who want a human.

Not because humans are always better.

Because a human can sometimes hear a story the form cannot represent.

The citizen says, “I know the rule says this, but here is what happened.”

The government needs some place for that sentence to go.

An agent can be the first listener.

It can summarize the story beautifully.

At some point a person with authority may still need to decide whether the rule bends.

This is the importance of appeal.

Appeal is the institutional acknowledgment that the first decision can be wrong.

A system that produces more decisions needs an appeal system able to absorb them.

Otherwise automation makes government efficient at producing unreviewable error.

The appeal itself can use agents.

An agent can help the citizen understand why the decision occurred.

It can identify missing evidence.

It can compare similar cases.

It can prepare a record for a reviewer.

It can reduce the cost of challenging government rather than only the cost of government acting.

This is a profound design principle.

Agency should exist on both sides of power.

If the state gets agents, citizens should get agents too.

A taxpayer should have software capable of reading the government's notice and explaining the basis.

A benefits applicant should have software capable of checking whether the agency followed its own rules.

A small business should have software capable of navigating permitting requirements.

A defendant should be able to organize a record without hiring a team merely to match the state's administrative capacity.

The agentile economy can democratize bureaucracy if capability is available to both the institution and the person facing it.

This creates a new form of procedural equality.

The old concern was access to counsel, expertise, and time.

The new concern includes access to capable machine representation.

A government agent that can search ten thousand pages should not create a world in which only wealthy citizens can afford an equally capable agent to respond.

Public-interest tooling may become part of access to justice.

The same logic applies to regulation.

A large corporation can hire experts to interpret every new rule.

A small business struggles.

A reliable public agent that explains compliance could reduce the fixed cost of regulation without weakening the rule itself.

This is one of the most attractive uses of government AI.

Make the state legible.

The citizen should not need to become an expert in the bureaucracy in order to comply with it.

The government can publish its rules in a form agents can use, while preserving the legal source beneath the explanation.

That last clause matters.

The agent's answer is not the law.

The regulation is the law.

The statute is the law.

The court decision may be the law.

The agent is an interface.

A helpful interface can become dangerous when people cannot tell where interpretation ends and authority begins.

The state should therefore be unusually explicit about provenance.

Here is the rule.

Here is the current guidance.

Here is the version date.

Here is what is uncertain.

Here is who can give a binding answer.

The verification economy enters government through this door.

Public trust will depend less on whether an answer sounds official and more on whether its authority can be traced.

A government voice synthesized by a model should not become authoritative merely because it is fluent.

The source must remain inspectable.

This is also where records law becomes interesting.

Government decisions create public records under rules that vary by institution and jurisdiction.

Agents produce intermediate reasoning, tool calls, prompts, retrieved documents, generated drafts, and internal messages at enormous volume.

Which of these are records?

Which must be retained?

Which can be deleted?

Which are discoverable in litigation?

Which reveal sensitive information?

Which are necessary to reconstruct a decision?

The volume can be overwhelming.

Keeping everything forever is not accountability.

It is storage.

Keeping nothing is not efficiency.

It is amnesia.

The state needs a theory of consequential trace.

Preserve what is needed to explain and audit the act.

Do not preserve every meaningless token merely because it exists.

This will require law, records management, technical design, and judgment.

The agentile state also changes federalism and administrative scale.

A small municipality can rent capabilities once available only to a large agency.

A county can improve public-record search.

A rural school district can automate administrative work.

A state regulator can monitor markets more continuously.

This can reduce the capacity gap between large and small governments.

The same tools can create dependency on shared national vendors.

Local autonomy can grow at the application layer while infrastructure centralizes underneath.

Chapter 14 returns.

Who owns the agentile force used by the state?

And Chapter 16 returns too.

Which protocols determine what public agents can do with private agents?

A business files a permit through its agent.

The agency's agent validates it.

A payment agent collects the fee.

An inspection agent schedules the visit.

A citizen agent monitors the status.

This is not science fiction in structure.

It is ordinary e-government with more delegation and less clicking.

The institutional design challenge is to make the interaction fast without making public authority opaque.

The best agentile government may therefore feel paradoxical.

More automation.

More human accountability.

Fewer routine interactions.

Better appeal paths.

Less waiting.

More traceability.

More personalized service.

Stricter limits on inappropriate data use.

More machine decision support.

Clearer human ownership of consequential decisions.

The goal is not to preserve bureaucracy as employment.

The goal is to preserve the reasons democratic bureaucracy exists.

Consistency.

Legality.

Equal treatment.

Notice.

Review.

Records.

Constraint on arbitrary power.

Capacity to execute legitimate public decisions.

Agents can strengthen all of these.

They can weaken all of these.

That is the uncomfortable symmetry of the technology.

The private company gets an agent and asks how much labor it can save.

The citizen gets an agent and asks how much life it can simplify.

The state gets an agent and should be asked a different first question.

What power just became cheaper to exercise?
