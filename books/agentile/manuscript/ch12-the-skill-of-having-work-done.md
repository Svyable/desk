# The Skill of Having Work Done

The apprentice watches the master and misunderstands what the master is doing.

The visible act looks simple because the invisible selection has already happened.

A senior engineer opens three files instead of three hundred. A lawyer asks one question that makes ten pages of research unnecessary. A mechanic hears an engine and checks the cheap failure before dismantling the expensive system. An editor removes the paragraph the writer spent all morning polishing. A good executive ignores nine urgent-looking items and calls one person.

Expertise often appears as less work.

This becomes important when agents make more work available than anyone can reasonably inspect.

The first popular intuition about capable AI was that expertise would matter less because everyone could access expert-like answers. In some domains, access really does broaden. A person can ask for an explanation, draft code in an unfamiliar language, translate a document, or obtain a competent first pass without locating a specialist first.

But the agentile economy introduces a second effect in the opposite direction.

When execution becomes cheap, choosing what to execute becomes more valuable.

A novice and an expert can both launch ten agents. The expert is more likely to give them ten useful problems.

This is not a permanent law of nature. Agents can themselves improve at planning, decomposition, evaluation, and strategy. Yet current evidence from agentic coding already points toward a pattern worth taking seriously: experienced users often obtain more value because they make better planning decisions, provide better constraints, recognize bad output faster, and know what successful work should look like.

The machine amplifies the principal.

That sounds comforting to experts, so it deserves suspicion. Every generation of incumbents finds reasons its judgment will remain scarce. Sometimes they are right until they are not. Calculators did reduce the value of routine arithmetic. Spreadsheets removed work that once justified clerical expertise. Search engines changed the value of factual recall. Better models can absorb more of the planning that experts currently supply.

The point is narrower.

As long as a human remains responsible for directing a capable system, the skill of having work done is distinct from the skill of doing every step oneself.

We have seen this distinction before in management, directing, surgery, construction, and research. A film director does not operate every camera. An architect does not pour concrete. A principal investigator may not perform every assay. A general contractor coordinates specialists whose technical abilities exceed his in particular trades.

The agentile version spreads the distinction into ordinary knowledge work.

A marketing manager may no longer write every campaign draft but needs to know which customer truth the campaign should express. A programmer may spend less time typing functions and more time defining behavior, selecting architectures, constructing tests, and tracing failures. An analyst may generate models quickly and spend more time asking whether the assumptions make economic sense. A lawyer may delegate research while concentrating on strategy, fact development, and the client's actual risk.

Work moves up a level of abstraction.

Then the abstraction becomes work.

This can be disorienting because many professions use execution as proof of competence. We trust the person who has done the thing. The analyst earns judgment by building models. The editor earns taste by editing sentences. The manager earns authority by having performed the frontline role.

If agents remove too much execution from the learning path, where does judgment come from?

This is the training problem again, but now at the level of individual skill.

A person cannot become an excellent critic of machine work merely by being told to “use judgment.” Judgment is compressed experience. It includes failures, edge cases, embodied feedback, social consequences, and thousands of small comparisons between what looked plausible and what actually worked.

The easiest work to automate is often the work that produced those comparisons.

A junior programmer learns from bugs. A junior accountant learns from reconciliations. A young doctor learns from cases under supervision. A writer learns by writing sentences that fail. If the machine produces the first draft perfectly enough that the novice only approves it, the novice can become a manager without having built the internal model that makes management trustworthy.

This is the hollow expertise problem.

It will not appear immediately because experienced professionals can supervise agents well. It appears later when an organization asks who will replace the experienced professionals.

Professions will need to redesign deliberate practice.

A trainee may work on cases the agent has already solved but without seeing the solution first. An engineering team may require juniors to explain and test generated code rather than merely merge it. A medical training program may use simulations aggressively because routine documentation no longer supplies the same exposure. A law firm may give young lawyers direct responsibility for strategy earlier while agents handle document volume.

The goal is not to preserve obsolete drudgery for moral reasons.

It is to preserve contact with reality.

Some drudgery teaches nothing and should disappear. Nobody becomes wiser by copying data between identical forms for the thousandth time. The challenge is separating repetitive burden from formative repetition.

Agents force institutions to decide deliberately instead of letting the labor market make the curriculum by accident.

This can produce better education if we take the opportunity.

A machine can generate endless practice at the right level, give immediate feedback, simulate rare cases, expose a learner to competing solutions, and vary examples more widely than a single workplace can. Apprenticeship can become more intentional and less dependent on the luck of which assignments happen to arrive.

But simulation has to stay anchored to real consequences.

A model can tell a trainee that a negotiation was good. A real counterparty may disagree. A simulated customer can reward empathy too predictably. A coding benchmark can pass while users hate the product. A synthetic market can teach strategies that fail when capital is at risk.

The skill of having work done includes knowing which feedback is real enough to trust.

That is evaluation literacy.

In an agentile economy, people will need to become better at specifying observable outcomes. “Make this better” can work for an exploratory draft. It is a weak operating instruction for a payment system. “Research the market” is a useful beginning but not a stopping condition. “Find the best supplier” hides criteria that may conflict.

The principal needs to translate intention into evidence.

This does not mean every worker becomes a prompt engineer. That phrase describes an interface, not a durable profession. Interfaces change. The durable skill is objective articulation: knowing what you want, what constraints matter, what would count as success, and what evidence would change your mind.

These are old skills with new leverage.

Scientific method is partly the discipline of making a claim vulnerable to evidence. Engineering is partly the discipline of defining requirements and testing against them. Good management is partly the discipline of making priorities explicit enough that other people can act. Law is partly the discipline of turning intentions into enforceable boundaries.

Agents reward these traditions because vague goals can now produce a great deal of activity.

There is a second durable skill: decomposition.

Complex work often becomes tractable when broken into pieces with useful interfaces. Experts know which pieces can be separated and which interactions make separation dangerous. A novice may divide a problem according to the nouns in the request. An expert divides it according to causal structure.

An agent can help with decomposition, but somebody has to judge whether the decomposition destroyed the problem.

Consider launching a new product. One can assign market research, pricing, legal review, customer interviews, technical feasibility, design, and distribution to separate agents. The decomposition appears sensible. Yet the important insight may be that one customer constraint links all six. If each stream optimizes locally, the combined recommendation can be incoherent.

Coordination quality becomes a skill.

A third skill is adversarial reading.

Generated work is often fluent enough to reduce the psychological friction that normally alerts us to weak reasoning. A badly written memo invites scrutiny. A beautiful memo can glide past it. Professionals need habits for asking what evidence is missing, what assumption is doing too much work, what source would falsify the claim, and what the agent had no way to know.

This is not cynicism toward machines.

It is ordinary professional skepticism applied to a fast colleague.

A fourth skill is escalation judgment.

The productive user does not inspect every token. She knows which decisions are safe to delegate and which deserve direct attention. This is risk-based supervision. Reversible, low-cost, well-tested actions receive broad autonomy. Irreversible, high-stakes, novel actions receive more review.

The rule sounds obvious until everything is moving quickly.

Agents create a temptation to extend autonomy from the easy ninety percent into the dangerous final ten because stopping for review feels like losing the point of automation. Mature users will resist that vanity. Human attention is expensive; that is precisely why it should be concentrated where error is expensive too.

A fifth skill is context design.

People who know a domain understand which information matters. An agent with access to every document in a company can still perform worse than one given the five relevant documents and a clear instruction. More context can add distraction, contradiction, stale policy, and privacy risk.

Knowing what the machine should know becomes part of knowing the work.

This can favor experienced employees because they possess a map of institutional knowledge. It can also favor newcomers who are willing to formalize what veterans keep tacit. The best teams will pair the two.

There is evidence that AI tools can help lower-skilled or less-experienced workers more on certain bounded tasks, reducing performance gaps. Customer-support research has found especially large gains among less experienced workers using generative assistance. Other research on agentic coding suggests experienced users can extract greater returns from autonomy because they retain more of the planning role.

These results are not contradictory.

A tool can compress skill differences in execution while increasing the return to skill in direction.

That may become one of the defining labor-market patterns of the transition.

For a task with a clear local objective, the agent acts as a tutor and scaffold. The novice reaches competent output faster. For a long-horizon objective with ambiguous tradeoffs, the principal's judgment shapes the entire search tree. Expertise compounds through delegation.

The boundary between these regimes will move as models improve.

We should therefore avoid freezing today's division of labor into a permanent forecast. The important educational strategy is not to train people for the exact boundary. It is to train the meta-skills that remain useful as the boundary shifts: learning new tools, understanding systems, testing claims, communicating objectives, and acquiring enough domain contact to know when the machine's answer is strange.

There is also a social skill in having work done.

Agents can cross occupational boundaries more easily than institutions can. A designer can ask for code. A programmer can ask for legal concepts. A marketer can analyze data. A small-business owner can request financial modeling. Recent usage research suggests AI systems are already used for tasks associated with occupations other than the user's own.

This can make people more generalist at the interface while specialists remain important underneath.

The user does not need to become an attorney to ask an agent to identify clauses worth discussing with an attorney. She does not need to become a statistician to explore a dataset, though she needs enough understanding not to turn an exploratory result into a false conclusion. She can trespass productively across professional borders.

The economic value of curiosity rises.

A person who sees a problem can attempt more of the path toward solving it before asking permission from an organizational specialty. This can reduce handoffs, speed experimentation, and give individuals a wider sense of agency.

It can also produce amateur overconfidence.

The agent makes the foreign domain feel fluent before the user has learned its traps. A generated contract looks contractual. Generated code looks like code. Generated analysis looks quantitative. The surface resemblance can outrun understanding.

The skill of having work done therefore includes knowing when the work is no longer yours to judge.

That humility is a competence.

In mature professions, experts learn the edges of their own authority. A general practitioner refers to a specialist. A lawyer knows which jurisdiction requires another lawyer. An engineer asks for a safety review. Agents make it easy to wander beyond those edges without feeling the crossing.

Good systems should help users notice.

They can mark uncertainty, require stronger review for certain actions, expose sources, preserve provenance, and route high-stakes work to qualified people. But users still need a mental model of consequence.

This may be the central educational challenge of the agentile economy: abundance of capability without abundance of accountability.

A teenager can access tools that perform fragments of professional work. A founder can coordinate functions once distributed across a company. An employee can reach across the org chart. The capacity is real.

Authority is not automatically transferred with it.

The world will contain many people who can cause work to happen outside the domains in which institutions recognize them as experts.

That can be liberating.

It can be reckless.

Most transformative technologies produce both by lowering a gate that once bundled access with training. Publishing became easy without making everyone a journalist. Investing became easy without making everyone a portfolio manager. Coding became accessible without making every program secure.

Agents lower the gate on organized action.

The response cannot be to rebuild every old gate. Some existed only because coordination was expensive. The response is to identify which gates protected scarce expertise, safety, or rights and which merely protected incumbents.

That is an institutional problem.

For the individual, the practical shift is simpler to describe.

The valuable worker of the agentile economy may look unusually lazy from the perspective of the old office.

She does not type every line, open every file, or attend every meeting. She launches work, checks evidence, kills bad branches, asks better questions, and reserves her own effort for the decisions that do not compress well.

Her productivity is visible in what happens around her.

Her expertise is visible in what she refuses to delegate.

This way of working has an uncomfortable effect on performance measurement.

Organizations are good at measuring visible production. Tickets closed, documents written, calls made, lines of code changed, analyses delivered, meetings attended. Agents can inflate many of these counts until they become nearly meaningless. The person who launches twenty agents may appear twenty times as productive according to artifact volume even if nineteen artifacts should never have existed.

Metrics have to move from activity toward consequence.

Did the customer problem stay solved? Did the software become more reliable? Did the decision improve? Did the cycle time fall without errors rising? Did revenue come from customers who remained? Did the experiment reduce uncertainty? Did the organization learn something reusable?

These questions are harder to answer, which is why activity metrics survive.

Agents make the cost of surviving bad metrics larger. If a metric rewards documents, the system can produce documents at industrial scale. If a customer-service team is rewarded for closure, agents can close aggressively. If a development team is rewarded for code output, agents can generate code faster than the organization can maintain it.

Automation amplifies the objective function.

The worker skilled at having work done will therefore need metric skepticism. Before optimizing a measured outcome, ask what behavior the measure creates when production is no longer constrained by human effort.

This is a version of Goodhart's law with a turbocharger.

It also changes career signaling. A résumé traditionally lists jobs, responsibilities, and artifacts because those facts proxy for capability. If a single person can generate an extraordinary volume of competent-looking work with agents, raw portfolio size becomes less informative.

Employers may care more about verified outcomes, judgment under uncertainty, the difficulty of the problems selected, and evidence that the person can distinguish generated competence from genuine understanding.

Interviews may change accordingly.

Banning AI from every assessment is unlikely to reflect the workplace if the workplace is agentile. Allowing unrestricted agents on a task and judging only the polished result can reveal almost nothing about the candidate. A better assessment can observe how the person uses agents: what questions they ask, which outputs they distrust, how they test, when they change direction, and whether they understand the final work well enough to defend it.

The object of evaluation becomes orchestration under responsibility.

Schools face the same problem at larger scale.

An essay once provided imperfect evidence that a student had read, organized, and written. A model can now produce the surface artifact cheaply. The obvious response is surveillance or prohibition. That may be necessary in some assessments, especially where unaided competence itself matters. But education also needs assessments for the world students will inhabit.

A student might be asked to use an agent and then identify its strongest unsupported claim, design a test that could disprove its conclusion, compare two agent strategies, or explain why a polished output is wrong. These tasks treat AI neither as contraband nor as oracle.

They teach supervision.

The deeper educational goal remains unchanged: build a mind capable of forming its own judgment. The presence of external cognition makes the goal more important because fluent assistance can hide whether judgment formed at all.

This is why reading, mathematics, writing, and domain fundamentals do not become obsolete simply because a model can perform them. Foundations are what let a person notice when delegated work violates reality. A pilot still understands flight when automation is functioning. A physician still studies physiology when diagnostic tools improve. A programmer still benefits from understanding computation when agents write code.

The amount of manual practice required can change.

The need for an internal model does not vanish as quickly.

There is a personal economic strategy hidden here. When the cost of execution falls, do not compete by executing the same specification slightly faster than the machine. Move toward work where you can improve the specification, connect domains, own a relationship, gather unique evidence, take responsibility, or identify a problem nobody has formulated well.

This is not universally available advice. Many workers do not control how jobs are designed. Institutions decide which people receive training, autonomy, and access to tools. Telling everyone to “become strategic” while removing entry-level pathways would be evasive.

Still, at the level of capability, the direction is visible.

The scarce human contribution moves from producing every unit toward shaping the system that produces units.

That movement has occurred before. A farmer operating machinery does not become less agricultural because fewer muscles touch the soil. A photographer working with automatic exposure does not cease making choices. A pilot using automation still owns a flight in a meaningful sense when training and authority remain intact.

The danger is not delegation itself.

The danger is delegating the experiences required to know what good means before that knowledge has formed.

A mature agentile culture will distinguish leverage from abdication.

Leverage is when the machine extends a judgment the person actually possesses.

Abdication is when the machine substitutes for a judgment the person is still pretending to possess.

From the outside, both can produce the same beautiful document.

Reality eventually tells them apart.
