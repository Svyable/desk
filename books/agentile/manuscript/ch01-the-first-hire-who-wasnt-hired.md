# The First Hire Who Wasn't Hired

A new worker entered the economy without filling out a tax form.

Nobody gave it a desk.

It did not negotiate salary, commute, request insurance, ask where the bathroom was, or sit through an onboarding meeting about the company’s values. It arrived as a button, a command line, a browser tab, an API call. The accounting system still classified the expense as software.

Then somebody gave it an errand.

The difference between an answer and an errand is easy to miss because both may begin with a sentence. “What are the largest customers by overdue balance?” is a question. “Review the overdue accounts, reconcile obvious errors against the payment ledger, draft polite follow-ups for the rest, and flag anything unusual before you send” is the beginning of delegated work.

The first request ends when information comes back.

The second request enters the world.

It has objects to inspect, decisions to make, tools to use, intermediate states to remember, and a criterion for being finished. It may fail halfway through. It may discover that the question was badly framed. It may need to ask for permission. If it is useful, it handles some of those complications without returning after every click to ask what to do next.

That is where software begins to acquire economic agency.

The word “agency” carries enough philosophical, legal, and political freight to make this sound grander than it needs to be. An airline’s automatic rebooking system exercises a narrow kind of agency when it selects an alternative itinerary after a cancellation. A thermostat does something even narrower when it acts to maintain a temperature. Humans have surrounded themselves with mechanisms that sense conditions and respond.

The new thing is the breadth of the response.

Language-model agents can often move through systems built for people because those systems already expose their logic in language, menus, forms, code, documents, and visual interfaces. They can be given a goal rather than a fully specified sequence. They can choose among actions using a general model of what the task seems to require.

That does not make them reliable. It makes them economically interesting.

Reliability comes later in the sentence than capability but earlier in the purchase order.

A machine that can do something useful only half the time may still matter if attempts are cheap, failures are visible, and successful runs replace expensive work. A machine that succeeds ninety-nine times out of a hundred may still be unusable if the hundredth failure transfers money to the wrong account or deletes a production database. The relevant question is never simply, can it act?

The question is: under what consequences can we afford to let it act?

In 2025 and 2026, the public evidence began to show a migration from conversation toward delegation. Anthropic’s Economic Index described the growing share of long-running agentic sessions in products such as Claude Code and Cowork. OpenAI reported a similar transition around Codex: users were increasingly assigning tasks estimated to represent hours of human work, and the heaviest internal users were running many hours of agent turns in parallel within a single day. METR, an independent research organization, measured the length of software tasks frontier systems could complete with a given probability and found a steep historical rise in that time horizon.

These measurements are imperfect in different ways.

A task that takes a human eight hours does not necessarily take an agent eight hours. Model-estimated task duration is not a stopwatch. Software benchmarks are not the economy. Vendor usage logs overrepresent the people who have already decided to use the vendor. The most enthusiastic employees at an AI company are not a random sample of office workers.

Still, the direction is difficult to miss.

The unit of interaction is stretching.

A chatbot turn is measured in seconds. A delegated task may occupy minutes, hours, or longer. The human is no longer necessarily present for every intermediate step.

That extension in time changes the economics before it changes employment statistics.

Imagine a junior analyst in an ordinary company. Much of the analyst’s day is not the decisive analytical insight the job description celebrates. It is collecting files, cleaning columns, checking definitions, refreshing a model, finding the latest version, following up with somebody who has not replied, turning notes into slides, reconciling why one system says 412 and another says 417.

The work is valuable because somebody has to carry state across those fragments.

Traditional software attacks the fragments one at a time. There is a script for one export, a macro for one workbook, a dashboard for one metric. The human remains the glue.

An agent attacks the glue.

It can be told what the analyst is trying to accomplish and then move among fragments. When it works, it does not merely accelerate a step. It absorbs some of the connective tissue that made the job a job.

This is why early debates framed only around “which tasks can AI do?” often feel strangely static. Jobs are not sacks of independent tasks. They are systems for preserving context, responsibility, priorities, exceptions, and unfinished business across time.

The analyst knows that the chief financial officer hates surprises in the Friday meeting. The customer-service representative knows that a particular customer is angry for reasons not captured in the ticket. The operations manager knows which supplier is technically cheapest and which supplier actually answers the phone when a shipment disappears.

Work contains memory that is not written down and judgment that is only partly visible in the final artifact.

Agents matter to the extent they can begin carrying some of that context.

They also matter because they can be multiplied.

A person can send ten emails quickly but cannot conduct ten careful negotiations at the same instant. A person can monitor a hundred dashboards by cycling attention among them but cannot stare continuously at all one hundred. A person can supervise several projects, though the price appears as meetings, reminders, status reports, and the slightly haunted look of someone whose brain is functioning as a badly indexed queue.

Software can copy a process.

Agentic software can copy a process that contains decisions.

That is the hinge.

Industrial machinery multiplied muscle by making mechanical work reproducible. Computers multiplied calculation by making formal procedures reproducible. Networks multiplied communication by making information cheap to move.

Agents begin to multiply situated cognitive action.

The adjective “situated” matters. A model sitting in isolation and producing text is not yet much of an economic actor. Give it access to a code repository, a browser, a payment system, company documents, a calendar, an inbox, or a laboratory instrument and the model gains a place to act.

Each tool connection turns capability into leverage.

It also turns mistakes into consequences.

A harmless hallucination in a brainstorming session becomes a different object when an agent can file the invented fact into a customer record. A fabricated citation is annoying in a draft and dangerous in a regulatory submission. A misunderstood instruction can become a purchase.

The old software industry tried to reduce this risk by demanding precision from the programmer. The program could act quickly because its permitted behavior had been specified in advance. The new agent industry tries something stranger: give a probabilistic system a goal and enough context to improvise toward it, then wrap that improvisation in tests, permissions, monitoring, and recovery.

The result is neither ordinary software nor ordinary labor.

Ordinary software has near-zero marginal replication cost but low autonomous discretion. Human labor has extraordinary general discretion but high marginal replication cost. A capable agent begins to combine pieces of both.

You can copy it.

You can also ask it to figure things out.

Economic categories begin to wobble here.

If a company buys a robot, accountants call it capital. If the robot requires a human operator, the operator is labor. If a company subscribes to an AI service that independently performs invoice reconciliation, drafts correspondence, contacts vendors, and escalates exceptions, what exactly has the company purchased?

The invoice may say software.

The workflow may say employee.

The law may say neither.

The budget may not care.

Companies routinely adopt important technologies before theory develops an elegant category for them. The accounting treatment does not prevent the organization from rearranging itself around the capability. Email reduced the need for internal mailrooms long before economists finished measuring the productivity effect of electronic communication. Spreadsheets allowed financial models to proliferate through companies without creating a new occupational category called spreadsheet capital.

The agentile change may arrive the same way: first as a line item, then as a habit, then as an assumption embedded in how work is designed.

A team stops hiring one role because the remaining employees can cover the queue with agents.

A small company starts operating twenty-four hours because software keeps working after the founders sleep.

A lawyer becomes able to inspect a larger document set before deciding what deserves attention.

A recruiter writes a small tool to reconcile candidates across systems despite not being a software engineer.

An engineer spends more time specifying tests and less time typing implementation.

A manager discovers that the bottleneck is no longer producing drafts. It is deciding which drafts deserve to exist.

None of these changes, individually, requires a science-fiction labor market.

They require only enough useful delegation to alter the local arithmetic of a decision.

This is how new economic forces usually arrive. They do not ring a bell and announce a new epoch. They make one old tradeoff slightly less binding.

The steam engine made power less dependent on a particular river. The telegraph made information less dependent on physical transport. The container made freight less dependent on the cost and chaos of piece-by-piece handling. Cloud computing made experimentation less dependent on owning servers.

Agents make action less dependent on occupying one human mind at one moment.

The first consequence is not necessarily fewer minds.

Sometimes cheap action increases demand for minds because the number of possible projects expands. Cheap computing did not eliminate programmers. It enabled more software and more elaborate computation. Cheap photography did not eliminate images. It drowned the world in them. Cheap messages did not free us from communication. It created inboxes.

Abundance has a sense of humor.

The safest forecast about agentic labor is therefore not a number of jobs lost or created. It is a multiplication of attempted action.

More analysis will be run because analysis is cheaper.

More software will be written because software is cheaper.

More customer segments will be tested.

More vendors will be compared.

More personalized outreach will be generated.

More contracts will be reviewed.

More experiments will be launched.

More compliance evidence will be produced.

More fraud will be attempted.

More fraud will be investigated.

More negotiations will occur.

More decisions will be queued for someone’s approval.

This matters because economies are constrained not only by what is physically possible but by what is administratively worth doing.

There are thousands of useful actions that never occur because nobody has time.

A small business does not perform a sophisticated procurement analysis before buying a $700 printer. A household does not run a reverse auction among twenty insurance carriers every month. A city does not individually optimize every maintenance schedule every afternoon. A researcher does not reproduce every relevant paper before citing it. The cost of thinking, searching, coordinating, and following through is larger than the expected benefit.

Those are not technological impossibilities.

They are economic omissions.

Agentile systems attack the omission layer.

If the administrative cost of an action falls far enough, the action enters the feasible economy.

That is why the long-term effect may be less like replacing a clerk and more like discovering that the world contains an enormous reservoir of errands nobody previously found worth assigning.

The reservoir is visible in every organization.

Fix the naming inconsistency in the old files.

Check whether every customer contract contains the current clause.

Look through the last three years of support tickets for recurring product failures.

Compare every vendor price to the current market.

Test the backup.

Write the missing documentation.

Call the customers who quietly stopped using the product.

Review the permissions nobody has reviewed since the employee who created them left.

Try five versions instead of one.

Follow up.

Follow up again.

These tasks live in the shadow backlog of civilization. They are known, useful, and undone.

A sufficiently cheap agent does not need to replace a person to matter. It can colonize the undone.

That sounds benign until one notices that the same shadow backlog exists for persuasion, surveillance, litigation, speculation, political influence, price discrimination, and cyberattack.

The economy contains beneficial omissions because effort is expensive.

It also contains protective omissions.

A creditor does not pursue every five-dollar balance across five jurisdictions. A marketer does not personalize a campaign to the psychological history of every recipient. A landlord does not continuously re-evaluate the bargaining weakness of every tenant. A bureaucracy does not inspect every possible violation every minute. A scammer does not converse patiently with every potential victim for months.

Friction restrains bad projects along with good ones.

Agentile capacity removes friction without asking whether the underlying intention deserves help.

That is why it is a force rather than merely a productivity tool.

A force changes the motion of whatever it touches.

We will spend much of this book asking where it pushes, where it meets resistance, and which institutions become more important once action itself is easier to manufacture.

For now, the smallest useful distinction is enough.

The old program waited for a command.

The chatbot waited for a question.

The agent gets an errand.

And the economy has never been indifferent to a new kind of worker who can be sent somewhere.

The first productivity studies make the ambiguity easier to see.

In customer support, researchers Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond studied the staggered introduction of a generative AI assistant across more than five thousand support agents. The average productivity gain was meaningful, but the average hid the more interesting result: less experienced and lower-performing workers gained much more than the strongest workers. One interpretation is that the system carried pieces of expert practice toward people who had not yet accumulated it themselves.

That is a tool story, but it contains the seed of an agent story.

A novice who once had to remember the next step can have the next step suggested. A worker who once had to search the knowledge base can have relevant material surfaced. As the system gains the ability to execute those steps itself, assistance can become delegation without a clean moment of transition.

Other evidence warns against treating the direction as automatic. METR’s 2025 randomized study of experienced open-source developers found that developers using then-current AI tools took longer, not less time, on tasks in codebases they knew deeply. The participants expected the opposite. Even after the work, they believed the tools had sped them up.

That result belongs near the beginning of any serious book about the economics of agents because it interrupts an intoxicating assumption: observable activity is not the same as useful output.

An agent can write thousands of lines of code and create work.

It can conduct a long search and return a worse answer than a careful person would have produced in twenty minutes. It can cause the supervisor to spend an hour verifying a task that would have taken forty minutes to do directly. It can make progress look cheap by moving costs into review.

This is not a temporary embarrassment to be edited out of the story. It is part of the economics.

The productivity of an agentile system depends on the whole loop: instruction, execution, supervision, verification, correction, and consequence. If the execution cost falls while the verification cost rises faster, the organization has not become more productive. It has manufactured a larger inspection department.

The relationship can change quickly because models, interfaces, and user habits are all moving. A study of tools available in early 2025 is evidence about early 2025, not a permanent law. Vendor logs from 2026 are evidence about highly selected users of 2026, not a permanent law either.

The correct posture is neither worship nor dismissal.

It is to watch the tradeoff move.

Early industrial factories also contained machines that were spectacularly good at one operation and awkward everywhere else. Their value depended on redesigning the surrounding process. The electric motor did not transform manufacturing merely because electricity could spin a shaft. Factories had been arranged around steam-era power transmission. The gains from electrification arrived with organizational change: smaller motors, different layouts, new flows of material, altered supervision, and eventually new kinds of factory.

This pattern is so common that economists have a vocabulary for it. General-purpose technologies often require complementary investments before their measured productivity gains become obvious. The technology arrives. The organization is still shaped around the old constraint.

Agents expose an especially intimate version of this lag because the old constraint is often the human job itself.

A company buys an agent and inserts it into a workflow designed around one person doing one thing at a time.

The worker receives an AI assistant but remains responsible for manually copying its result into three systems.

The agent drafts code but cannot run the test suite.

It can run the tests but cannot access the deployment environment.

It can access the environment but every action requires a human approval that arrives through a queue checked twice a day.

The company then concludes that agents are disappointing.

Perhaps they are.

Or perhaps the organization has installed an electric motor and kept the leather belts.

The reverse mistake is just as easy. A firm can give an immature agent broad authority because the demo looked magical, discover that exception handling was the actual job, and spend months rebuilding controls around damage that did not need to occur.

Both errors come from thinking about an agent as a faster person.

It is not.

It has a different cost structure.

It can be copied. It can run in parallel. It does not become tired in the human sense, though performance can degrade for other reasons. It can hold enormous amounts of retrieved text in working context and still miss something obvious. It can be excellent at the median case and strange at the edge. It can produce evidence of its own work. It can also produce persuasive evidence that is wrong.

A useful economic comparison therefore needs more than “hours saved.”

It needs to ask what becomes possible when the worker has a different shape.

This worker can be instantiated at 2:00 a.m.

This worker can be deleted after the project.

This worker can be upgraded across the whole organization by changing a model or instruction.

This worker can make the same mistake a thousand times before anyone notices.

This worker can be monitored with a completeness no human employee would tolerate.

This worker can leave detailed logs and still conceal the important reason a decision was made.

This worker may have no durable identity between tasks unless the system gives it one.

This worker owns nothing, wants nothing, and can be turned off, yet acts through accounts and permissions that belong to someone who does.

Every one of those differences creates an economic advantage somewhere and a governance problem somewhere else.

The birth of the agentile economy is therefore not the appearance of artificial employees in cubicles.

It is the arrival of a new cost curve for getting things done.

At first, only a few errands fall beneath it.

Then more do.
