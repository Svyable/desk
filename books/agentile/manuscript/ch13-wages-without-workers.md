# Wages Without Workers

A payroll is a map of where an organization has decided human presence is worth paying for.

The map changes slowly until it does not.

A factory adds a machine and removes a station. A bank installs an ATM and changes what branches do. A company buys software and stops hiring clerks for a particular process. A retailer opens an online channel and adds warehouses while closing some stores. Technological change rarely subtracts one occupation cleanly and leaves the rest of the economy untouched. It changes prices, demand, complementary work, organizational structure, and the kinds of jobs that become worth creating.

Agents enter this history at an awkward angle because the work they touch is unusually close to what white-collar labor markets have treated as evidence of being human.

Write the memo. Analyze the spreadsheet. Answer the customer. Draft the code. Research the issue. Prepare the presentation. Schedule the work. Compare the documents. Follow up. Coordinate.

These are not all of a job, but they are parts of many jobs.

The first mistake is to confuse task exposure with job disappearance.

An occupation is a bundle. A nurse documents, observes, reassures, coordinates, moves, teaches, interprets, responds to emergencies, and performs regulated clinical acts. A lawyer researches, drafts, negotiates, advises, discovers facts, manages risk, appears before institutions, develops business, and assumes professional responsibility. A software engineer writes code, but also understands systems, makes tradeoffs, talks to users, responds to incidents, reviews other work, and decides what should be built.

An agent can become excellent at some components without making the bundle unnecessary.

The bundle can still change enough to matter.

Suppose half of an occupation's hours become much cheaper to perform. Several outcomes are possible.

The employer can produce the same output with fewer workers.

It can produce more output with the same workers.

It can lower prices and stimulate demand.

It can raise quality by doing work that was previously skipped.

It can redesign the job around the remaining tasks.

It can use the productivity gain to enter adjacent markets and hire different people.

It can capture the saving as profit.

Workers can bargain for some of the gain as higher wages or shorter hours.

Competitors can force the gain into lower prices before anyone enjoys a durable margin.

Labor-market outcomes are the result of this entire adjustment, not a benchmark score.

That is why early empirical work on AI and employment should be read with patience. Studies of exposure can tell us which occupations contain tasks models appear capable of performing. Usage data can tell us where people are actually applying the tools. Workplace experiments can estimate productivity effects on particular tasks. Employment data can reveal hiring, unemployment, wages, hours, and occupation shifts.

Each answers a different question.

The International Labour Organization's work on generative-AI exposure has emphasized that transformation is more likely than complete redundancy for many occupations. Anthropic's labor-market research has similarly tried to distinguish theoretical capability from observed use and, in early data, has not found a simple economy-wide unemployment shock concentrated exactly where exposure measures would predict, while noting possible weakness in hiring for some younger workers in exposed professions.

Those are snapshots during a moving technological transition.

They do not prove the future is safe.

They do prove that “the model can do a task” is not a labor-market statistic.

The hiring margin may move first.

An employer with fifty experienced employees does not need to fire twenty people the day agents become useful. It can stop replacing departures. It can hire fewer juniors. It can expect each new employee to carry more output. Headcount then changes gradually even if the production function changed quickly.

This is one reason young workers deserve special attention.

Entry-level jobs contain a high share of bounded work because beginners are not yet trusted with the ambiguous parts. Research, drafting, routine coding, preparation, monitoring, and documentation are exactly the places where agents can be useful. An organization that used to hire ten juniors to produce enough work for three seniors to review may discover that five juniors plus agents produce the same volume.

The immediate saving is obvious.

The long-term cost is not.

Five fewer juniors mean five fewer possible seniors later.

Companies can respond by changing training rather than preserving old staffing for nostalgia. But the transition can be rough because an individual graduating into the labor market cannot wait ten years for institutions to redesign apprenticeship.

This is where aggregate optimism can conceal local pain.

An economy can gain output while a cohort loses its first rung.

The second mistake is to assume automation always lowers wages for the people who remain.

If agents complement scarce expertise, the opposite can happen.

A radiologist who can safely review more cases, an engineer who can direct more implementation, an accountant who can supervise more clients, or a salesperson who can manage more revenue may become more valuable because the machine multiplies the reach of a scarce judgment.

The wage effect depends on whether the worker is a substitute for the agent or a complement to it and on how scarce the complementary skill remains.

This can increase inequality within occupations.

If routine execution becomes cheap while exceptional judgment commands more leverage, the middle of a profession can hollow. A small number of highly trusted people supervise large volumes of machine work while fewer humans perform the standardized tasks that once filled the pyramid.

Or the technology can compress differences by giving less experienced workers access to procedures and knowledge that help them reach competent performance. Customer-support research has found large productivity gains for less experienced workers using generative assistance, a reminder that a technology can reduce skill gaps on some tasks even as it increases returns to expertise on others.

Both patterns can coexist inside one job.

The junior writes a better first draft with an agent.

The senior becomes more productive because she can direct ten drafts at once and knows which one is wrong.

The distribution of value then depends on organizational design and bargaining.

There is a third mistake: treating wages as the only household effect.

If agents reduce the cost of goods and services, real income can rise even when nominal wages do not. Cheap translation, tutoring, software, tax preparation, administrative assistance, or legal information can create consumer surplus. A small firm can access capabilities previously affordable only to a large one. A person can accomplish tasks that would never have justified hiring a professional.

Those gains matter.

So does the way people get money to spend on them.

An economy cannot consume output in the aggregate. Households consume it. If a larger share of production is performed by capital owned narrowly, then the distribution of claims on output becomes politically salient even if total output rises.

This is the old automation question with a new surface area.

Industrial machines substituted for physical labor while creating enormous new industries and raising living standards over time. The transition also produced displacement, labor conflict, urban upheaval, new institutions, and long political fights over how the gains were shared.

There is no rule that a productive technology arrives with an equitable distribution contract attached.

Agents may create a particularly visible form of the problem because firms can purchase labor-like output without creating an employment relationship.

Imagine a company spends ten million dollars a year on model providers and agent infrastructure instead of ten million dollars on additional payroll. The money has not vanished. It becomes revenue for providers, wages for their employees, returns to shareholders and creditors, purchases of chips and energy, and taxes somewhere in the chain.

But the geography and ownership of the income changed.

A local professional salary circulates differently from a payment to a global compute supplier. A town that loses fifty office jobs may not care that the national economy gained efficiency if none of the new income arrives locally.

This is why the geography of agentile production will matter politically.

The fourth mistake is to imagine that every unit of machine work substitutes for a unit of human work.

Cheap capability creates demand.

When photography became cheaper, the number of photographs exploded. When computing became cheaper, society did not perform the same amount of computation with fewer people and stop. It embedded computation everywhere. When communication became cheaper, we produced more messages than prior generations could imagine.

Cognition may exhibit a similar rebound.

If a small business can afford daily financial analysis, it may consume analysis it never purchased from a human. If every student can receive individualized practice, tutoring volume can expand. If software becomes cheaper to produce, companies may create more internal tools. If legal document review falls in price, more contracts can be reviewed rather than ignored.

This new demand can support human complements.

The elasticity matters.

If the price of a service falls by half and demand more than doubles, employment in complementary roles can rise even as labor per unit falls. If demand is fixed, substitution dominates more easily. Economists have studied these mechanisms across technological changes because “labor-saving” at the task level does not mechanically imply labor-saving at the economy level.

Some markets are naturally bounded. A household needs only so many tax returns per year. Others can expand enormously. We can consume almost unlimited entertainment, software, research, education, design, and personalized service up to the constraint of attention and money.

Agents will affect each differently.

The fifth mistake is to ignore quality.

A company can replace work with a cheaper agent and call it productivity even if customers receive worse service. A worker can use AI to produce more code that later requires more maintenance. A school can generate more feedback that students stop reading. A health system can process more messages while clinicians spend more time fixing errors.

True productivity is output relative to inputs at a meaningful quality level.

This sounds elementary. It becomes difficult when the output is cognitive and quality is delayed.

A customer-support answer can be wrong in a way the ticket system never records. A financial analysis can influence a decision months later. A persuasive legal draft can contain a subtle defect. A piece of software can pass tests and accumulate security risk.

Labor replacement that depends on hidden quality degradation is not a productivity miracle.

It is borrowing from the future.

The reverse is possible too. An agent can make quality better by checking every case, enforcing consistent procedure, translating clearly, and giving employees more time for exceptions. Counting only labor hours saved can understate the benefit.

The economy will need better measures than “percent automated.”

The sixth mistake is to assume the labor market adjusts through a single firm.

Workers move. Industries emerge. Tasks migrate between occupations. New services become economical. A person displaced from one role can enter another, though the transition may require training, geography, time, and luck. The speed of technological change matters because adjustment is not instantaneous and people are not frictionless inputs.

A forty-five-year-old worker with a mortgage experiences “reallocation” differently from a chart.

Policy needs to remember the person while economics remembers the aggregate.

That can mean wage insurance, portable benefits, training tied to real employers, income support during transitions, stronger unemployment systems, mobility assistance, competition policy, and institutions that help workers share in productivity gains. The exact mix depends on what labor-market evidence actually shows rather than on a predetermined ideology.

There is no virtue in designing compensation for a mass unemployment event that does not occur.

There is no virtue in waiting for one to occur before building administrative capacity either.

The agentile economy gives governments the same problem companies face: act under uncertainty without confusing uncertainty with permission to do nothing.

One useful principle is to make transition support valuable even in less dramatic futures. Better training, portable credentials, easier business formation, portable benefits, and effective unemployment administration help workers through ordinary economic change too. Policies that are useful only if one precise AI scenario occurs are more fragile.

Another principle is to watch flows, not just stocks.

Employment levels can look stable while entry into a profession collapses. Wages can look stable while hours intensify. Productivity can rise while bargaining power falls. Vacancy postings, junior hiring, career ladders, occupational transitions, self-employment, and wage distributions can reveal changes before headline unemployment does.

The agentile labor market may announce itself through missing jobs rather than visible layoffs.

That makes history harder to photograph.

There is also a conceptual challenge around work performed directly for individuals.

If a household agent spends ten hours comparing services, organizing records, and resolving administrative problems, it has produced something that resembles unpaid household labor transformed into machine service. No worker was displaced because no worker was hired before. Yet the household has gained productive capacity.

Traditional economic statistics have always struggled with unpaid household production. Agents move some of it into market transactions for compute and software, making previously invisible work partly visible as expenditure.

This can make the economy look more productive because an activity acquired a price.

It can also genuinely make people better off by returning time.

The distinction between market production and welfare matters. A parent who spends less time fighting an insurance claim and more time with a child has gained something GDP measures poorly.

The birth of synthetic labor should therefore widen our question beyond jobs.

What kinds of human time become available?

Who controls that time?

Who receives the income produced by the machines?

Which forms of expertise become more valuable?

Which pathways into expertise shrink?

Which services become cheap enough to reach people who never had them?

Which communities lose payroll before new activity arrives?

These questions do not fit on one unemployment chart.

The phrase “wages without workers” points to the oddness at the center of the transition. An agent can perform activity that would once have justified a wage, but the payment goes elsewhere: to a model provider, a cloud, an owner, an energy producer, a software vendor, or the margin of the firm using it.

The work still has an economic value.

The worker is no longer the necessary vessel through which that value enters production.

This is not the end of wages.

It is the beginning of a bargaining problem over a new source of output.
