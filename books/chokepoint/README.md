The Hidden War for the Metals That Run the World.

The working thesis is that the twenty-first century is being rebuilt around a short list of elements — copper, lithium, cobalt, nickel, and the seventeen rare earths — the way the twentieth century was rebuilt around oil. Every AI data center, electric vehicle, wind turbine, and guided missile now depends on the same narrow set of inputs, mined in a handful of countries and, more importantly, refined in fewer still. Whoever controls the choke points in that chain — a single Chinese processing region, a Congolese copper belt, an Indonesian smelter park, one Chilean mine descending two kilometers under the Andes — controls a lever that outlasts any single election cycle or export ban.

The book resists two easy stories. It does not argue that a mineral cartel is about to seize the global economy the way OPEC once did; the analogy holds in places and fails in others, and the failures matter. Nor does it argue that markets and substitution will quietly solve the problem, the way optimists assumed cheap shale would solve the oil problem. Instead it traces the actual machinery: the geology that concentrates certain elements in certain places, the capital cycle that makes new mines slow to arrive even at high prices, the refining chemistry that China spent forty years building and the rest of the world is only now trying to relearn, and the human and environmental costs paid by the countries that hold the ore but not the leverage.

The aim is not to predict which country wins the mineral race. It is to make the chokepoints visible before the next shortage does it for us.
