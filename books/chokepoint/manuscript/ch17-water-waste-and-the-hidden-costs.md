# Water, Waste, and the Hidden Costs

At 12:28 in the afternoon on January 25, 2019, a tailings dam at Vale's Córrego do Feijão iron-ore mine near Brumadinho, Brazil, failed with extraordinary speed.

There was no long public countdown. No earthquake announced the moment. The dam slope collapsed in seconds. An expert panel later estimated that about 9.7 million cubic metres of material escaped, roughly three-quarters of what the impoundment held, and that most of it left within minutes.

The mud moved through an operating mine complex and into the landscape below. The retrospective death toll recorded by the government of Minas Gerais is 272.

A number like that can become an argument too easily. It can be recruited as proof that mining is uniquely reckless, or answered with the observation that modern life requires mines. Neither response explains much about what failed.

Brumadinho matters because the disaster exposes something harder than a morality play. The physical system had a history. The tailings were loose and saturated. The dam had been raised upstream, a construction method in which later stages are built partly over previously deposited tailings. Water conditions, drainage, deformation, and the mechanical behavior of the stored material all mattered. The expert investigation described a sudden slope failure associated with liquefaction. Brazil's mining regulator later said that anomalies appearing in Vale's internal records had not been correctly reported to the agency.

That does not mean everybody looking at the dam knew it was about to collapse. It means a high-consequence system contained information whose significance was uncertain until the uncertainty disappeared in the worst possible way.

This is a recurring problem in dangerous industries. A warning can be ambiguous before an accident and obvious afterward. Institutions have to decide how much production to interrupt, how much investigation to demand, and how much money to spend when the probability of catastrophe is unclear but the consequence is enormous.

Mining adds a particular difficulty: the waste does not go away when the useful material leaves the gate.

An ore body is not a warehouse of pure metal. It is rock containing a valuable mineral at a concentration high enough, under the right prices and technology, to justify extracting it. The mine removes enormous quantities of material, crushes and grinds rock, separates what it wants, and is left with what it does not.

Some waste remains as coarse rock. Some becomes finely ground tailings mixed with water and processing residues. Those tailings have to be managed somewhere, sometimes for longer than the mine itself will operate.

The saleable product can cross an ocean. The waste stays home.

That asymmetry sits underneath much of the mineral transition. Copper may end up in a transmission line in Germany. Lithium may end up in a battery in California. Nickel may end up in a cell assembled in Korea. The physical disturbances that made those products possible remain concentrated around mines, processing plants, roads, ports, waste facilities, and water systems.

The geography of benefit and the geography of burden are rarely identical.

Brumadinho is an extreme case, which makes it useful and dangerous as an example. Extreme cases reveal failure modes, but they can distort the ordinary record if they are made to stand for every mine. Most tailings facilities do not collapse. Engineering practice can improve. Regulation can improve. Monitoring can improve. Designs can change.

After Brumadinho, the United Nations Environment Programme, the Principles for Responsible Investment, and the International Council on Mining and Metals helped produce the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management. Launched in 2020, it set requirements spanning affected communities, human rights, environmental protection, governance, independent oversight, emergency response, and public disclosure. In 2025, the same institutional effort produced an independent institute intended to assess implementation of the standard.

That is not cosmetic counterevidence. Catastrophe can change practice.

The mistake would be to treat better practice as proof that the underlying problem has been eliminated. A safer tailings facility still contains waste. Monitoring still has to be interpreted. Independent assurance still depends on competent people and institutions. A standard still has to survive contact with cost pressure, production targets, organizational hierarchy, and the ordinary human desire to believe that a system which worked yesterday will work tomorrow.

The more interesting question is not whether mining can be made impact-free. It cannot. The question is which impacts can be reduced, which can be shifted, and which cannot be undone.

The distinction matters because the energy transition is creating an awkward political habit. Clean technologies are often described at the point where they operate. An electric car has no tailpipe. A wind turbine burns no fuel while generating electricity. A battery can store power generated without combustion. Those are real advantages.

But a technology can be clean in operation and material-intensive in construction.

The minerals have an upstream life.

Copper begins with rock moved and processed. Lithium begins with brine or hard rock and a chemical chain. Nickel can carry the energy signature of the power system feeding its processing. Rare earths require separation chemistry that Chapter 8 showed is itself a strategic bottleneck. Every improvement in the bill of materials changes the upstream burden without making materiality disappear.

Chapter 16 ended with substitution for exactly this reason. Replacing cobalt with iron and phosphorus changes the chain. It does not abolish one.

Water makes this visible.

Mines need water for different purposes depending on the deposit and process: grinding, separation, dust control, transport, washing, cooling, and the management of tailings. The important number is not simply how much water a mine uses. Source matters. Location matters. Recycling matters. Competing demand matters. So does what happens after the water passes through the process.

A cubic metre withdrawn in a wet region is not equivalent to a cubic metre withdrawn in an arid basin. A litre of seawater is not the same political object as a litre of groundwater used by farms and communities. Gross withdrawal and net consumption are not interchangeable. A serious mineral policy has to care about those distinctions even when a headline does not.

Chile shows how quickly the engineering response can change the story.

Copper mining is central to the Chilean economy, and many of its major deposits sit in extraordinarily dry parts of the country. That combination makes water a production constraint, an environmental issue, and a political one. For years the easiest version of the story was that more copper meant more pressure on scarce continental water.

The industry has been changing its source mix.

In its 2026 outlook, Chile's copper commission, Cochilco, projected total water use by copper mining to rise from 18.5 cubic metres per second in 2024 to 20.6 in 2034. Yet it projected seawater to provide 67.6 percent of sector water use by the end of that period.

That is a genuine technological and infrastructure response. It matters because it weakens a static scarcity argument. A mining region is not condemned to use the same water source forever. Desalination and direct seawater use can reduce pressure on continental sources. Process water can be reused. New projects can be designed around constraints that older operations treated as external.

But the constraint has not vanished. It has changed form.

The Pacific is downhill from mines high in the Andes.

Water taken from the coast may need treatment and pumping through pipelines over long distances and large elevation gains. Plants, pipelines, pumping stations, rights of way, electricity, maintenance, and redundancy become part of the mineral system. Cochilco's electricity outlook projects copper-mining electricity consumption rising from 27.6 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 33.2 in 2034, with increased seawater use among the forces pushing demand upward.

The solution to one environmental constraint creates an infrastructure and energy bill somewhere else.

That does not make the solution fraudulent. Quite the opposite. It makes it real.

A mature account of industrial environmental performance has to permit improvement without demanding magic. If a mine replaces scarce freshwater with seawater, that is an improvement on the water dimension. The fact that pumping consumes electricity does not cancel it. It tells us to examine the electricity system next.

This is how environmental burdens behave in engineered systems. They move through tradeoffs.

A dry tailings design may reduce water stored in a facility while requiring more filtration and material handling. Higher recovery can extract more useful metal from ore while consuming additional energy or reagents. Lower ore grades can make a deposit economically valuable while increasing the amount of rock moved per tonne of product. A process that reduces one hazardous chemical may need more of another input. Recycling can reduce demand for virgin material while creating its own collection, transport, disassembly, and refining chain.

The honest comparison is rarely good versus bad.

It is one burden against another, under a set of physical constraints.

This is why declining ore grade matters even when it is not dramatic enough for a geopolitical headline. If the concentration of valuable material in the rock falls, producing the same amount of metal generally requires moving and processing more material, all else equal. More material can mean more energy, more waste, more equipment movement, and larger facilities. Technology can offset some of this. Better sensing, sorting, recovery, automation, and process control can make lower-grade deposits workable. But making a resource economically recoverable is not the same as making its physical footprint irrelevant.

The industry knows this. Mine plans are exercises in optimization under constraint: grade, recovery, strip ratio, energy, water, geotechnical stability, capital, operating cost, environmental obligations, and time. The public argument often flattens that complexity into two incompatible caricatures.

One says that mining companies simply dig holes wherever they please and leave everyone else with the damage.

The other says that because modern civilization needs minerals, objections to individual projects are sentimental obstacles to progress.

Both stories remove the decisions.

A mine can be necessary in aggregate and badly designed in particular. A community can oppose a project for rational reasons even if the mineral it would produce is strategically important. A regulator can impose a condition that raises costs and still improve the project's economics once catastrophic risk is priced properly. A company can spend heavily on environmental mitigation and still leave irreversible losses.

The word externality is useful because it identifies a cost not fully captured in a transaction. It can also become anesthetic. Tailings are not an externality to the people living downstream. Water drawdown is not external to a farmer whose well changes. Dust is not external to the lungs receiving it. A damaged wetland is not external to a community whose food, work, or cultural practice depends on it.

From a distant balance sheet, these effects appear as liabilities, permitting conditions, remediation provisions, insurance questions, or delays.

From nearby, they are geography.

That difference helps explain why mineral projects that look indispensable from a national capital can look intolerable at the mine site. The benefits are dispersed through jobs, tax receipts, industrial capacity, national security, cleaner electricity, and products consumed far away. The most immediate risks are concentrated.

The mismatch does not prove that local opposition should always prevail. It explains why the politics are durable.

A national government can say, correctly, that copper is needed for grids. A mining company can say, correctly, that a project meets a growing demand. A community can say, correctly, that the proposed waste facility sits above its watershed. Those statements can all be true at once.

The conflict begins when one scale of truth is used to erase another.

This is especially important for the rhetoric of sacrifice zones. The phrase captures something real: industrial systems have repeatedly concentrated pollution and danger in places with less political power. But it can also imply that every impact is intentionally imposed on a powerless community and that no mitigation changes the moral calculation. The record is more varied. Mines operate under radically different legal regimes. Communities contain disagreement. Some residents want jobs. Some oppose the project. Some want it under different terms. Indigenous nations are governments and rights holders, not a single interest category. Benefits can be substantial and still be distributed unfairly.

The harder work is institutional design.

Who gets information before a project is approved? Who can say no? Who negotiates? Who receives royalties or tax revenue? Who pays for monitoring after production stops? How much financial assurance exists if a company fails? Who owns the data from environmental sensors? What happens when scientific uncertainty remains? Who bears the cost of a delay caused by caution, and who bears the cost of a disaster caused by haste?

Brumadinho sits at the severe end of that spectrum because the cost of failure became irreversible almost instantly.

The aftermath also shows the limits of money as a repair mechanism.

The 2021 judicial reparation agreement involving Vale and public authorities in Minas Gerais was valued at roughly R$37.7 billion. It supports compensation, recovery obligations, and public projects. That scale matters. It is evidence that accountability and remediation are not merely rhetorical.

It is also evidence of a category problem.

Some losses can be compensated. Infrastructure can be rebuilt. Environmental restoration can proceed. Income can be replaced in part. Monitoring can continue for years.

A dead person cannot be restored.

The difference sounds obvious, but industrial accounting encourages us to put unlike things into the same column. Expected losses can be modeled. Provisions can be booked. Insurance can transfer some financial risk. A settlement can attach a currency value to obligations after catastrophe.

None of those tools means every loss is fungible.

The practical consequence is that prevention deserves a different status when the downside is irreversible. If a failure can be repaired cheaply, organizations can rationally tolerate more uncertainty. If failure can kill hundreds of people or permanently damage a watershed, the threshold for accepting ambiguity should be different.

This is not a mining principle. It is a high-consequence-system principle.

The difficulty is keeping it alive during ordinary operations, when nothing has failed for years and every shutdown has an immediate cost.

Safety culture is easy to admire after a disaster. Before one, it often looks like friction.

The same is true of environmental safeguards. A redundant monitoring system costs money before it proves useful. A larger buffer zone sterilizes land that could otherwise be developed. A conservative water plan may require expensive infrastructure years before scarcity becomes acute. Independent review can delay a schedule. Emergency planning consumes time for an event everyone hopes will never happen.

The absence of catastrophe makes these expenditures look inefficient right up to the moment it makes them look inadequate.

That tension will intensify as the world asks mining to expand faster.

The demand case laid out earlier in this book creates pressure for shorter permitting timelines, faster financing, new refining capacity, more exploration, and more projects in jurisdictions that want a share of the mineral boom. Some of that acceleration is reasonable. A permitting system that takes years to answer basic procedural questions can block both industry and environmental improvement. Slow is not automatically careful.

Fast is not automatically reckless either.

The useful distinction is between eliminating idle process and eliminating scrutiny.

A government can staff agencies better, set clear deadlines, collect data once instead of repeatedly, coordinate reviews, and give developers earlier certainty. Those reforms can shorten timelines without pretending geology, hydrology, community rights, and waste management are paperwork.

The mineral race creates incentives to blur the distinction. A project delayed by environmental review can be described as a strategic vulnerability. Sometimes it is. A project approved without resolving a serious tailings or water problem can create another kind of strategic vulnerability, one measured in cleanup, litigation, public opposition, and lost legitimacy.

A supply chain is not resilient if its production depends on communities eventually refusing to tolerate it.

This is where the hidden costs become strategic costs.

A mine that loses its social license can strand capital. A tailings failure can shut operations and change regulation across an industry. A water conflict can constrain expansion. Pollution can turn an allied supplier into a political liability. A project that looks cheap because some costs sit outside the commodity price may be expensive once those costs return through lawsuits, remediation, delays, taxes, or political backlash.

Cheap minerals can be expensive in ways the spot market does not record promptly.

The reverse is also true. A project with higher upfront environmental costs may produce a more durable supply chain. Desalination can raise capital and electricity requirements while reducing competition for freshwater. Better waste management can raise operating costs while lowering catastrophic risk. More consultation can lengthen development while reducing the probability that unresolved conflict explodes later.

There is no guarantee. Money spent under an environmental label can be wasted too. Consultation can become procedural theater. Certification can become a logo. Monitoring can produce data nobody acts on.

Institutions still have to work.

That is the uncomfortable conclusion Brumadinho leaves behind. The physical cause of a failure can be investigated with extraordinary technical precision after the fact. The institutional cause is harder because it is distributed through reporting, incentives, interpretation, authority, and decisions made under uncertainty.

The dam failed in seconds.

The system that made those seconds possible had been accumulating for years.

The mineral transition will build many more systems whose consequences accumulate slowly: pits that deepen, waste that remains, aquifers that respond, pipelines that climb mountains, refineries that consume power, communities that renegotiate what development is worth. Most will not become disasters. Some will improve on the practices that came before them. The difference will depend less on whether mining is declared clean or dirty than on whether its costs are made visible early enough to shape the design.

That visibility changes the next question.

If a country bears the waste, water demand, infrastructure burden, and environmental risk of supplying the mineral economy, what does it receive in return?

A mine can create wages, taxes, exports, infrastructure, skills, and industrial capability. It can also create an enclave whose profits leave while the liabilities stay. The answer is not contained in the ore body.

It is contained in institutions.
