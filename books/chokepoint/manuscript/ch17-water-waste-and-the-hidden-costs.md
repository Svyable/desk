# Water, Waste, and the Hidden Costs

At 12:28 in the afternoon on January 25, 2019, a tailings dam at Vale's Córrego do Feijão iron-ore mine near Brumadinho, Brazil, failed with extraordinary speed.

There was no long public countdown. The dam slope collapsed in seconds. An expert panel later estimated that about 9.7 million cubic metres of material escaped, roughly three-quarters of what the impoundment held, and that most of it left within minutes.

The mud moved through an operating mine complex and into the landscape below. The retrospective death toll recorded by the government of Minas Gerais is 272.

A number like that can become an argument too easily. It can be recruited as proof that mining is uniquely reckless or answered with the observation that modern life requires mines.

Neither response explains what failed.

The tailings were loose and saturated. The dam had been raised upstream, with later stages built partly over previously deposited material. Water conditions, drainage, deformation, and the mechanical behavior of the tailings all mattered. The expert investigation described a sudden slope failure associated with liquefaction. Brazil's mining regulator later said anomalies appearing in Vale's internal records had not been correctly reported to the agency.

That does not mean everyone looking at the dam knew it was about to collapse. It means a high-consequence system contained ambiguous information until the ambiguity disappeared in the worst possible way.

Mining adds another difficulty: the waste remains after the valuable material leaves.

An orebody is not a warehouse of pure metal. Rock is moved, crushed, ground, and processed until a much smaller valuable fraction can be sold. Coarse waste stays. Finely ground tailings stay. Water and processing residues have to be managed, sometimes for far longer than the mine will operate.

The saleable product can cross an ocean.

The waste stays home.

That asymmetry runs through the mineral transition. Copper may end up in a German transmission line. Lithium may end up in a California battery. Nickel may end up in a Korean cell. The physical disturbance remains concentrated around the mine, processing plant, roads, ports, waste facilities, and water system.

The geography of benefit and the geography of burden are rarely identical.

Brumadinho is an extreme case and should not stand in for every mine. Most tailings facilities do not collapse. Engineering improves. Regulation can improve. Monitoring and design can improve.

After Brumadinho, the United Nations Environment Programme, the Principles for Responsible Investment, and the International Council on Mining and Metals helped produce the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management. Launched in 2020, it set requirements spanning affected communities, human rights, environmental protection, governance, independent oversight, emergency response, and disclosure. In 2025, the same effort produced an independent institute intended to assess implementation.

Catastrophe can change practice.

A safer tailings facility still contains waste. A standard still has to survive cost pressure, production targets, organizational hierarchy, and the ordinary human desire to believe that a system which worked yesterday will work tomorrow.

The useful question is not whether mining can be made impact-free. It cannot. It is which impacts can be reduced, which can be shifted, and which cannot be undone.

Water makes the tradeoffs visible.

Mines use water for grinding, separation, dust control, transport, washing, cooling, and tailings management. The important number is not simply total withdrawal. Source matters. Location matters. Recycling matters. Competing demand matters. So does what happens after water passes through the process.

A cubic metre withdrawn in a wet region is not equivalent to one withdrawn in an arid basin. Seawater is not the same political object as groundwater shared with farms and communities. Gross withdrawal and net consumption are not interchangeable.

Chile shows how quickly the engineering response can change a static scarcity story.

Many of its major copper deposits sit in extraordinarily dry parts of the country. For years, the easy story was that more copper meant more pressure on scarce continental water.

The industry has been changing its source mix.

In its 2026 outlook, Chile's copper commission, Cochilco, projected total water use by copper mining to rise from 18.5 cubic metres per second in 2024 to 20.6 in 2034. Yet it projected seawater to provide 67.6 percent of sector water use by the end of that period.

That is genuine improvement on the freshwater dimension.

It is not the disappearance of constraint.

The Pacific is downhill from mines high in the Andes.

Water taken from the coast can require desalination or treatment and pumping through long pipelines over enormous elevation gains. Plants, pumping stations, rights of way, electricity, maintenance, and backup systems become part of the copper supply chain. Cochilco's electricity outlook projects mining consumption rising from 27.6 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 33.2 in 2034, with increased seawater use among the drivers.

The burden migrated from scarce continental water toward infrastructure and energy.

That does not make the solution fraudulent. It makes it real.

Industrial environmental performance often improves through tradeoffs rather than escape. Dry tailings may reduce water stored in a facility while requiring more filtration and handling. Higher recovery can extract more metal while consuming more energy or reagents. Lower ore grades can be made economic through better sensing, sorting, recovery, and automation while still requiring more physical throughput.

A clean-technology supply chain is still a supply chain.

The honest comparison is rarely good versus bad. It is one burden against another under physical constraints.

That is why environmental arguments become weak when they flatten individual projects into morality plays. A mine can be necessary in aggregate and badly designed in particular. A community can oppose a strategically important project for rational local reasons. A regulator can impose a costly condition that improves project economics once catastrophic risk is priced properly. A company can spend heavily on mitigation and still leave irreversible losses.

The word externality can become anesthetic here.

Tailings are not external to people downstream. Water drawdown is not external to a farmer whose well changes. Dust is not external to the lungs receiving it. A damaged wetland is not external to a community whose food, work, or cultural practice depends on it.

From a distant balance sheet, these effects appear as liabilities, permitting conditions, remediation provisions, or delays.

From nearby, they are geography.

That difference explains why a mineral project that looks indispensable from a national capital can look intolerable at the mine site. The benefits are dispersed through jobs, taxes, industrial capacity, national security, cleaner electricity, and products consumed elsewhere. The most immediate risks are concentrated.

A national government can say, correctly, that copper is needed for grids. A mining company can say, correctly, that demand is rising. A community can say, correctly, that the proposed waste facility sits above its watershed.

The conflict begins when one scale of truth is used to erase another.

This is also why local opposition cannot be treated as one thing. Some residents want jobs. Some oppose the project. Some want it under different terms. Indigenous nations are governments and rights holders, not a single interest category.

The hard work is institutional: who gets information before approval, who negotiates, who receives royalties or tax revenue, who pays for monitoring after production stops, how much financial assurance exists if the company fails, who owns environmental data, and what happens when uncertainty remains.

Brumadinho sits at the severe end of that spectrum because the cost of failure became irreversible almost instantly.

The 2021 judicial reparation agreement involving Vale and public authorities in Minas Gerais was valued at roughly R$37.7 billion and supports compensation, recovery obligations, and public projects.

That scale is evidence that accountability and remediation can be substantial.

It is also evidence of a category problem.

Infrastructure can be rebuilt. Income can be compensated in part. Environmental restoration can proceed. A dead person cannot be restored.

When the downside is irreversible, prevention deserves a different status. If failure can kill hundreds of people or permanently damage a watershed, the threshold for tolerating ambiguous warning signals should be different from the threshold for a failure that can be repaired cheaply.

The difficulty is keeping that principle alive during ordinary operations, when nothing has failed for years and every shutdown has an immediate cost.

Safety culture looks like friction before an accident.

Environmental safeguards often do too.

A redundant monitoring system costs money before it proves useful. A larger buffer zone sterilizes land. A conservative water plan may require expensive infrastructure before scarcity becomes acute. Independent review can delay a schedule.

The absence of catastrophe makes these measures look inefficient right up to the moment it makes them look inadequate.

That tension will intensify as governments push mining to expand faster.

Some acceleration is reasonable. A permitting system that takes years to answer basic procedural questions can block both supply and environmental improvement. Better staffing, clearer deadlines, coordinated review, and earlier data collection can shorten timelines without pretending geology, hydrology, community rights, and waste management are paperwork.

The useful distinction is between eliminating idle process and eliminating scrutiny.

A project delayed by environmental review can create strategic risk. A project rushed past a serious tailings or water problem can create another kind of strategic risk measured in cleanup, litigation, shutdowns, opposition, and lost legitimacy.

A supply chain is not resilient if its production depends on communities eventually refusing to tolerate it.

This is where hidden costs become strategic costs.

A mine that loses social license can strand capital. A tailings failure can shut operations and change regulation across an industry. A water conflict can constrain expansion. Pollution can turn an allied supplier into a political liability. A project that looks cheap because costs sit outside the commodity price may become expensive when they return through lawsuits, remediation, taxes, delays, or political backlash.

The reverse is also true. A higher-cost project may be more durable. Desalination can increase capital and electricity requirements while reducing freshwater conflict. Better waste management can raise operating costs and lower catastrophic risk. More consultation can lengthen development and reduce the chance that unresolved conflict explodes later.

There is no guarantee. Consultation can become theater. Certification can become a logo. Monitoring can produce data nobody acts on.

Institutions still have to work.

That is the uncomfortable lesson Brumadinho leaves behind. The physical cause of failure can be investigated with extraordinary technical precision after the fact. The institutional cause is harder because it is distributed through reporting, incentives, interpretation, authority, and decisions made under uncertainty.

The dam failed in seconds.

The system that made those seconds possible accumulated for years.

The mineral transition will build many more systems whose consequences accumulate slowly: pits that deepen, waste that remains, aquifers that respond, pipelines that climb mountains, refineries that consume power, communities that renegotiate what development is worth.

Most will not become disasters. Some will improve materially on what came before them. The difference will depend less on declaring mining clean or dirty than on making costs visible early enough to shape the design.

That visibility leads to the next question.

If a country bears the waste, water demand, infrastructure burden, and environmental risk of supplying the mineral economy, what does it receive in return?

A mine can create wages, taxes, exports, infrastructure, skills, and industrial capability. It can also create an enclave whose profits leave while the liabilities stay.

The answer is not contained in the orebody.

It is contained in institutions.
