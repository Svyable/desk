# The Lithium Triangle

Lithium taught the mineral market a lesson it should have remembered from every commodity boom before it.

Scarcity can be real and temporary at the same time.

In 2021 and 2022, battery demand and investor enthusiasm pushed lithium prices sharply higher. Automakers signed direct supply agreements with miners. Governments began talking about lithium in the same register they used for semiconductors and oil. Developers revived projects that had looked marginal. Exploration companies discovered that the word lithium could do useful work in a capital raise.

Then supply arrived.

Prices fell hard through 2023 and 2024. By the first half of 2025, the U.S. Geological Survey still described the market as weighed down by concern about short-term oversupply. Producers curtailed output. Expansions slowed. Projects that had looked inevitable at the top of the cycle became negotiable again.

The battery transition had not stopped. Global lithium consumption rose again in 2025, by roughly 20 percent according to the USGS, while the International Energy Agency still projected demand rising more than threefold by 2040 under stated policies.

Demand was growing.

Supply had simply grown faster for a while.

That is the important distinction. A mineral does not have to be permanently scarce to become strategically important. A few years of mismatch can change industrial plans, financing decisions, and national policy long before geology becomes the limiting factor.

Lithium makes that mismatch unusually visible because its supply comes from very different kinds of deposits.

In Western Australia, lithium is commonly mined from spodumene-bearing hard rock: drill, blast, crush, concentrate, ship. The concentrate then has to be chemically converted into lithium compounds suitable for batteries.

In parts of Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia, the starting point is brine beneath salt flats. Producers pump lithium-bearing fluid to the surface and concentrate it through evaporation and processing. The traditional method can take months and operates in landscapes where water is already scarce and the hydrology is more complicated than the word desert suggests. A salt flat that looks dry can be part of a connected system of brines, lagoons, freshwater flows, wetlands, and habitats.

The phrase Lithium Triangle makes the region sound more unified than it is.

Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia share geology. They do not share an industrial model.

Chile became a major producer through large Salar de Atacama operations inside a tightly controlled legal regime. Argentina gave more room to provincial governments and private developers and has moved from perennial future supplier toward actual production growth. Bolivia became famous for enormous resources while repeatedly demonstrating the distance between a resource estimate and commercial output.

The triangle is three political economies sitting on related geology.

That difference helps explain why lithium is both concentrated and surprisingly responsive. Australia, Chile, China, Argentina, Zimbabwe, and newer producers have added or advanced supply in a market that looked much narrower only a few years ago.

This is the counterargument the scarcity story has to absorb.

High prices worked.

They did not create lithium atoms. They changed which deposits, expansions, chemical plants, financing packages, and national strategies made economic sense. Capital moved toward the bottleneck. The response was uneven, but it was real.

Response is not the same thing as resilience.

The steps in the chain do not expand together. Mining can grow faster than conversion capacity. Conversion can grow faster than qualified battery-grade supply. A new producer can miss nameplate targets. A refinery can make a chemical product that exists physically and still fail a customer's impurity specifications.

That is why China belongs in a chapter named for South America.

The IEA's 2025 lithium analysis estimated that the three largest mining countries supplied about 77 percent of mined lithium in 2024, while the top three refining countries accounted for roughly 96 percent of refining. Mining concentration was falling faster than refining concentration.

A country can diversify where lithium is dug or pumped without diversifying where it becomes usable battery material.

The customer does not buy the word lithium. It buys a chemical at the right purity, delivered on time.

During a boom, every tonne can look equally valuable. During a bust, quality, cost, contracts, and processing routes come roaring back.

That made the post-2022 price collapse an industrial filter. Higher-cost operations came under pressure. Expansion plans slowed. Governments that had imagined a permanent seller's market were reminded that a strategic mineral could still behave like a commodity.

Chile's response shows how awkward that can become.

In 2023, President Gabriel Boric's government announced a National Lithium Strategy seeking a larger state role in future development while keeping private capital and technical partners in the system. Codelco moved into lithium through partnerships rather than trying to recreate the industry alone, reaching an agreement with SQM around the Salar de Atacama and selecting Rio Tinto as a partner for Salar de Maricunga, where Codelco retained a slight majority stake and the project was planned around direct lithium extraction.

DLE is often described as an escape from the evaporation ponds and long residence times associated with conventional brine production. In principle, it can extract lithium more selectively, reduce surface evaporation, and improve recovery speed.

The environmental promise is not automatic.

A DLE process can still require energy, chemicals, freshwater, reinjection systems, and careful management of altered brines. Different salars have different chemistry. Reinjection may reduce one burden while introducing another question: how does the subsurface reservoir respond over decades?

The right question is not whether DLE is good. It is what a specific process does to a specific hydrological system.

That is also what local communities have been asking.

In the Atacama, Indigenous communities have pressed for greater participation and better understanding of cumulative impacts from lithium and copper operations. Their concern is not an abstraction about batteries. It arrives as pumps, wells, roads, evaporation ponds, groundwater models, and promises about water that is difficult to observe directly.

A mineral market sees lithium carbonate equivalent.

A community sees water.

The benefits are real too. Lithium can bring tax revenue, royalties, jobs, infrastructure, technical training, export earnings, and bargaining power. Argentina has made that possibility especially visible as projects entered production in provinces hungry for investment and foreign exchange.

A rich-country discussion of responsible sourcing can forget that a mine is also somebody else's development strategy. The opposite error is to treat national revenue as proof that local costs are fairly shared.

Bolivia shows another version of the problem.

For years, the Salar de Uyuni has been described as one of the world's great lithium resources. Yet production remained limited. The brine chemistry is challenging. Infrastructure is difficult. Partnerships and policy changed. Governments tried to capture more domestic value. Political instability complicated execution.

The resource existed the whole time.

The supply did not.

That distinction is more useful than any headline reserve number.

A resource is not supply. A reserve is not a mine. A mine is not a chemical plant. A chemical plant is not a qualified battery supply chain.

The boom compressed all of those steps into one word: shortage.

The bust compressed them into another: glut.

Both descriptions were true for moments. Neither described the system.

The system is a portfolio of projects with different costs, geologies, contracts, political risks, chemistries, and clocks. When prices rose high enough, marginal projects became financeable. When enough of them entered the market, the shortage eased. When prices fell, the next layer of marginal supply became harder to finance.

The commodity cycle is not noise around the strategic story. It is part of the strategic story.

Governments often discover critical minerals near the top of a price cycle, when companies are loud and shortage forecasts look obvious. They announce subsidies, strategic partnerships, permitting reforms, and domestic-content rules.

Then prices fall.

The projects they wanted for security become less attractive precisely because the market appears better supplied. If policy support disappears with the price, the next shortage can be prepared during the period when nobody feels short.

Lithium's recent history is therefore a warning against designing resilience policy around spot prices.

A low price can mean supply responded. It can also mean projects needed later are being canceled now.

The IEA's 2026 outlook captures that tension: the expected 2035 lithium gap narrowed as more projects advanced. That is good news, and it is conditional on those projects being built and surviving the next downturn.

Forecasts contain financing assumptions even when they look like geology.

Demand can move too. Lithium iron phosphate batteries avoid nickel and cobalt. Sodium-ion batteries could reduce lithium demand at the margin if they become competitive across more applications. The battery industry is not a passive consumer waiting for miners to save it. It redesigns around constraints.

That flexibility weakens the strongest scarcity story without eliminating lithium's importance. Use less material per unit, build far more units, and total demand can still rise.

By 2025, lithium prices had begun recovering from their lows, and the IEA reported a sharper rebound into early 2026, helped by storage demand and tighter supply conditions.

The direction changed again.

Anyone asking a single lithium price to summarize the future is asking the market for certainty it does not possess.

What matters is the amplitude. Boom makes scarcity look permanent. Bust makes abundance look permanent. Projects are financed inside those beliefs.

A resilient lithium system needs enough confidence in long-run demand to keep useful projects alive through low prices and enough skepticism to avoid financing every deposit that acquires a strategic label.

Neither markets nor states solve that neatly. States can overpay for security. Markets can underinvest in redundancy. Producers can game subsidies. Communities can be promised participation after the important decisions have already been made.

Lithium's strategic lesson is not that the world is running out.

It is that the world can build itself into and out of a shortage faster than the politics around the shortage can adjust.

Copper made scarcity look slow and structural. Lithium made it look cyclical.

Cobalt is different again. Its mine supply is concentrated in one country to a degree that makes lithium look diversified, and the central question is not how fast supply can grow.

It is who can turn concentration into power—and who pays when they try.
