# The Lithium Triangle

Lithium taught the mineral market a lesson it should have remembered from every commodity boom before it.

Scarcity can be real and temporary at the same time.

In 2021 and 2022, the battery industry behaved as though lithium had been discovered to be rare only after electric vehicles became popular. Prices surged. Automakers signed direct supply agreements with miners. Governments began talking about lithium in the same register they used for semiconductors and oil. Developers dusted off projects that had looked marginal a year earlier. Exploration companies with little more than acreage and a presentation deck discovered that the word lithium could do useful work in a capital raise.

Then supply arrived.

Prices fell hard through 2023 and 2024. By the first half of 2025, the U.S. Geological Survey described the market as still weighed down by concern about short-term oversupply. Some producers curtailed output. Some expansions were postponed. Projects that had looked inevitable at the top of the cycle became negotiable again.

The collapse did not mean the battery transition had stopped.

Global lithium consumption rose again in 2025, by roughly 20 percent according to the USGS. Electric-vehicle sales remained strong in China and Europe. Grid batteries became a larger source of demand. The International Energy Agency still projects lithium demand rising more than threefold by 2040 under its stated-policies scenario.

Demand was growing.

Supply had simply grown faster for a while.

That distinction matters because it changes the meaning of a chokepoint.

A mineral does not have to be permanently scarce to become strategically important. It only has to be difficult enough to expand, concentrated enough geographically, and volatile enough financially that a few years of mismatch can alter industrial plans.

Lithium is unusually good at producing that mismatch because its supply comes from two very different kinds of geology.

One is hard rock.

The other is brine.

In Western Australia, lithium is commonly mined from spodumene-bearing hard-rock deposits. The work looks recognizably like mining: drill, blast, crush, concentrate, ship. The resulting spodumene concentrate can then be chemically converted into lithium compounds suitable for batteries.

In the high desert of South America, the starting point can look almost like the opposite of a mine.

Beneath salt flats in parts of Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia sits underground brine carrying dissolved lithium. Producers pump that brine to the surface and concentrate it through a combination of evaporation and processing. The traditional process can take months and depends on a landscape where water is already scarce, altitude is punishing, and the hydrology is complicated enough that the phrase desert can mislead. A salt flat may look dry while being part of a connected system of brines, lagoons, freshwater flows, wetlands, and habitats that local communities depend on.

The famous phrase Lithium Triangle makes the region sound more unified than it is.

Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia share geology.

They do not share an industrial model.

Chile became a major producer through large operations in the Salar de Atacama and a tightly controlled legal regime in which lithium has long been treated as strategically sensitive. Argentina opened more space for provincial governments and private developers and, over the past several years, moved from perennial future supplier toward actual production growth. Bolivia became famous for possessing enormous lithium resources while repeatedly demonstrating the distance between a resource estimate and commercial output.

The triangle is therefore not one supply source.

It is three different political economies sitting on related geology.

That helps explain why lithium is both concentrated and surprisingly responsive.

The USGS estimates show how quickly the map has changed. Australia remains a major producer. Chile remains one. China is both a miner and the dominant force in conversion and battery manufacturing. Argentina has climbed rapidly as new brine projects entered production. Zimbabwe and other producers have added supply. Canada, Brazil, Mali, and the United States have projects or operating mines contributing to a market that looked much narrower only a few years ago.

This is the counterargument to the most alarmist lithium story.

High prices worked.

They did not conjure lithium from the periodic table. They made deposits, expansions, chemical plants, financing packages, and national strategies more valuable. Capital moved toward the bottleneck. The result was not smooth, but it was visible.

That should count as evidence.

It should not be mistaken for proof that the system is now easy.

Commodity cycles punish anyone who confuses response with resilience.

Lithium supply can expand and still be fragile because the different steps do not expand together. Mining can increase faster than conversion capacity. Conversion can expand faster than qualified battery-grade supply. A new producer can reach nameplate capacity later than expected. A refinery can make a chemical product that exists physically but still fails a customer's exact impurity specifications.

The chain has to work end to end.

That is why China appears in a chapter named for South America.

The world can diversify where lithium is dug or pumped out of the ground without diversifying where it becomes battery material.

The IEA's 2025 lithium analysis estimated that the three largest mining countries supplied about 77 percent of mined lithium in 2024, while the top three refining countries accounted for roughly 96 percent of refining. The mining concentration was already falling faster than the refining concentration.

That difference is the strategic center of the market.

A mine produces geological leverage.

A converter produces industrial leverage.

The same distinction will become even more important with rare earths later in this book, but lithium makes it easier to see because the raw material can travel in several forms: spodumene concentrate, lithium carbonate, lithium hydroxide, intermediate products whose value depends on where they sit in the battery chemistry.

The customer does not buy the word lithium.

The customer buys a usable chemical at a usable purity, delivered on time.

During a boom, those distinctions blur.

Every tonne looks valuable.

During a bust, they come roaring back.

The lithium price collapse after 2022 was not simply a financial event. It became an industrial filter. Higher-cost operations came under pressure. Expansion plans slowed. Equity values fell. Governments that had imagined a permanent seller's market had to confront the possibility that their strategic mineral could still behave like a commodity.

Chile's response has been especially revealing.

For decades, the Salar de Atacama was dominated by SQM and Albemarle under contracts with the Chilean state. In 2023, President Gabriel Boric's government announced a National Lithium Strategy that sought a larger state role in future development while keeping private capital and technical partners inside the system.

The policy was easy to caricature.

To critics, it looked like a return to resource nationalism: the state reaching for control of an industry precisely when the market had become attractive.

To supporters, it looked like a country trying not to repeat the old mining pattern in which the resource leaves, the environmental burden stays, and the highest-value industrial capabilities accumulate somewhere else.

The actual model has been more complicated than either slogan.

Codelco, Chile's state copper company, moved into lithium through partnerships rather than by trying to recreate an entire industry alone. It reached an agreement with SQM to continue development in the Salar de Atacama through a joint venture. It also selected Rio Tinto as a partner for the Salar de Maricunga, with Codelco retaining a slight majority stake and the project planned around direct lithium extraction technology.

That phrase, direct lithium extraction, deserves caution.

DLE is often presented as the technology that will solve the central weakness of brine lithium production: the enormous evaporation ponds and the long residence times associated with conventional concentration.

In theory, a DLE system extracts lithium more selectively from brine and returns a larger share of the remaining fluid to the subsurface or otherwise reduces the evaporation footprint. The commercial promise is obvious. Faster recovery, potentially higher yields, less surface area, perhaps less water stress.

The environmental promise is not automatic.

A process can use less evaporation and still require energy, chemicals, freshwater, reinjection systems, and careful management of altered brines. Different salars have different chemistry. A method that works economically in one basin may fail in another. Reinjection can sound benign until one asks how the subsurface reservoir actually responds over decades.

The right question is not whether DLE is good.

It is what the specific process does to the specific hydrological system.

This is where lithium becomes a story about measurement.

A brine project can report how much brine it pumps.

A regulator can count freshwater withdrawals.

A company can publish recovery rates.

The hardest variable may be the one local communities care about most: what happens to the connected water system over time.

In the Atacama, Indigenous communities have pressed for greater participation and better understanding of cumulative impacts from lithium and copper operations. Their concern is not abstract opposition to batteries. It is the fact that a global industrial transition can arrive locally as pumps, wells, roads, evaporation ponds, groundwater models, and promises about a hydrology that is difficult to observe directly.

A mineral market sees lithium carbonate equivalent.

A community sees water.

Those are not competing units in the same spreadsheet.

The difficulty is political because the benefits are also real.

Lithium can provide tax revenue, royalties, jobs, infrastructure, technical training, and bargaining power. Argentina has made that possibility especially visible as projects entered production in provinces that want investment and export earnings badly. For a country with recurring foreign-exchange shortages, a fast-growing mineral export sector is not merely an environmental burden imported from the North.

It is money.

That fact can disappear in rich-country discussions of responsible sourcing.

So can the opposite fact: a mine or brine field can generate national revenue while imposing local costs on communities with limited political power.

The lithium triangle sits exactly inside that conflict.

Bolivia shows a different version of it.

For years, the Salar de Uyuni has been described as one of the world's great lithium resources. The number appears in articles, investor decks, geopolitical maps, and speeches because it is enormous.

Yet production remained limited.

The chemistry of Uyuni's brines is challenging. Infrastructure is difficult. Policy changed. Partnerships came and went. Governments tried to preserve more value domestically instead of simply exporting raw material. Political instability complicated execution.

The resource existed the whole time.

The supply did not.

This is one of the most useful sentences in the book.

A resource is not supply.

A reserve is not a mine.

A mine is not a chemical plant.

A chemical plant is not a qualified battery supply chain.

Every arrow on a critical-minerals map hides those conversions.

The 2022 lithium boom encouraged the world to compress them into one idea: shortage.

The following bust encouraged the opposite compression: glut.

Both descriptions were true for moments.

Neither described the system.

The system is a set of projects with different costs, geologies, contract structures, political risks, chemistries, and clocks.

When prices rose high enough, marginal projects became financeable. When those projects and expansions entered the market, the shortage eased. When prices fell, the next layer of marginal supply became harder to finance.

That creates the commodity cycle.

The cycle is not noise around the strategic story.

It is the strategic story.

Governments usually discover critical minerals near the top of a price cycle, when companies are loud, investors are excited, and shortage forecasts look obvious. They announce subsidies, strategic partnerships, permitting reforms, and domestic-content rules.

Then prices fall.

The projects they wanted for security become less attractive precisely because the market appears better supplied.

If policy support disappears at the bottom, the next shortage is prepared during the period when nobody feels short.

Lithium's recent history is a warning against designing resilience policy around spot prices.

A low price can mean the problem was solved.

It can also mean the projects needed five years from now are being canceled today.

The IEA's 2026 outlook captures this tension. Its project pipeline suggests the expected lithium supply gap in 2035 has narrowed as more projects advance. That is good news. It is also conditional on announced projects actually being built and surviving the next downturn.

Forecasts contain financing assumptions even when they look like geology.

There is another reason lithium may prove less dangerous than the scarcity panic suggested.

Battery chemistry can move.

Not all lithium-ion batteries use the same cathode. Lithium iron phosphate batteries avoid nickel and cobalt. They still use lithium, but their rise has changed the demand outlook for other battery metals. Sodium-ion batteries, if they become competitive across more applications, could reduce lithium demand at the margin as well.

The battery industry is not a passive consumer waiting for miners to save it.

It redesigns around constraints.

That flexibility weakens the strongest version of the chokepoint thesis.

It does not eliminate it.

Lithium remains difficult to replace in many high-energy-density applications today, and the scale of battery deployment means even a smaller amount per kilowatt-hour can be overwhelmed by growth in total battery production. Efficiency can lower intensity while absolute demand rises.

This is the recurring arithmetic of the transition.

Use less per unit.

Build far more units.

The total can still go up.

By 2025, lithium prices had begun to recover from their lows. USGS data showed Chinese spot lithium carbonate prices rising through the year, while the annual average U.S. contract price remained far below the prior year's level. The IEA reported a sharper recovery into early 2026, helped by strong storage demand and tighter supply conditions.

The direction changed again.

Anyone who wants a single lithium price to summarize the future is asking the market to provide certainty it does not possess.

The important pattern is the amplitude.

Boom makes everyone believe the mineral is scarce forever.

Bust makes everyone believe supply is abundant forever.

Projects are financed inside those beliefs.

A resilient lithium system therefore requires a strange kind of patience: enough faith in long-run demand to keep building through low prices, but enough skepticism about forecasts to avoid financing every deposit that acquires a strategic label.

That is not a problem markets solve neatly.

It is not a problem states solve neatly either.

States can overpay for security. Markets can underinvest in redundancy. Producers can game subsidies. Governments can use strategic language to justify ordinary protectionism. Communities can be promised participation after the important decisions have already been made.

Lithium does not reward moral simplicity.

The mineral can support cleaner transport and power systems.

It can also shift environmental pressure into fragile salt-flat ecosystems.

Its price collapse can prove that supply responds.

The same collapse can kill projects needed for later diversification.

State ownership can preserve more national value.

It can also politicize investment decisions.

Private capital can move faster.

It can also leave when the price cycle turns.

The triangle is not a treasure chest waiting to be opened.

It is a negotiation among geology, water, capital, technology, communities, and states.

Lithium's biggest strategic lesson is therefore not that the world is running out.

It is that the world can build itself into and out of a shortage faster than the politics around the shortage can adjust.

Copper was slow enough to make scarcity feel structural.

Lithium moved fast enough to make scarcity feel temporary.

Both can be chokepoints.

The next mineral is different again.

Cobalt is not simply volatile or slow.

Its mine supply is concentrated in one country to a degree that makes lithium look diversified, and the deepest argument around it is not geological.

It is about who carries the cost of our batteries.
