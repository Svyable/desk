# Everything Runs on Rock

The phrase critical mineral sounds as if a committee invented it because strategic rock did not fit on the letterhead.

That is not far from the truth.

There is no universal periodic-table category called critical. A mineral becomes critical because somebody has decided that an economy depends on it and that the supply can be disrupted badly enough to matter. Change the economy, the technology, the source of supply, or the political relationship and the list can change with it.

In November 2025, the United States Geological Survey's final critical-minerals list contained sixty mineral commodities. Fifty had been on the 2022 list. Ten were new. Copper was among the additions.

Copper did not become a different element in the intervening three years.

America's dependence on it became more strategically visible.

That distinction is the point.

A list of critical minerals is not a list of the rarest things in the ground. It is a snapshot of a production system asking itself what it cannot afford to lose.

The European Union uses a related but not identical framework. Its Critical Raw Materials Act distinguishes critical raw materials from a narrower group of strategic raw materials selected for their importance to the green and digital transitions, defense and aerospace, expected demand growth, and the difficulty of increasing production. The strategic list includes cobalt, copper, gallium, germanium, battery-grade lithium, battery-grade nickel, graphite, and rare-earth elements used in permanent magnets, among others.

The United States and Europe are looking at much of the same geology and producing different administrative categories because their methods and policy purposes differ.

That should make us cautious about the word critical.

It should not make us dismiss it.

The underlying problem is very real: the same industrial transition is asking several sectors to reach into the earth for overlapping materials at the same time.

An electric vehicle needs a battery, power electronics, wiring, motors, charging equipment, and a grid capable of supplying the electricity. Depending on the chemistry and motor design, that can mean lithium, graphite, nickel, cobalt, copper, and magnet rare earths.

A wind turbine is a different machine, but it also needs large quantities of copper and, in some designs, rare-earth permanent magnets.

A transmission line does not contain an EV battery, yet it consumes copper and aluminum on a scale that can overwhelm the mineral demand of more glamorous technologies.

A data center may be described as a collection of processors, memory, networking equipment, cooling systems, and software. Physically, it is also an electrical project: transformers, substations, switchgear, cables, backup systems, cooling infrastructure, and often new generation or grid capacity. Artificial intelligence arrives in the economy through silicon, but silicon has to be powered and connected.

A guided missile and an electric sedan have almost nothing in common as products.

They can still meet one another upstream in a materials market.

This is what makes the current mineral cycle different from a normal commodity boom in one sector.

Demand is not coming from one new product.

It is coming from a stack of systems being rebuilt at once.

The International Energy Agency's 2025 critical-minerals outlook captured the scale without requiring an apocalyptic forecast. Under its Stated Policies Scenario, lithium demand grows about fivefold from 2024 to 2040. Nickel and graphite roughly double. Cobalt and rare-earth demand increase by about half or more. Copper, already a much larger market than the battery metals, grows by about 30 percent.

Those numbers are scenarios, not promises.

They can be wrong.

Battery chemistry can change. EV adoption can slow. Power grids can be built differently. Recycling can supply more material than expected. Recessions can erase demand for years at a time. A forecast that looks conservative during a boom can look absurd after a bust.

The useful part of the IEA numbers is not the exact endpoint.

It is that the growth occurs on top of existing industrial demand rather than instead of it.

Copper illustrates the problem best.

The world was already using copper for buildings, electrical equipment, machinery, transport, telecommunications, plumbing, and consumer goods before anyone began planning vast AI campuses or rewriting national power systems around electrification. In the IEA's 2025 outlook, total copper demand rises from about 26.7 million tonnes in 2024 to roughly 34.1 million tonnes in 2040 under stated policies. Clean-technology demand grows faster than other uses, but the old uses do not politely disappear to make room.

The new economy has to compete with the old economy for the same metal.

This is one reason slogans about the energy transition can be physically misleading. The word transition implies replacement: one system goes away and another takes its place.

The material reality is often additive for decades.

A country builds wind and solar while continuing to maintain gas plants. It builds electric vehicles while millions of combustion vehicles remain on the road. It adds data centers while homes, factories, railways, ports, hospitals, and offices continue drawing electricity. It upgrades transmission while maintaining the grid it already has.

For a long time, both systems exist.

Both systems need material.

Mining companies live inside that overlap.

Lithium looks different because its market was smaller to begin with. In the IEA's 2025 outlook, total lithium demand rises from about 205,000 tonnes in 2024 to roughly 928,000 tonnes in 2040 under stated policies. Most of the growth comes from clean technologies, especially batteries.

That kind of curve attracts capital and storytelling in equal measure.

When lithium prices surged during the early 2020s, it became easy to speak as though the world had run out. It had not. New supply arrived, inventories changed, demand expectations shifted, and prices collapsed. The IEA noted that lithium prices had fallen more than 80 percent from their 2021–22 surge by the time of its 2025 outlook.

That collapse is not evidence that lithium ceased to matter.

It is evidence that commodity markets punish straight-line thinking.

A material can be strategically important and temporarily abundant.

It can be projected to face long-term pressure while producers are losing money today.

It can be essential to a technology without being scarce enough to support every mine proposed during the boom.

The distinction between importance and price will matter throughout this book.

High prices are loud. Dependence is quieter.

A company can depend on a material that currently feels cheap because the real vulnerability is not annual global supply. It may be one grade, one processing step, one geography, or one qualified producer.

Nickel demonstrates another version of the problem. Global nickel supply expanded rapidly after Indonesia used export policy and Chinese-backed investment to build a huge downstream processing industry. That growth helped push nickel prices down and narrowed long-term supply concerns compared with earlier forecasts. Yet the IEA's project pipeline still shows an increasingly concentrated market, with the top three mining countries projected to account for a larger share of supply by the mid-2030s.

More supply did not necessarily mean more diversification.

Cobalt tells a similar story from a different geography. The Democratic Republic of the Congo remains central to mining, while Chinese companies play major roles in ownership and refining. At the same time, battery makers have been reducing cobalt intensity and shifting some vehicle segments toward lithium iron phosphate chemistry, which uses no nickel or cobalt in the cathode.

If you wanted to argue that mineral chokepoints are overhyped, cobalt would give you ammunition.

Technology adapted.

A material once treated as indispensable to the EV future became less important to some of the fastest-growing battery chemistries.

The wrong conclusion would be that substitution always arrives in time.

The right conclusion is that mineral demand is an engineering target, not a law of nature.

Designers respond to price, supply risk, performance, regulation, safety, and geopolitics. When a material becomes expensive or politically awkward, somebody receives a powerful incentive to use less of it.

That is one of the main ways minerals differ from oil.

A vehicle designed around gasoline cannot decide, midway through its life, to burn recycled gasoline that has already been used once. Oil is consumed. Metals remain in the physical economy. They can sometimes be recovered. They can be thrifted. They can be substituted. A motor can be redesigned. A battery chemistry can change. A transmission project can use aluminum where copper would otherwise have been preferred, within engineering constraints.

These escape routes are real.

They are also slower and messier than saying the word substitution makes them sound.

A new chemistry has to work.

A new motor design has to be manufacturable.

A recycled material has to be collected, processed, purified, and returned at a cost and quality customers accept.

An alternative metal may move the bottleneck somewhere else.

There is no mineral-free version of industrial civilization waiting behind the current one.

There are only different material choices.

The challenge becomes clearer if you imagine the economy not as sectors but as nested physical systems.

Start with an AI server.

The chips themselves depend on a semiconductor supply chain drawing on silicon and a long tail of specialty materials. The server needs power supplies, circuit boards, cooling, racks, networking, and cabling. Thousands of servers require a building designed around extraordinary electrical and thermal loads. Several buildings require substations, transformers, transmission, generation, and sometimes new water infrastructure.

The public argument may be about artificial intelligence.

The construction site is full of copper.

Now take the electric vehicle.

Its battery demand is obvious because lithium-ion is in the name of the dominant technology. Less obvious are the copper conductors, the graphite anode, the possible nickel and cobalt in the cathode, the potential rare-earth magnets in the motor, the aluminum in the structure and electrical system, and the minerals embodied in the charging network and expanded grid that make the vehicle useful.

The product sits at the end of multiple supply chains that policy debates often discuss separately.

Then add a wind farm.

Then utility-scale storage.

Then a naval vessel.

Then a missile program.

Then millions of ordinary air conditioners, heat pumps, appliances, phones, industrial motors, and transformers.

The surprising fact is not that all these objects contain minerals.

Everything physical contains something.

The strategic fact is that several of the same materials recur in systems whose demand is rising for unrelated reasons.

Copper does not care whether the electron is headed toward a GPU or a washing machine.

A rare-earth separation plant does not create a separate terbium stream for climate policy and another for defense policy.

The market sees material specification, quantity, delivery date, price, and customer requirements. The political categories converge upstream.

This convergence produces strange coalitions.

Environmental groups that spent decades opposing mines can find themselves supporting faster permitting for selected critical-mineral projects needed for electrification. Defense officials can become interested in battery supply chains. Automobile companies sign agreements with mining companies. Governments offer loans, tax credits, grants, price guarantees, and offtake support for facilities that would once have been treated as ordinary industrial investments.

The mineral has not merely become more valuable.

It has crossed bureaucratic boundaries.

A copper project can now be discussed as energy policy, industrial policy, climate policy, trade policy, national-security policy, and regional development policy in the same meeting.

That convergence can help projects get built.

It can also create bad decisions.

The word critical is politically useful. Once attached, it can become a shield against ordinary scrutiny. Every marginal deposit can be marketed as national security. Every subsidy can be justified as resilience. Every environmental objection can be framed as helping a strategic rival.

The fact that a material is important does not make every project wise.

This book will repeatedly return to that boundary.

Security of supply is a legitimate objective.

It is not a blank check.

The European Critical Raw Materials Act makes the tradeoff unusually explicit. It does not merely name strategic materials. It sets benchmarks intended to increase European extraction, processing, and recycling capacity and to reduce excessive dependence on any single third country. The policy is built around the idea that a supply chain can be too concentrated even when the market is functioning normally.

That is a different way of measuring efficiency.

Traditional procurement asks who can deliver the material at the required specification and lowest acceptable cost.

Resilience adds another question: what happens if the cheapest route stops being available?

The second question is expensive because answering it usually requires duplication.

A second mine.

A second refinery.

A larger stockpile.

An alternate chemistry.

A supplier in a higher-cost jurisdiction.

Inventory that sits unused.

Engineers paid to qualify a backup process they hope never to need.

For most of modern corporate history, those costs have been tempting targets for optimization.

Globalization rewarded the company that found the most efficient supplier and scaled with it. Manufacturing systems learned to reduce inventories. Financial managers learned to punish idle assets. Consolidation removed spare capacity. Specialized firms became extraordinarily good at narrow tasks.

The system got cheaper.

It also got thinner.

Critical-minerals policy is partly an attempt to put thickness back into the physical economy.

The attempt will be uneven because not every mineral deserves the same response.

Copper is a giant, liquid global market with geographically diverse mining compared with cobalt. Rare-earth separation is extremely concentrated. Lithium mining has diversified faster than many pessimists expected. Nickel supply has grown explosively in Indonesia. Graphite carries its own processing concentration. Gallium can be produced as a by-product of other industrial processes, but building alternative refining still takes investment and know-how.

To put all of them under one heading is administratively convenient and analytically dangerous.

There is no single critical-mineral problem.

There are many problems that share a vocabulary.

Some are problems of geology.

Some are problems of refining.

Some are problems of project finance.

Some are problems of national concentration.

Some are problems of environmental legitimacy.

Some are temporary price cycles wearing geopolitical costumes.

Some are genuine strategic dependencies that markets ignored because the risk was difficult to price.

The purpose of the official lists is not to settle which one a mineral is.

The lists are invitations to investigate.

USGS added copper to the American list in 2025 because its updated methodology judged the combination of economic importance and supply disruption risk differently than before. Europe separately classifies copper as both critical and strategic under its current framework. The IEA's outlook shows why policymakers are paying attention: copper has the largest established market among the key transition minerals it follows, and announced mine projects still fall short of its stated-policies demand projection in the 2030s.

That does not mean a copper crisis is guaranteed.

Forecast deficits have a way of attracting investment, changing demand, and embarrassing forecasters.

It means the margin for error is worth watching.

The same IEA report that warns about copper is noticeably less alarming about nickel, cobalt, and rare-earth volumes under current project pipelines. Expected supply for those materials has improved relative to demand forecasts. In other words, the evidence does not support one sweeping story in which every critical mineral becomes scarcer forever.

Good.

A useful theory should survive good news.

The chokepoint thesis does not require every metal to be in permanent shortage. It requires us to notice where supply, processing, technology, and politics become concentrated enough that interruption can travel far downstream.

Sometimes price will solve the problem.

Sometimes substitution will.

Sometimes a government will spend heavily to create redundant capacity.

Sometimes a producer will flood the market and destroy the scarcity premium.

Sometimes none of those things will happen quickly enough.

The task is to know which physical system we are looking at before the shortage begins.

That requires leaving the official lists behind and following the materials one by one.

The twentieth century's most famous strategic commodity offers a useful warning about what happens when governments learn this lesson late.

Oil produced embargoes, wars, strategic reserves, naval doctrines, alliances, recessions, windfall fortunes, and repeated declarations that dependence had finally been solved.

It is an irresistible analogy for the mineral age.

It is also dangerous.

Oil is a fuel. Copper is infrastructure. Lithium is a battery input. Rare earths are a family of chemically similar elements with highly concentrated processing. Cobalt is often a by-product. Nickel serves enormous stainless-steel markets beyond batteries. Metals remain after use and can re-enter the economy.

If we are going to borrow the political memory of oil, we need to be precise about what we are borrowing.

That is where the next chapter begins.
