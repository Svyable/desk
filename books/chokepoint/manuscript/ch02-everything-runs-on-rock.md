# Everything Runs on Rock

The phrase critical mineral sounds as if a committee invented it because strategic rock did not fit on the letterhead.

That is not far from the truth.

There is no universal periodic-table category called critical. A mineral becomes critical because somebody has decided that an economy depends on it and that the supply can be disrupted badly enough to matter. Change the economy, the technology, the source of supply, or the political relationship and the list can change with it.

In November 2025, the United States Geological Survey's final critical-minerals list contained sixty mineral commodities. Fifty had been on the 2022 list. Ten were new. Copper was among the additions.

Copper did not become a different element in the intervening three years.

America's dependence on it became more strategically visible.

A critical-mineral list is not a list of the rarest things in the ground. It is a snapshot of a production system asking itself what it cannot afford to lose.

The European Union uses a related but not identical framework. Its Critical Raw Materials Act distinguishes critical raw materials from a narrower group of strategic raw materials selected for their importance to the green and digital transitions, defense and aerospace, expected demand growth, and the difficulty of increasing production. The strategic list includes cobalt, copper, gallium, germanium, battery-grade lithium, battery-grade nickel, graphite, and rare-earth elements used in permanent magnets, among others.

The United States and Europe are looking at much of the same geology and producing different administrative categories because their methods and policy purposes differ. The label is a policy judgment, not a property of the atom.

The underlying problem is physical: several sectors are reaching into the earth for overlapping materials at the same time.

An electric vehicle needs a battery, power electronics, wiring, motors, charging equipment, and a grid capable of supplying the electricity. Depending on the chemistry and motor design, that can mean lithium, graphite, nickel, cobalt, copper, and magnet rare earths.

A wind turbine is a different machine, but it also needs large quantities of copper and, in some designs, rare-earth permanent magnets. A transmission line does not contain an EV battery, yet it consumes copper and aluminum on a scale that can overwhelm the mineral demand of more glamorous technologies.

A data center may be described as processors, memory, networking equipment, cooling systems, and software. Physically, it is also an electrical project: transformers, substations, switchgear, cables, backup systems, cooling infrastructure, and often new generation or grid capacity. Artificial intelligence arrives in the economy through silicon, but silicon has to be powered and connected.

A guided missile and an electric sedan have almost nothing in common as products. They can still meet one another upstream in a materials market.

That overlap is more important than any one forecast.

The International Energy Agency's 2025 critical-minerals outlook projected strong growth under its Stated Policies Scenario: lithium demand rising about fivefold from 2024 to 2040, nickel and graphite roughly doubling, cobalt and rare-earth demand increasing by about half or more, and copper—already a much larger market—growing by about 30 percent.

Those numbers are scenarios, not promises. Battery chemistry can change. EV adoption can slow. Recycling can supply more material than expected. Recessions can erase demand for years. Forecast deficits attract investment, and forecasts have a long history of being embarrassed by the responses they provoke.

The useful part is not the exact endpoint. It is that new demand arrives on top of old demand rather than instead of it.

Copper shows this best.

The world was already using copper for buildings, electrical equipment, machinery, transport, telecommunications, plumbing, and consumer goods before anyone began planning vast AI campuses or rewriting national power systems around electrification. In the IEA's 2025 outlook, total copper demand rises from about 26.7 million tonnes in 2024 to roughly 34.1 million tonnes in 2040 under stated policies. Clean-technology demand grows faster than other uses, but the old uses do not politely disappear to make room.

The new economy has to compete with the old economy for the same metal.

This is one reason the word transition can be physically misleading. It suggests replacement: one system goes away and another takes its place. For decades, the material reality is often additive. Countries build wind and solar while maintaining gas plants, add electric vehicles while combustion vehicles remain on the road, expand data centers while homes and factories keep drawing electricity, and upgrade transmission while maintaining the grid they already have.

Mining companies live inside that overlap.

Lithium is a useful warning against turning overlap into a permanent-shortage story. Its market was smaller, and battery demand rose quickly enough to attract capital and storytelling in equal measure. When lithium prices surged during the early 2020s, it became easy to speak as though the world had run out. It had not. New supply arrived, inventories changed, demand expectations shifted, and prices collapsed. The IEA noted that lithium prices had fallen more than 80 percent from their 2021–22 surge by the time of its 2025 outlook.

Lithium did not cease to matter. The market changed.

A material can be strategically important and temporarily abundant. It can face plausible long-term pressure while producers are losing money today. It can be essential to a technology without being scarce enough to support every mine proposed during the boom.

High prices are loud. Dependence is quieter.

Nickel demonstrates another version. Global supply expanded rapidly after Indonesia used export policy and Chinese-backed investment to build a huge downstream processing industry. That growth helped push prices down and narrowed long-term supply concerns compared with earlier forecasts. Yet the IEA's project pipeline still showed an increasingly concentrated market, with the top three mining countries projected to account for a larger share of supply by the mid-2030s.

More supply did not necessarily mean more diversification.

Cobalt supplies the strongest counterargument to mineral fatalism. The Democratic Republic of the Congo remains central to mining, and Chinese companies play major roles in ownership and refining. But battery makers have reduced cobalt intensity and shifted some vehicle segments toward lithium iron phosphate chemistry, which uses no nickel or cobalt in the cathode.

Technology adapted.

That does not mean substitution always arrives in time. It means mineral demand is an engineering target, not a law of nature. Designers respond to price, supply risk, performance, regulation, safety, and geopolitics. When a material becomes expensive or politically awkward, somebody receives a powerful incentive to use less of it.

The escape routes are real, but they are not instantaneous. A new chemistry has to work. A new motor design has to be manufacturable. Recycled material has to be collected, purified, and returned at a cost and quality customers accept. An alternative metal can move the bottleneck somewhere else.

There is no mineral-free version of industrial civilization waiting behind the current one. There are different material choices.

The overlap becomes easier to see if the economy is treated as nested physical systems rather than as policy sectors.

Start with an AI server. The chips depend on a semiconductor supply chain drawing on silicon and a long tail of specialty materials. The server needs power supplies, circuit boards, cooling, racks, networking, and cabling. Thousands of servers require a building designed around extraordinary electrical and thermal loads. Several buildings require substations, transformers, transmission, generation, and sometimes new water infrastructure.

The public argument may be about artificial intelligence.

The construction site is full of copper.

An electric vehicle reaches the same upstream market from another direction. Its battery demand is obvious. Less obvious are the copper conductors, graphite anode, possible nickel and cobalt in the cathode, potential rare-earth magnets in the motor, aluminum in the structure and electrical system, and the minerals embodied in the charging network and expanded grid that make the vehicle useful.

Add a wind farm, utility-scale storage, a naval vessel, a missile program, and millions of ordinary air conditioners, heat pumps, appliances, phones, industrial motors, and transformers. The strategic fact is not that all these objects contain minerals. Everything physical contains something. It is that several of the same materials recur in systems whose demand is rising for unrelated reasons.

Copper does not care whether the electron is headed toward a GPU or a washing machine. A rare-earth separation plant does not create a separate terbium stream for climate policy and another for defense policy. Political categories converge upstream.

That convergence produces strange coalitions. Defense officials become interested in battery supply chains. Automobile companies sign agreements with mining companies. Governments offer loans, tax credits, grants, price guarantees, and offtake support for facilities that would once have been treated as ordinary industrial investments. Environmental groups can find themselves debating whether faster permitting for selected mines is necessary for electrification.

A copper project can now arrive in the same meeting as energy policy, industrial policy, climate policy, trade policy, national security, and regional development.

The word critical gains power in a room like that. It can also become a shield against scrutiny. Every marginal deposit can be marketed as national security. Every subsidy can be called resilience. Every environmental objection can be framed as helping a strategic rival.

The fact that a material is important does not make every project wise.

The European Critical Raw Materials Act makes the underlying tradeoff explicit. It sets benchmarks intended to increase European extraction, processing, and recycling capacity and to reduce excessive dependence on any single third country. The policy assumes that a supply chain can be too concentrated even when the market is functioning normally.

Traditional procurement asks who can deliver the required material at an acceptable price. Resilience adds another question: what happens if the cheapest route stops being available?

Answering that question costs money. It may mean a second mine or refinery, a larger stockpile, an alternate chemistry, a higher-cost supplier, unused inventory, or engineers paid to qualify a backup process they hope never to need.

For much of modern corporate history, those costs were tempting targets for optimization. Globalization rewarded firms that found efficient suppliers and scaled with them. Manufacturing systems reduced inventories. Financial managers punished idle assets. Consolidation removed spare capacity. Specialized firms became extraordinarily good at narrow tasks.

The system got cheaper.

It also got thinner.

Critical-minerals policy is partly an attempt to put thickness back into the physical economy. But not every mineral deserves the same response.

Copper is a giant global market with geographically diverse mining compared with cobalt. Rare-earth separation is extremely concentrated. Lithium mining has diversified faster than many pessimists expected. Nickel supply has grown explosively in Indonesia. Graphite carries its own processing concentration. Gallium can be produced as a by-product of other industrial processes, but building alternative refining still takes investment and know-how.

Putting them all under one heading is administratively convenient and analytically dangerous. Some problems sit in geology, others in refining, finance, national concentration, environmental legitimacy, or a temporary price cycle. The official list does not settle which one a mineral is. It tells policymakers where the cost of being wrong has become important enough to investigate.

Copper's addition to the American list in 2025 is useful for exactly that reason. The element did not change. The assessment of economic importance and disruption risk did. Europe separately classifies copper as both critical and strategic, while the IEA's outlook shows announced mine projects falling short of its stated-policies demand projection in the 2030s.

That does not guarantee a copper crisis. Forecast deficits can attract investment, change demand, and disappear. The same IEA outlook was less alarming about the future volume balance for nickel, cobalt, and rare earths than earlier scarcity narratives suggested.

A useful theory should survive good news.

The chokepoint thesis does not require every metal to become scarcer forever. It asks where supply, processing, technology, and politics are concentrated enough that an interruption can travel far downstream—and how quickly the system can respond when it does.

Oil is the obvious historical analogy. It is also where this argument can go wrong.

Oil is a fuel. Copper is infrastructure. Lithium is a battery input. Rare earths are a family of chemically similar elements with highly concentrated processing. Cobalt is often a by-product. Nickel serves enormous stainless-steel markets beyond batteries. Metals remain after use and can re-enter the economy.

If we are going to borrow the political memory of oil, we need to be precise about what we are borrowing.
