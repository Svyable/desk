# The List of Seventeen

On April 4, 2025, the Chinese government published a list that most people outside a few corners of industry could not have read without a periodic table.

Samarium. Gadolinium. Terbium. Dysprosium. Lutetium. Scandium. Yttrium.

Seven names, each followed in the official announcement by the kinds of detail that make trade regulation look like clerical work: metals, alloys, oxides, compounds, targets, permanent magnets, customs classifications. Exporters handling the covered items would have to apply for licenses. Customs could question a shipment and hold it while the classification was resolved.

Nothing in the document said that every rare-earth shipment would stop. It was not a blanket embargo. There was no dramatic sentence announcing that factories abroad would be starved of material.

The power sat in the paperwork.

A magnet manufacturer in Europe did not need China to prohibit all exports to feel the change. It needed to wonder whether a license would arrive before its inventory ran down. An aerospace supplier did not need a permanent ban. A delay long enough to miss a production schedule could be sufficient. A defense contractor could have money, a purchase order, and a willing customer and still find that the relevant material had acquired a new border between the mine and the machine.

The seven elements were not chosen because they were the seven rare earths that existed. There are seventeen elements commonly grouped under that name: the fifteen lanthanides, plus scandium and yttrium. The category is chemically awkward and historically accumulated. They are not literally rare in the ordinary sense. Several are more abundant in the Earth's crust than metals whose names are familiar to everyone. The difficulty is that they tend to occur mixed together, often at concentrations that are not attractive to mine, and their chemical similarity makes separation laborious.

That last word matters more than the first.

The rare-earth problem is not simply finding rare-earth-bearing rock.

It is turning mixed mineral feed into separated oxides, metals, alloys, powders, and magnets at industrial scale, with acceptable yields, costs, waste handling, and consistency.

By 2024, according to the International Energy Agency, China accounted for about 60 percent of global mined production of the magnet rare earths it tracks. That is a commanding share, but not an absolute monopoly. The more revealing numbers come later in the chain. China represented about 91 percent of global refined output and 94 percent of production of sintered permanent magnets.

The mine is the beginning of the story.

The refining step is where leverage accumulates.

This is easy to miss because mining photographs are more persuasive than chemical plants. A mine has scale you can see: open pits cut into mountains, haul trucks taller than houses, waste rock piled against a horizon. Processing is harder to turn into a national myth. It looks like pipes, tanks, furnaces, solvent-extraction circuits, wastewater treatment, technicians, maintenance schedules, and decades of process knowledge.

Yet the industrial system cares less about what looks dramatic than about what is difficult to replace.

A deposit can exist in California or Australia and still depend on processing capacity elsewhere. A country can own ore in the ground without owning the step that converts it into a material a motor manufacturer can use. A government can count mineral reserves and discover, during a crisis, that reserves are not magnets.

That is the first chokepoint in this book.

The word chokepoint suggests a narrow canal or a mountain pass, somewhere traffic visibly compresses. Mineral chokepoints are often less theatrical. They appear as one step in a supply chain that few people had reason to notice until it became interruptible.

A permanent magnet is a good example.

The strongest modern rare-earth magnets typically rely on neodymium and praseodymium, sometimes with additions such as dysprosium or terbium to preserve performance at higher temperatures. Those magnets end up in electric motors, wind turbines, robotics, industrial equipment, consumer electronics, and military systems. They are valuable not because a rare-earth element performs one magical task by itself but because particular combinations allow designers to make compact, powerful machines.

The more successful the design, the easier it becomes to forget the material inside it.

A car buyer does not shop for dysprosium.

A data-center operator does not advertise how much copper, nickel, lithium, or rare-earth material sits behind each megawatt of computing capacity.

A military planner thinks about aircraft availability, missile inventories, radar performance, maintenance cycles, and spare parts. The metals disappear into the noun describing the finished system.

Then a licensing rule changes, and the noun splits open.

This is why the phrase critical mineral can sound more bureaucratic than it is. A mineral becomes strategically interesting when a small physical input sits inside a much larger economic object and when the path to replacing that input is slower than the path to losing access to it.

The finished product may be worth millions of dollars. The constrained material may account for a tiny fraction of that value. Scarcity at the bottom can still immobilize the whole stack.

The logic is familiar from other industries. A semiconductor fabrication plant can be held up by one specialized component. A hospital can have hundreds of employees and still cancel procedures for lack of one sterile item. A jet can contain hundreds of thousands of parts and remain grounded because one certified part is missing.

What makes the mineral version politically charged is geography.

The missing component is not merely manufactured by another company. It may pass through a country that has learned its position in the chain can be used as leverage.

China did not discover this possibility in 2025.

Rare earths had been strategic long before they became a recurring subject of trade policy. China had spent decades building extraction, separation, metals, alloys, magnet manufacturing, and the surrounding supplier base. Western countries had, in many cases, been happy to buy the resulting materials at prices that made alternative capacity difficult to finance.

This is one of the uncomfortable features of chokepoints. They are often created collaboratively.

A dominant supplier invests, subsidizes, scales, tolerates low margins, builds expertise, and sometimes accepts environmental costs that other countries are unwilling to accept. Customers elsewhere respond rationally to cheap, reliable inputs. Mines close. Processing lines disappear. Engineers retire. Supplier networks thin out. The arrangement looks efficient until resilience suddenly has a price.

It is tempting, in retrospect, to describe this as a strategic trap that everyone should have seen.

That is too easy.

For years, concentration can be economically attractive. Redundant plants are expensive. Strategic stockpiles tie up capital. A domestic refinery that costs more than the incumbent supplier looks like a subsidy project rather than infrastructure. Shareholders do not reward a company for maintaining a second supply chain that never gets used.

The economics of resilience are awkward because the benefit often appears only after the cheap system fails.

In July 2025, the United States Department of Defense and MP Materials made that logic unusually explicit. MP operates the Mountain Pass rare-earth mine in California. Under a broader public-private partnership, the Defense Department agreed to purchase $400 million of a new series of MP preferred stock. The agreement also included a ten-year price-floor commitment of $110 per kilogram for MP's neodymium-praseodymium products, as well as support for additional separation and magnet capacity. A month later, the Pentagon announced a $150 million loan tied to adding heavy rare-earth separation capability at Mountain Pass.

The details are remarkable because governments normally prefer markets to tell producers what something is worth.

Here, the government was helping tell the market what a domestic rare-earth supply chain needed to be worth in order to survive.

A price floor is an admission about industrial structure. If an alternative supply chain has to compete only against the current spot price from the dominant supplier, it may never be built. The incumbent system benefits from scale, sunk investment, supplier density, accumulated process knowledge, and, at times, policy support that cannot be recreated by signing a purchase order.

So the United States did something that would have looked strange in a textbook version of commodity competition. It paid for optionality.

The intervention does not prove that the Chinese supply chain is about to fail. It proves that American officials had decided dependence itself carried a cost not visible in the market price.

That distinction will recur throughout this book.

A chokepoint is not the same thing as a shortage.

There can be abundant supply and still be a chokepoint if the supply passes through a narrow enough set of countries, firms, technologies, or facilities. Prices can be low right up until access becomes uncertain. The vulnerability lies in the structure, not necessarily in the current inventory level.

Nor does concentration automatically mean coercion.

A refinery can dominate because it is good at refining. A government can impose export controls for stated national-security reasons that resemble controls used by other governments. Firms can face licensing friction without being targets of a grand geopolitical plan. It is possible to describe a vulnerable supply chain without inventing a villain behind every delayed shipment.

The harder question is what a concentrated system permits when political relations deteriorate.

April 2025 gave one answer: permission could be inserted into the flow.

The word permission is more accurate than prohibition.

An embargo is blunt. It tells a buyer no.

A licensing system can say maybe.

Maybe after documentation.

Maybe for this customer but not that one.

Maybe for civilian use but not a military program.

Maybe this month, depending on the state of negotiations.

That flexibility can make the tool more useful, not less. A government that closes the valve entirely risks encouraging every customer to build a substitute as fast as possible. A government that can tighten and loosen access has room to bargain.

This became clearer as 2025 progressed. China announced additional rare-earth export-control measures in October, including measures with broader reach into products and technology. Those later measures became part of a trade negotiation rather than a permanent one-way escalation. In April 2026, China's Ministry of Commerce said the export-control measures announced on October 9, 2025 were suspended through November 10, 2026 under the China-U.S. Kuala Lumpur economic and trade consensus.

The April 2025 licensing architecture should not be confused with the later suspended package. The chronology is messy because real leverage is messy. Rules are announced, revised, suspended, interpreted, licensed, and negotiated.

That is precisely why the simple word ban is so often misleading.

A ban is easy to explain on television.

Administrative power is harder to see.

Yet a manufacturer deciding whether to commit billions of dollars to a factory cares about the administrative power. The relevant question is not only whether today's shipment is legal. It is whether the company can model access five years from now well enough to justify the investment.

Uncertainty becomes part of the cost of capital.

This helps explain why the rare-earth issue did not disappear when particular controls were eased. A suspension can solve an immediate trade dispute while leaving the underlying industrial geometry unchanged. If one country still performs most refining and makes almost all of the highest-value magnets, the option to tighten the system later remains valuable.

At the same time, every use of the option weakens it at the margin.

Japan's response to earlier rare-earth tension was not simply to complain. Companies diversified suppliers, invested in recycling and material thrift, and supported alternative production. The United States has pushed money into Mountain Pass and other parts of a domestic mine-to-magnet chain. Australia has Lynas, the most significant non-Chinese rare-earth processor. Governments have begun stockpiling, subsidizing, financing, and signing long-term offtake agreements for materials that once seemed too small to deserve a cabinet-level meeting.

This is the paradox of weaponizing a chokepoint.

Using it demonstrates its power.

Using it also teaches customers to leave.

The process of leaving can take years, sometimes decades, which is why the leverage remains real. But it is not permanent. No country owns the laws of chemistry. China owns an extraordinary industrial position built from investment, policy, expertise, scale, and time. Other countries can build competing systems if they are willing to spend enough money and endure enough mistakes.

The question is how long that takes.

That, more than any headline about an export restriction, is the subject of this book.

The seventeen rare-earth elements are a useful beginning because their names make the problem feel exotic. It is easy to imagine that the vulnerability is special to strange materials most people never learned in school.

It is not.

Copper is familiar enough to be stolen from construction sites, yet modern grid expansion and data centers can make its mine-development cycle strategic. Lithium went from obscure specialty chemical to emblem of the electric-vehicle boom, then suffered a price collapse severe enough to remind investors that scarcity narratives can overshoot. Cobalt concentrates ethical and geopolitical questions in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Nickel shows what happens when a government refuses to remain merely an ore exporter and uses policy to force downstream industry onto its soil.

Rare earths are only the cleanest demonstration because they expose the difference between owning rock and owning the industrial process that makes the rock useful.

The same distinction will appear again and again.

A country can mine lithium and still send intermediate material abroad for processing.

It can produce nickel ore and lack battery-grade refining.

It can hold copper reserves that will not become operating mines for fifteen years.

It can possess a strategic resource and still watch most of the value, expertise, financing, and leverage accumulate somewhere else.

This is why a list of mineral reserves is not a map of power.

Power sits at the difficult step.

Sometimes that step is geological.

Sometimes it is financial.

Sometimes it is chemical.

Sometimes it is a permit, a port, a rail line, a smelter, a tailings facility, a magnet factory, or a technician who knows how to keep a separation circuit running without poisoning the product stream.

The twentieth century trained governments to think strategically about oil fields, pipelines, tankers, refineries, and sea lanes. Oil became inseparable from national security because economies learned what happened when fuel stopped moving.

The mineral system is different in ways that matter. Metals are not burned once and gone. They can be stockpiled, recycled, substituted, and designed out. There is no single organization controlling all the relevant minerals. A copper market does not behave like a rare-earth market, and neither behaves like cobalt or lithium. Treating them as one new OPEC would erase more than it explains.

But the family resemblance is already visible.

Industrial civilization is discovering that the materials inside its most advanced machines are not background scenery. They have geography. They have processing histories. They have politics.

And they have owners.

The official Chinese announcement on April 4, 2025 did not need to explain any of that. It only had to list seven elements and add a licensing requirement.

The world had already built the rest of the mechanism.
