# Rare Earths and the Refining Monopoly

Mountain Pass is easy to misunderstand.

There is a mine in California. It sits on one of the richest known rare-earth deposits in the world. For decades it was one of the most important rare-earth sources on Earth. It closed. It reopened. It now produces a meaningful share of global rare-earth material again.

From a distance, that sounds like a solved problem.

The United States has rare earths.

It has a mine.

Dig more.

The flaw is in the verb.

Rare earths are not useful because they have been dug out of the ground.

They become useful after a chain of ugly, difficult, chemically intensive transformations separates elements that nature deposits together and industry needs apart.

The mine is the beginning of the story.

The chokepoint is often in the middle.

The International Energy Agency's 2026 rare-earth assessment puts the imbalance plainly. China accounted for about 60 percent of global mine production of the magnet rare earths in 2024.

That is dominant.

It is not a monopoly.

At the refining stage, China's share was about 91 percent.

At the production of sintered permanent magnets, it was about 94 percent.

Those three numbers describe three different worlds.

Sixty percent means a competitor can imagine finding or expanding ore elsewhere.

Ninety-one percent means the competitor has to rebuild an industrial process.

Ninety-four percent means the customer at the end of the chain may still depend on the incumbent even after the mine and refinery have been diversified.

This is why counting mines can create false comfort.

The rare-earth value chain begins by crushing and concentrating ore. Then comes chemical upgrading. Then separation into individual oxides. Then refining into metals. Then alloying. Then powder. Then magnet production, shaping, coating, qualification, and integration into motors and other components.

Each stage can fail independently.

Each stage needs a different kind of plant.

Each stage has waste, yield, cost, purity, and customer-qualification problems.

A new mine does not automatically create the next one.

Rare earths make this unusually painful because they are chemically similar.

The name suggests seventeen distinct commodities waiting in neat seams underground.

The reality is closer to a crowded family that resists being separated at dinner.

Neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, terbium, cerium, lanthanum, and their relatives often occur together. Industry cares deeply about the differences among them. Chemistry does not make those differences easy to exploit.

Separation frequently requires long solvent-extraction circuits in which small chemical differences are repeated over many stages until the desired purity appears.

The equipment is not magical.

The operating knowledge is.

A plant can be built from drawings and still spend years learning how to hold yield, purity, reagent consumption, waste handling, and throughput in a commercially stable range.

That accumulated competence is one reason China is difficult to dislodge.

The country did not wake up one morning with ninety percent of refining because its rocks were ninety percent better.

It built capacity through decades of extraction, chemical processing, magnets, manufacturing demand, infrastructure, engineering practice, environmental tolerance, state support, and price competition.

The result is an ecosystem.

Ecosystems are harder to sanction into existence elsewhere than factories.

Mountain Pass demonstrates the problem almost perfectly.

The mine became globally important in the second half of the twentieth century, supplying much of the world's rare earths for a time. It later suffered from changing market conditions, environmental problems, competition, and underinvestment. Production was idled in 2015.

MP Materials acquired the site and restarted mining in 2017.

By 2019, it had stabilized production at a scale the company said represented roughly 15 percent of global rare-earth content.

Yet the important thing it was selling was concentrate.

Ore had been mined in California and upgraded in California, but the full separation chain had not been restored.

The United States could possess the deposit and still lack the industrial step that made the deposit strategically independent.

That gap is the thesis of this chapter in one mine.

MP began restoring separation and refining at Mountain Pass in 2023.

By 2025, the company reported record production of neodymium-praseodymium oxide, the material that feeds the most important class of high-performance permanent magnets.

It also began producing metal and magnets at its Texas facility and announced additional partnerships and expansion.

The progress is real.

So is the scale of the problem it is trying to solve.

The U.S. Department of Defense has spent hundreds of millions of dollars across rare-earth projects because it does not treat the mine as sufficient.

Its own mine-to-magnet strategy identifies sourcing, separation, processing, metallization, alloying, and magnet manufacturing as separate critical nodes.

That bureaucratic list is more illuminating than a dozen speeches about mineral independence.

A chain has as many chokepoints as steps the customer cannot skip.

The Department's support for MP Materials illustrates the point.

A 2025 financing package included a $150 million loan specifically to add heavy-rare-earth separation capability at Mountain Pass.

Not to discover the deposit.

Not to reopen the mine.

To separate the elements.

That is what strategic scarcity looks like after the geology has already been solved.

The United States is not alone in trying to build around China.

Lynas Rare Earths provides the most important counterexample to the idea that rare-earth separation cannot be done competitively elsewhere.

Lynas mines at Mt Weld in Western Australia. It carries out initial processing in Australia and operates a large advanced-materials plant in Malaysia where mixed material is separated into individual rare-earth products.

The company describes itself as the world's only significant producer of separated rare-earth materials outside China.

That claim comes from the company and should be treated as such, but the broader point is supported by the IEA's market data: Malaysia is the distant second to China in rare-earth refining.

Lynas has also added a processing facility at Kalgoorlie in Western Australia, where concentrate is cracked and leached before mixed rare-earth carbonate is sent on for further separation.

Again, the nouns matter.

Mine.

Concentrate.

Crack.

Leach.

Separate.

Refine.

Metal.

Magnet.

Policy debates flatten this into supply.

Industry experiences it as a sequence.

A country can succeed at six verbs and still depend on someone else for the seventh.

This explains one of the stranger features of current rare-earth policy.

Governments are subsidizing businesses that may look uneconomic under ordinary commodity assumptions.

The reason is not that officials suddenly became fascinated by solvent extraction.

It is that the market price of a material does not include the full value of having a second supplier during a crisis.

If Chinese material is cheap and reliable, a duplicate plant elsewhere may look wasteful.

If exports stop, the same plant becomes strategic infrastructure.

The market does not price those two futures easily.

A private company sees uncertain demand, volatile rare-earth prices, large capital requirements, and an incumbent with enormous scale.

A defense ministry sees a missile program that cannot accept a ninety-four-percent foreign concentration in magnets forever.

Both can be rational.

They are solving different objective functions.

The gap between them becomes industrial policy.

That policy is expensive because the incumbent ecosystem has advantages that subsidies cannot erase quickly.

China has domestic magnet manufacturers that buy refined material. Those magnet manufacturers serve large automotive, electronics, industrial, and clean-energy markets. The upstream and downstream businesses reinforce each other.

A new separator outside China can face the opposite problem.

Customers want to know it can produce reliably before signing long contracts.

Investors want long contracts before financing the plant.

The plant needs financing before it can prove reliability.

This is the industrial version of being asked for three years of experience before getting the first job.

The IEA describes the problem in less comic terms: diversified projects face higher costs, complex permitting, smaller scale, uncertain offtake, and shortages of specialized equipment and know-how.

Even some of the machinery needed to build alternatives is concentrated.

A supply chain can be dependent on the incumbent while constructing the equipment intended to end dependence on the incumbent.

This is why rare-earth diversification advances in awkward increments.

A mine opens.

A separator reaches commissioning.

A magnet line qualifies one customer.

A government provides a price floor.

An automaker signs a long-term purchase agreement.

A refinery learns to produce one element reliably before adding another.

None of these announcements means the chain is complete.

Together they may eventually matter more than a headline mine discovery.

There is important counterevidence to the monopoly story.

Concentration is not moving in only one direction.

The IEA's 2026 critical-minerals outlook reported that rare-earth refining was a notable exception to the broader trend of rising concentration. New U.S. projects and increased Malaysian production caused a modest decline in concentration between 2023 and 2025.

That matters.

A chokepoint is not permanent merely because it is severe.

Mountain Pass is refining again.

Lynas is expanding.

Projects are moving in Australia, the United States, Europe, and elsewhere.

Recycling could recover more magnet material over time.

Manufacturers can redesign products to use fewer heavy rare earths or, in some cases, different motor architectures.

Japan's response to earlier rare-earth disruptions already demonstrated that demand can move. The IEA estimates Japanese policies and industrial adaptation helped reduce total rare-earth demand by roughly 30 percent from 2010 levels.

The system reacts.

That does not make the current concentration trivial.

It makes the timing decisive.

The IEA estimates that even with existing and announced projects, diversified capacity outside China would cover only a fraction of projected 2035 needs at several stages. Mining is the least constrained. Refining is tighter. Magnets are tighter still.

This is exactly the opposite of the mental picture created by a mineral map.

The map highlights deposits.

The risk lives in factories.

There is also an environmental reason the processing monopoly cannot be understood only as a story of superior industrial policy.

Rare-earth separation creates difficult waste.

Ores can contain thorium and uranium. Chemical processing produces acidic streams, sludge, and residues that require careful handling. Poorly managed extraction and refining can contaminate water and soil and leave liabilities long after production stops.

China's historical willingness to absorb some of these costs helped lower the visible price of the product while raising costs elsewhere.

New facilities in Australia, Malaysia, the United States, or Europe face stricter environmental review and higher disposal costs.

That can make diversification slower and more expensive.

It can also make it better.

A supply chain is not resilient if the alternative to geopolitical concentration is simply exporting pollution to a new jurisdiction.

Lynas, for example, has had to build its business around waste management, water treatment, radiological monitoring, and intense public scrutiny in Malaysia and Australia. The company reports compliance and presents this as a competitive advantage.

Critics have challenged aspects of rare-earth waste management for years.

Both facts belong in the analysis.

Environmental rules can raise the cost of a non-Chinese supply chain.

They can also prevent the new supply chain from repeating the practices that helped make the old one cheap.

Strategic independence purchased by ignoring waste is only another kind of deferred dependence.

Someone eventually inherits the tailings.

The hardest part of rare-earth diversification may therefore be accepting what the material actually costs when resilience, environmental control, and redundant capacity are included.

That price can look absurd beside Chinese spot material in a calm market.

Then the export license does not arrive.

In April 2025, China imposed export controls on seven heavy rare earths, related compounds, and some magnets.

The IEA later reported that export volumes fell sharply in April and May. Automakers outside China struggled to secure magnets. Some reduced production or temporarily stopped lines. Licenses were eventually granted and shipments recovered.

This was not a theoretical exercise in supply-chain concentration.

Factories discovered that the magnet was a tiny component with a large veto.

A modern car can contain thousands of dollars of sophisticated electronics and still be stopped by a comparatively small permanent magnet that cannot be qualified from another supplier on Tuesday afternoon.

This is what refining power becomes downstream.

The chokepoint travels.

It begins as chemical separation.

It appears later as a missing component on an assembly line.

The dollar value of the missing rare earth can be tiny compared with the product it prevents from shipping.

This asymmetry is why concentration matters beyond the commodity's market size.

The IEA estimates that permanent magnets account for around 95 percent of rare-earth consumption by value and are embedded in electric vehicles, wind turbines, industrial motors, robotics, data centers, medical equipment, aerospace systems, and defense hardware.

The rare earth itself is often not the expensive thing.

The unavailable machine is.

That gives a supplier enormous potential leverage.

It also creates the incentive to destroy that leverage.

Every export restriction strengthens the business case for Mountain Pass.

Every licensing delay improves the economics of Lynas.

Every supply scare makes an automaker more willing to sign a costly long-term contract with a second supplier.

Every premium paid for a magnet made outside China finances another argument for duplication.

This is the paradox of a chokepoint used aggressively.

Power reveals itself by creating the coalition that wants to escape it.

China's advantage remains formidable because rebuilding the chain is slow.

But the act of using the advantage can accelerate the rebuilding.

The same thing happened after the 2010 rare-earth crisis.

Japan diversified suppliers, invested abroad, improved recycling, reduced material intensity, and supported substitutes. The response did not end Chinese dominance.

It made that dominance less absolute than it otherwise would have been.

This is why the word monopoly needs care.

China does not own every rare-earth mine.

It does not control every separator.

It does not manufacture every magnet.

The market is changing.

But in the stages that matter most for magnet rare earths, concentration is high enough that normal commercial dependency can become geopolitical dependency very quickly.

The sharper phrase is not mining monopoly.

It is processing gravity.

Ore, capital, customers, knowledge, and manufacturing have been pulled toward the same industrial center for decades.

Breaking that gravity takes more than opening a mine.

It takes building a rival system dense enough that the parts reinforce one another.

Mountain Pass matters because it is becoming more than a mine again.

Lynas matters because it proves separation at scale outside China is not imaginary.

The new U.S., Australian, Malaysian, European, and allied projects matter because they create options.

None of them, alone, is liberation.

That is the point.

Resilience is rarely one giant replacement plant.

It is enough independent capacity at enough stages that one government cannot stop an entire downstream industry with a licensing form.

Rare earths reveal the deepest version of the chokepoint thesis.

The scarce thing is not always the element.

It can be the competence to transform the element reliably, cheaply, and at scale.

Geology creates possibility.

Industry creates leverage.

Once that is clear, the next question becomes unavoidable.

What happens when a country decides to use the leverage deliberately?

The answer did not begin in 2025.

The supply chain had already been tested as an instrument of statecraft years earlier.
