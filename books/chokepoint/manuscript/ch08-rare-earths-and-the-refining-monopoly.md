# Rare Earths and the Refining Monopoly

Mountain Pass is easy to misunderstand.

There is a mine in California sitting on one of the richest known rare-earth deposits in the world. For decades it was one of the most important rare-earth sources on Earth. It closed. It reopened. It now produces a meaningful share of global material again.

From a distance, that sounds like a solved problem.

The United States has rare earths. It has a mine. Dig more.

The flaw is in the verb.

Rare earths do not become useful because they have been dug out of the ground. They become useful after a chain of chemically demanding transformations separates elements that nature deposits together and industry needs apart.

The International Energy Agency's 2026 assessment puts the imbalance plainly. China accounted for about 60 percent of global mine production of magnet rare earths in 2024.

At refining, its share was about 91 percent.

At production of sintered permanent magnets, about 94 percent.

Those numbers describe three different problems. Sixty percent mining share still leaves obvious geological alternatives. Ninety-one percent refining share means competitors must rebuild an industrial process. Ninety-four percent magnet share means a customer can remain dependent on the incumbent even after ore and refining have begun to diversify.

Counting mines can therefore create false comfort.

Rare-earth material has to be crushed, concentrated, chemically upgraded, separated into individual oxides, refined into metals, alloyed, turned into powder, made into magnets, shaped, coated, qualified, and integrated into components. Each stage has its own yield, purity, waste, cost, equipment, and customer requirements.

Nature does not make the separation easy. The rare earths are chemically similar and often occur together. Industry cares enormously about their differences. Solvent-extraction circuits exploit small differences over many repeated stages until the required purity appears.

The equipment is not magical.

The operating knowledge can be.

A plant can be built from drawings and still spend years learning how to hold yield, purity, reagent consumption, throughput, and waste handling inside a commercially stable range. That accumulated competence is one reason China's position is difficult to dislodge.

It was not created by better rocks alone. It grew through decades of extraction, chemical processing, magnets, domestic manufacturing demand, infrastructure, engineering practice, state support, price competition, and willingness to bear environmental costs that other jurisdictions often resisted.

The result is an ecosystem.

Mountain Pass demonstrates what that means.

The mine was once globally dominant, then suffered through changing markets, environmental problems, competition, and underinvestment. Production was idled in 2015. MP Materials acquired the site and restarted mining in 2017. By 2019, the company said its output represented roughly 15 percent of global rare-earth content.

Yet much of what it sold was concentrate.

Ore had been mined and upgraded in California without recreating the full separation chain that once made the site strategically self-contained.

The United States could possess the deposit and still lack the industrial step that made the deposit useful on its own terms.

MP began restoring separation and refining at Mountain Pass in 2023. By 2025 it reported record production of neodymium-praseodymium oxide and had begun producing metal and magnets at a Texas facility.

The progress is real. So is what had to be rebuilt after the geology was already solved.

The U.S. Department of Defense's support makes the distinction unusually visible. Its rare-earth strategy separates sourcing, separation, processing, metallization, alloying, and magnet manufacturing into distinct nodes. A 2025 financing package included a $150 million loan specifically to add heavy-rare-earth separation capability at Mountain Pass.

Not to discover the deposit.

Not to reopen the mine.

To separate the elements.

Lynas Rare Earths provides the most important countercase to the idea that large-scale separation is impossible outside China. Lynas mines at Mt Weld in Western Australia, carries out initial processing in Australia, and operates a large advanced-materials plant in Malaysia that separates mixed material into individual products. It has also added cracking and leaching capacity at Kalgoorlie.

The nouns matter because policy debates flatten them into supply while industry experiences a sequence.

Mine. Concentrate. Crack. Leach. Separate. Refine. Metal. Magnet.

A country can succeed at six and still depend on somebody else for the seventh.

That is why governments are subsidizing facilities that can look expensive under ordinary commodity assumptions. A duplicate separator may be uncompetitive while Chinese material is cheap and reliable. During a disruption, the same plant can become strategic infrastructure.

The private company and the defense ministry are solving different problems. The company sees volatile prices, a large capital bill, uncertain offtake, and an incumbent with enormous scale. The ministry sees a missile, aircraft, motor, or radar supply chain that cannot tolerate one foreign industrial center forever.

The gap between those calculations becomes industrial policy.

The difficulty is that subsidies cannot quickly reproduce an ecosystem. A new separator needs customers willing to sign long contracts. Customers want proof of reliable output before they commit. Investors want contracts before they finance the plant. The plant needs financing before it can prove reliability.

Diversification therefore advances in awkward increments: a mine opens, a separator commissions, a magnet line qualifies one customer, a government provides a price floor, an automaker signs an offtake agreement, a refinery learns one product before adding another.

No single announcement means the chain is complete.

The good news is that the chain is changing.

The IEA's 2026 outlook reported rare-earth refining as a notable exception to the broader trend of rising concentration. New U.S. projects and increased Malaysian production caused a modest decline between 2023 and 2025. Mountain Pass is refining again. Lynas is expanding. Projects are moving in Australia, the United States, Europe, and elsewhere. Recycling and material thrift can reduce future dependence.

Japan's response to earlier rare-earth disruptions already showed that demand can move. The IEA estimates Japanese policies and industrial adaptation helped reduce total rare-earth demand by roughly 30 percent from 2010 levels.

A chokepoint is not permanent merely because it is severe.

But timing still matters. Even with announced projects, diversified capacity outside China remains much thinner downstream than at the mine. The mental picture created by a mineral map is almost backwards.

The map highlights deposits.

The risk increasingly lives in factories.

There is an environmental reason the incumbent system became so hard to copy cheaply. Rare-earth separation creates difficult waste. Ores can contain thorium and uranium. Chemical processing produces acidic streams, sludge, and residues that require careful handling. Poorly managed operations can contaminate water and soil and leave liabilities long after production stops.

China's historical willingness to absorb part of those costs lowered the visible price of the product while moving costs elsewhere. New facilities in Australia, Malaysia, the United States, or Europe face environmental review, waste-management requirements, and disposal costs that can make diversification slower and more expensive.

That can also make it better.

A second supply chain is not resilient if it merely recreates the pollution that made the first one cheap.

The harder question is what rare-earth material actually costs once environmental control, redundancy, and industrial learning are included.

That price can look absurd beside spot material in a calm market.

Then the export license does not arrive.

On April 4, 2025, China's Ministry of Commerce and customs authority put export controls on seven categories of medium- and heavy-rare-earth-related items. The notice covered specified metals, alloys, oxides, compounds, targets, and, within some categories, permanent-magnet materials.

The IEA later reported export volumes falling sharply in April and May. Automakers outside China struggled to secure magnets. Some reduced production or temporarily stopped lines. Licenses were eventually granted and shipments recovered.

Factories discovered that the magnet was a tiny component with a large veto.

A modern car can contain thousands of dollars of sophisticated electronics and still be stopped by a comparatively small permanent magnet that cannot be qualified from another supplier on Tuesday afternoon.

This is what refining power becomes downstream.

The commodity may be small. The unavailable machine is not.

That asymmetry gives the incumbent leverage and gives customers a reason to destroy it. Every export restriction strengthens the business case for Mountain Pass, Lynas, recycling, magnet thrift, and alternate motor designs. Every licensing delay makes a costly second supplier look less like waste.

The same dynamic followed the 2010 rare-earth crisis. Japan diversified suppliers, invested abroad, improved recycling, reduced material intensity, and supported substitutes. Chinese dominance remained, but it became less absolute than it otherwise would have been.

This is why monopoly is too simple a word. China does not own every rare-earth mine, separator, or magnet factory. The market is changing. But in the stages that matter most for magnet rare earths, concentration is still high enough that ordinary commercial dependence can become geopolitical dependence quickly.

The sharper phrase is processing gravity.

Ore, capital, customers, knowledge, equipment, and manufacturing have been pulled toward the same industrial center for decades. Breaking that gravity takes more than another mine. It takes a rival system dense enough that the parts reinforce one another.

Mountain Pass matters because it is becoming more than a mine again. Lynas matters because it proves separation at scale outside China is not imaginary. New projects matter because they create options.

None of them alone is liberation.

Resilience is enough independent capacity at enough stages that one government cannot stop an entire downstream industry with a licensing form.

Rare earths therefore reveal the deepest version of the chokepoint thesis.

The scarce thing is not always the element.

It can be the competence to transform the element reliably, cleanly, and at scale.

Once that is clear, the next question is what happens when a government decides to use the leverage deliberately.
