# Deep Sea and Desperate Measures

Several kilometers below the Pacific, the argument over critical minerals becomes almost absurdly literal. The metal is sitting on the seafloor.

Polymetallic nodules lie across parts of the abyssal plain carrying manganese, nickel, cobalt, and copper. There is no mountain to blast, no conventional overburden to remove, no open pit, and no town sitting directly above the ore body. If the question were only where to find more metal, the ocean would look like an escape hatch.

That is the sales pitch. The harder question is what the escape hatch opens into.

Deep-sea nodules do avoid some of the constraints that make terrestrial mining difficult. They are exposed at the surface of the seabed. Several valuable metals occur in the same object. A collector would not need to move the same volumes of waste rock associated with many land mines. Those advantages are real enough that they should survive contact with environmental criticism.

But different is not harmless, and new geology does not mean supply without chokepoints. The ore sits in an ecosystem we understand less well than most landscapes already mined, under legal regimes that are still being contested and completed, behind specialized vessels and collector systems, and upstream of the same processing, financing, qualification, and customer relationships that turn any mineral resource into usable supply.

The distance between a nodule visible on a resource map and a tonne of qualified metal delivered to a customer is the industry.

In 2026, that distance became politically important because the United States moved its regulatory process faster than the physical industry moved.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration changed its rules on January 21, 2026 so a qualified applicant could seek an exploration license and commercial recovery permit through one consolidated application rather than two sequential applications. That shortened the process. It did not erase the two authorizations or the underlying statutory requirements.

Then, on August 19, NOAA publicly noticed a consolidated application from The Metals Company USA. The comment period runs through October 19, with a virtual public hearing scheduled for October 13.

The application is real.

The permit is not.

As of September 2026, NOAA's own table lists no existing commercial recovery permits under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act. That is a useful fact because policy debates routinely compress procedural motion into commercial reality. "Deep-sea mining advances through U.S. review" and "deep-sea mining has been approved" describe very different states of the world.

The distinction is more than legal housekeeping. If a mine is not permitted, financed, built, operated, monitored, processed, and connected to customers, it is not supply. A faster permit route may remove one bottleneck while revealing the others.

Offshore there is also more than one legal map.

The United States is not a party to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. Under U.S. law, NOAA describes a domestic route for U.S. companies seeking licenses and permits for activity beyond national jurisdiction. The International Seabed Authority, by contrast, governs mineral activity in the international seabed Area for parties to the convention and maintains that the Mining Code must be completed within the multilateral framework before future commercial activity in the Area proceeds.

That code is still unfinished.

In March 2026, the ISA said its Council had discussed twenty-nine of thirty-two outstanding issues in the draft exploitation regulations and reached agreement in principle on several. That sounds close until you look at what remains bundled inside the word "code": environmental rules, financial terms, benefit sharing, compliance, standards and guidelines, impact assessment, governance, responsibility for damage, and the conditions under which industrial activity in a global commons should begin at all.

A mining code is usually written for an industry whose basic existence is settled. Here the institution is writing rules while the commercial industry itself remains contingent.

That makes regulation part of the supply chain rather than paperwork surrounding it.

The ecological question is equally resistant to slogans. A nodule is not only ore. On much of the abyssal plain, hard substrate is scarce. Organisms attach to nodules, and removing them changes the physical habitat as well as the inventory of metal. The nodules themselves form on geological timescales, so a collector cannot meaningfully replace what it removes on a human planning horizon.

The strongest environmental argument against mining therefore does not need the abyss to be a pristine aquarium full of familiar charismatic species. It is enough to observe that industrial disturbance would occur in an ecosystem with slow physical processes, incomplete baselines, and uncertain recovery.

But uncertainty cuts both ways. It is not evidence that every feared effect will occur at the largest imaginable scale.

A 2025 *Nature* study returned to tracks left by an experimental mining vehicle forty-four years earlier. Physical disturbance was still visible, and biological communities in directly disturbed areas remained altered. At the same time, several groups of organisms had begun to re-establish, while areas affected mainly by the old sediment plume showed less persistent change than the collection tracks themselves.

That is a much more useful result than either headline version. The seabed did not simply bounce back. Neither was every disturbed patch biologically empty four decades later.

The study also makes comparison difficult because the 1978 collector was not a modern commercial machine. Its propulsion system penetrated the seafloor differently from current tracked designs, the disturbed area was small relative to a commercial mine, and nodule removal was incomplete in places. It is evidence about persistence and recovery, not a full-scale dress rehearsal for a future industry.

Another 2025 field study complicated the sediment-plume debate. Under the test conditions, much of the benthic plume remained close to the seabed, and measured concentrations roughly fifty metres above bottom returned near background levels. That undercuts the most cinematic version of a plume spreading indefinitely through the water column.

It does not make the collector track benign. Direct erosion and local sediment redeposition still occurred near the mining lanes. "The plume did not travel as far as feared" and "the seabed was materially altered" can both be true.

Midwater discharge creates a different comparison. Work published in 2025 found that particles returned to mesopelagic or bathypelagic waters may be nutritionally poor relative to the natural particles supporting food webs there. In that case, the issue is not merely turbidity. Animals may encounter particles that resemble food but deliver less energy.

The engineering choice of where to discharge material therefore creates ecological tradeoffs rather than a convenient place to make waste disappear.

None of this settles whether nodules are environmentally worse than the minerals they might replace on land. That is the comparison that matters, and it is also the comparison most likely to be rigged by choosing the wrong baseline.

Opponents can compare an untouched seabed with a world in which no additional nickel, cobalt, copper, or manganese is mined anywhere. Proponents can compare nodules with the worst terrestrial mines and treat the absence of an open pit or tailings dam as proof of superiority.

Neither comparison is especially useful.

If demand persists, declining one source shifts some burden elsewhere. Nickel can come with forest clearing and waste. Cobalt and copper production can create pollution, water use, tailings, and community disruption. New mines also require roads, power, ports, capital, permits, and years of construction. Deep-sea mining deserves to be judged against those real alternatives, not against an imaginary zero-impact supply chain.

A 2022 systems analysis argued that producing some battery metals from polymetallic nodules could generate less mining and processing waste than several terrestrial routes. That finding deserves attention, especially because waste mass is one of the legitimate attractions of the ocean route. It also deserves skepticism: one author was affiliated with The Metals Company, and lower waste tonnage does not settle the ecological comparison.

A tonne of tailings and a square kilometre of altered abyssal habitat are not naturally commensurable units.

Any serious comparison has to make value judgments explicit. How should freshwater use be weighed against deep-seabed habitat disturbance? How much avoided deforestation offsets a habitat that may recover slowly? How should community displacement on land be compared with harm to a place without resident human communities but with uncertain ecological function? How much weight belongs on tailings risk, carbon intensity, biodiversity, and reversibility?

Engineering can estimate flows and probabilities. It cannot decide the exchange rate among all harms by itself.

That is why deep-sea mining is a useful test for this book. It looks at first like a way around concentrated terrestrial geology. Then every other layer of supply reappears.

Who has the legal right to collect? Which environmental threshold applies? Which vessel can do the work? Who manufactures the collector? Where are the nodules processed? Which refinery can separate the metals economically? Who finances a project whose permitting regime is unsettled? Which customers will qualify the output? Who monitors the operation? Who pays if environmental assumptions prove wrong?

A resource in the international seabed is not trapped inside one country's mountain range, but that does not make it geopolitically neutral. Geography is only one source of concentration.

Specialized ships can be chokepoints. Processing knowledge can be a chokepoint. Environmental approval can be a chokepoint. A small number of capital providers can be a chokepoint. A disputed jurisdiction can be a chokepoint. A technology supplied by one vendor can be a chokepoint.

New ore can diversify geology while concentrating everything required to reach it.

The current policy race is trying to compress some of those constraints. The United States accelerated its domestic regulatory route in 2026. The ISA continues negotiating the multilateral regime. Companies naturally prefer a path that reaches a commercial decision sooner. Environmental groups want enough time to establish baselines and test uncertain impacts. Governments worried about mineral dependence operate on a strategic clock. Marine science operates on another.

The ocean does not negotiate the schedule.

A rule can change in months. Ecological recovery can take decades. A company can accelerate an application but cannot retroactively create a baseline after disturbance begins. A regulator can demand more study but cannot make uncertainty disappear. A critic can ask for perfect knowledge, but industrial policy almost never arrives under conditions of perfect knowledge.

The question is not whether uncertainty can be eliminated. It is which uncertainties are tolerable, which harms are reversible, which safeguards are worth delaying supply for, and who has legitimate authority to make those judgments.

That last question becomes unusually difficult in the international seabed. Under the Law of the Sea framework, the Area and its resources are treated as the common heritage of humankind. Whatever one thinks of the phrase, it changes the political problem.

A terrestrial mine is already entangled with ownership, local consent, taxation, royalties, environmental law, and national sovereignty. An industrial site beyond national jurisdiction adds another layer: what claim does any company or state have to convert shared space into a mine, and under what rules should benefits and liabilities be distributed?

This is why the unfinished Mining Code matters. A permissive regime could authorize damage that later proves difficult to reverse. A regime designed around worst-case assumptions could block an industry that ultimately turns out to reduce some serious terrestrial harms. A slow process can push companies toward alternative jurisdictions. A fast one can lock in technical and ecological assumptions before enough evidence exists to challenge them.

There is no option without cost, including the option to wait.

That is the strongest counterargument to treating deep-sea mining as simply reckless. Delay has a shadow price too. If new terrestrial production is slower, dirtier, more concentrated, or more politically fragile, refusing an ocean route may increase dependence elsewhere. The relevant comparison is not mining versus innocence. It is one supply portfolio against another.

But that same logic cuts against the industry's easiest promise. A new source of ore does not abolish scarcity politics. It can merely relocate them from geology into permission, technology, processing, legitimacy, and finance.

Deep-sea mining begins as an attempt to escape a chokepoint and quickly becomes a lesson in how chokepoints move.

The evidence available in 2026 does not support certainty that polymetallic nodules will become a major commercial source. It also does not support certainty that they can never be developed responsibly. What it supports is narrower and more useful: the seabed is not a frictionless frontier, and a mineral resource is only as diversified as the system required to turn it into supply.

That brings the book back to land with a different question. If there is no simple frontier beyond the existing mineral system, perhaps resilience comes from building more routes through the system we already have.

That is the promise of friend-shoring.
