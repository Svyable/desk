# Deep Sea and Desperate Measures

Several kilometers below the Pacific, the argument over critical minerals becomes almost absurdly literal.

The metal is sitting on the seafloor.

Not in a mountain. Not under a village. Not beneath a forest. Fist-sized polymetallic nodules lie scattered across the abyssal plain carrying manganese, nickel, cobalt, and copper.

The sales pitch almost writes itself.

Why cut another road through another watershed if the metal can be collected from a place with no farms, no towns, and no conventional mine pit? Why accept another tailings dam if the ore arrives as loose objects on the ocean floor?

The difficulty begins with the word `different`.

Different is not the same as harmless.

Nodules appear to evade several constraints that dominate terrestrial mining. They are exposed at the surface. They contain several useful metals in one object. There is no conventional overburden or open pit.

Those are real advantages.

The other side of the comparison is an ecosystem we understand far less well than most places already mined, a legal regime still being written, and an industry that remains between test operation, regulatory experiment, and commercial ambition.

As of September 2026, no U.S. company holds a commercial recovery permit under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act.

That matters because the politics have moved faster than the physical industry.

In January 2026, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration changed its regulations so a qualified company could submit a consolidated application for both an exploration license and a commercial recovery permit. The change combined parts of the process; it did not make the underlying statutory requirements disappear.

In August, NOAA publicly noticed a consolidated application from The Metals Company USA.

The application is real.

The permit is not.

That distinction sounds procedural until a policy debate hardens into a headline. `Deep-sea mining approved` is much more exciting than `a government is reviewing a consolidated application under a statute written for an industry that does not yet operate commercially at scale.`

Offshore, there is also more than one legal map.

The United States is not a party to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. Under U.S. law, NOAA asserts authority to license U.S. companies for exploration and commercial recovery beyond national jurisdiction if statutory requirements are met.

For countries that are parties to the convention, the International Seabed Authority governs mineral activity in the international seabed Area.

The ISA has spent years building an exploitation code.

It is still unfinished.

In March 2026, the Authority said its Council had discussed twenty-nine of thirty-two outstanding issues in the draft regulations and reached agreement in principle on several. That sounds close until one considers what the code must do: decide how an industry may begin altering a global commons before commercial operation has been demonstrated and while many ecological baselines remain incomplete.

This is not merely a mining code. It is an attempt to write the first rules for an industry while deciding what the first industry should be allowed to do.

That institutional uncertainty is not separate from supply. It is part of the route from resource to supply.

The ocean adds another difficulty: the nodule is not only ore.

It is habitat.

Hard substrate is scarce on an abyssal plain dominated by soft sediment. Organisms attach to nodules and communities organize around them. Removing a nodule therefore removes both the mineral and physical structure that took geological time to form.

That does not mean every disturbed patch becomes permanently sterile.

A 2025 *Nature* study revisited an experimental mining track created forty-four years earlier. Directly disturbed areas were still visibly altered and biological communities remained changed. Several organism groups had re-established, and areas affected mainly by sediment plumes showed more recovery than the tracks disturbed directly by machinery.

The useful finding is neither apocalypse nor reassurance.

Some damage persists for decades. Some communities recover. Direct removal and indirect sediment exposure are different disturbances.

A 2025 *Nature Communications* field study added another boundary. Much of the benthic sediment plume in the test remained close to the seabed; at roughly fifty metres above the bottom, measured concentrations had returned near background levels under the test conditions.

That cuts against the most dramatic picture of a plume spreading indefinitely through the water column.

It does not make the collector track benign. The same work documented direct erosion and centimetre-scale sediment redeposition near mining lanes.

`The plume did not travel as far as feared` and `the seafloor was physically altered` can both be true.

Midwater discharge creates a different problem. Another 2025 study found that particles returned to mesopelagic or bathypelagic waters may be nutritionally poor relative to the natural particles supporting local food webs. The concern is not only cloudiness. Animals can encounter more particles that look like food but carry less energy.

A disturbance can travel through appetite.

That makes discharge depth an engineering choice with ecological tradeoffs rather than a convenient place to put the unwanted fraction.

The strongest environmental case for nodules should not pretend the abyss is empty.

It should look back at land.

Nickel mines can clear tropical forest. Cobalt production can create large waste streams and pollution risks. Copper mines move immense volumes of rock and can consume large amounts of water and energy. Tailings facilities remain after the price cycle that financed them.

If the world wants more nickel, cobalt, copper, and manganese, leaving nodules alone does not make demand vanish. Some of that demand will be met elsewhere.

A peer-reviewed systems analysis published in 2022 found that producing battery metals from polymetallic nodules could create less mining and processing waste than several terrestrial routes.

That deserves attention. So do its limits. One author was affiliated with The Metals Company, and a lower mass of waste rock does not settle the ecological comparison. A tonne of terrestrial tailings and a square kilometre of disturbed abyssal habitat are not naturally commensurable units.

The comparison requires judgments about freshwater, deforestation, community displacement, tailings risk, habitat recovery, and ecosystems whose species inventories remain incomplete.

Engineering can estimate flows.

It cannot decide what society should value without help.

That is why the easy arguments fail in both directions. Opponents can compare an untouched seabed with a world in which no additional mining happens anywhere. Proponents can treat the absence of an open pit as proof the ocean route is environmentally superior.

The real comparison is between portfolios of harm under uncertainty.

Nodules also scramble the geography of mineral power. A resource in the international seabed is not inside one country's mountain range. That looks like diversification until one asks who owns the collector technology, the specialized vessels, the processing plants, the capital, the permits, and the customer relationships needed to turn nodules into qualified metal.

A new ore source does not abolish chokepoints.

It moves them.

Rights, ships, collector systems, processing knowledge, environmental approval, ports, and jurisdiction can all become constraints. The distance between a nodule visible on a map and a permitted, financed, operating, monitored, processed, qualified stream of material is the industry itself.

That distance is what the current policy fight is trying to compress.

The United States accelerated its domestic regulatory route in 2026. The ISA continued negotiating the multilateral route. Companies prefer the path that reaches a commercial decision first. Environmental groups prefer enough time to understand uncertain impacts. Governments worried about mineral dependence have their own clock.

Marine science proceeds on a fourth.

The ocean does not negotiate the schedule.

A government can change an application process quickly. It cannot make decades of ecological recovery happen quickly. A company can accelerate a permit request. It cannot create a missing environmental baseline after disturbance begins. A critic can demand perfect knowledge, but industrial choices are rarely offered under perfect knowledge.

The decision is about how much uncertainty can be tolerated and who gets to decide.

That last question matters because the deep seabed is not merely remote territory. Under the international legal framework, the Area and its resources are treated as the common heritage of humankind.

Whatever one thinks of the phrase, it changes the politics. A mine inside a national border is already tangled with local consent, law, taxes, royalties, and environmental authority. A mine in the international seabed adds the question of what claim any one company or state has to turn shared space into an industrial site.

That is why the unfinished Mining Code matters beyond paperwork. It has to address benefit sharing, environmental thresholds, compliance, financial terms, and responsibility for damage before anyone knows whether the regime is protective, permissive, or commercially workable.

A bad rule could stop a useful industry. A weak rule could authorize damage that cannot be repaired on a human timetable. A slow rule can push companies toward another jurisdiction. A fast rule can lock in assumptions the science later overturns.

There is no option without cost.

Deep-sea mining therefore begins as an attempt to escape terrestrial constraints and ends by revealing new ones.

Nodules may reduce dependence on some land deposits. They do not remove the need for law, capital, processing, engineering, environmental judgment, or legitimacy. They do not tell us how much avoided tailings or freshwater use is worth against disturbance to an abyssal ecosystem.

And they do not become commercial supply simply because the metal is visible on a map.

The evidence available in 2026 supports neither certainty that deep-sea mining will become a major supply source nor certainty that it can never do so responsibly.

What it does support is a narrower conclusion.

There is no frictionless frontier.

Every new source arrives embedded in a place, a process, a politics, and a set of harms that have to be compared with alternatives.

If the deep ocean cannot provide a simple escape from concentrated supply, the next response is less dramatic and probably more important: build more routes through the supply system we already have.

That is the promise of friend-shoring.
