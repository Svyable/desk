# Deep Sea and Desperate Measures

Several kilometers below the Pacific, the argument over critical minerals becomes almost absurdly literal.

The metal is sitting on the seafloor.

Not in a mountain. Not under a village. Not beneath a forest. Not trapped inside an orebody that has to be blasted, hauled, crushed, and stripped of enormous amounts of waste rock before the useful fraction appears.

It sits in dark, fist-sized nodules scattered across the abyssal plain.

Manganese. Nickel. Cobalt. Copper.

The sales pitch almost writes itself.

Why cut another road through another watershed if the metal can be collected from a place with no farms, no towns, and no conventional mine pit?

Why accept another tailings dam if the ore arrives already concentrated in loose objects on the ocean floor?

Why fight over a new terrestrial deposit if the deep sea offers a different map entirely?

The difficulty begins with the word `different`.

Different is not the same as harmless.

Deep-sea mining is attractive because it appears to evade several of the constraints that dominate the previous chapters. The nodules are already exposed. They contain several useful metals in one object. There is no overburden in the conventional sense. No open pit has to be excavated. No forest has to be cleared to reach the deposit.

Those are real advantages.

They are also only one side of the comparison.

The other side is an ecosystem we understand far less well than almost any place where mining already happens, a legal regime still being written, and an industry that remains somewhere between test operation, regulatory experiment, and commercial ambition.

As of September 2026, no U.S. company holds a commercial recovery permit under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act.

That sentence matters because the politics have moved faster than the physical industry.

In January 2026, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration changed its regulations so a qualified company could submit a consolidated application for both an exploration license and a commercial recovery permit. The change shortened and combined parts of the process. It did not make the underlying statutory requirements disappear.

Then, in August, NOAA publicly noticed a consolidated application from The Metals Company USA.

The application is real.

So is the public comment period.

So is the commercial ambition.

The permit is not.

This distinction sounds procedural until one remembers how quickly a policy debate can harden into a headline. `Deep-sea mining approved` is a much more exciting sentence than `a government is reviewing a consolidated application for permission to explore and eventually recover material under a statute written for an industry that does not yet exist at commercial scale.`

The second sentence is less elegant.

It is also the current situation.

The regulatory problem becomes stranger offshore because there is more than one legal map.

The United States is not a party to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. Under U.S. law, NOAA asserts authority to license U.S. companies for exploration and commercial recovery of polymetallic nodules beyond national jurisdiction, provided the statutory requirements are met.

For countries that are parties to the convention, the International Seabed Authority governs mineral activities in the international seabed Area.

The ISA has spent years building an exploitation code.

It is still unfinished.

In March 2026, the Authority said its Council had discussed twenty-nine of thirty-two outstanding issues in the draft regulations and reached agreement in principle on several of them. That sounds close until one notices what the document is supposed to do.

It has to decide how an industry may begin altering large areas of a global commons before the industry has demonstrated commercial operation and before many ecological baselines are well understood.

This is not merely a mining code.

It is an attempt to write rules for the first version of an industry while simultaneously deciding whether that first version should exist.

The legal tension became explicit in 2026 when contractor disputes involving Nauru Ocean Resources Inc. and Tonga Offshore Mining Ltd. moved before the Seabed Disputes Chamber of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea.

The Chamber issued provisional-measures orders in July.

Those orders did not settle jurisdiction or the merits.

The important point is not that one side won.

It is that the disagreement has reached the stage where institutions are litigating the authority to investigate contractor conduct while the larger exploitation regime remains incomplete.

This is what desperation looks like in policy form.

The minerals are strategically valuable enough that governments and companies are no longer willing to wait for every institutional question to become tidy.

The ocean, inconveniently, remains untidy too.

A polymetallic nodule looks geologically simple compared with a terrestrial orebody.

Ecologically it is part of the habitat.

The nodule is not merely a pebble containing metal. It is hard substrate on a seafloor otherwise dominated by soft sediment. Organisms attach to nodules. Communities live around them. Removing the nodule therefore removes both the mineral and a piece of physical structure that took geological time to form.

That does not mean every disturbed patch becomes permanently sterile.

One of the most useful studies in this debate exists because somebody disturbed the seafloor long before anyone expected the argument to become this politically important.

A 2025 study in *Nature* revisited an experimental mining track created forty-four years earlier. The directly disturbed areas were still visibly altered. Biological communities remained changed.

But the result was not `nothing came back`.

Several organism groups had re-established. Areas affected mainly by sediment plumes showed more recovery than the tracks directly disturbed by machinery.

That is a less cinematic finding than ecological apocalypse.

It is also more disturbing in a useful way.

The effects are neither obviously temporary nor uniformly permanent.

Some damage persists for decades.

Some communities recover.

Direct removal and indirect sediment exposure are not the same disturbance.

A serious decision therefore cannot be made from one word like `plume` or one photograph of a collector vehicle crawling through sediment.

The mechanisms matter.

A collector running across the abyssal plain disturbs the seafloor directly. Sediment is lifted. Some settles nearby. Some moves farther. A commercial system may also return processed water and fine particles into the ocean at another depth.

Those are separate environmental problems.

A 2025 *Nature Communications* field study of collector disturbance found that much of the benthic sediment plume remained close to the seabed. At roughly fifty metres above the bottom, measured concentrations had returned near background levels in the test conditions.

That result cuts against the most dramatic version of a plume spreading indefinitely through the water column.

It does not make the collector track benign.

The same work documented direct erosion and centimetre-scale sediment redeposition near the mining lanes.

Near the bottom, `the plume did not travel as far as feared` and `the seafloor was physically altered` can both be true.

A different 2025 study looked higher in the water column and found another problem. If mining waste is discharged into mesopelagic or bathypelagic waters, the added particles may be nutritionally poor compared with the natural particles that support local food webs. The concern is not simply that the water becomes cloudy. It is that animals encounter a larger volume of particles that look like food but carry less energy.

A disturbance can therefore travel through appetite.

That is a stranger mechanism than most terrestrial mining debates prepare us for.

It also makes the location of discharge a design choice rather than an afterthought. Returning material closer to the seafloor may reduce one class of midwater risk while increasing or concentrating another benthic burden.

There is no clean place to put the unwanted fraction of a process simply because the mine is underwater.

The strongest environmental argument for mining nodules starts here, not with claims that the abyss is empty.

It starts by looking back at land.

Nickel mines can clear tropical forest.

Cobalt production can generate large waste streams and expose communities to pollution.

Copper mines can move staggering quantities of rock and consume large volumes of water and energy. Tailings facilities remain long after the commodity cycle that financed them. Processing can create acidic waste, toxic residues, and emissions.

The land-mining comparison is not a rhetorical trick.

It is the correct counterfactual.

If the world wants more nickel, cobalt, copper, and manganese, leaving the nodules alone does not make demand disappear.

Some of that demand will be met elsewhere.

A peer-reviewed systems analysis published in 2022 found that producing battery metals from polymetallic nodules could create less mining and processing waste than several terrestrial routes.

That deserves to be taken seriously.

So do its boundaries.

One author was affiliated with The Metals Company. More importantly, a lower mass of waste rock does not settle the ecological comparison. A tonne of terrestrial tailings and a square kilometre of disturbed abyssal habitat are not naturally commensurable units.

The spreadsheet has to assign values before it can declare a winner.

How much weight belongs on habitat recovery that may take decades?

How much on freshwater use?

How much on deforestation?

How much on community displacement?

How much on an ecosystem whose full species inventory is incomplete?

How much on tailings risk that is familiar enough to regulate but still capable of catastrophic failure?

Engineering can estimate flows.

It cannot decide what society should value without help.

This is why deep-sea mining makes environmental arguments uncomfortable in both directions.

Opponents can speak as though the choice is between an untouched seabed and no additional mining anywhere.

Proponents can speak as though avoiding a conventional open pit automatically makes the ocean option superior.

Neither comparison survives contact with the whole system.

The real choice is between portfolios of harm under uncertainty.

That sounds bureaucratic.

It is morally harder than choosing a villain.

There is another reason the debate has become so intense: nodules scramble the geography of mineral power.

The previous chapters have dealt with chokepoints created by geology and industrial concentration. Copper occurs widely but takes a long time to develop. Cobalt mine supply is concentrated in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Nickel growth has shifted toward Indonesia. Rare-earth separation and magnet manufacturing remain heavily concentrated in China.

A large nodule resource in the international seabed suggests a route around part of that geography.

The material is not inside one country's mountain range.

It lies in an area whose legal status is itself international.

That sounds like diversification until one asks who owns the collector technology, the vessels, the processing plants, the permits, the capital, and the political relationships required to turn nodules into qualified metal.

A new ore source does not abolish chokepoints.

It moves them.

Mining rights become a chokepoint.

Specialized ships become a chokepoint.

Collector systems become a chokepoint.

Processing knowledge becomes a chokepoint.

Environmental approval becomes a chokepoint.

A port capable of handling the material can become a chokepoint.

The jurisdiction willing to authorize the activity becomes one too.

This should feel familiar by now.

The book's recurring mistake to avoid is treating a resource as supply before the institutions and machinery around it exist.

A nodule on the seabed is potential supply.

A permitted, financed, operating, monitored, processed, and qualified stream of material is supply.

The distance between those two things is the industry.

That distance is exactly what the policy fight is trying to compress.

The United States accelerated its domestic route in 2026. The ISA continued negotiating its multilateral one. Companies have every incentive to prefer the path that reaches a commercial decision first.

Environmental groups have the opposite incentive where speed reduces the opportunity to understand uncertain impacts.

Governments that worry about mineral dependence have yet another clock.

They are watching supply chains tighten while marine science proceeds at the speed required to understand places that are expensive to reach and slow to recover.

Every actor is impatient for a different reason.

The ocean does not negotiate the schedule.

This is the deepest similarity between the deep sea and the mines already discussed on land.

Physical systems do not become politically convenient because a crisis makes them urgent.

A government can change a licensing process quickly.

It cannot make decades of ecological recovery happen quickly.

A company can accelerate an application.

It cannot retroactively create a baseline survey after commercial disturbance has begun.

A critic can demand perfect knowledge before action.

The world rarely offers perfect knowledge before any industrial choice.

The decision is therefore about how much uncertainty can be tolerated, and who gets to decide.

That last question matters because the deep seabed is not merely remote territory.

Under the international legal framework, the Area and its resources are treated as the common heritage of humankind.

Whatever one thinks of the phrase, it changes the politics.

A mine inside a national border is already tangled with local consent, national law, taxes, royalties, and environmental authority. A mine on the international seabed raises all of those questions while adding another: what claim does any one company or state have to turn a shared space into an industrial site?

That is why the unfinished Mining Code matters beyond paperwork.

It has to answer questions about benefit sharing, environmental thresholds, compliance, financial terms, and responsibility for damage before anyone can know whether the regime is merely permissive, actually protective, or economically workable.

A bad rule could stop a useful industry.

A weak rule could authorize damage that cannot be repaired on a human timetable.

A slow rule could encourage companies to seek another jurisdiction.

A fast rule could lock in assumptions the science later overturns.

There is no option without cost.

This makes deep-sea mining a fitting middle chapter in a book about chokepoints.

It begins as an attempt to escape scarcity.

It ends by revealing new forms of constraint.

The nodules may reduce dependence on some terrestrial deposits.

They do not remove the need for law, capital, processing, engineering, environmental judgment, or social legitimacy.

They do not settle whether a tonne of avoided terrestrial waste justifies a square kilometre of disturbed seabed.

They do not tell us how much uncertainty is acceptable before machinery crosses an ecosystem that may still show the track decades later.

And they do not make commercial supply appear simply because the metal is visible on a map.

That is the boundary worth preserving.

Deep-sea mining may eventually become part of the mineral system.

It may also remain too legally contested, ecologically uncertain, or economically awkward to scale as its advocates hope.

The evidence available in 2026 supports neither certainty.

What it does support is a more general conclusion.

There is no frictionless frontier.

Every new source of critical material arrives embedded in a place, a process, a politics, and a set of harms somebody has to compare with the alternatives.

Chapter 12 ended by asking what happens when terrestrial mines are slow and recycling cannot supply enough material yet.

The answer is that industry looks somewhere more extreme.

Then extreme becomes administrative.

A collector design becomes a permit application. A global commons becomes a jurisdictional argument. A patch of abyssal plain becomes a line in an environmental-impact statement. A nodule becomes an asset class.

That is how frontiers become supply chains.

If the deep ocean cannot provide a simple escape from concentrated supply, the next response is less dramatic and probably more important.

Do not find a new planet for the minerals.

Build more routes through the one we already trade across.

The next chapter is about friend-shoring: the attempt to replace dependence on a narrow chain with a wider network of mines, processors, financing, and alliances.
