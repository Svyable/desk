# Boom, Bust, Ghost Town

Kitsault was built to last longer than eighteen months.

That much is obvious from the town.

You do not build houses, roads, recreation facilities, a school, and the rest of a functioning community because you expect the mine beside it to disappear before the paint dries. You build them because the mine is remote, workers need somewhere to live, families need reasons to stay, and somebody has decided that the orebody beneath the mountains will remain valuable long enough to repay a great deal of concrete.

In northern British Columbia, near the Alaska border, that decision was tied to molybdenum.

Molybdenum is one of those metals that becomes important without becoming famous. Added to steel, it improves strength, hardness, and resistance to heat and corrosion. It matters in pipelines, power plants, tools, aircraft, chemical equipment, and other places where ordinary steel has an unpleasant job to do.

By the late 1970s, it was also expensive.

Kitsault had already been mined once, from 1967 to 1972. The later project was not a prospector's fantasy built on a handful of drill holes. The deposit was known. The site had history. When construction on the renewed operation began in 1979, the average U.S. spot price of molybdenum was $23.86 a pound. The mine that followed represented roughly $200 million of late-1970s capital and, once operating, employed about 500 people.

The project did what Chapter 10 described. It converted an expectation about future scarcity into fixed capital.

Then the expectation expired before the concrete did.

The reopened mine began operating in 1981. By November 1982 it was shutting down.

Consumption had weakened. Production had continued to rise. Inventories accumulated. Molybdenum prices fell far enough that the market price dropped below Kitsault's reported operating cost.

The new town had barely had time to become ordinary.

This is the part of a commodity cycle that is easy to flatten into morality. A boom is described as greed. A bust becomes punishment for believing the boom. The abandoned houses supply the visual metaphor.

But hindsight gets an unfair advantage.

A mine is not built because managers know the future. It is built because, after years of geology, engineering, market analysis, permitting, financing, and argument, enough people become willing to bet that the future price will cover the cost of turning rock into saleable metal.

Sometimes they are right for thirty years.

Sometimes for eighteen months.

Kitsault is useful because it exposes the mismatch between the durability of an investment and the volatility of the price that justified it.

The molybdenum price could fall in weeks. The mine could not unbuild itself at the same speed. A power line does not become liquid because a commodity does. Roads, mills, tailings systems, housing, and civic infrastructure remain stubbornly attached to the place where they were poured.

So do people, at least for a while.

The town was not merely decorative spending around the mine. Its remoteness made the community part of the production system. If you need hundreds of workers in a place with no existing labor pool large enough to commute, housing becomes industrial infrastructure. So does a school if you want workers with children to stay. Recreation matters for the same reason. The social architecture of the town was an attempt to solve a labor problem created by geography.

That means the capital cycle did not stop at the mill gate.

The company made a bet on the mine. Workers made a different kind of bet on the company. Families organized years of their lives around a place whose economic reason for existing could be repriced by traders far away.

The asymmetry is severe.

A corporation can impair an asset. A lender can restructure a loan. Equipment can sometimes be sold. Geological information can be stored indefinitely and reused when conditions improve.

A school year is less fungible.

This does not make Kitsault unique. Mining history is full of towns that expanded with ore and contracted with price. Nor does every mine create a company town anymore. Workers may commute from established communities, fly in and fly out, or live in camps designed to disappear when the project does. Many mines operate through several commodity cycles and support durable local economies.

That is the countercase worth keeping in view.

A boom-bust cycle is not a law of nature in the sense that every mine ends in boarded windows. Good deposits can survive bad prices. Low-cost operations may continue producing through downturns that kill competitors. Existing towns can diversify. Infrastructure built for one project can lower the cost of another. A mine that closes can reopen.

Kitsault itself proves the last point almost too neatly.

The molybdenum did not leave when the workers did.

Decades after the 1982 closure, the deposit was still large enough to attract another serious development effort. By 2011, provincial geological material again described a substantial resource and a proposed mine life of roughly sixteen years. In 2013 British Columbia granted the redevelopment conditional environmental approval. In 2014 the province issued a Mines Act permit for a project estimated at about C$812 million, with roughly 700 construction jobs and 300 operating jobs. Federal approval followed.

This is where a simple story about regulation also breaks.

The project had permits.

It still did not get built.

Later provincial environmental-assessment documentation recorded that lower metal prices and difficulty securing financing put construction on hold even after approvals had been obtained and a camp established.

The same place had now lived through two versions of the capital cycle.

First, high prices helped justify a mine and town that opened just as the market deteriorated. Decades later, a known brownfield deposit advanced through modern permitting only to meet a different veto: the project could not attract money on terms that made sense at the prevailing price.

Permits can make a mine possible.

They cannot make it economic.

That distinction matters because mineral policy often looks for a single villain. In one telling, governments delay projects until shortages become inevitable. In another, mining companies refuse to invest until prices are absurdly high. In another, financial markets are too short-term to fund strategic capacity. In another, local opposition makes new supply impossible.

All of those mechanisms can matter.

None is the whole machine.

Kitsault's history is a reminder that the commodity cycle can defeat a project even after geology is known, engineering is advanced, and governments have said yes. The price still gets a vote. So does the cost curve. So do lenders looking several years ahead and asking whether today's market will survive long enough to service tomorrow's debt.

This is why high prices contain the seed of low prices.

Scarcity attracts investment. Investment expands capacity. Capacity arrives slowly. By the time it arrives, demand may have weakened, competitors may have responded, substitutes may have improved, inventories may have accumulated, or a recession may have changed the market entirely.

The projects approved at the top of the cycle then enter production together.

The shortage solves itself with enough enthusiasm to create a surplus.

That is not a failure of markets. It is one of the ways markets work when production has long lead times.

But the human experience of that correction is not a line crossing another line on a chart.

An economic model can write `capacity exits the market`.

A town has to decide what to do with Tuesday.

There is another reason not to call every abandoned mine a total waste.

Fixed capital can retain option value.

Kitsault's later redevelopment attempt did not begin at year zero. The orebody had been drilled. Decades of geological information existed. Roads and power infrastructure existed. The site had a mine footprint. The place carried knowledge that a greenfield prospect did not.

A bust can therefore leave behind an option.

The option may remain out of the money for years. It may never be exercised. But if prices rise, technology improves, infrastructure changes, or strategic policy lowers the cost of financing, a brownfield project can return to the queue with years of uncertainty already retired.

This is the more precise meaning of stranded capital.

Some capital is dead.

Some is sleeping.

The difficult part is that nobody knows which kind at the moment the gate closes.

That uncertainty changes investment behavior across the whole industry. After a bust, investors remember the projects that opened late and lost money quickly. Management teams become cautious. Exploration budgets shrink. Marginal projects are deferred. Companies prefer expansions at existing operations to heroic greenfield bets. Balance sheets are repaired.

Then demand begins growing again.

The projects that might have been ready are not.

Prices rise. Scarcity returns. Capital becomes brave at exactly the point when contractors are expensive and everyone else has rediscovered the same opportunity.

Mining's boom-bust problem is therefore not merely that prices fluctuate.

It is that the industry learns from the last cycle while building for the next one.

The lesson is often correct and badly timed.

After a bust, discipline is rational. After several years of weak prices, refusing to approve a marginal mine may be the most responsible decision a company can make. Yet if enough companies reach the same conclusion, future supply becomes tighter. The very caution produced by yesterday's surplus helps prepare tomorrow's shortage.

Then scarcity makes yesterday's rejected projects look visionary.

Until the cycle turns again.

This can tempt governments to intervene as the investor with a longer memory. If a mineral is strategically important, perhaps the state should help keep marginal capacity alive through low-price years, fund projects before scarcity makes them profitable, or treat a dormant mine as insurance rather than failure.

Sometimes that logic is compelling.

A country may rationally pay for redundancy that a private buyer would not. Strategic stockpiles are built on the same principle. Nobody complains that the fire extinguisher has poor utilization in a year without a fire.

But mines are very expensive fire extinguishers.

Once security becomes a reason to suspend ordinary economic tests, every weak project acquires an argument for public support. The distinction between resilience and subsidy becomes political rather than geological. Governments can preserve useful options. They can also keep uneconomic assets alive long after the strategic case has become a slogan.

Kitsault does not settle that debate.

It makes the price of getting it wrong easier to see.

The first version left a nearly new town without the mine it had been built to serve. The later version reached the permitted-project stage and still could not cross the financing gap. In both cases, the orebody persisted while the economic case changed around it.

Rock is patient that way.

People are less so.

The capital cycle is often discussed as if its central tragedy were money stranded in steel and concrete. There is a cleaner loss that accountants can measure: sunk construction cost, impaired asset value, idle processing capacity.

The messier loss is time organized around a forecast that failed.

That is why the most interesting thing about a ghost town is not the ghost.

It is the forecast.

Somebody once expected enough future in that place to build for it.

Chapter 10 ended with time already spent as a hidden asset. Kitsault shows the other side of the same idea. Time can be invested in a project and still fail to earn a return. A mine can arrive too late for the price that summoned it. A town can arrive with the mine and discover that social infrastructure has an even worse resale market than industrial equipment.

The cycle leaves a question behind.

If digging new rock repeatedly takes years, attracts capital late, and sometimes delivers supply after the market has turned, how much of the next shortage can be answered without another new hole in the ground?

The metal already mined did not vanish when its first product reached the end of its life.

Some of it is sitting in cars, wires, motors, buildings, batteries, phones, transformers, and scrap yards.

That sounds like a mine with no geology problem at all.

It is not that simple.
