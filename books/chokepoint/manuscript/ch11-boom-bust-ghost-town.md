# Boom, Bust, Ghost Town

Kitsault was built to last longer than eighteen months.

That much is obvious from the town.

You do not build houses, roads, recreation facilities, a school, and the rest of a functioning community because you expect the mine beside it to disappear before the paint dries. You build them because the mine is remote, workers need somewhere to live, families need reasons to stay, and somebody has decided the orebody beneath the mountains will remain valuable long enough to repay a great deal of concrete.

In northern British Columbia, near the Alaska border, that decision was tied to molybdenum.

Molybdenum is one of those metals that becomes important without becoming famous. Added to steel, it improves strength, hardness, and resistance to heat and corrosion. It matters in pipelines, power plants, tools, aircraft, chemical equipment, and other places where ordinary steel has an unpleasant job to do.

By the late 1970s, it was also expensive.

Kitsault had already been mined once, from 1967 to 1972. The later project was not a prospector's fantasy built on a few drill holes. The deposit was known. The site had history. When construction on the renewed operation began in 1979, the average U.S. spot price of molybdenum was $23.86 a pound. The mine that followed represented roughly $200 million of late-1970s capital and, once operating, employed about 500 people.

Then the expectation expired before the concrete did.

The reopened mine began operating in 1981. By November 1982 it was shutting down.

Consumption had weakened. Production kept rising. Inventories accumulated. Molybdenum prices fell below Kitsault's reported operating cost.

The new town had barely had time to become ordinary.

Hindsight makes this easy to moralize. The boom becomes greed. The bust becomes punishment for believing it. The abandoned houses supply the metaphor.

But a mine is not built because managers know the future. It is built because, after years of geology, engineering, market analysis, permitting, financing, and argument, enough people become willing to bet that the future price will cover the cost of turning rock into saleable metal.

Sometimes they are right for thirty years.

Sometimes for eighteen months.

Kitsault exposes the mismatch between the durability of the investment and the volatility of the price that justified it. Molybdenum could collapse in weeks. Roads, mills, tailings systems, power lines, housing, and civic infrastructure could not unbuild themselves at the same speed.

Neither could the lives organized around them.

The town was not decorative spending around the mine. In a remote location, housing is part of the production system. So is a school if workers with children are expected to stay. Recreation matters because retaining labor matters.

The company made a bet on the mine. Workers and families made a different kind of bet on the company.

A corporation can impair an asset. A lender can restructure a loan. Equipment can sometimes be sold. Geological information can wait on a hard drive until prices improve.

A school year is less fungible.

That does not make Kitsault the destiny of every mining town. Many mines operate through several cycles. Low-cost deposits survive prices that kill competitors. Existing communities diversify. Infrastructure built for one project can lower the cost of another. A closed mine can reopen.

Kitsault itself proves the last point.

The molybdenum did not leave when the workers did.

Decades after the 1982 closure, the deposit attracted another serious development effort. By 2011, provincial geological material again described a substantial resource and a proposed mine life of roughly sixteen years. British Columbia granted conditional environmental approval in 2013 and a Mines Act permit in 2014 for a project estimated at about C$812 million, with roughly 700 construction jobs and 300 operating jobs. Federal approval followed.

The project had permits.

It still did not get built.

Later provincial environmental-assessment documentation recorded that lower metal prices and difficulty securing financing put construction on hold even after approvals had been obtained and a camp established.

The same place had now lived through two versions of the capital cycle.

First, high prices helped justify a mine and town that opened just as the market deteriorated. Decades later, a known brownfield deposit advanced through modern permitting only to meet a different veto: the project could not attract money on terms that made sense at the prevailing price.

Permits can make a mine possible.

They cannot make it economic.

That is why searching for one villain in mine delay is usually unsatisfying. Regulation can matter. So can financing, cost inflation, project design, community trust, geology, and the commodity price years after the original decision.

Kitsault's history shows that the price still gets a vote even after the orebody is known and governments have said yes.

This is also why high prices contain the seed of low prices. Scarcity attracts investment. Investment expands capacity. Capacity arrives slowly. By the time it does, demand may have weakened, competitors may have responded, substitutes may have improved, inventories may have accumulated, or a recession may have changed the market.

The shortage solves itself with enough enthusiasm to create a surplus.

That is not a failure of markets. It is one way markets work when production has long lead times.

The human experience of the correction is another matter.

An economic model can write `capacity exits the market`.

A town has to decide what to do with Tuesday.

There is also a reason not to call every abandoned mine a total waste.

Fixed capital can retain option value.

Kitsault's later redevelopment attempt did not begin at year zero. The orebody had been drilled. Geological information existed. Roads and power existed. The site had a mine footprint. A greenfield prospect would have had to spend years learning what Kitsault already knew.

A bust can leave behind a dormant option.

That option may remain out of the money for decades or never be exercised. But if prices rise, technology improves, infrastructure changes, or policy lowers financing costs, a brownfield project can return with years of uncertainty already retired.

Rock is patient that way.

People are less so.

After a bust, investors remember the projects that opened late and lost money quickly. Management teams become cautious. Exploration budgets shrink. Marginal projects are deferred. Companies prefer expansions at existing operations to heroic greenfield bets.

That discipline can be rational and still prepare the next shortage.

When demand begins growing again, the projects that might have been ready are not. Prices rise. Scarcity returns. Capital becomes brave at exactly the point when contractors are expensive and everyone else has rediscovered the same opportunity.

Mining's boom-bust problem is not merely that prices fluctuate.

It is that the industry learns from the last cycle while building for the next one.

This is where governments are tempted to act as investors with longer memories. Perhaps a strategically important project should be kept alive through low-price years. Perhaps dormant capacity should be treated as insurance rather than failure.

Sometimes that is sensible. Nobody complains that a fire extinguisher has poor utilization in a year without a fire.

Mines are very expensive fire extinguishers.

Once security becomes a reason to suspend ordinary economic tests, every weak project acquires an argument for public support. Governments can preserve valuable options. They can also keep uneconomic assets alive long after the strategic case has become a slogan.

Kitsault does not settle that debate. It makes the cost of getting it wrong easier to see.

The first project left a nearly new town without the mine it had been built to serve. The later project reached the permitted stage and still could not cross the financing gap. In both cases, the orebody persisted while the economic case changed around it.

The clean accounting loss is sunk construction cost and impaired asset value.

The messier loss is time organized around a forecast that failed.

That is why the most interesting thing about a ghost town is not the ghost.

It is the forecast.

Somebody once expected enough future in that place to build for it.

The capital cycle has another response available, at least in theory. If new rock repeatedly takes years to reach the market and can arrive on the wrong side of the price cycle, perhaps some of the next shortage can be answered with metal that has already been mined.

Cars, wires, motors, buildings, batteries, phones, transformers, and scrap yards contain an enormous above-ground stock.

It sounds like a mine with no geology problem at all.

The next chapter is about the clock hidden inside that promise.
