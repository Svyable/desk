# Who Gets Rich, Who Gets Left

A copper concentrate ship leaving a port carries two things away. One is obvious: ore that took a geological age to make and a few years to dig out. The other is harder to see because it remains behind in ledgers. The mine has paid wages, contractors, taxes, royalties, dividends, power bills, interest, and perhaps a community fund. The government may have acquired foreign exchange. A railway may have been repaired. Engineers may have learned a process they can use elsewhere. Or the country may have acquired little more than a temporary stream of cash, a larger public payroll, a stronger currency, new debts, and a tailings dam that will require attention long after the ore body is exhausted.

That difference is the political economy of a mineral boom.

The phrase “resource curse” is useful partly because it is memorable and dangerous partly for the same reason. It can make geology sound like destiny. Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner’s influential 1995 work found that countries with high natural-resource exports relative to GDP at the beginning of the 1970s tended to grow more slowly over the following years, even after controlling for several other factors. The result helped organize a large literature around an old puzzle: why did extraordinary natural wealth so often coexist with disappointing economic outcomes?

But a correlation across countries is not a curse in the supernatural sense, and later research complicated the story. Osmel Manzano and Roberto Rigobon argued that debt overhang could explain much of the apparent relationship. Commodity-rich governments and economies borrowed heavily during the high-price 1970s. When prices fell, debt remained. What looked like a punishment for possessing resources could instead be the balance-sheet aftermath of borrowing against a boom.

That distinction matters because the remedies are different. You cannot legislate away geology. You can change how much a government borrows against a volatile revenue stream, how it taxes a mine, where it saves the proceeds, what it builds with them, and whether citizens can see the bargain.

There is no single resource-curse mechanism. A boom can push up a currency and make other exporters less competitive. It can invite borrowing on the assumption that today's price is normal. It can concentrate rents in a small number of permits and contracts, increasing the reward for political capture. A mine can produce a large share of national exports while employing a small share of the population. Conflict can grow around control of a valuable deposit. Governments can spend temporary revenue on permanent obligations. These mechanisms can reinforce one another, but they need not. Calling all of them “the curse” hides the machinery the phrase is supposed to reveal.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo makes the problem impossible to romanticize. Its mineral endowment is extraordinary. Copper and cobalt from the country's mining regions sit inside the supply chains described throughout this book. Extractive industries have accounted for an enormous share of exports and a large share of government revenue. Yet the World Bank still places the DRC among the world's poorest countries, and mining has not generated employment on a scale remotely proportional to its macroeconomic importance.

That does not mean mining caused Congolese poverty. The causal history is much larger and darker: colonial extraction, conflict, regional insecurity, weak infrastructure, state capacity, political choices, and an economy whose formal productive base remains narrow. The useful observation is more modest. A sector can dominate a country's exports without becoming a broad employment machine. It can generate foreign exchange without automatically creating local supplier networks. It can produce government revenue without guaranteeing public services. The value of what leaves the ground and the welfare of the people living above it are connected only through institutions, firms, budgets, bargaining power, and time.

This is why the usual argument about whether a mining tax rate is “high” or “low” is incomplete. A government first has to capture a reasonable share of the rent without making investment impossible. It then has to collect what the law says is owed. The revenue has to survive corruption and administrative leakage. It has to arrive in a budget that distinguishes temporary commodity income from durable tax capacity. The state then has to turn money into infrastructure, health, education, security, or financial assets at tolerable cost. Each arrow in that sequence is a place where value can disappear.

Zambia illustrates the difficulty. Copper is central to its economy, so arguments over mining taxation naturally become arguments over sovereignty and development. Yet a higher statutory claim on mining income is not the same thing as a stronger public balance sheet. The World Bank's recent public-finance work on Zambia has emphasized revenue administration, tax expenditures, public investment, and the quality of spending alongside the country's reliance on mining. A state can win a tax argument and still lose the development argument if the proceeds are volatile, poorly collected, badly allocated, or pledged against debt.

The hard question is therefore not merely how much of the rent belongs to the state. It is what remains when the rent is gone.

Botswana became famous because, for decades, its answer was unusually good. The country did not simply tax diamonds extracted by a foreign company. It negotiated ownership. Debswana, the major diamond producer, is owned equally by the Government of Botswana and De Beers. The arrangement evolved through bargaining rather than arriving fully formed. Botswana's government initially held a smaller stake in the Orapa venture and later renegotiated to equal ownership. It also developed a state-owned route to market through the Okavango Diamond Company.

Those institutional details matter more than the slogan that Botswana “beat the resource curse.” Equity changes the state's claim on profits. Marketing access changes information and bargaining power. Repeated negotiation builds administrative knowledge. Diamond revenues helped finance public investment and state development over decades. None of this proves that a 50 percent government stake is a universal formula. A weak state can own half a company and still manage it badly. A strong state can capture rents through taxes without owning shares. The Botswana case is valuable because it shows that the distribution of mineral value is designed, contested, and revised.

It is also valuable because the story has become less comfortable.

Botswana remains highly exposed to diamonds. When diamond demand weakened, the economy contracted in 2024, fiscal deficits widened, and reserves came under pressure. The IMF's subsequent work stressed diversification. Lab-grown diamonds add a technological challenge to the ordinary commodity cycle. A country can manage a mineral endowment intelligently for half a century and still discover that dependence is dependence.

That is a better lesson than the usual victory lap. Good institutions can convert resource rents into public wealth. They cannot make a concentrated export base cease to be concentrated.

Chile offers another answer, built less around state equity in a single marketing structure and more around fiscal engineering. Copper has repeatedly delivered large gains and large temptations. The central budgeting problem is simple to state: when the copper price rises, how much of the resulting revenue should a government treat as permanent?

The politically easy answer is too much.

A boom makes fiscal choices feel inexpensive. New programs can be funded without visibly raising broad taxes. Public wages can rise. Projects that failed a cost-benefit test at lower prices become affordable. Lenders become friendlier. Forecasts drift upward because recent prices are the evidence everyone can see. Then the cycle turns, but salaries, benefits, debt service, and half-built infrastructure do not turn with it.

Chile's structural-balance framework is an attempt to interrupt that psychology with procedure. Rather than budgeting as though the current copper price will persist, the framework uses estimates of longer-run copper prices and economic capacity to calculate a cyclically adjusted fiscal position. Independent experts contribute to the reference assumptions. The Economic and Social Stabilization Fund, created in 2007 largely from an earlier copper stabilization mechanism, gives the government a financial buffer that can be accumulated in stronger periods and drawn down when conditions deteriorate or extraordinary needs arise.

The fund's history makes the point more clearly than the institutional vocabulary. Money went in during strong years. Money came out during the global financial crisis, later social and pandemic shocks, and other fiscal needs. By June 2026, Chile's Ministry of Finance reported a market value of roughly $3.9 billion after cumulative contributions and withdrawals each totaling tens of billions of dollars. A stabilization fund is not a treasure chest that only grows. Its purpose is to make time less tyrannical—to separate, imperfectly, the year a commodity rent arrives from the year public spending is needed.

Chile has not solved fiscal policy. Recent IMF assessments still identify structural deficits and rising debt pressures. Nor has it solved the environmental and distributional conflicts around mining described in the previous chapter. The narrower achievement is important enough: commodity volatility has been turned into an explicit design problem. A copper boom is not allowed, at least in principle, to define the permanent spending baseline simply because it happens to be occurring now.

This reveals a useful divide among mineral economies. Some consume the income. Some borrow against expected future income. Some convert part of it into financial assets. Others try to convert it into physical and human capital: roads, ports, schools, electricity, technical training, public health. None of those categories is automatically right.

Saving everything can be absurd in a country with severe infrastructure deficits. A government earning five percent on foreign securities while its citizens lack reliable power may be preserving financial wealth while wasting development opportunities. Spending everything can be equally reckless when the revenue source is exhaustible and volatile. Building infrastructure can raise future productivity, but only if the project is useful, competently procured, maintained, and not priced at the inflated construction costs of a boom. Paying down debt can look unambitious until the commodity price falls.

The real problem is conversion: turning a depleting natural asset into a portfolio of other assets that can outlive it.

Economists sometimes express this through national balance sheets and concepts of adjusted saving. The language can sound bloodless, but the intuition is concrete. A mineral deposit is wealth before it is mined. Extraction changes its form. If a country removes a billion dollars of mineral wealth and uses the proceeds to create a billion dollars of productive infrastructure, financial assets, institutional capacity, or human capability, the national balance sheet may remain strong even though the ore body shrinks. If the same extraction finances consumption that leaves no durable benefit, the country is selling an inheritance to pay current bills.

Of course, measurement is not so clean. A school is not worth what it cost to build. A road to nowhere is an asset in an accounting system and a liability in a maintenance budget. Human capital cannot be priced with the confidence of a government bond. Environmental damage may not appear on the same ledger as royalty income. The previous chapter's water, waste, and tailings costs complicate every cheerful calculation of rent conversion. A country can save the fiscal proceeds of a mine while leaving local communities with contaminated water or lost land. National wealth and local welfare are not interchangeable.

That geographic split is one reason mining politics becomes so combustible. Revenue often flows to a national treasury. Costs are concentrated near the mine, railway, smelter, waste facility, or port. The people who lose grazing land or water access may not be the people whose schools are built with royalties hundreds of kilometers away. A project can be positive on a national spreadsheet and still be experienced locally as extraction in the oldest sense of the word.

Governments respond with local-content rules, royalty-sharing formulas, community agreements, procurement requirements, and demands for domestic processing. Each can correct a real imbalance. Each can also be badly designed.

Local-content requirements can build supplier capability, but forcing a mine to buy an unavailable input domestically can merely raise costs or create politically connected intermediaries. Domestic-processing mandates can capture more value, but a smelter requires power, logistics, technical skill, environmental controls, and enough throughput to operate competitively. Revenue-sharing can give producing regions a visible stake, but it can overwhelm local governments that lack the capacity to spend a sudden windfall well. Community funds can finance useful services or become parallel patronage systems.

There is no shortcut around capability.

The distribution question also extends beyond governments. A modern mine is capital intensive. Its payroll can be large in absolute terms and small relative to the value of output. The highest-paid technical roles may require training unavailable in nearby communities. Contractors can capture more employment than the mining company itself. Shareholders may live on another continent. Equipment can be imported. Profits can leave through dividends, debt payments, management fees, or perfectly legal transfer arrangements within multinational groups.

This creates a recurring political disappointment. Citizens hear that a deposit is worth tens of billions of dollars and reasonably ask why their incomes do not change by the same order of magnitude. But gross in-ground value is not a pile of cash. Ore must be financed, excavated, processed, transported, and sold over years or decades. Prices move. Grades vary. Capital has to be repaid. Some projects fail. The economically recoverable reserve is smaller than the geological resource. A government's task is to bargain over the surplus after those realities without allowing technical complexity to become an excuse for opacity.

Transparency helps because mineral contracts and revenue systems are unusually vulnerable to information asymmetry. The company may know more about the deposit and costs than the state negotiating with it. The ministry of mines may know more than parliament. The central government may know more than the producing community. Citizens may see only a headline investment figure and a royalty percentage. Initiatives such as the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative try to narrow those gaps by making payments, revenues, ownership, and sector governance more visible.

Transparency is not the same as accountability. Publishing a payment does not guarantee that it was fair, or that the money was well spent. But secrecy makes every later step harder. It is difficult to debate the distribution of a rent nobody can measure.

The new mineral race raises the stakes because governments in producing countries have read the same supply-chain reports as buyers. They know that copper, lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, and rare-earth inputs are being described in Washington, Brussels, Beijing, Tokyo, and Seoul as strategic. They can see subsidies being offered to processors and manufacturers in wealthy consuming countries. The old bargain—dig the ore, export it, import the expensive finished product—looks less defensible when the customer simultaneously declares the raw material essential to national security.

That creates leverage, but not unlimited leverage. A country can demand more processing, ownership, technology transfer, or local procurement. Push too hard and marginal investment may go elsewhere, substitution may accelerate, recycling may improve, or a project may simply wait. Push too little and a once-in-a-generation rent can leave almost no industrial inheritance. The correct bargain changes with geology, market concentration, infrastructure, financing conditions, and what alternatives buyers actually possess.

The most successful policy may therefore look inconsistent from the outside. A government can be aggressive on transparency, patient on downstream processing, conservative on debt, generous toward exploration, strict on environmental liability, and willing to save a large fraction of windfall revenue. There is no ideological package that fits every deposit.

What does fit is a balance-sheet question.

When the tonne of ore leaves, what remains?

In one country, the answer may be a dividend paid into a stabilization fund, a trained generation of geologists, a transmission line that also serves other industries, and public debt lower than it would otherwise have been. In another, it may be a larger foreign loan secured during the boom, an overvalued currency, a half-finished prestige project, and a fiscal crisis waiting for the price chart to turn downward. In a third, the national accounts may look healthy while the producing district carries the environmental liability.

None of these outcomes is written into the rock.

That is why the phrase “resource curse” should be treated as the beginning of an investigation, not its conclusion. Mineral wealth changes the scale and timing of political choices. It creates rents large enough to fight over, revenues volatile enough to misread, and assets exhaustible enough that a generation can spend what another generation cannot replace. It can also finance infrastructure, savings, public services, technical capability, and a stronger state.

The difference is not virtue. Botswana's diamond model, Chile's fiscal rules, Congo's mining economy, and Zambia's public-finance challenges arose from different histories and constraints. Institutions themselves are products of politics, bargaining, conflict, administrative competence, and previous choices. Saying “good institutions matter” explains very little unless we can point to the mechanism: who owns the equity, who sets the reference price, where the windfall is saved, how debt is limited, what revenue is disclosed, which government receives it, and what asset exists afterward.

Those mechanisms are visible. They can be compared. They can fail.

And even when they work, they face one problem no institutional design can remove. Every budget rule, mine valuation, tax regime, investment decision, and sovereign fund begins with an assumption about the future price of the thing being dug from the ground.

That assumption is where the next danger begins.
