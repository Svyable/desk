# Who Gets Rich, Who Gets Left

A copper concentrate ship leaving a port carries two things away.

One is obvious: ore that took a geological age to make and a few years to dig out.

The other is harder to see because it remains behind in ledgers. The mine has paid wages, contractors, taxes, royalties, dividends, power bills, and interest. The government may have acquired foreign exchange. A railway may have been repaired. Engineers may have learned a process they can use elsewhere.

Or the country may have acquired little more than a temporary stream of cash, a larger public payroll, new debts, and a tailings dam that will require attention long after the orebody is exhausted.

That difference is the political economy of a mineral boom.

The phrase *resource curse* is memorable enough to become misleading. Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner's influential 1995 work found that countries with high natural-resource exports relative to GDP at the beginning of the 1970s tended to grow more slowly over the following years, even after controlling for several other factors. The result helped organize a large literature around an old puzzle: why did extraordinary natural wealth so often coexist with disappointing economic outcomes?

Later work complicated the story. Osmel Manzano and Roberto Rigobon argued that debt overhang could explain much of the apparent relationship. Commodity-rich governments and economies borrowed heavily during the high-price 1970s. When prices fell, debt remained.

What looked like punishment for possessing resources could instead be the balance-sheet aftermath of borrowing against a boom.

The distinction matters because the remedies are different. Geology cannot be legislated away. Borrowing, taxation, saving, procurement, transparency, and investment can be changed.

There is no single curse mechanism. A boom can strengthen a currency and weaken other exporters. It can invite borrowing against a price that looks permanent because it is high today. It can concentrate rents in a small number of permits and contracts. A mine can dominate exports while employing a small share of the population. Governments can turn temporary revenue into permanent obligations.

The phrase is useful only if it sends us toward those mechanisms rather than replacing them.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo makes the problem impossible to romanticize. Its mineral endowment is extraordinary. Copper and cobalt from the country's mining regions sit inside the supply chains described throughout this book. Extractive industries account for an enormous share of exports and a large share of government revenue.

Yet the World Bank still places the DRC among the world's poorest countries, and mining has not generated employment on a scale proportional to its macroeconomic importance.

That does not mean mining caused Congolese poverty. The history is much larger: colonial extraction, conflict, regional insecurity, weak infrastructure, state capacity, political choices, and a narrow formal productive base.

The useful observation is narrower. A sector can dominate exports without becoming a broad employment machine. It can generate foreign exchange without automatically creating local suppliers. It can produce government revenue without guaranteeing public services.

The value of what leaves the ground and the welfare of the people living above it are connected only through institutions, firms, budgets, bargaining power, and time.

That is why arguing about whether a mining tax rate is simply high or low gets us only part of the way. A government has to capture a reasonable share of rent without making investment impossible. It has to collect what the law says is owed. The revenue has to survive leakage. The budget has to distinguish a temporary commodity windfall from durable tax capacity. Then the state has to turn money into infrastructure, health, education, security, or financial assets at tolerable cost.

Each arrow is a place where value can disappear.

Zambia illustrates the difficulty. Copper is central to its economy, so arguments over mining taxation become arguments over sovereignty and development. Yet a stronger statutory claim on mining income is not automatically a stronger public balance sheet. The World Bank's recent public-finance work has emphasized revenue administration, tax expenditures, public investment, and spending quality alongside reliance on mining.

A state can win a tax argument and still lose the development argument.

The hard question is what remains when the rent is gone.

Botswana became famous because, for decades, its answer was unusually strong. Debswana, the major diamond producer, is owned equally by the Government of Botswana and De Beers. That arrangement evolved through bargaining rather than arriving fully formed. Botswana also developed a state-owned route to market through the Okavango Diamond Company.

Those institutional details matter more than the slogan that Botswana beat the resource curse. Equity changes the state's claim on profits. Marketing access changes information and bargaining power. Repeated negotiation builds administrative knowledge. Diamond revenue helped finance public investment and state development over decades.

None of this makes fifty-percent state ownership a universal formula. A weak state can own half a company badly. A strong state can capture rents through taxes without owning shares.

Botswana matters because it shows that the distribution of mineral value is designed, contested, and revised.

It also shows that good design does not eliminate concentration risk.

When diamond demand weakened, Botswana's economy contracted in 2024, fiscal deficits widened, and reserves came under pressure. The IMF subsequently stressed diversification. Lab-grown diamonds added a technological challenge to the ordinary commodity cycle.

A country can manage a mineral endowment intelligently for half a century and still discover that dependence is dependence.

That does not erase Botswana's institutional achievement. It gives it a more useful boundary. Good institutions can convert resource rents into public wealth. They cannot make a concentrated export base cease to be concentrated.

Chile offers another answer, built less around state equity in one marketing structure and more around fiscal engineering.

Copper booms create a deceptively simple budgeting question: how much of today's revenue should be treated as permanent?

The politically easy answer is too much.

A boom makes new programs feel inexpensive. Public wages can rise. Projects that failed a cost-benefit test at lower prices become affordable. Lenders become friendlier. Forecasts drift upward because recent prices are the evidence everyone can see.

Then the cycle turns. Salaries, benefits, debt service, and half-built infrastructure do not turn with it.

Chile's structural-balance framework tries to interrupt that psychology with procedure. Rather than budgeting as though the current copper price will persist, it uses estimates of longer-run copper prices and economic capacity to calculate a cyclically adjusted fiscal position. Independent experts contribute to the reference assumptions. The Economic and Social Stabilization Fund gives the government a buffer that can be accumulated in stronger periods and drawn down when conditions deteriorate or extraordinary needs arise.

By June 2026, Chile's Ministry of Finance reported the fund at roughly $3.9 billion after cumulative contributions and withdrawals each totaling tens of billions of dollars.

A stabilization fund is not a treasure chest that only grows.

Its purpose is to make time less tyrannical—to separate, imperfectly, the year a commodity rent arrives from the year public spending is needed.

Chile has not solved fiscal policy. Recent IMF assessments still identify structural deficits and rising debt pressures. Nor has the country solved the environmental and distributional conflicts around mining described in the previous chapter.

The narrower achievement is important enough: commodity volatility has been made an explicit design problem.

That leads to the real conversion problem.

A mineral deposit is wealth before it is mined. Extraction changes the form of that wealth. If a country removes mineral wealth and turns the proceeds into productive infrastructure, financial assets, institutional capacity, or human capability, some value can outlive the orebody.

If the same extraction finances consumption that leaves no durable benefit, the country is selling an inheritance to pay current bills.

The arithmetic is not clean. A school is not worth what it cost to build. A road to nowhere can appear as an asset and behave like a maintenance liability. Human capability cannot be priced like a government bond. Environmental damage does not sit neatly in the same ledger as royalty income.

A country can save the fiscal proceeds of a mine and still leave the producing community with contaminated water or lost land.

National wealth and local welfare are not interchangeable.

That geographic split is one reason mining politics becomes so combustible. Revenue often flows to a national treasury. Costs are concentrated near the mine, railway, smelter, waste facility, or port. The people who lose grazing land or water access may not be the people whose schools are built with royalties hundreds of kilometers away.

A project can be positive on a national spreadsheet and still be experienced locally as extraction in the oldest sense of the word.

Governments respond with local-content rules, royalty-sharing formulas, community agreements, procurement requirements, and demands for domestic processing. Each can correct a real imbalance. Each can be badly designed.

Local-content rules can build supplier capability or create politically connected intermediaries. Processing mandates can capture more value or strand an expensive plant without reliable power, logistics, skills, and throughput. Revenue sharing can give producing regions a visible stake or overwhelm local governments with a windfall they lack the capacity to spend well.

There is no shortcut around capability.

The same applies to employment. A modern mine is capital intensive. Its payroll can be large in absolute terms and small relative to the value of output. The highest-paid technical roles may require training unavailable nearby. Contractors can capture more jobs than the mining company itself. Equipment can be imported. Profits can leave through dividends, debt payments, management fees, or perfectly legal transfers inside multinational groups.

This creates a recurring political disappointment. Citizens hear that a deposit is worth tens of billions of dollars and reasonably ask why their incomes do not change by the same order of magnitude.

But gross in-ground value is not a pile of cash. Ore has to be financed, excavated, processed, transported, and sold over years. Prices move. Grades vary. Capital has to be repaid. Some projects fail. Economically recoverable reserves are smaller than geological resources.

A government's job is to bargain over the surplus after those realities without allowing technical complexity to become an excuse for opacity.

Transparency helps because mineral contracts and revenue systems are unusually vulnerable to information asymmetry. The company may know more about the deposit and costs than the state negotiating with it. The ministry may know more than parliament. The national government may know more than the producing community.

Initiatives such as the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative try to narrow those gaps by making payments, revenues, ownership, and sector governance more visible.

Transparency is not accountability. Publishing a payment does not prove it was fair or well spent.

But secrecy makes every later argument harder.

The new mineral race has also changed producer-country bargaining. Governments can see buyers in Washington, Brussels, Beijing, Tokyo, and Seoul calling copper, lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, and rare-earth inputs strategic while subsidizing processing and manufacturing at home.

The old bargain—dig the ore, export it, import the expensive finished product—looks less defensible when the customer simultaneously declares the raw material essential to national security.

That creates leverage, not unlimited leverage.

A producer can demand more processing, ownership, technology transfer, or local procurement. Push too hard and marginal investment may go elsewhere, substitution may accelerate, recycling may improve, or the project may wait. Push too little and a once-in-a-generation rent can leave almost no industrial inheritance.

There is no ideological package that fits every deposit.

What does fit is a balance-sheet question.

When the tonne of ore leaves, what remains?

In one country, the answer may be a dividend paid into a stabilization fund, trained geologists, a transmission line serving other industries, and lower public debt. In another, it may be a larger foreign loan secured during the boom, an overvalued currency, a half-finished prestige project, and a fiscal crisis waiting for the price chart to turn downward. In a third, the national accounts may look healthy while the producing district carries the environmental liability.

None of those outcomes is written into the rock.

That is why *resource curse* should be the beginning of an investigation, not its conclusion. Mineral wealth changes the scale and timing of political choices. It creates rents large enough to fight over, revenues volatile enough to misread, and assets exhaustible enough that one generation can spend what another cannot replace.

It can also finance infrastructure, savings, public services, technical capability, and a stronger state.

The difference becomes visible only when we point to mechanisms: who owns the equity, who sets the reference price, where windfalls are saved, how debt is limited, what revenue is disclosed, which level of government receives it, and what asset exists afterward.

Even when those mechanisms work, they face one problem no institutional design can remove.

Every mine valuation, tax regime, budget rule, sovereign fund, and investment decision begins with an assumption about the future price of the thing being dug from the ground.

A mine is an argument about a price that does not exist yet.
