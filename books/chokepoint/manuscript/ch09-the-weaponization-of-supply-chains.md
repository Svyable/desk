# The Weaponization of Supply Chains

On September 21, 2010, Japanese importers began having trouble getting rare earths out of China.

The timing was terrible enough to look deliberate.

Two weeks earlier, a Chinese fishing trawler had collided with Japanese Coast Guard vessels near the Senkaku Islands, which China calls the Diaoyu Islands. Japan detained the captain. Beijing protested. The diplomatic temperature rose quickly.

Then the rare earths slowed.

News reports called it an embargo.

China denied there was one.

The distinction matters.

Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry later recorded that exports bound for Japan had reportedly been stalled from September 21 because Chinese customs procedures were being enforced more strictly. The Chinese Ministry of Commerce said there was no trade embargo and described the delays as an anti-smuggling measure. The stagnation continued for nearly two months.

There was no famous decree titled *Rare Earth Embargo Against Japan*.

There did not need to be.

A supply chain can be weaponized without the weapon being ceremonially unveiled.

A customs officer can ask another question.

A license can wait.

A shipment can require one more document.

A company can be told that the material is controlled and the application is under review.

Nothing in that sequence looks like a blockade.

A factory waiting for magnets may experience it as one.

This is the modern form of resource leverage.

It is less theatrical than an oil embargo and often more ambiguous.

Oil is visible. Tankers move. Prices print every second. Strategic reserves are measured in days of consumption. When a producer refuses to sell crude, the market understands the act.

Critical minerals are harder to see.

A few tonnes of terbium do not fill television screens. Gallium is not traded in a market that the public watches each morning. A graphite license does not feel like geopolitical history while someone in a ministry is reviewing it.

But a small material can sit inside a very large machine.

That is the source of the leverage.

The mineral does not need to be valuable enough to impoverish the buyer.

It needs to be difficult enough to replace that the buyer cannot ship something much more valuable without it.

The veto can be worth more than the commodity.

Japan understood that in 2010.

The country was heavily dependent on Chinese rare earths. METI later reported that China supplied 85 percent of Japan’s rare-earth imports in 2009.

That dependence had seemed economically rational.

China had supply. Chinese processors had scale. Japanese manufacturers had customers and factories. The chain worked.

Then a diplomatic dispute made the chain look like a balance sheet with one line circled in red.

The first lesson was not that China had invented economic coercion.

States have used trade, finance, food, fuel, technology, shipping, and access as leverage for centuries.

The lesson was that an obscure industrial input could suddenly carry the political weight of a strategic commodity.

Japan responded like a country that believed the episode might happen again.

It invested in alternative supply. It supported upstream projects outside China. It pushed companies to reduce rare-earth intensity, develop substitutes, and recycle more material. It joined the United States and European Union in challenging Chinese rare-earth export restrictions at the World Trade Organization.

The response worked enough to complicate the story.

By 2020, METI says China’s share of Japan’s rare-earth imports had fallen from 85 percent to 58 percent.

The International Energy Agency says Japan’s post-2010 demand-side policies, working closely with industry, helped reduce the country’s total rare-earth demand by about 30 percent from 2010 levels.

China remained dominant.

Japan became less easy to squeeze.

This is the first paradox of supply-chain coercion.

Using a chokepoint proves that the chokepoint is real.

It also gives every customer a reason to spend money making it less real next time.

The same dynamic has played out more explicitly since 2023.

In July of that year, China announced export controls on gallium and germanium-related items. The measures took effect on August 1. Exporters now needed licenses.

China described the controls in national-security and non-proliferation terms. Its Ministry of Commerce emphasized that export control was not the same thing as an export prohibition and said compliant applications could be approved.

That was true.

It was also beside the point.

The economic power of a licensing regime does not require every application to fail.

The power comes partly from the fact that permission has become a variable.

A manufacturer planning production now has another uncertainty to price.

Will the license arrive?

When?

For which customer?

For what quantity?

Will the rule change before the next shipment?

Can the buyer carry enough inventory to survive the delay?

Can the product be redesigned if the answer is no?

A binary embargo gives the buyer one problem.

A licensing system can give the buyer six.

Gallium and germanium are especially useful examples because their markets are small compared with the industries that depend on them.

They are used in semiconductors, telecommunications, infrared systems, solar technologies, and defense applications. Much of their supply is recovered as a by-product of other industrial processes rather than produced from mines that can simply be expanded in response to a gallium price spike.

That makes the supply response awkward.

If copper becomes scarce, high prices can eventually justify another copper mine.

If a minor material is recovered only because an aluminium or zinc operation already exists, its economics are chained to another commodity.

The market signal has to travel through someone else’s factory.

Graphite followed.

In October 2023, China revised its controls on certain graphite products, including high-specification artificial graphite and natural flake graphite used in battery supply chains. The rules took effect in December.

Again, the language was licensing rather than blanket prohibition.

Again, China said the measures did not target a specific country.

And again, approvals could be granted. In early 2024, the Ministry of Commerce said qualified graphite export applications had been approved, including applications involving South Korean battery companies.

This is where the word *weaponization* can become sloppy.

A government regulating dual-use materials is not automatically conducting economic warfare every time it requires a license. The United States, Europe, Japan, and other governments also use export controls for security reasons. Non-proliferation rules exist because some civilian technologies have military applications. Countries are entitled to care where sensitive goods go.

The useful question is not whether export controls are inherently illegitimate.

It is what leverage becomes available when security policy is layered onto a supply chain one country already dominates.

That question became easier to answer in December 2024.

China’s Ministry of Commerce announced country-specific restrictions on dual-use exports to the United States. Military users and military uses were barred. Gallium, germanium, antimony, and superhard-material exports to the United States would, in principle, not be licensed. Graphite would face stricter end-user and end-use review.

The administrative lever was now explicitly geopolitical.

The same material could be commercially available in one relationship and far harder to obtain in another.

This is not OPEC.

There is no cartel meeting in Vienna setting a production quota.

There is a list.

A licensing rule.

An end-use test.

A customs declaration.

A ministry with discretion.

A supply chain whose most concentrated step happens to sit inside that ministry’s jurisdiction.

That is enough.

The power became more visible in April 2025, when China imposed export controls on seven heavy rare earth elements and related products, including certain permanent-magnet materials.

The consequences moved quickly downstream.

The IEA reports that export volumes of affected rare earths and permanent magnets fell sharply in April and May. Automakers in the United States, Europe, and elsewhere struggled to source magnets. Some cut utilization. Some temporarily stopped production.

Eventually, licenses were granted and trade recovered.

That sentence is the counterargument and the warning at the same time.

The controls did not permanently shut the world out.

They did not need to.

A few weeks of uncertainty was enough to show which factories had no comfortable second source.

Imagine the production meeting.

The missing component is not an engine block or a battery pack. It is a magnet.

The magnet may be small enough to hold in one hand.

The vehicle around it contains steel, glass, copper, software, semiconductors, seats, airbags, sensors, motors, thousands of parts, and tens of thousands of dollars of value.

None of that matters if the qualified magnet does not arrive.

This is why the market size of a chokepoint can be misleading.

The economic importance of the input is not its invoice price.

It is the value of everything waiting behind it.

The IEA’s 2026 work makes the asymmetry almost absurd. It estimates that if the broad rare-earth controls announced in 2025 were implemented in full, roughly $6.5 trillion in annual downstream production outside China could be exposed.

That is not a forecast of $6.5 trillion in losses.

It is a map of dependency.

The number is large because rare earths sit inside products whose value is determined by far more than the mineral content.

A few grams can have the veto.

China broadened the rare-earth controls again in October 2025, announcing measures that reached additional elements, processing technologies, and, in some cases, internationally made products containing Chinese-sourced material or made using Chinese technology.

Then, in November, the broader package was suspended for a year.

That sequence is important.

Announce.

Expand.

Suspend.

The supply chain has already learned something before the first full enforcement case.

Companies hire lawyers.

Governments call suppliers.

Procurement teams ask where the oxide came from and where the magnet was sintered.

Boards approve capital that looked too expensive six months earlier.

An announcement can move investment before it moves tonnage.

This is another way chokepoint power differs from simple scarcity.

Scarcity raises a price.

Administrative control changes the probability distribution around access.

Those are not the same shock.

A price spike says the material is available if you can pay enough.

A licensing rule says money may not be the deciding variable.

That is strategically more unsettling.

Markets are good at solving expensive.

They are less good at solving unavailable.

January 2026 provided an even cleaner example of the political logic.

China tightened controls on dual-use exports to Japan, prohibiting exports to Japanese military users, for Japanese military uses, and to other end users or uses that could contribute to Japan’s military capability. The Ministry of Commerce explicitly linked the decision to political tensions over remarks concerning Taiwan.

This was not a ban on every rare-earth shipment to Japan.

Precision matters here.

But the structure is unmistakable.

Supply-chain access had become one instrument inside a diplomatic dispute.

The lesson from 2010 had not disappeared.

It had become formalized.

There is a temptation to treat all of this as proof that concentrated mineral supply guarantees geopolitical domination.

It does not.

A weapon is only useful while the target cannot adapt faster than the weapon can hurt.

Japan is the obvious case.

After 2010, it diversified. It reduced material intensity. It funded alternatives. It recycled. It brought a trade case. It did not become independent of China, but it bought options.

Those options matter most in the second crisis, not the first.

The same logic is now reshaping rare-earth and battery policy elsewhere.

Governments support separation plants that would have looked uneconomic under ordinary commodity accounting. Automakers sign long-term contracts with higher-cost suppliers. Defense ministries fund processing capacity. Companies hold more inventory than a textbook just-in-time system would recommend.

The premium buys resilience.

Whether it is worth paying depends on the probability and cost of disruption.

This is where mineral security becomes difficult to govern.

Every duplicate plant is expensive when nothing goes wrong.

Every single-source dependency looks cheap until something does.

A finance team can calculate the utilization penalty of excess capacity.

It is harder to price the Tuesday when the only supplier stops answering.

That uncertainty invites bad policy as well as good.

A country can use resilience as an excuse to subsidize politically connected projects that will never compete. It can confuse domestic ownership with actual capability. It can stockpile the wrong material. It can build a mine without building separation. It can announce a refinery without qualifying the output for customers.

Security money is still money.

It can be wasted.

The answer is not to duplicate everything.

It is to identify where one failure can stop too much downstream value and buy enough alternatives that the failure becomes survivable.

Sometimes the alternative is another country.

Sometimes another chemistry.

Sometimes a smaller amount of the material.

Sometimes recycling.

Sometimes inventory.

Sometimes a product redesign that removes the dependency entirely.

This is why export controls can be self-eroding.

A supplier with overwhelming market share wants customers to believe there is no substitute.

A coercive episode pays the customer to prove otherwise.

The proof may take years.

That delay is the source of present power.

China’s rare-earth position remains formidable because alternatives are not built by declaration. Separation plants have to work. Magnet factories have to qualify product. Mines have to produce. Customers have to sign contracts. Waste streams have to exist before recycling can scale. Engineers have to redesign motors before less material is required.

The buyer can decide to diversify this afternoon.

The diversified supply may not exist for a decade.

That gap is the weapon.

It is also the bridge to the next chapter.

An export-control notice can be published overnight.

A mine cannot.

A government can change a license rule before breakfast. A mining company has to find an orebody, prove what is in it, raise money, obtain permits, build roads and power, sink shafts or move mountains of waste, construct a plant, hire a workforce, qualify the product, and survive at least one price cycle before the first dependable tonne reaches a customer.

The asymmetry is not merely between one country and another.

It is between administrative time and geological time.

That is why the chokepoint matters.
