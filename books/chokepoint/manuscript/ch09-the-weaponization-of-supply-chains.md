# The Weaponization of Supply Chains

On September 21, 2010, Japanese importers began having trouble getting rare earths out of China.

The timing was terrible enough to look deliberate.

Two weeks earlier, a Chinese fishing trawler had collided with Japanese Coast Guard vessels near the Senkaku Islands, which China calls the Diaoyu Islands. Japan detained the captain. Beijing protested. Diplomatic tension rose quickly.

Then the rare earths slowed.

News reports called it an embargo.

China denied there was one.

Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry later recorded that exports bound for Japan had reportedly been stalled from September 21 because Chinese customs procedures were being enforced more strictly. China's Ministry of Commerce said there was no trade embargo and described the delays as an anti-smuggling measure. The stagnation continued for nearly two months.

There was no famous decree titled *Rare Earth Embargo Against Japan*.

There did not need to be.

A customs officer can ask another question. A license can wait. A shipment can need one more document. Nothing in that sequence looks like a blockade. A factory waiting for magnets may experience it as one.

This is the modern form of resource leverage: less theatrical than an oil embargo and often more ambiguous.

Oil is visible. Tankers move. Prices print continuously. Strategic reserves are measured in days of consumption. When crude is withheld, the market understands the act.

Critical minerals can be much smaller and harder to see. A few tonnes of terbium do not fill television screens. A graphite license does not feel like geopolitical history while somebody in a ministry is reviewing it.

But a small input can sit inside a much larger machine.

The mineral does not need to be valuable enough to impoverish the buyer. It needs to be difficult enough to replace that the buyer cannot ship something much more valuable without it.

The veto can be worth more than the commodity.

Japan understood that in 2010. METI later reported that China supplied 85 percent of Japan's rare-earth imports in 2009. That dependence had looked economically rational while the chain worked.

After the disruption, Japan behaved like a country that expected a second crisis. It supported alternative supply, invested upstream outside China, pushed companies to reduce rare-earth intensity, developed substitutes, expanded recycling, and joined the United States and European Union in challenging Chinese export restrictions at the World Trade Organization.

The response worked enough to complicate the story. By 2020, METI said China's share of Japanese rare-earth imports had fallen from 85 percent to 58 percent. The International Energy Agency estimates Japan's post-2010 policies and industrial adaptation helped reduce total rare-earth demand by roughly 30 percent from 2010 levels.

China remained dominant.

Japan became less easy to squeeze.

That is the central paradox of supply-chain coercion. Using a chokepoint proves the chokepoint is real. It also gives every customer a reason to spend money making it less real next time.

Since 2023, the same logic has become more explicit.

China announced export controls on gallium- and germanium-related items in July 2023, with licensing requirements taking effect in August. In October it revised controls on certain graphite products, including material relevant to battery supply chains, with the new rules beginning in December.

China described these controls in national-security and non-proliferation terms and emphasized that licensing was not the same thing as prohibition. Compliant applications could be approved.

That distinction is important.

It is also where the leverage sits.

The economic power of a licensing regime does not require every application to fail. It comes partly from making permission a variable.

A manufacturer planning production now has to ask not only what the material costs but whether the license will arrive, when, for which customer, for what quantity, and under what end-use interpretation.

A price spike says the material is available if you can pay enough.

A licensing regime says money may not be the deciding variable.

Markets are good at solving expensive. They are less comfortable with unavailable.

Gallium and germanium make the problem especially clear because their markets are small relative to the industries that depend on them. They are used in semiconductors, telecommunications, infrared systems, solar technologies, and defense applications, and much of the supply is recovered as a by-product of other industrial processes rather than from mines that can be expanded on a simple gallium price signal.

The signal has to travel through someone else's factory.

Graphite showed the same ambiguity. China's revised controls did not amount to a blanket ban, and in early 2024 the Ministry of Commerce said qualified applications had been approved, including some involving South Korean battery companies.

This is where the word *weaponization* can become lazy.

A government regulating dual-use material is not automatically conducting economic warfare. The United States, Europe, Japan, and others also use export controls for security reasons. The useful question is narrower: what additional leverage becomes available when security policy is layered onto a supply chain one country already dominates?

That question became easier to answer in December 2024, when China's Ministry of Commerce announced country-specific restrictions on dual-use exports to the United States. Military users and military uses were barred. Gallium, germanium, antimony, and superhard-material exports to the United States would, in principle, not be licensed. Graphite faced stricter end-user and end-use review.

The administrative lever was now explicitly geopolitical.

The same material could be commercially available in one relationship and far harder to obtain in another.

April 2025 made the downstream consequences visible. China imposed export controls on seven heavy rare-earth elements and related products, including certain permanent-magnet materials. The IEA reported export volumes of affected rare earths and magnets falling sharply in April and May. Automakers in the United States, Europe, and elsewhere struggled to source magnets. Some reduced utilization. Some temporarily stopped production.

Licenses were eventually granted and trade recovered.

That sentence is both the counterargument and the warning.

The controls did not permanently shut the world out. They did not need to. A few weeks of uncertainty were enough to reveal which factories lacked a comfortable second source.

Imagine the production meeting. The missing component is not an engine block or battery pack. It is a magnet small enough to hold in one hand. The vehicle around it contains steel, glass, copper, software, semiconductors, seats, airbags, sensors, motors, thousands of parts, and tens of thousands of dollars of value.

None of that ships if the qualified magnet does not arrive.

This is why the market size of a chokepoint can be misleading. The economic importance of the input is not its invoice price. It is the value of everything waiting behind it.

The IEA's 2026 work estimates that if the broad rare-earth controls announced in 2025 were implemented in full, roughly $6.5 trillion in annual downstream production outside China could be exposed. That is not a forecast of $6.5 trillion in losses. It is a map of dependency.

China broadened rare-earth controls again in October 2025, reaching additional elements, processing technologies, and in some cases foreign-made products containing Chinese-sourced material or made using Chinese technology.

Then, in November, the broader package was suspended for a year.

Announce. Expand. Suspend.

A supply chain can learn something before a rule reaches full enforcement. Procurement teams map origins. Companies hire lawyers. Boards approve inventory or alternate suppliers that looked too expensive six months earlier. An announcement can move capital before it moves tonnage.

January 2026 offered an even clearer example of the political logic. China tightened controls on dual-use exports to Japan, prohibiting exports to Japanese military users, for military uses, and to other end users or uses that could contribute to Japan's military capability. The Ministry of Commerce explicitly linked the decision to political tension over remarks concerning Taiwan.

It was not a ban on every rare-earth shipment to Japan.

Precision matters.

But supply-chain access had become one instrument inside a diplomatic dispute.

None of this proves concentrated mineral supply guarantees geopolitical domination.

A weapon is useful only while the target cannot adapt faster than the weapon can hurt.

Japan's post-2010 response is the obvious case. It did not become independent of China. It bought options.

The same logic is now reshaping policy elsewhere. Governments support separation plants that look expensive under ordinary commodity accounting. Automakers sign long-term contracts with higher-cost suppliers. Defense ministries fund processing capacity. Companies hold more inventory than a textbook just-in-time system would recommend.

The premium buys resilience.

It can also buy nonsense. A country can subsidize politically connected projects that will never compete, stockpile the wrong material, confuse domestic ownership with capability, or build a mine without building the processing step customers need.

The answer is not to duplicate everything. It is to identify where one failure can stop too much downstream value and buy enough alternatives to make that failure survivable.

Sometimes the alternative is another supplier. Sometimes another chemistry. Sometimes less material per product. Sometimes recycling or inventory. Sometimes redesign removes the dependency entirely.

This is why coercive use of a chokepoint can be self-eroding.

A dominant supplier wants customers to believe there is no substitute. A disruption pays the customer to prove otherwise.

The proof may take years.

That delay is the source of present power.

China's rare-earth position remains formidable because alternatives are not built by declaration. Separation plants have to work. Magnet factories have to qualify product. Mines have to produce. Customers have to sign contracts. Engineers have to redesign products. Recycling needs waste streams large enough to matter.

The buyer can decide to diversify this afternoon.

The diversified supply may not exist for a decade.

That gap is the weapon.

It is also the bridge to the next chapter.

An export-control notice can be published overnight.

A mine cannot.

A government can change a licensing rule before breakfast. A mining company has to find an orebody, prove what is in it, raise money, obtain permits, build roads and power, construct a plant, hire a workforce, qualify the product, and survive the commodity cycle before dependable tonnes reach customers.

The asymmetry is between administrative time and physical time.
