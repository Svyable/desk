# The Capital Cycle, Underground

The fastest thing in a mineral shortage is the price.

It can move before lunch.

The slowest thing may be the hole in the ground.

That difference is easy to miss because markets teach us a reassuring sequence. Demand rises. Prices rise. Producers notice the profit. Supply arrives. Prices fall. The mechanism is real. Mining simply forces it to travel through rock, laboratories, permitting offices, financing committees, construction camps, power lines, processing plants, and several years in which the commodity price is free to change its mind.

The International Energy Agency studied major mines that entered production between 2010 and 2019 and found that, on average, more than sixteen years passed between discovery and first production. The number is often repeated as if somebody spent sixteen years waiting for a permit.

That is not what it measures.

More than twelve of those years, on average, were spent in exploration and feasibility. Construction took roughly another four to five. Permitting matters, sometimes enormously, but the famous sixteen-year figure is a discovery-to-production clock, not a government-review stopwatch.

That distinction makes the problem more interesting.

A mine is not one project waiting behind one gate. It is a portfolio of clocks.

First someone has to find something worth investigating. Then drill enough of it to understand the deposit. The orebody has to become a geological model, then a metallurgical problem, then an engineering design, then a financial proposition. Recovery rates matter. So do waste volumes, water, electricity, roads, rail, ports, labor, local consent, environmental controls, royalties, taxes, currency, and the price of the mineral several years from now.

Only after enough of those questions have stopped being questions can a company make the expensive part irreversible.

Concrete is wonderfully indifferent to next quarter's commodity forecast.

This is the capital cycle underground.

A software company can discover demand on Monday and rent more computing capacity on Tuesday. A manufacturer may add a shift, order another machine, or expand an existing plant. Mining has faster responses too: inventories can be released, an existing operation can push recovery, a mothballed asset can restart, a brownfield expansion can add capacity, or a processor can buy feed from somewhere else.

But a greenfield mine starts much farther back.

A higher copper price does not discover a deposit that nobody has found yet. It does not retroactively drill it. It cannot purchase five years of metallurgical knowledge from the future.

The market can raise the reward for doing those things. It cannot make them already done.

That is why project maturity matters as much as the headline number of announced tonnes.

Imagine two copper projects that each promise the same annual output. One has a defined resource, years of drilling, a completed feasibility study, permits, financing, power, roads, and contractors. The other has encouraging drill results and a presentation containing the word `world-class`.

A spreadsheet may place both in a future-supply column.

The rocks know the difference.

This is also why shortages can seem to appear suddenly after years of warnings. The underlying response is not binary. Projects are quietly retiring uncertainty long before metal reaches the market. When demand surprises to the upside, the useful question is not simply how many mines exist on paper. It is how many projects are close enough to construction that money can still change the outcome on the relevant timetable.

The copper problem makes this particularly visible.

Copper is not geologically rare in the everyday sense. It is mined across many countries, unlike cobalt's extraordinary concentration in the Democratic Republic of the Congo or the processing concentration of magnet rare earths in China. Yet large new copper projects can be punishingly capital-intensive. Ore quality has declined at many mature operations, which means more rock, energy, water, equipment, and waste may be required for the same tonne of metal. The IEA's 2025 outlook singled out copper as a difficult supply challenge because of declining grades, higher project costs, and a slowdown in major discoveries.

The orebody does not care that the grid needs copper quickly.

Neither does the balance sheet.

Mining finance has a peculiar cruelty. The moment when investment looks most attractive can also be the moment when equipment, labor, contractors, and acquisition targets are most expensive. High commodity prices improve projected revenue while inviting everyone else to build at once. Costs rise. Schedules stretch. Investors extrapolate the boom. Then new supply arrives into a market whose price was set by scarcity.

A mine can therefore be conceived in one commodity regime, financed in another, built in a third, and opened into a fourth.

This is how a rational response to a shortage helps manufacture the next surplus.

The recent battery-metal cycle is useful precisely because it complicates the story.

Lithium, nickel, and cobalt supply did respond. New capacity, particularly in China, Indonesia, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, expanded fast enough that prices fell sharply from their peaks. The IEA has noted that battery-metal supply demonstrated an ability to scale faster than traditional metals such as copper and zinc.

So “mining takes sixteen years” cannot be the rule.

Some deposits were already known. Some expansions were brownfield rather than greenfield. Indonesian nickel benefited from extraordinary investment in processing capacity and industrial parks. Lithium projects differ by geology and processing route. Cobalt can arrive as a by-product of copper or nickel. A market with projects already advancing through the pipeline can respond much faster than one waiting for the next major discovery.

The supply response is slow and uneven, not absent.

This matters because a dramatic statistic can produce lazy policy.

If every mine is assumed to require two decades, governments can conclude that nothing done today matters before the 2040s. Companies can use geology as an alibi for mediocre execution. Permitting systems can treat delay as inevitable. Investors can mistake scarcity for permanence.

None of those conclusions follows.

In 2025 the United States Department of the Interior offered an almost comically sharp counterexample to the idea that every part of mine development runs on geological time. Using emergency procedures, it completed the federal environmental review for Utah's Velvet-Wood uranium-vanadium project in fourteen days. The department said the same emergency framework could compress environmental assessments normally taking up to a year to roughly fourteen days, and environmental impact statements normally taking about two years to roughly twenty-eight.

Whatever one thinks of the policy, the timing proves something important.

A review procedure is a human institution. Humans can change it.

Fourteen days from environmental review to approval is not fourteen days from discovery to commercial production. The deposit had not materialized two weeks earlier. The company still had to do the work that comes before and after a federal review. But the example separates two clocks that political arguments often fuse together.

Some delay is geological.

Some is engineering.

Some is financial.

Some is administrative.

Some is social, because a mine changes a place that other people already inhabit.

Calling all of it `permitting` is analytically convenient and usually wrong.

Calling all of it unavoidable is wrong too.

The useful question is which clock is binding on this project now.

For one mine, the constraint may be a federal authorization. For another, a provincial water permit. For another, a tailings design. For another, a transmission line. For another, financing. For another, a community that has excellent reasons not to trust the company. For another, metallurgy that refuses to produce saleable concentrate at the recovery rate promised in the investor deck.

There is no universal shovel-ready button.

There are, however, ways to buy time before a crisis.

Exploration is one. It is politically unglamorous because a successful exploration program mostly produces information. Many targets become nothing. A few become deposits. Fewer become economic deposits. Fewer still become mines.

But information has option value.

A country that knows where its deposits are, has drilled them, understands the metallurgy, and has advanced credible projects through early studies owns something more useful in a shortage than a map colored `critical minerals`.

It owns years that have already been spent.

The same logic applies to processing.

Chapter 8's rare-earth problem was not simply that ore existed in the wrong country. The bottleneck sat in separation, metallization, magnet manufacturing, qualification, and the industrial knowledge connecting them. A new mine that sends concentrate back through the old chokepoint may diversify geology without diversifying leverage.

Mine lead time therefore understates the capital cycle the book actually cares about.

The customer needs qualified material, not a ribbon-cutting ceremony at a pit.

That can add another clock after first production. A new supplier has to demonstrate consistency. A battery maker, automaker, aerospace contractor, or defense customer may need to qualify the product. Processing routes have to stabilize. Volumes have to ramp. A mine producing its first tonne is not necessarily a supply chain producing dependable tonnes at scale.

The distinction is especially important when policymakers count announced capacity.

Announcements are cheap enough to be renewable.

Projects can appear in pipelines for years. Some will be delayed. Some redesigned. Some acquired. Some abandoned when prices fall. Some will become excellent mines after everyone has stopped talking about them.

The IEA's 2026 outlook gives the optimistic side its due. Announced projects have narrowed projected supply gaps for copper and lithium. Earlier-stage nickel and graphite projects could cover substantially more demand if they arrive on schedule.

That is evidence that price, policy, and investment work.

It is also a conditional sentence disguised as a supply forecast: *if they arrive on schedule*.

The capital cycle lives inside the if.

This is why a mineral chokepoint can survive obvious economic incentives to remove it.

Suppose an export restriction makes a material suddenly expensive. Customers begin searching for alternatives. Governments offer loans. Investors fund projects that looked marginal six months earlier. Engineers reduce material intensity. Recyclers improve collection. Existing producers expand.

All of those responses attack the chokepoint.

They operate on different clocks.

Inventory can help this quarter. Substitution may take a product cycle. A brownfield expansion may take a few years. A new processing plant has to be built and qualified. A greenfield mine may still be proving the orebody when the political crisis that justified it has disappeared from the front page.

This creates an uncomfortable problem for capital allocation.

The project needed most during a shortage may look least attractive by the time it opens.

A company considering a new mine has to ask whether today's scarcity price will survive years of competitor response. Investors remember the last bust. Lenders prefer projects that work at less heroic prices. Governments that want security may therefore support capacity the private market considers uneconomic at normal prices.

Sometimes that is sensible insurance.

Sometimes it is an expensive way to discover that a strategic asset can also be a bad mine.

Security does not repeal geology or arithmetic.

The strongest version of the mineral-security argument is not that every domestic deposit should be developed regardless of cost. It is that concentrated supply creates risks ordinary spot prices may not fully compensate anyone for carrying. A redundant processor, a stockpile, a second supplier, or an advanced but temporarily idle project can have option value even when its utilization looks inefficient in peaceful years.

The strongest objection is equally important: once `security` becomes a reason to ignore economics, almost any project can ask taxpayers to rescue it.

A resilient supply chain needs alternatives that can actually operate, not patriotic geology.

That brings the argument back to time.

The goal is not to make every mine fast.

It is to avoid beginning every response at year zero.

Keep geological knowledge alive. Advance credible projects far enough that they retain option value. Build processing competence before a crisis makes it fashionable. Qualify more than one supplier where the downstream veto is large enough to justify the cost. Know which permits can be improved without pretending environmental and community questions are paperwork. Use substitution and recycling where they are faster than new extraction.

Then, when a shock arrives, the market has something to accelerate.

This is the hidden asset inside a mature project pipeline.

Not ore.

Time already spent.

Chapter 9 ended with a government able to change a licensing rule before breakfast while a mining company faced years of physical work.

The asymmetry remains. But geological time is not a sentence handed down by nature in one fixed number. It is a stack of uncertainties, some physical and some institutional, that can be retired before they become urgent.

The next chapter is about what happens when the cycle is handled badly—or simply arrives too late.

A high price summons capital. Capital summons capacity. Capacity arrives after the shortage that justified it. The town fills before the market turns.

Then the price falls.

The mine remains.
