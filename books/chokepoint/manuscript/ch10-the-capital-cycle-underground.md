# The Capital Cycle, Underground

The fastest thing in a mineral shortage is the price.

It can move before lunch.

The slowest thing may be the hole in the ground.

Markets teach a reassuring sequence. Demand rises. Prices rise. Producers notice the profit. Supply arrives. Prices fall.

The mechanism is real. Mining simply forces it to travel through rock, laboratories, permitting offices, financing committees, construction camps, power lines, processing plants, and years in which the commodity price is free to change its mind.

The International Energy Agency studied major mines that entered production between 2010 and 2019 and found that, on average, more than sixteen years passed between discovery and first production. The number is often repeated as though somebody spent sixteen years waiting for a permit.

That is not what it measures.

More than twelve years, on average, were spent in exploration and feasibility. Construction took roughly another four to five. Permitting can matter enormously, but the famous statistic is a discovery-to-production clock, not a government-review stopwatch.

A mine is not one project waiting behind one gate.

It is a portfolio of clocks.

Someone has to find something worth investigating, drill enough to understand it, turn the orebody into a geological model, solve the metallurgy, design the mine, price the waste, water, power, roads, rail, ports, labor, royalties, taxes, community relationships, environmental controls, and then persuade investors that the mineral price years from now will justify making most of the capital irreversible.

Concrete is wonderfully indifferent to next quarter's commodity forecast.

Mining does have fast responses. Inventories can be released. Existing operations can push recovery. Mothballed assets can restart. Brownfield expansions can add capacity. Processors can switch feed.

A greenfield mine starts farther back.

A higher copper price does not discover a deposit that nobody has found. It does not retroactively drill it or purchase five years of metallurgical knowledge from the future.

The market can raise the reward for doing those things. It cannot make them already done.

That is why project maturity matters as much as announced tonnage.

Imagine two copper projects promising the same annual output. One has a defined resource, years of drilling, a completed feasibility study, permits, financing, power, roads, and contractors. The other has encouraging drill results and a presentation containing the word `world-class`.

A spreadsheet may place both in a future-supply column.

The rocks know the difference.

This is also why shortages can look sudden after years of warning. Projects are quietly retiring uncertainty long before metal reaches the market. When demand surprises to the upside, the useful question is not how many mines exist on paper. It is how many projects are mature enough that money can still change the outcome on the relevant timetable.

Mining finance adds another complication. The moment investment looks most attractive can also be the moment equipment, labor, contractors, and acquisition targets are most expensive. High prices improve projected revenue and invite everyone to build at once. Costs rise. Schedules stretch. New supply eventually enters a market whose price was set by scarcity.

A mine can be conceived in one commodity regime, financed in another, built in a third, and opened into a fourth.

That is how a rational response to a shortage helps manufacture the next surplus.

The recent battery-metal cycle is useful because it refuses the simple story. Lithium, nickel, and cobalt supply did respond. New capacity in China, Indonesia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and elsewhere expanded fast enough to push prices down sharply from their peaks. The IEA has noted that battery-metal supply scaled faster than traditional metals such as copper and zinc.

So “mining takes sixteen years” cannot be a rule.

Some deposits were already known. Some projects were expansions rather than new mines. Indonesian nickel was pulled forward by massive investment in industrial parks and processing. Lithium projects differ by geology. Cobalt can arrive as a by-product of copper or nickel.

The response is slow and uneven, not absent.

That distinction matters because dramatic lead-time statistics can produce lazy policy. If every mine is assumed to require two decades, governments can conclude that nothing done today matters before the 2040s. Companies can use geology as an alibi for poor execution. Permitting systems can treat delay as inevitable. Investors can mistake scarcity for permanence.

In 2025, the U.S. Department of the Interior supplied an unusually sharp counterexample to the idea that every part of mine development runs on geological time. Using emergency procedures, it completed the federal environmental review for Utah's Velvet-Wood uranium-vanadium project in fourteen days. The department said the same framework could compress environmental assessments normally taking up to a year to roughly fourteen days and environmental impact statements normally taking about two years to roughly twenty-eight.

Whatever one thinks of the policy, a review procedure is a human institution. Humans can change it.

Fourteen days from environmental review to approval is not fourteen days from discovery to commercial production. The deposit had not materialized two weeks earlier. The example simply separates clocks that political arguments often fuse together.

Some delay is geological. Some is engineering. Some financial. Some administrative. Some social, because a mine changes a place other people already inhabit.

Calling all of it `permitting` is usually wrong.

Calling all of it unavoidable is wrong too.

The useful question is which clock is binding now.

For one project, it may be federal authorization. For another, water, tailings design, a transmission line, financing, community trust, or metallurgy that refuses to produce saleable concentrate at the recovery rate promised in the investor deck.

There is no universal shovel-ready button.

There are, however, ways to buy time before a crisis.

Exploration is one. It is politically unglamorous because most targets become nothing. A few become deposits. Fewer become economic deposits. Fewer still become mines.

But information has option value.

A country that knows where credible deposits are, has drilled them, understands their metallurgy, and has advanced projects through early studies owns something more useful in a shortage than a map colored `critical minerals`.

It owns years already spent.

The same is true downstream. A new mine that sends concentrate through the old processing chokepoint may diversify geology without diversifying leverage. A supplier can also face years of qualification after first production while battery makers, automakers, aerospace contractors, or defense customers test consistency.

The customer needs dependable material, not a ribbon-cutting ceremony at a pit.

This is why announced capacity has to be read as a set of conditional sentences. Some projects will be delayed, redesigned, acquired, or abandoned when prices fall. Others will become excellent mines after everyone has stopped talking about them.

The IEA's 2026 outlook gives the optimistic side its due. Announced projects narrowed projected supply gaps for copper and lithium, and earlier-stage nickel and graphite projects could cover more demand if they arrive on schedule.

That is evidence that price, policy, and investment work.

The capital cycle lives inside the *if*.

Suppose an export restriction makes a material suddenly expensive. Inventory can help this quarter. Engineers can reduce material intensity over a product cycle. A brownfield expansion may take a few years. A processing plant must be built and qualified. A greenfield mine may still be proving the orebody when the political crisis that justified it has disappeared from the front page.

The project needed most during a shortage may therefore look least attractive by the time it opens.

Investors remember the last bust. Lenders prefer projects that work at less heroic prices. Governments seeking security may support capacity the private market considers uneconomic at normal prices.

Sometimes that is sensible insurance.

Sometimes it is an expensive way to discover that a strategic asset can also be a bad mine.

Security does not repeal geology or arithmetic.

The strongest mineral-security argument is not that every domestic deposit should be developed. It is that concentrated supply creates risks spot prices may not fully reward anyone for carrying. A redundant processor, stockpile, second supplier, or advanced but temporarily idle project can have option value even when utilization looks inefficient in peaceful years.

The objection is obvious and important: once `security` becomes a reason to ignore economics, almost any project can ask taxpayers to rescue it.

A resilient supply chain needs alternatives that can actually operate, not patriotic geology.

That brings the argument back to time.

The goal is not to make every mine fast. It is to avoid beginning every response at year zero.

Keep geological knowledge alive. Advance credible projects far enough that they retain option value. Build processing competence before a crisis makes it fashionable. Qualify more than one supplier where the downstream veto justifies the cost. Improve administrative delays without pretending environmental and community questions are paperwork. Use substitution and recycling where they are faster than new extraction.

Then, when a shock arrives, the market has something to accelerate.

The hidden asset inside a mature project pipeline is not only ore.

It is time already spent.

The next chapter shows what happens when capital arrives on the wrong side of that clock.

A high price summons investment. Investment summons capacity. Capacity arrives after the shortage that justified it. The town fills before the market turns.

Then the price falls.
