# The Recycling Illusion and Reality

A battery recycler can have too much factory and not enough dead batteries.

That sounds backwards.

If electric vehicles are multiplying, governments are warning about lithium and nickel supply, and every serious mineral-security plan includes recycling, then a recycler ought to face the pleasant problem of too much material arriving at the gate.

Instead, the early industry has often faced the opposite problem.

The plants can be ready before the waste is.

That contradiction is the best place to begin because it strips the word `recycling` of some of its magic. Recycling is not a machine that turns scarcity off. It is a supply chain whose raw material has to exist, become available, be collected, transported, sorted, processed, refined, and sold into a market that may have changed chemistry by the time the plant is paid for.

The first bottleneck is time.

An electric-vehicle battery is useful precisely because it lasts for years.

That is good for the driver and awkward for the recycler.

The International Energy Agency estimates that manufacturing scrap still accounts for about two-thirds of available battery-recycling feedstock in 2030. Only from around 2035 do end-of-life EV and storage batteries become the largest source. By 2050, they account for more than 90 percent of available feedstock.

The fleet has to age before it can become a mine.

This is why the recycling debate becomes confused so easily. People look at the number of batteries being manufactured and imagine an equivalent stream of batteries available for recycling.

Those are not the same stock.

A battery made this year joins the installed base. Unless it fails early or is scrapped in an accident, it stays there, doing the job it was built to do. The minerals are not lost. They are simply unavailable to the recycler because they are still busy being a battery.

Mining and recycling therefore operate on different inventories.

A mine looks for material that has not yet entered the economy.

A recycler waits for material to come back out.

That difference makes recycling a stock-and-flow problem before it becomes a technology problem.

The technology itself is increasingly real.

Redwood Materials says it now receives more than 20 gigawatt-hours of lithium-ion batteries each year and recovers more than 95 percent of the lithium, nickel, cobalt, and copper in the incoming material. The company reports producing more than 60,000 tons of critical materials annually from recycled batteries and manufacturing scrap.

Those are company-reported figures, not neutral market statistics, but they make one point difficult to dispute: commercial-scale recovery is no longer a laboratory promise.

The argument has moved on from `can these metals be recovered?`

They can.

The harder questions are whether enough material arrives, whether the economics work for the chemistry inside it, and whether the recovered material has somewhere valuable to go.

The second bottleneck is collection.

A dead battery that never reaches a recycler is not feedstock.

This sounds obvious until one looks at ordinary waste systems. Phones sit in drawers. Cars cross borders. Electronics are dismantled informally. Industrial equipment is resold. Packs are repurposed. Scrap moves toward whoever will pay for it, which may be in another jurisdiction entirely.

The IEA's estimates for future recycled battery metals move substantially depending on collection rates. Under improving collection, battery recycling could supply roughly 20 to 30 percent of lithium, nickel, and cobalt demand by 2050. With stronger collection that range can rise; with weaker collection it falls.

The interesting number is not a laboratory recovery percentage.

It is how much material makes it back to the laboratory in the first place.

That turns mundane systems into strategic infrastructure.

Vehicle registration. Ownership records. Take-back rules. Dealer networks. Scrap-yard economics. Export rules for used cars. Safe transport for damaged packs. The ability to identify what chemistry is inside a black metal box that may have spent fifteen years under a vehicle.

None of this has the romance of discovering a deposit.

It may matter just as much.

A country can import lithium once inside a battery and, in principle, keep some of those atoms circulating domestically through several product lives. But the strategic advantage exists only if the country can find the battery again.

The third bottleneck is chemistry.

Not all dead batteries are equally attractive.

Nickel-rich batteries contain metals with relatively high resale value. Lithium iron phosphate batteries avoid nickel and cobalt, which is one reason they have become so appealing for cost and supply security. The same feature makes them less lucrative feedstock for a recycler.

A technology can therefore solve one mineral problem while creating a business-model problem somewhere else.

LFP reduces exposure to nickel and cobalt.

It also reduces the value sitting in the waste stream.

The IEA noted that LFP batteries accounted for about 40 percent of EV batteries deployed in 2023 and specifically warned that their lower material value changes recycling economics.

This is not an argument against LFP.

It is an argument against assuming that recycling economics improve automatically as the battery market grows.

What gets built determines what eventually comes back.

The composition of today's fleet becomes the feedstock mix of the 2030s and 2040s.

A recycler is therefore making two forecasts at once: how many batteries will return, and what those batteries will be worth when they do.

Commodity prices get a vote too.

If virgin lithium, nickel, or cobalt becomes cheap, recovered material has to compete with cheaper primary supply. If prices rise, the waste stream becomes more valuable. A recycling plant can be strategically desirable and technically capable while still being a difficult financial asset.

Li-Cycle supplied an uncomfortable demonstration.

In May 2025, the company and its North American subsidiaries entered creditor-protection proceedings in Canada, while U.S. subsidiaries entered Chapter 15 proceedings. Glencore agreed to a stalking-horse bid of at least $40 million for specified assets and subsidiaries. Shortly before that, Li-Cycle had suspended operations at facilities in Arizona and Alabama while trying to preserve liquidity and find a buyer.

The safe conclusion is not that battery recycling failed.

A company can fail while an industry remains necessary.

The narrower lesson is more useful: recyclers have a capital cycle too.

They build expensive processing capacity against forecasts of future feedstock, commodity prices, customer demand, financing costs, and policy. If the factory arrives before the batteries, or costs more to finish than expected, strategic importance does not make the debt disappear.

This is where recycling begins to resemble mining in an unexpected way.

Both businesses are forced to commit fixed capital against uncertain future material flows.

A miner worries about ore grade, recovery, permitting, and the commodity price at first production.

A recycler worries about collection, chemistry, utilization, and the commodity price when black mass becomes battery-grade material again.

One digs into geology.

The other digs into yesterday's products.

Neither escapes arithmetic.

The early capacity numbers make the timing problem vivid. The IEA estimates that, if announced projects arrive as planned, global battery-recycling capacity in 2030 could be roughly seven times available feedstock.

Seven times.

That sounds like spectacular overbuilding.

Then the curve turns.

As the first large generations of EVs reach the end of their lives, available feedstock rises quickly. By 2040, the IEA projects global feedstock could exceed announced recycling capacity by about 60 percent. In Europe and the United States, excess recycling capacity dissipates after 2030 and announced capacity covers only about 30 percent of available feedstock by 2040.

The same industry can therefore have too much capacity and too little capacity within a decade.

Nothing mystical happened.

The batteries got older.

This is the stock-and-flow problem in its purest form.

Factories are built on one schedule.

Waste matures on another.

Policy operates on a third.

Commodity prices improvise.

The strategic challenge is not merely to build recycling plants. It is to build enough of the right capacity in the right places at roughly the moment the material becomes available.

Policy can change the economics.

The European Union's Batteries Regulation is unusually explicit. From August 2031, covered industrial, vehicle, and starting batteries placed on the EU market must contain minimum recycled shares including 16 percent cobalt, 6 percent lithium, and 6 percent nickel. The regulation also sets material-recovery targets that rise by the end of 2031 to 95 percent for cobalt, copper, lead, and nickel and 80 percent for lithium.

That matters because it creates demand for recycled material that does not depend entirely on today's spot price.

A virgin material can be cheaper and the manufacturer may still need recycled content.

In effect, regulation can manufacture a floor under part of the secondary-material market.

Whether that is elegant industrial policy or an expensive mandate depends on implementation, but the mechanism is clear. Governments do not have to wait for recycled material to beat virgin material on price in every cycle. They can decide that circular supply has strategic or environmental value worth paying for.

The obvious danger is that policy can also protect bad economics.

If every recycler with a weak balance sheet can claim strategic importance, the industry acquires the same problem discussed in Chapter 11. Security becomes an argument for keeping uneconomic capital alive.

A useful recycling policy has to distinguish between helping a young market overcome coordination problems and subsidizing capacity nobody needs.

That distinction will not always be obvious in real time.

Neither was the distinction between a sleeping mine and a dead one.

There is another complication hidden inside the word recycling.

Sometimes the best thing to do with an old battery is not to recycle it yet.

A pack may no longer meet the performance requirements of a vehicle but still have enough useful life for stationary storage. Redwood and Rivian, for example, announced a storage system using more than one hundred second-life EV packs rather than immediately sending them through mineral recovery.

From a materials perspective, reuse delays recycling.

From a resource-efficiency perspective, that can be a success.

A battery that gets another useful life is not wasted feedstock. It is feedstock deferred.

Circularity therefore contains its own hierarchy of clocks.

Use the product longer.

Repair it if practical.

Repurpose it when the original application becomes too demanding.

Recover the materials when the product finally stops being useful.

Only then send the atoms around again.

That is a more demanding idea than `recycle more`, because it asks the system to preserve value at the highest useful level rather than rushing everything toward a shredder.

It also makes forecasting harder.

If second-life markets improve, recyclers may wait longer for feedstock. If battery durability improves, they wait longer again. Both developments are good for consumers and material efficiency. Both can make a recycling plant look underutilized in the short run.

The recycler can become a victim of better products.

Copper shows why this chapter cannot remain only about batteries.

Unlike lithium-ion recycling, copper recycling is old industrial practice. Scrap from buildings, wires, motors, appliances, vehicles, and industrial equipment already moves through mature secondary markets. Yet even there the circular story is incomplete.

The IEA reports that secondary copper's share of total copper demand actually fell from 37 percent in 2015 to 33 percent in 2023 as overall demand grew faster than recycled supply.

A mature recycling industry can grow in absolute terms and still lose share.

That is the central arithmetic of a rapidly expanding system.

If the total stock of wires, vehicles, grids, and machines is still growing, recycled material mostly supplements primary production. Only when retirements become large relative to new demand can secondary supply begin replacing a substantial share of new extraction.

This is why the phrase `urban mining` is both useful and misleading.

Cities really do contain enormous metal stocks.

Buildings contain copper. Vehicles contain copper, aluminium, lithium, nickel, cobalt, rare earths, and steel. Motors and transformers contain windings and magnets. Electronics contain a chemically irritating periodic table in miniature.

But an orebody waits in one place.

Urban ore is dispersed through millions of objects owned by millions of people, built in different years, made from different chemistries, and scheduled to become waste according to human behavior rather than geology.

The mine has lower grades.

The city has worse logistics.

This is one reason high recovery rates at a plant can coexist with modest recycling rates across an economy. The plant can recover 95 percent of what enters its process and still touch only a fraction of the material theoretically available.

Efficiency inside the gate is not the same as circularity outside it.

There is also no reason to romanticize the recycling side of the supply chain.

Processing scrap consumes energy, uses chemicals, produces residues, and can create serious pollution when done badly. Informal electronics recycling in poorer countries can expose workers and communities to lead, acids, smoke, and contaminated waste. A recycled mineral is not automatically an ethical mineral.

The advantage is comparative, not magical.

The IEA estimates that recycled energy-transition minerals such as lithium, nickel, and cobalt can have substantially lower greenhouse-gas emissions than primary production on average. Recycling can also reduce the need for new mines and some of the land, waste, and water impacts associated with extraction.

But it moves environmental responsibility rather than abolishing it.

The circular economy still needs standards, traceability, safe collection, and competent processing.

There is a geopolitical consequence too.

A country that lacks major lithium deposits may still accumulate a significant lithium stock through imported batteries and vehicles. Over time, recycling can convert some of that historical import dependence into domestic secondary supply.

That does not make the country mineral-independent.

The IEA still expects substantial new mine and refining investment even with aggressive recycling. Under its Announced Pledges Scenario, successful recycling scale-up could reduce the need for new mine development by roughly 40 percent for copper and cobalt and 25 percent for lithium and nickel by 2050.

Reduce is not eliminate.

That is the strongest argument against the recycling-utopia version of this story.

The energy transition needs a great deal of material before enough old clean-energy equipment exists to recycle. You cannot recover lithium in 2027 from an EV battery that will still be driving in 2034. A rapidly growing stock consumes primary material faster than its early generations retire.

The buildout has to be built before it can become circular.

The strongest argument against the dismissive version is just as important.

Once the stock exists, it changes the long-run supply problem.

A tonne of lithium in a battery is different from a barrel of oil in a fuel tank. The oil is burned. The lithium remains matter. It can be lost through poor collection or dissipated through bad processing, but in principle much of it can be recovered and used again.

Critical minerals are not fossil fuels with different politics.

The atoms survive use.

That difference compounds over time.

Every battery, motor, cable, transformer, and magnet placed into service enlarges the future above-ground resource. If collection improves and processing capacity follows, yesterday's imports become tomorrow's feedstock.

The mineral system slowly acquires memory.

That memory is valuable because geological concentration is not the only kind of concentration that matters. A country may have no important nickel mine but a large vehicle fleet, mature scrap systems, battery plants, refiners, and customers willing to buy secondary material. Industrial history can become mineral endowment.

Not immediately.

That is the boundary the chapter has to keep.

Recycling is weakest as an answer exactly when a new technology is growing fastest, because the installed stock is still young. It becomes more powerful after the first waves of material have circulated through the economy and begun returning.

This explains why two apparently contradictory statements can both be true.

We need more mines.

We also need to build a system that makes some future mines unnecessary.

The first statement describes the buildout.

The second describes what happens after enough atoms have entered the loop.

Chapter 11 ended in a nearly new town whose economic reason for existing vanished when the price turned. Recycling offers no escape from cycles, financing, or bad forecasts. Recyclers can overbuild. Feedstock can arrive late. Chemistries can change. Virgin prices can collapse. A strategically important plant can still run out of cash.

But the underlying resource behaves differently.

A mine depletes an orebody as it produces.

A recycling system tries to keep the same material economically useful through several lives.

That is not perpetual motion. There are losses at every turn. Products are exported, discarded, contaminated, or simply too expensive to recover. Some applications hold material for decades. Collection will never be perfect.

Still, the direction matters.

The mature mineral economy is not one in which digging stops.

It is one in which digging competes with a growing stock of material already paid for, already extracted, and waiting—eventually—to come back.

The next chapter asks what happens when even that seems too slow.

If terrestrial mines take years and recycling takes generations of products, the search for new supply moves toward places with no towns, no farms, and no conventional orebody at all.

Several kilometers below the ocean surface, metal-rich nodules have been waiting far longer than any battery fleet.

The argument for mining them begins with a familiar promise.

Perhaps the next source can avoid the constraints of the last one.
