# The Recycling Illusion and Reality

A battery recycler can have too much factory and not enough dead batteries.

That sounds backwards.

If electric vehicles are multiplying, governments are worried about lithium and nickel supply, and every mineral-security plan includes recycling, a recycler ought to face the pleasant problem of too much material arriving at the gate.

The early industry has often faced the opposite problem.

The plants can be ready before the waste is.

That contradiction strips some magic from the word `recycling`. Recycling is not a machine that turns scarcity off. It is a supply chain whose raw material has to exist, become available, be collected, transported, sorted, processed, refined, and sold into a market that may have changed chemistry by the time the plant is paid for.

The first bottleneck is time.

An electric-vehicle battery is useful precisely because it lasts for years. That is good for the driver and awkward for the recycler.

The International Energy Agency estimates that manufacturing scrap still accounts for about two-thirds of available battery-recycling feedstock in 2030. Only around 2035 do end-of-life EV and storage batteries become the largest source. By 2050, they account for more than 90 percent.

The fleet has to age before it can become a mine.

A battery made this year joins the installed stock. Unless it fails early or is destroyed, its minerals remain unavailable to the recycler because they are still busy being a battery.

Mining and recycling therefore operate on different inventories. A mine looks for material that has not yet entered the economy. A recycler waits for material to come back out.

That is a stock-and-flow problem before it is a technology problem.

The technology itself is increasingly real. Redwood Materials says it receives more than 20 gigawatt-hours of lithium-ion batteries each year and recovers more than 95 percent of the lithium, nickel, cobalt, and copper in incoming material. Those are company-reported figures, not neutral market statistics, but they make one point difficult to dispute: commercial-scale recovery is no longer only a laboratory promise.

The harder questions are whether enough material arrives, whether the chemistry inside it is valuable enough to process, and where the recovered material goes next.

Collection is the next constraint.

A dead battery that never reaches a recycler is not feedstock. Phones sit in drawers. Cars cross borders. Electronics are dismantled informally. Packs are repurposed. Scrap moves toward whoever will pay for it.

The IEA's long-run estimates move substantially with collection rates. Under improving collection, battery recycling could supply roughly 20 to 30 percent of lithium, nickel, and cobalt demand by 2050; stronger collection raises the contribution and weaker collection reduces it.

The interesting number is not only what a plant can recover once material enters the gate.

It is how much material reaches the gate at all.

That turns mundane systems into strategic infrastructure: vehicle registration, take-back rules, dealer networks, scrap-yard economics, used-car exports, safe transport for damaged packs, and the ability to identify what chemistry sits inside a black metal box after fifteen years under a vehicle.

A country can import lithium once inside a battery and, in principle, keep some of those atoms circulating domestically. But the advantage exists only if the country can find the battery again.

Chemistry changes the business too.

Nickel-rich batteries contain relatively valuable metals. Lithium iron phosphate batteries avoid nickel and cobalt, which helps explain their appeal on cost and supply security. The same feature makes them less lucrative recycling feedstock.

A technology can reduce one mineral vulnerability and weaken the economics of the industry expected to recover it later.

The IEA noted that LFP accounted for about 40 percent of EV batteries deployed in 2023 and warned that lower material value changes recycling economics. Today's battery mix becomes the feedstock mix of the 2030s and 2040s.

Commodity prices get a vote as well. If virgin lithium, nickel, or cobalt becomes cheap, recovered material has to compete with cheaper primary supply. A recycling plant can be strategically desirable and technically capable while remaining a difficult financial asset.

Li-Cycle supplied an uncomfortable example. In May 2025, the company and its North American subsidiaries entered creditor-protection proceedings in Canada, while U.S. subsidiaries entered Chapter 15 proceedings. Glencore agreed to a stalking-horse bid of at least $40 million for specified assets and subsidiaries. Shortly before that, Li-Cycle had suspended operations at facilities in Arizona and Alabama while trying to preserve liquidity and find a buyer.

The safe conclusion is not that battery recycling failed.

A company can fail while an industry remains necessary.

The narrower lesson is that recyclers have a capital cycle too. They commit fixed capital against forecasts of future feedstock, commodity prices, customer demand, financing costs, and policy. If the factory arrives before the batteries, strategic importance does not make the debt disappear.

The timing inversion is dramatic. The IEA estimates that if announced projects arrive as planned, global battery-recycling capacity in 2030 could be roughly seven times available feedstock.

Then the curve turns.

As the first large EV generations reach end of life, available feedstock rises quickly. By 2040, the IEA projects global feedstock could exceed announced recycling capacity by about 60 percent. In Europe and the United States, announced capacity covers only about 30 percent of available feedstock by 2040.

The same industry can have too much capacity and too little capacity within a decade.

The batteries simply got older.

Factories are built on one schedule. Waste matures on another. Policy operates on a third. Commodity prices improvise.

The strategic challenge is not to build recycling plants as fast as possible. It is to build the right capacity in the right places as the material becomes available.

Policy can shift the economics. The European Union's Batteries Regulation requires minimum recycled shares in covered batteries from August 2031, including 16 percent cobalt, 6 percent lithium, and 6 percent nickel, and it raises material-recovery targets by the end of 2031.

That creates demand for recycled material that does not depend entirely on spot prices. A virgin input can be cheaper and a manufacturer may still need recycled content.

The danger is familiar from mining. If every weak recycler can claim strategic importance, policy can preserve capacity nobody needs. Supporting a young market through coordination problems and subsidizing bad capital are not the same thing, even when both arrive with a resilience argument.

There is another wrinkle: sometimes the best thing to do with an old battery is not to recycle it yet.

A pack that no longer meets vehicle requirements may still have useful life in stationary storage. Redwood and Rivian, for example, announced a storage system using more than one hundred second-life EV packs rather than immediately sending them through mineral recovery.

From a recycler's perspective, reuse delays feedstock.

From a resource-efficiency perspective, that can be a success.

A longer-lived product is not wasted material. It is material whose return has been deferred.

Better durability, repair, and second-life markets can therefore make recycling plants look underutilized in the short run while improving circularity overall. The recycler can become a victim of better products.

Copper shows why this story is bigger than batteries.

Copper recycling is old industrial practice. Scrap from buildings, wires, motors, appliances, vehicles, and equipment already moves through mature secondary markets. Yet the IEA reports that secondary copper's share of total demand fell from 37 percent in 2015 to 33 percent in 2023 because total demand grew faster than recycled supply.

A mature recycling industry can grow in absolute terms and still lose share.

That is the arithmetic of a rapidly expanding stock. While the economy is building more wires, vehicles, grids, and machines than it retires, secondary material supplements primary production rather than replacing most of it.

This is why `urban mining` is useful and misleading at once.

Cities really do contain enormous metal stocks. But an orebody waits in one place. Urban ore is dispersed through millions of objects owned by millions of people, built in different years, made from different chemistries, and scheduled to become waste according to human behavior rather than geology.

The mine has lower grades.

The city has worse logistics.

That is why a plant can recover 95 percent of what enters its process while the economy recovers only a fraction of the material theoretically available. Efficiency inside the gate is not the same as circularity outside it.

Nor is recycled material automatically clean or ethical. Processing scrap uses energy and chemicals and creates residues. Informal electronics recycling can expose workers and communities to lead, acids, smoke, and contaminated waste.

The advantage is comparative, not magical.

The IEA estimates recycled transition minerals can have substantially lower greenhouse-gas emissions on average than primary production and can reduce some land, water, and waste burdens from new extraction. But environmental responsibility moves; it does not disappear.

There is a geopolitical payoff too.

A country without major lithium or nickel deposits can accumulate a significant above-ground stock through imported batteries and vehicles. Over time, recycling can convert part of that historical import dependence into secondary feedstock.

That does not guarantee strategic independence. The recovered atoms may still need refining or conversion in concentrated processing hubs. Recycling can diversify the origin of the material without automatically diversifying every downstream step.

And even aggressive recycling does not eliminate the need for primary supply. Under the IEA's Announced Pledges Scenario, successful recycling scale-up could reduce the need for new mine development by roughly 40 percent for copper and cobalt and 25 percent for lithium and nickel by 2050.

Reduce is not eliminate.

The strongest argument against recycling utopia is the buildout itself. The energy and industrial transition needs enormous material before enough old equipment exists to recycle. You cannot recover lithium in 2027 from an EV battery still driving in 2034.

The strongest argument against dismissing recycling is what happens after the stock exists.

A tonne of lithium in a battery is different from a barrel of oil in a fuel tank. The oil is burned. The lithium remains matter. Collection can fail and processing can lose material, but the atom survives use.

Every battery, motor, cable, transformer, and magnet placed into service enlarges the future above-ground resource.

The mineral system slowly acquires memory.

Recycling is weakest as an answer exactly when a new technology is growing fastest, because the installed stock is still young. It becomes more powerful after the first waves of equipment have circulated through the economy and begun returning.

That is why two apparently contradictory statements can both be true.

We need more mines.

We also need to build a system that makes some future mines unnecessary.

The first statement describes the buildout. The second describes the mature stock.

Neither escapes the clock.

The next chapter follows the search for supply to a place where there are no towns, farms, or conventional ore bodies at all. Several kilometers below the ocean surface, metal-rich nodules have been waiting far longer than any battery fleet.

The promise is familiar: perhaps the next source can avoid the constraints of the last one.
