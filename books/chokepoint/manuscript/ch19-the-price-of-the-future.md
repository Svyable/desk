# The Price of the Future

A mine is an argument about a price that does not exist yet.

The argument begins years before the first sale. Geologists drill. Engineers turn a resource into a mine plan. Metallurgists test the ore. Lawyers assemble permits. A company negotiates power, water, roads, rail, ports, royalties, community agreements, financing, and contracts for equipment that may take years to arrive. By the time a board is asked to approve several billion dollars, everybody in the room has a spreadsheet full of prices extending into a future nobody can see.

This is unavoidable. A copper mine cannot be financed by saying copper might be expensive or might be cheap. Lenders need a debt-service model. Shareholders need some idea of return. Governments need revenue assumptions. Contractors need schedules. Yet the number that makes the model work is also one of the least controllable facts in it.

That is why the most dangerous sentence in a commodity boom is often not “this time is different.” It is the quieter sentence underneath it: this price is normal.

The mineral economy keeps punishing that assumption. High prices invite investment, substitution, thrift, recycling, inventories, new technology, political intervention, and sometimes cheating. Low prices kill marginal projects, weaken exploration, consolidate producers, discourage recycling, and make diversification look wasteful. The market does not merely reveal scarcity. It reacts to the price that scarcity creates.

The result is a system in which the future keeps changing because people are trying to prepare for it.

Consider how quickly an apparently distant balance can move. In its 2025 critical-minerals outlook, the International Energy Agency estimated that the copper project pipeline under its stated-policy assumptions left a supply gap of roughly 30 percent in 2035. A year later, the agency’s 2026 outlook put the gap closer to 25 percent as projects advanced. Lithium’s projected balance improved too. Cobalt moved the other way: the projected 2035 shortfall widened to more than 25 percent after the Democratic Republic of the Congo changed export policy and expected production shifted.

The lesson is not that one forecast was wrong and the next one right. The lesson is that a forecast is conditional. A project that secures financing moves the line. A quota moves it. A battery chemistry moves it. A recession moves it. A new refinery, a delayed permit, a failed tailings study, a larger recycling stream, or a government loan guarantee can all alter a balance that looked fixed when the previous report went to press.

This is why a book about mineral chokepoints should be suspicious of ending with a table of 2035 prices. It would look precise and age quickly. A more useful question is what evidence would tell us which kind of future is arriving.

Start with price, but do not stop there.

Between January 2025 and April 2026, according to the IEA, copper and other base-metal prices rose by about a third. Lithium prices more than doubled. Cobalt rose around 130 percent, driven in large part by restrictions from the DRC. Several strategic minor minerals moved even more violently. Those increases followed a period in which battery-metal prices had been pushed down by rapid supply growth in China, Indonesia, and the Congo even while underlying demand continued to expand.

Both periods produced confident stories. Falling prices were evidence that the shortage narrative had been exaggerated. Rising prices were evidence that scarcity had finally arrived.

Neither story was sufficient.

A low price can be the visible surface of a future shortage. If today’s surplus causes producers to cancel exploration, defer processing plants, close high-cost mines, and abandon diversification projects, the consumer enjoys cheap material while the next supply wave is quietly being removed. A high price can be the visible surface of a future surplus. It can finance precisely the mines, recovery systems, substitutes, and inventories that make the spike temporary.

Price is news about the market. It is also an instruction to the market.

For a mineral whose projects take a decade or more to move from discovery to meaningful production, that distinction is crucial. The fast variable can set the slow machinery in motion, but the slow machinery arrives after the conditions that justified it have changed.

The capital cycle is therefore a better guide to the future than the spot price alone.

In 2025, total investment in critical minerals fell 9 percent, the first substantial decline since 2020. Battery-material companies cut capital spending by more than 20 percent. Lithium-focused companies cut it by around 40 percent. Exploration spending declined by about 10 percent. Copper moved differently, with investment by copper-focused companies rising about 8 percent.

Those figures are more interesting than a price chart because they describe tomorrow’s options being created or removed. A mine operating today can keep producing through a weak investment year. A refinery can keep shipping. The shortage appears later, when the industry discovers that the replacement projects it assumed would exist were never financed.

The warning signal, then, is not simply that a mineral has become expensive. It is that demand continues to rise, spare capacity and inventories are thinning, and the companies that must build future supply are still not spending enough to do it.

The opposite signal matters too. A market can improve before the price says so. Exploration budgets can stabilize. Projects can reach final investment decisions. Construction can advance. Processing plants can commission. Customers can qualify new material. A second supplier can survive a down-cycle without closing. These are duller facts than a price spike, which is precisely why they deserve attention.

There is another trap in reading the future: counting tonnes before they exist.

A project announcement is not supply. A resource estimate is not a reserve. A reserve is not a financed mine. A financed mine is not a commissioned mine. A commissioned mine is not necessarily a qualified source for the downstream customer that needs its material.

And a new mine can leave the old chokepoint intact.

The rare-earth chain makes this especially clear. The IEA’s 2026 outlook found that mining projects outside the dominant refining countries could provide nearly 50,000 tonnes of capacity by 2035. Planned refining and separation capacity outside those dominant countries was lower, under 40,000 tonnes. Planned downstream capacity in metals, alloys, and magnets was lower still, around 18,000 tonnes on a rare-earth-content basis.

If the ore comes from a new country but still has to pass through the old processor, the geological map has diversified more than the industrial one.

This is a recurring error in mineral policy. Governments announce a mine and speak as though they have announced a supply chain. But the chain contains concentrators, chemical conversion, separation, refining, cathodes, anodes, metals, alloys, magnets, specialized reagents, technical standards, customer qualification, and the mundane logistics that connect each step. A tonne in the wrong form or the wrong place is not interchangeable with the tonne a factory needs on Tuesday.

The future price of a mineral therefore depends partly on something commodity models struggle to summarize: the topology of the chain.

A market can be balanced and brittle at the same time.

The IEA has tried to make this visible with an N-1 stress test. Remove the largest supplier and its own demand, then ask how much of everyone else’s demand the remaining system can serve. In the agency’s 2025 analysis, graphite and rare earths that looked much less alarming in aggregate became acutely exposed: remaining supply covered only about 35 to 40 percent of remaining demand. Nickel was below 55 percent. Lithium and cobalt were around 65 percent.

The exact percentages will change. The method is more durable.

A world with 101 units of supply for 100 units of demand is not secure if 60 of those units depend on one country, one refining region, or one process that cannot be replaced quickly. The average annual price may look comfortable until the dominant route fails. Then the relevant price is not the global benchmark. It is the price available to the buyer who can no longer obtain material.

This is why some apparent inefficiency is rational.

A second supplier that costs more in normal years can have option value. So can inventory. So can spare refining capacity. So can a domestic processor that would lose a pure cost comparison against the dominant global plant. The mistake is to treat resilience as free. The opposite mistake is to treat every redundancy as waste simply because it is underused when nothing has gone wrong.

Insurance also looks inefficient when the house is not burning.

That analogy has limits. A government can insure against a house fire without changing the probability of fire very much. Mineral policy can change the market it is insuring against. Subsidizing a second refinery may lower concentration. Stockpiling may raise current demand. A purchase guarantee can keep a high-cost producer alive through a downturn. A tariff can protect a local processor while raising costs for manufacturers. Strategic policy enters the price system and becomes part of the price system.

This makes public spending another indicator worth watching carefully.

By 2025, public-finance commitments for critical-mineral projects in advanced economies had reached about $65 billion, more than four times their 2023 level. That sounds like diversification arriving at speed. The IEA also warned of a substantial gap between commitments and actual disbursements.

The gap is where strategy becomes reality or dissolves into press releases.

A grant announcement does not separate rare earths. A loan guarantee does not make battery-grade material. A memorandum of understanding does not survive a commodity downturn. The evidence that policy is working appears later: money is disbursed, construction starts, plants commission, output meets specification, customers sign contracts, and the facility keeps operating after prices move against it.

This is especially important because many diversification projects are structurally disadvantaged at first. They lack the scale, supplier networks, trained labor, customer familiarity, and accumulated process knowledge of incumbents. If governments decide that redundancy has strategic value, they may have to support it through the part of the cycle when ordinary market economics says to close it.

That can be sensible. It can also become a blank check.

Every subsidy program attracts projects able to speak the language of the program. A marginal mine becomes a national-security asset. A processor that cannot find customers becomes strategic capacity. A stockpile becomes resilience even when nobody has tested whether the stored material can enter the required production line. The word “critical” can weaken the normal discipline of asking whether an asset actually works.

So the indicator cannot be dollars committed. It has to be usable capacity delivered.

The same discipline applies to recycling.

Secondary supply will matter more as the stock of batteries, motors, cables, electronics, and infrastructure ages. But recycling should not be added to a forecast as though it were an independent gift. Collection depends on regulation and economics. Recovery depends on chemistry and scale. Recovered material must meet customer specifications. Cheap virgin material can make a recycler’s business model suddenly unattractive. And the geography of recycling can reproduce the concentration of primary refining.

Substitution is similarly endogenous. Lithium iron phosphate batteries did not wait politely outside the cobalt forecast. Their growing market share changed it. Motor designs can reduce rare-earth intensity. Smaller batteries can reduce material demand. Sodium-ion systems can shift pressure toward different inputs. Product engineers are participants in commodity markets even when they never trade a futures contract.

Any high-price scenario that holds technology, recycling, product design, and policy fixed is missing the most predictable consequence of a high price: people try to use less of the expensive thing.

That does not mean they succeed.

Copper is difficult to replace across many electrical uses without accepting losses in conductivity, weight, size, or system design. Some rare-earth magnets offer performance that substitutes struggle to match in compact applications. New chemistries can move dependence rather than eliminate it. Recycling cannot supply material that has not yet reached end of life. The existence of a response does not guarantee the response is large enough or fast enough.

But a forecast that deletes the response is not conservative. It is incomplete.

There are at least three futures worth keeping in view.

In the first, the mineral economy achieves something like managed abundance. Demand grows, but projects advance fast enough. Processing capacity diversifies. Recycling begins returning meaningful volumes. Product design lowers the intensity of the most exposed materials. Governments keep some strategic capacity alive. Prices still spike because commodity markets do that, but spikes do not become persistent physical shortages or permanent political leverage.

This is not a world of cheap everything. Resilience costs money. It is a world in which the system has enough alternative routes that a disruption remains a disruption rather than becoming a shutdown.

The second future is cheaper at first and more dangerous later.

A wave of supply arrives. Prices fall. High-cost projects are cancelled. Exploration budgets shrink. New refiners outside the dominant hubs cannot compete and close. Governments tire of subsidies. Recycling plants struggle for feedstock or margins. Demand keeps growing quietly underneath the surplus. Several years later, the industry discovers that the cheap period removed the very pipeline required to replace it.

This is the capital cycle’s cruelest trick. The warning arrives disguised as good news.

The third future contains enough tonnes but too few routes.

Mine supply looks adequate globally. Average prices look ordinary. Yet refining, reagents, processing technology, shipping, or one large exporter remains concentrated. A trade restriction or physical failure suddenly divides the world into buyers with access and buyers without it. The problem is not a shortage in the crust. It is the inability to move the right material through the right chain.

All three futures can coexist across different minerals.

Copper may face a genuine project-development problem while lithium cycles through periods of surplus. Rare earths may have adequate ore but concentrated separation and magnet capacity. Nickel can look abundant while the environmental and geographic profile of new supply creates political risk. Cobalt demand can be weakened by chemistry while export policy creates a short-run price shock anyway.

“The mineral market” is a convenient phrase for several systems with different geology, chemistry, capital cycles, and political structures.

This is why the book’s central analogy to oil has to end where it began: with caution. Oil taught governments to watch spare capacity, inventories, shipping routes, producer policy, and investment rather than treating the spot price as a complete description of security. Critical minerals demand a similar habit, but with more attention to processing stages and product design. A barrel of crude can be difficult to replace, but it does not become a different element when an engineer changes a cathode.

The useful forecast is therefore conditional.

A prolonged mineral crisis becomes more likely when demand keeps growing while investment weakens, projects fail to reach production, processing remains concentrated, inventories and spare capacity thin, recycling disappoints, and substitution arrives too slowly. It becomes less likely when those responses arrive before the system loses its margin for error.

This is not a refusal to predict. It is a prediction about mechanism rather than a number.

The mechanisms can be watched.

Watch exploration when prices are boring. Watch which announced projects actually secure financing. Watch whether processing grows where new mines grow. Watch whether customers qualify alternative suppliers. Watch secondary supply rather than recycling announcements. Watch whether public money is disbursed. Watch concentration at the stage that matters, not only at the mine. Watch inventories and spare capacity. Watch engineers when one material becomes painfully expensive.

Most of all, watch whether the system is acquiring a second route.

A commodity price can tell you that the first route is under pressure. The more important question for the next decade is what happens when it fails.

That question leads beyond the rush itself. A mineral economy cannot remain forever in the posture of emergency procurement, emergency subsidies, emergency permits, and emergency stockpiles. Eventually the equipment built during this boom begins to age. Batteries return from vehicles. Motors are replaced. Transmission lines are retired. New mines that were once speculative become ordinary suppliers. Some strategic projects fail. Others become boring infrastructure.

Then the question changes.

Not: what will copper cost in 2035?

What kind of system will exist around it?
