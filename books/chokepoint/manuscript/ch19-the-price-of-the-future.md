# The Price of the Future

A mine is an argument about a price that does not exist yet.

The argument begins years before the first sale. Geologists drill. Engineers turn a resource into a mine plan. Metallurgists test the ore. Lawyers assemble permits. A company negotiates power, water, roads, rail, ports, royalties, community agreements, financing, and contracts for equipment that may take years to arrive.

By the time a board is asked to approve several billion dollars, everybody in the room has a spreadsheet full of prices extending into a future nobody can see.

That is unavoidable. A copper mine cannot be financed by saying copper might be expensive or might be cheap. Lenders need a debt-service model. Shareholders need some idea of return. Governments need revenue assumptions.

Yet the price that makes the model work is one of the least controllable facts in it.

That is why the dangerous sentence in a commodity boom is often not *this time is different*. It is the quieter sentence underneath it: this price is normal.

The mineral economy keeps punishing that assumption. High prices invite investment, substitution, thrift, recycling, inventories, new technology, and political intervention. Low prices kill marginal projects, weaken exploration, discourage recycling, and make diversification look wasteful.

Price is not only news about the market.

It is an instruction to the market.

The future keeps changing because people are trying to prepare for it.

Consider the IEA's changing 2035 balances. In its 2025 outlook, the copper project pipeline under stated policies left an implied supply gap of roughly 30 percent. A year later, the 2026 outlook put the gap closer to 25 percent as projects advanced. Lithium's projected balance improved too. Cobalt moved the other way after the Democratic Republic of the Congo changed export policy and expected available production shifted.

The lesson is not that one forecast was wrong and the next one right.

A forecast is conditional.

A project that secures financing moves the line. A quota moves it. A battery chemistry moves it. A recession moves it. A new refinery, failed study, larger recycling stream, or government loan guarantee can all alter a balance that looked fixed when the previous report went to press.

That is why this book should not end with a table of 2035 prices. It would look precise and age quickly.

A better question is what evidence tells us which future is arriving.

Start with price, but do not stop there.

Between January 2025 and April 2026, according to the IEA, copper and other base-metal prices rose by about a third. Lithium more than doubled. Cobalt rose around 130 percent, driven largely by DRC restrictions. Those increases followed a period in which battery-metal prices had been pushed down by rapid supply growth in China, Indonesia, the Congo, and elsewhere even while demand continued expanding.

Both periods produced confident stories. Falling prices were taken as proof that scarcity fears were exaggerated. Rising prices became proof that scarcity had finally arrived.

Neither story was enough.

A low price can be the visible surface of a future shortage. If today's surplus causes producers to cancel exploration, defer processing plants, close high-cost mines, and abandon diversification, consumers enjoy cheap material while the next supply wave is quietly being removed.

A high price can be the visible surface of a future surplus. It can finance the mines, recovery systems, substitutes, and inventories that make the spike temporary.

The capital cycle is therefore more useful than the spot price alone.

In 2025, total investment in critical minerals fell 9 percent, the first substantial decline since 2020. Battery-material companies cut capital spending by more than 20 percent. Lithium-focused companies cut it by around 40 percent. Exploration spending fell by about 10 percent. Copper moved differently, with investment by copper-focused companies rising about 8 percent.

Those figures describe tomorrow's options being created or removed.

They should not be read as proof that every decline in investment is irrational underinvestment. Battery-metal markets had just lived through a wave of new supply and lower prices. Some projects deserved to be deferred. Some exploration dollars would have produced poor mines. New chemistries can also reduce the demand a forecast once assumed.

The useful warning signal is narrower: demand keeps growing, inventories and spare capacity are thinning, and the companies that must replace future supply are still not spending enough to do it.

The opposite signal matters too. Exploration stabilizes. Projects reach final investment decisions. Construction advances. Processing plants commission. Customers qualify new material. A second supplier survives a down-cycle without closing.

Those facts are duller than a price spike.

That is one reason they matter.

There is another trap: counting tonnes before they exist.

A project announcement is not supply. A resource estimate is not a reserve. A reserve is not a financed mine. A financed mine is not a commissioned mine. A commissioned mine is not necessarily qualified supply for the downstream customer that needs it.

And a new mine can leave the old chokepoint intact.

Rare earths make that clear. The IEA's 2026 outlook found mining projects outside dominant refining countries capable of providing nearly 50,000 tonnes by 2035. Planned refining and separation outside those dominant countries was lower, under 40,000 tonnes. Planned downstream capacity in metals, alloys, and magnets was lower still, around 18,000 tonnes on a rare-earth-content basis.

If ore comes from a new country but still passes through the old processor, the geological map has diversified more than the industrial one.

A tonne in the wrong form or the wrong place is not interchangeable with the tonne a factory needs on Tuesday.

This is why a market can look balanced and remain brittle.

The IEA has tried to make that visible with an N-1 stress test: remove the largest supplier and its own demand, then ask how much of everyone else's demand the remaining system can serve. In the agency's 2025 analysis, graphite and rare earths that looked less alarming in aggregate became acutely exposed, with remaining supply covering only about 35 to 40 percent of remaining demand. Nickel was below 55 percent. Lithium and cobalt were around 65 percent.

The exact percentages will change.

The method is more durable.

A world with 101 units of supply for 100 units of demand is not secure if 60 of those units depend on one country, one refining region, or one process that cannot be replaced quickly.

That is why some apparent inefficiency is rational.

A second supplier that costs more in normal years can have option value. So can inventory, spare refining capacity, or a domestic processor that loses a pure cost comparison against the incumbent.

Insurance also looks inefficient when the house is not burning.

The analogy is imperfect because mineral policy changes the market it is insuring against. Subsidizing a second refinery can lower concentration. Stockpiling raises current demand. A purchase guarantee can keep a high-cost producer alive through a downturn. A tariff can protect a processor while raising manufacturers' costs.

Strategic policy enters the price system and becomes part of the price system.

Public spending therefore has to be judged by what it delivers rather than what it announces.

By 2025, public-finance commitments for critical-mineral projects in advanced economies had reached about $65 billion, more than four times their 2023 level. The IEA also warned of a substantial gap between commitments and actual disbursements.

That gap is where strategy becomes reality or dissolves into press releases.

A grant announcement does not separate rare earths. A memorandum does not make battery-grade material. The evidence appears later: money is disbursed, construction starts, plants commission, output meets specification, customers sign contracts, and the facility remains alive after the price cycle turns against it.

This is where support can be sensible and where it can become a blank check.

A marginal mine can call itself a national-security asset. A processor without customers can call itself strategic capacity. A stockpile can be called resilience even when nobody has tested whether the stored material can enter the production line that supposedly needs it.

The useful metric is not dollars committed.

It is usable capacity delivered.

Recycling and substitution have to be treated with the same discipline. Secondary supply depends on collection, chemistry, scale, qualification, and economics. Cheap virgin material can damage a recycler's business model. Recycling can also reproduce primary-processing concentration if recovered material still travels through the same conversion hubs.

Substitution is endogenous too. LFP did not wait politely outside the cobalt forecast. Its growing market share changed the forecast. Motor designers can reduce rare-earth intensity. Sodium-ion can shift pressure toward different materials. Engineers participate in commodity markets even when they never trade a futures contract.

Any high-price scenario that holds technology, recycling, product design, and policy fixed deletes the most predictable response to a high price: people try to use less of the expensive thing.

That does not mean they succeed on schedule.

Copper is difficult to replace in many electrical uses without accepting losses in conductivity, weight, size, or system design. Some rare-earth magnets deliver performance substitutes struggle to match in compact applications. Recycling cannot recover material that has not reached end of life. A new chemistry can move dependence instead of reducing it.

A forecast that ignores response is incomplete.

A forecast that assumes response always wins is fantasy.

The useful distinction is whether the system is acquiring alternatives before its margin for error disappears.

One future looks like managed abundance. Demand grows, but projects advance fast enough. Processing diversifies. Recycling begins returning meaningful volumes. Product design lowers exposure to the most difficult materials. Governments keep some strategic capacity alive. Prices still spike because commodity markets do that, but the spike does not become a prolonged physical shutdown.

Another future is cheaper at first and more dangerous later. Supply arrives, prices fall, high-cost projects are cancelled, exploration shrinks, new refiners outside dominant hubs close, and governments tire of subsidies. Demand keeps growing under the surplus. Years later, the market discovers that the cheap period removed the pipeline it assumed would replace aging supply.

The warning arrives disguised as good news.

A third future contains enough tonnes but too few routes. Mine supply looks adequate globally. Average prices look ordinary. Yet refining, reagents, processing equipment, shipping, or one large exporter remains concentrated. A trade restriction or plant failure suddenly divides the world into buyers with access and buyers without it.

All three futures can coexist across different minerals.

Copper can face a project-development problem while lithium cycles through surplus. Rare earths can have adequate ore and concentrated processing. Nickel can be abundant while its environmental and geographic profile creates political risk. Cobalt demand can weaken structurally while export policy still creates a short-run shock.

There is no single mineral future.

There are several systems moving on different clocks.

That is the final lesson to borrow from oil. Energy security eventually taught governments to watch spare capacity, inventories, shipping routes, producer policy, and investment rather than treating the spot price as a complete description of security.

Critical minerals require the same habit with additional attention to processing stages, qualification, recycling, and product design.

The mechanisms can be watched.

Watch exploration when prices are boring. Watch which announced projects actually secure financing. Watch whether processing grows where mining grows. Watch whether customers qualify alternative suppliers. Watch secondary supply rather than recycling announcements. Watch whether public money is disbursed. Watch concentration at the stage that matters, not only at the mine. Watch inventories and spare capacity. Watch what engineers do when one material becomes painfully expensive.

Most of all, watch whether the system is acquiring a second route.

A commodity price can tell you the first route is under pressure.

The more important question is what kind of system will exist around it when that route fails.
