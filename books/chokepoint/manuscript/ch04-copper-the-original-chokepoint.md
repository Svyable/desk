# Copper: The Original Chokepoint

On July 31, 2025, the ground moved inside El Teniente.

The copper mine descends beneath the Andes south of Santiago, a place where generations of miners have followed ore bodies through a mountain that has been worked for more than a century. The mine is so large that maps of it resemble maps of cities: levels, access routes, production sectors, tunnels disappearing into a volume of rock most people will never see.

That afternoon, a seismic event and rockburst tore through part of the underground operation. Six workers died.

A year later, Codelco, Chile's state-owned copper company, named them again in a public commemoration and described the accident as one of the most painful days in its recent history. Production records carried the same event in another language. El Teniente's output fell 13 percent in 2025, to about 310,000 tonnes, and Codelco said the fatal accident was a major reason.

Human loss became a line in a supply table.

That is not a comfortable sentence.

It is how mining works.

Every tonne in a commodity forecast comes from a physical operation where rock must be drilled, blasted, hauled, crushed, ground, concentrated, transported, smelted or leached, refined, and delivered by people and machines working inside geological conditions that cannot be negotiated with.

Copper is the best metal for remembering this because it is so familiar that its physical origins almost disappear.

A copper wire looks simple.

A copper mine is not.

The world produced roughly 23 million tonnes of mined copper in 2025. Chile alone produced about 5.3 million tonnes, according to the U.S. Geological Survey's 2026 Mineral Commodity Summaries. That made one country responsible for something close to a quarter of global mine output.

Chile's position is powerful, but it does not resemble China's rare-earth position.

Other major copper producers include the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Peru, China, Indonesia, the United States, Australia, Zambia, Russia, Mexico, and Kazakhstan. Copper refining is also geographically distributed, even though China has become the dominant refiner by a wide margin.

If concentration alone created a chokepoint, copper would be a weaker case than rare earths.

The problem is time.

Copper deposits are abundant enough that the world is not facing geological exhaustion. The issue is converting identified resources into productive mines quickly enough, cheaply enough, and with enough political legitimacy to meet demand.

The International Energy Agency's 2026 critical-minerals outlook projects that copper supply from the current project pipeline could fall about 25 percent short of demand in 2035. That is an outlook, not a prophecy. Last year's edition had put the implied gap closer to 30 percent, and the forecast improved as projects advanced.

That revision should make anyone suspicious of a straight line drawn ten years into the future.

It should not make the constraint disappear.

The more durable evidence is what sits underneath the forecast: declining ore grades, rising capital intensity, fewer large discoveries, long development schedules, and increasingly complex projects.

The IEA has noted that average copper ore grades have fallen sharply over recent decades. Lower grade means more rock must be moved and processed to produce the same amount of copper. More rock means more equipment, energy, water, tailings, labor, maintenance, and capital.

A price chart does not show any of that.

A price chart says copper is expensive.

A mine plan asks what has to be built before the expensive copper exists.

That gap between price signal and physical response is the copper chokepoint.

Economics textbooks teach that high prices call forth supply.

They do.

They just do not say how long the phone rings.

S&P Global's mine-development work has found that large discoveries can take well over a decade to become operating mines. The IEA has put the typical copper project lead time at around seventeen years from discovery to production. That number should not be treated as a law. Some projects move faster. Brownfield expansions at existing mines can add supply more quickly. A deposit with roads, power, water, permits, community agreements, and experienced operators has a different clock from a remote greenfield mine.

But the broad point survives variation.

The demand signal can arrive much faster than the supply response.

An AI company can announce a data-center campus this year.

A utility can decide to accelerate transmission investment this year.

An automaker can change its electric-vehicle forecast this year.

A government can pass a defense bill this year.

A copper mine discovered today is not a mine next year.

This makes copper a peculiar kind of strategic material. It is neither a tiny specialty mineral nor an irreplaceable fuel. It is a giant industrial market with thousands of uses, liquid trading, established recycling, and many producers.

Yet the sheer scale of new demand means even a diversified market can become difficult to expand.

The metal sits everywhere electricity moves.

Copper conducts efficiently, resists corrosion, bends without breaking, and can be drawn into wire. It is difficult to electrify an economy without using more of it somewhere.

A data center needs copper in power distribution, busbars, cabling, cooling systems, transformers, substations, and the grid feeding the site. An electric vehicle contains more copper than a comparable internal-combustion vehicle. A wind farm needs copper inside generators and cables and in the transmission connecting it to load. A solar farm needs cabling and grid connections. A heat pump uses electric motors and wiring. A conventional building already contains copper before any of those technologies arrive.

This last point matters.

Copper's new demand is layered onto an old civilization.

The world is not beginning with an empty wiring closet.

Cities continue building apartments. Factories continue replacing motors. Telecommunications networks continue expanding. Developing economies continue urbanizing. Water systems, railways, hospitals, ports, appliances, and ordinary power grids continue consuming copper whether or not an electric vehicle is ever sold.

The strategic demand story therefore does not depend on one transition succeeding perfectly.

If EV adoption slows, grids can still need copper.

If AI investment cools, construction can still need copper.

If wind development stalls in one country, power demand can rise somewhere else.

This diversity reduces the risk that copper becomes a pure boom commodity tied to one technology.

It also makes supply expansion harder to target.

Lithium producers can look at the battery market and know what they are largely betting on.

Copper miners are betting on the physical economy.

Chile made that bet a national project long before anyone used the phrase energy transition.

The country has lived with copper as wealth, politics, foreign influence, labor, nationalism, and fiscal revenue for generations.

In the 1960s, the Chilean state increased its ownership of major foreign-controlled mines through a process known as Chileanization. In July 1971, under President Salvador Allende, Chile's Congress unanimously approved the constitutional change that completed nationalization of large-scale copper mining.

The compensation fight became as important as the transfer of ownership.

Chile's government argued that compensation should deduct what it called excessive past profits. Sebastian Edwards' recent historical work describes how that calculation reduced compensation for the largest American companies to zero and, in the Chilean accounting, left some of them owing the state rather than being owed by it.

The episode is useful here not because this book needs another argument about Allende.

It shows that copper had already become something more than a commodity in Chile.

Control over the mine was control over national income and national autonomy.

Decades later, the same tension lives inside Codelco.

A state-owned miner is expected to do contradictory things at once.

It must behave like a commercial mining company: control costs, maintain equipment, invest before production declines, manage geological risk, keep workers safe, and deliver projects on time.

It is also expected to behave like a national institution: provide fiscal revenue, sustain employment, embody sovereignty over the resource, support industrial policy, and sometimes carry political expectations a private operator could reject.

The company cannot distribute today's cash to the treasury and invest the same cash underground tomorrow.

That tradeoff becomes severe in an aging mining system.

Codelco reported its own 2025 production at 1,334,445 tonnes, only slightly above 2024. Its total attributable production was higher when stakes in other mines were included. The company also reported EBITDA of $6.67 billion and a consolidated profit of $2.423 billion.

Those figures do not describe a company on the edge of disappearance.

They describe a company trying to stabilize output while some of its most important mines become technically harder and more capital intensive.

El Teniente is the human face of that difficulty, but it is not the only one.

Chuquicamata, another of Chile's great copper names, has had to transition from a vast open pit toward underground mining as the accessible geometry changed. Lower grades and deeper operations increase the engineering burden. Structural projects require billions of dollars before they produce replacement tonnes.

The word replacement is important.

Mining companies often spend enormous sums merely to prevent output from falling.

A mine is a wasting asset.

Every year of production removes some of the ore that justified the investment. To keep annual output flat, operators must open new areas, move more material, improve recoveries, expand plants, extend mine lives, or build the next project before the old one declines.

Capital expenditure that looks like growth from outside can be a fight against depletion from inside.

This is why copper forecasts can mislead investors and policymakers in opposite directions.

A high copper price can create the impression that producers are minting money and will expand rapidly.

A long-run deficit forecast can create the impression that every copper project is automatically valuable.

Neither is true.

Projects compete on ore grade, strip ratio, metallurgy, water availability, power cost, infrastructure, labor, tax, political stability, community relationships, permitting, and the price assumptions used to finance billions of dollars that may not be repaid for years.

A mine can sit on a world-class deposit and still be a bad investment at the wrong capital cost.

A small improvement in price can transform project economics.

A small deterioration in grade can erase the improvement.

The copper market sees tonnes.

The project sees a unique piece of geology.

This uniqueness is another way the oil analogy breaks down.

Oil fields differ, but crude can be priced through well-developed benchmarks and moved through a global refining and transport system. Copper concentrates vary in grade and impurities; smelters charge treatment and refining fees based on market conditions and material quality. Some concentrates contain arsenic or other elements that complicate processing. Cathode has different logistics from concentrate. Scrap has different economics again.

The strategic question is not simply how much copper exists.

It is what form exists, where, and how quickly the next stage can use it.

Escondida offers the countercase to the story of inevitable decline.

The mine in Chile's Atacama Desert is the world's largest copper producer. BHP owns 57.5 percent. In its 2025 annual report, BHP said Escondida achieved its highest production in seventeen years, up 16 percent from the prior year, helped by record concentrator throughput, improved recoveries, higher feed grade, and first production from a new leaching project.

Old mines can improve.

Existing districts can surprise to the upside.

Brownfield investment can arrive faster than a brand-new mine.

Technology can unlock material previously treated as waste or low-value inventory.

The copper shortage is therefore not a simple race between demand and a fixed geological ceiling.

It is a race between demand and the industry's ability to keep making old rocks economic.

That is a more hopeful story than depletion.

It is also a more capital-intensive one.

As mines get deeper and grades fall, engineering can preserve output by processing more material, automating equipment, improving recovery, reworking waste, and extending underground access. These responses consume money, energy, water, equipment, and time.

The paradox is that high copper prices make those investments more attractive while also making copper-intensive projects elsewhere more expensive.

The cure raises the symptom.

Copper's current strategic status has drawn governments deeper into the market.

The United States added copper to its critical-minerals list in 2025. It has also used national-security trade law to impose tariffs on certain semi-finished copper products and copper-intensive derivatives. Europe classifies copper as both critical and strategic under its Critical Raw Materials Act.

None of this means copper suddenly became rare.

It means governments no longer assume the global market will always deliver enough of the right material at the right time without policy attention.

That assumption deserves scrutiny, but policy can create its own chokepoints.

A tariff may encourage domestic fabrication while raising costs for users.

A domestic-content rule may create demand before domestic mines or smelters can respond.

A faster permitting system may accelerate supply while weakening trust if communities believe review has become ceremonial.

A subsidy can finance useful redundancy or merely protect an uncompetitive project.

Security policy is not exempt from tradeoffs just because the word critical appears in the statute.

The environmental tradeoff is especially sharp in copper because the volumes are so large.

A rare specialty mineral can have severe local impacts, but a metal used by the tens of millions of tonnes requires mining landscapes on a different scale.

Lower ore grades mean larger waste and tailings streams per unit of copper. Arid districts can create conflict over water. New mines need roads, power, ports, and worker settlements. Tailings facilities remain for generations after the revenue has been spent.

The same copper that enables lower-carbon power systems can create immediate environmental burdens where it is extracted.

There is no contradiction in that sentence.

There is a supply chain.

This is where the language of the energy transition can become morally lazy. A clean technology is clean at the point where it performs its advertised service. Its materials arrived from somewhere else.

The somewhere else does not disappear because the end product has no tailpipe.

Chile has lived with this displacement for a century. So have mining regions in Peru, Zambia, the Congo, the United States, and elsewhere. The mineral age will ask them to produce more while consuming countries congratulate themselves for becoming cleaner.

That bargain can work only if producing regions receive enough value and enough institutional capacity to accept the costs.

The alternative is delay.

Delay is not always irrational obstruction.

A community living beside a proposed mine sees risks that a national demand forecast does not. Water may be local. Tailings are local. Road traffic is local. Cultural damage is local. The projected climate benefit may arrive in another country.

From the consumer's perspective, the permitting process is a bottleneck.

From the community's perspective, the permitting process may be the only moment when the outside world has to listen.

A useful chokepoint theory has to hold both views at once.

If it treats every delay as a defect, it becomes an argument for bulldozing legitimacy.

If it treats every local objection as an absolute veto, it becomes an argument for pretending strategic demand can be met without physical projects.

The real problem is institutional time: how to make difficult decisions credibly enough that a mine can proceed, change, or be rejected without consuming the better part of a generation in uncertainty.

Copper exposes that problem because the world already knows how to mine it.

We are not waiting for a scientific breakthrough.

We are waiting for projects.

The IEA's deficit numbers will move again. Prices will move first. Some demand forecasts will be cut. Recycling will grow. Aluminum will substitute for copper in applications where engineering and economics permit it. New mines in Africa and South America will advance. Existing mines will extend their lives. A recession could make today's shortage talk look melodramatic.

All of that should be expected.

The chokepoint is not the forecasted number.

It is the lag between a changing world and a mining system that can only change at the speed of geology, engineering, finance, politics, and human tolerance.

El Teniente makes that lag visible.

The mine has operated for more than a century and still contains enormous resources. Its problem is not that copper vanished from the mountain.

Its problem is that reaching the next tonne becomes a new engineering act.

That act can succeed.

It can also fail catastrophically.

The workers who died in July 2025 are a reminder that every optimistic supply curve ultimately passes through a real place underground.

Copper is called the original chokepoint because civilization has depended on it for so long.

The more important lesson is that familiarity does not make supply elastic.

If anything, maturity can conceal the work required to keep an old system growing.

The next metal in this story created the opposite illusion.

Lithium looked new, scarce, and almost magical when battery demand exploded. Its price boom made fortunes, pulled capital into salt flats and hard-rock mines, and encouraged a generation of forecasts about permanent shortage.

Then the price collapsed.

That is what the capital cycle does when scarcity meets investment.
