# Copper: The Original Chokepoint

On July 31, 2025, the ground moved inside El Teniente.

The copper mine descends beneath the Andes south of Santiago, a place where generations of miners have followed ore bodies through a mountain worked for more than a century. The operation is so large that its maps resemble maps of cities: levels, access routes, production sectors, tunnels disappearing into rock most people will never see.

That afternoon, a seismic event and rockburst tore through part of the mine. Six workers died.

A year later, Codelco, Chile's state-owned copper company, named them again in a public commemoration and described the accident as one of the most painful days in its recent history. Production records carried the same event in another language. El Teniente's output fell 13 percent in 2025, to about 310,000 tonnes, and Codelco said the fatal accident was a major reason.

Human loss became a line in a supply table.

That is not a comfortable sentence. It is how mining works.

Every tonne in a commodity forecast comes from a physical operation where rock must be drilled, blasted, hauled, crushed, ground, concentrated, transported, smelted or leached, refined, and delivered by people and machines working inside geological conditions that cannot be negotiated with.

Copper is the best metal for remembering this because it is so familiar that its physical origins almost disappear.

A copper wire looks simple.

A copper mine is not.

The world produced roughly 23 million tonnes of mined copper in 2025. Chile produced about 5.3 million tonnes, according to the U.S. Geological Survey's 2026 Mineral Commodity Summaries—close to a quarter of global mine output.

Chile's position is powerful, but copper is not rare-earths-by-another-name. Major production also comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Peru, China, Indonesia, the United States, Australia, Zambia, Russia, Mexico, and Kazakhstan. Refining is geographically broader too, although China has become the dominant refiner by a wide margin.

If concentration were the whole story, copper would be one of the weaker cases in this book.

The problem is time.

Copper deposits are not disappearing from the planet. The constraint is converting known resources into productive mines quickly enough, cheaply enough, and with enough political legitimacy to meet demand.

The International Energy Agency's 2026 critical-minerals outlook projects that the current copper project pipeline could leave supply about 25 percent short of demand in 2035. That is an outlook, not a prophecy. The previous edition put the implied gap closer to 30 percent, and the estimate improved as projects advanced.

Forecasts move because projects move.

The more durable evidence sits underneath the number: declining ore grades at many mature mines, higher capital intensity, fewer large discoveries, and long development schedules.

Lower grade means more rock for the same tonne of metal. More rock means more equipment, energy, water, tailings, labor, maintenance, and capital.

A price chart says copper is expensive.

A mine plan asks what must exist before the expensive copper does.

That is the copper chokepoint.

High prices call forth supply, but not on one clock. Brownfield expansions can move faster than remote greenfield mines. A known deposit with roads, power, water, permits, community agreements, and experienced operators is not equivalent to an early-stage prospect that has only begun drilling.

The IEA has put the typical copper project lead time at around seventeen years from discovery to production. The number should not be turned into a law or a permitting slogan. Discovery, feasibility work, metallurgy, environmental review, financing, construction, and commissioning all sit inside it.

The important mismatch is simpler.

An AI company can announce a data-center campus this year. A utility can accelerate transmission investment this year. An automaker can change its electric-vehicle forecast this year. A government can pass a defense bill this year.

A copper discovery this year is not a mine next year.

That lag matters because copper demand is layered onto an old civilization rather than tied to one new technology. Cities still build apartments. Factories still replace motors. Telecommunications networks still expand. Developing economies still urbanize. Water systems, railways, hospitals, ports, appliances, and conventional power grids consume copper whether or not an electric vehicle is sold.

Then electrification adds more.

A data center needs copper in power distribution, busbars, cabling, cooling systems, transformers, substations, and the grid feeding the site. An electric vehicle contains more copper than a comparable internal-combustion vehicle. Wind and solar projects need generators, cabling, and grid connections. Heat pumps use electric motors and wiring.

Copper's strategic demand story therefore does not depend on one transition forecast being exactly right. If EV adoption slows, grids can still need copper. If AI investment cools, construction can still need copper. The metal is a bet on the physical economy itself.

Chile made that bet a national project long before anyone used the phrase energy transition.

In the 1960s, the state increased its ownership of major foreign-controlled mines through Chileanization. In July 1971, under President Salvador Allende, Congress unanimously approved the constitutional change that completed nationalization of large-scale copper mining.

The compensation fight became part of the story. Chile's government argued that compensation should deduct what it called excessive past profits. Sebastian Edwards' recent historical work describes how that calculation reduced compensation for the largest American companies to zero and, in Chilean accounting, left some of them owing the state.

Whatever one's view of the politics, the episode shows how early copper had become more than an export commodity. Control over the mine was bound up with national income and national autonomy.

That tension still lives inside Codelco.

A state-owned miner must behave like a mining company: control costs, maintain equipment, invest before production declines, manage geological risk, keep workers safe, and deliver projects. It is also expected to behave like a national institution: provide fiscal revenue, sustain employment, embody sovereignty over the resource, and carry political expectations a private operator can reject.

The company cannot distribute today's cash to the treasury and invest the same cash underground tomorrow.

That tradeoff becomes severe in an aging mining system.

Codelco reported 2025 production of 1,334,445 tonnes, only slightly above 2024, alongside EBITDA of $6.67 billion and consolidated profit of $2.423 billion. Those are not the figures of a company vanishing. They describe a company spending heavily to keep a mature system from slipping backward.

A mine is a wasting asset. Each year of production removes some of the ore that justified the investment. Holding output flat can require new underground access, extensions, plant upgrades, better recovery, or an entirely new project.

Capital expenditure that looks like growth from outside can be a fight against depletion from inside.

Chuquicamata makes that visible as its famous open pit transitions toward underground mining. El Teniente makes it visible in a mountain where each new production area is another engineering problem.

Escondida provides the necessary countercase.

The Atacama mine, the world's largest copper producer, achieved its highest production in seventeen years in BHP's 2025 reporting. Output rose 16 percent, helped by record concentrator throughput, better recoveries, higher feed grade, and first production from a new leaching project.

Old mines can improve. Existing districts can surprise to the upside. Brownfield capital can arrive faster than a brand-new mine. Technology can recover material that previous economics left behind.

The copper problem is therefore not depletion against a fixed geological ceiling. It is a race between demand and the industry's ability to keep making old rocks economic while bringing new rocks through a long project pipeline.

That distinction matters for policy because copper's strategic label has drawn governments deeper into the market. The United States added copper to its critical-minerals list in 2025 and used national-security trade law to impose tariffs on certain semi-finished copper products and copper-intensive derivatives. Europe classifies copper as both critical and strategic.

None of that makes every copper project wise.

A tariff can encourage domestic fabrication while raising costs for users. A domestic-content rule can create demand before mines or smelters can respond. Faster permitting can accelerate supply while weakening trust if communities conclude that review has become ceremonial. A subsidy can finance useful redundancy or protect a bad project.

The environmental bargain is especially sharp because copper is a bulk metal. Lower grades can mean larger waste and tailings streams per unit of product. Arid districts face water constraints. New mines require roads, power, ports, and worker settlements. Tailings remain long after the revenue has been spent.

The same copper that enables lower-carbon power systems can create immediate environmental burdens where it is extracted.

There is no contradiction in that sentence. There is a supply chain.

A community beside a proposed mine sees risks that a national demand forecast does not. Water is local. Tailings are local. Road traffic is local. Cultural damage is local. The projected climate or industrial benefit may arrive somewhere else.

From a consuming economy's perspective, permitting can look like delay. From a community's perspective, it may be the only moment when the outside world has to listen.

A workable copper system has to hold both facts at once. Treat every delay as a defect and mineral security becomes an argument for bulldozing legitimacy. Treat every local objection as an absolute veto and strategic demand becomes an abstraction disconnected from physical projects.

The hard problem is institutional time: making difficult decisions credibly enough that a mine can proceed, change, or be rejected without spending the better part of a generation in uncertainty.

Copper exposes that problem because the world already knows how to mine it. We are not waiting for a scientific breakthrough. We are waiting for projects, expansions, upgrades, financing, permits, and operating competence.

The IEA's deficit estimate will move again. Prices will move first. Some demand forecasts will be cut. Recycling will grow. Aluminum will substitute where engineering permits it. New mines in Africa and South America will advance. Existing mines will extend their lives. A recession could make shortage talk look melodramatic.

The chokepoint is not the forecasted number.

It is the lag between a changing economy and a mining system that can only change at the speed of geology, engineering, finance, politics, and human tolerance.

El Teniente makes that lag visible. The mountain still contains enormous resources after more than a century of mining. The problem is not that copper vanished.

Reaching the next tonne is another engineering act.

Sometimes that act succeeds spectacularly. Sometimes it fails catastrophically.

The workers who died in July 2025 are a reminder that every optimistic supply curve eventually passes through a real place underground.

Copper's familiarity can conceal the work required to keep an old system growing.

Lithium created the opposite illusion. Its price boom made a relatively small market look permanently scarce, pulled capital into salt flats and hard-rock mines, and inspired forecasts of shortages that seemed almost structural.

Then supply arrived.
