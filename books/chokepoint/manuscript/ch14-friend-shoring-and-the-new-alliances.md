# Friend-Shoring and the New Alliances

A supply chain can be diversified on a map long before it is diversified in a factory.

That is the problem with friend-shoring.

The phrase sounds geographic. Move a mine here. Finance a refinery there. Sign an agreement with a country whose government is less likely to use exports as leverage.

Then the ore arrives.

It still needs to be concentrated, converted, separated, refined, qualified, financed, insured, transported, and sold into a market in which the incumbent supplier may be cheaper.

The alliance can be signed in an afternoon.

The plant takes years.

By 2026, the United States, the European Union, Japan, Australia, Canada, South Korea, and other partners had spent several years trying to build policy around that mismatch. The effort produced real money, projects, rules, and institutions.

It did not produce independence.

That is partly because independence is the wrong target.

Modern mineral chains are too complicated for every industrial country to reproduce every stage domestically. Australia has geology and mining expertise. Japan has materials science and manufacturing depth. Canada has deposits, hydropower, and access to the North American market. Europe has industrial demand and regulatory power. The United States has capital, research institutions, defense demand, and a huge consumer market.

None has everything in the right proportions for every mineral.

Friend-shoring is therefore less like bringing production home than building a club whose members decide that dependence on one another is preferable to dependence on one dominant outside supplier.

That is a profound change from the commercial logic that built the old system.

For decades, processors and manufacturers had little reason to pay more for geographic redundancy if Chinese material met specification, arrived reliably, and cost less. Governments are now paying for optionality the market once treated as waste.

The shift became visible in the Inflation Reduction Act's clean-vehicle rules. Under the original critical-minerals requirement, a rising share of battery critical-mineral value had to be extracted or processed in the United States or a qualifying free-trade partner, or recycled in North America. The statutory schedule rose from forty percent in 2023 to eighty percent thereafter.

Later tax legislation changed the details and the consumer credit itself, a reminder that industrial policy is not politically permanent. But the design mattered.

Washington was no longer merely subsidizing an electric car.

It was asking where the atoms came from.

That question immediately made the word friend awkward. The United States has formal free-trade agreements with some major mineral producers and not others. The European Union is an ally but not a conventional U.S. FTA partner. Japan negotiated a critical-minerals agreement partly to bridge that gap.

And real supply chains refuse to fit diplomatic shorthand. Ore can be mined in Australia, converted in China, incorporated into a cathode in South Korea, assembled into a cell in the United States, and installed in a vehicle in Mexico. Ownership can cross the map again.

Which step determines the nationality of the material?

That legal question is not secondary. It decides which projects qualify for subsidies and which supply chains count as diversified.

The Minerals Security Partnership, created in 2022, attacks another part of the problem. Mining and processing projects outside the dominant system often fail for reasons that have little to do with geology: a road, port, power supply, bankable offtake contract, political-risk insurance, environmental agreement, or community relationship can be the binding constraint.

A diplomatic initiative can help assemble those pieces across borders.

It cannot repeal the capital cycle.

Europe's Critical Raw Materials Act makes the scale problem more explicit. It set 2030 benchmarks for strategic raw materials: at least ten percent of EU consumption from EU extraction, forty percent from EU processing, twenty-five percent from recycling, while seeking to keep dependence on any single third country below sixty-five percent at a relevant processing stage.

In March 2025, the European Commission approved forty-seven strategic projects inside the Union. In June it added thirteen outside the EU. The second call, closing in January 2026, attracted more than 160 applications.

That is evidence of movement, not completion.

A strategic designation can bring permitting attention, financing guidance, and political support. It does not build a concentrator, create engineers, supply reagents, connect a grid, settle local opposition, or guarantee commodity prices high enough to service the debt.

Europe's 2026 Raw Materials Mechanism moves closer to the commercial bottleneck by trying to connect buyers, suppliers, financiers, storage providers, and strategic projects.

This sounds less dramatic than opening a mine.

It may be more useful.

A new supplier needs a buyer. An incumbent already has one. A long-term offtake agreement can turn future demand into present financing. Government-backed credit can absorb risks private lenders will not. Demand aggregation can make a fragmented customer base bankable.

Friend-shoring becomes credible when it reaches this level of boring detail.

The United States has moved there too, funding projects across unconventional feedstocks, rare-earth separation, battery processing, manufacturing, and recycling rather than treating the mine as the whole problem.

The policy is beginning to resemble the chain.

That creates another problem: strategically desirable capacity can be commercially fragile.

A new separator or refinery competes with an incumbent ecosystem that already has customers, suppliers, operating knowledge, and scale. The Pentagon's 2025 agreement with MP Materials, including a ten-year price floor for neodymium-praseodymium oxide and long-term support for magnet output, admits this plainly.

If a country wants a second supplier only during emergencies, the second supplier has to survive normal times.

That is an insurance problem.

Redundant industrial capacity looks inefficient in years when imports are cheap and reliable. Unlike a fire extinguisher, however, a refinery employs people, consumes capital, competes every day, and can go bankrupt before the emergency arrives.

Governments can keep it alive through grants, loans, tax credits, procurement guarantees, price floors, tariffs, stockpiles, or diversification rules.

Every instrument moves the cost somewhere.

The taxpayer pays. The consumer pays. The downstream manufacturer pays. Or the supplier carries the risk and may not build.

Friend-shoring is therefore a decision about who pays for spare capacity.

That becomes politically difficult when the disruption being insured against is invisible. A factory manager sees the higher price of a non-Chinese input today. The shortage avoided three years from now cannot be booked on the income statement.

This is why mineral policy accelerates after shocks. Japan diversified after 2010 because dependence became tangible. Europe rebuilt parts of its energy strategy after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The United States moved more aggressively on magnets after Chinese controls revealed how quickly licensing could become leverage.

Crisis makes insurance easier to sell.

Then prices fall, urgency fades, and projects struggle.

That cycle may be friend-shoring's greatest enemy. Mineral projects require policy consistency across electoral cycles shorter than mine-development cycles. A refinery approved under one administration may commission under another. A tax credit can disappear before a project reaches full production.

China's advantage is often described as patience. Some of that patience is institutional continuity.

Allies also have interests of their own.

Indonesia wants downstream industry, not a return to exporting cheap ore. Australia wants more processing at home. African governments increasingly want local value addition. European communities may support strategic autonomy in principle and oppose a mine nearby. The United States wants secure supply and domestic jobs, which can conflict with the development ambitions of partner countries.

A durable alliance cannot mean one country keeps the clean manufacturing while another keeps the pit and the waste.

The bargain has to distribute value as well as risk.

That matters because many mineral-rich countries remember an earlier order in which resources left, profits left, and environmental liabilities stayed. Calling the new arrangement a partnership does not erase that history.

The strongest projects will probably be those where the host country can point to something beyond export volume: processing capacity, infrastructure, skilled jobs, tax revenue, technology, or an industry that survives the first mine.

That makes projects slower and more complicated.

It may also make them durable.

Friend-shoring contains one final trap. Done badly, it can replace one visible concentration with a hidden one inside the alliance.

If every country subsidizes battery assembly but nobody builds precursor capacity, the chain remains exposed. If several governments fund magnet factories that all depend on the same separator, the redundancy is downstream theater. If projects share one equipment supplier, port, reagent, or financing channel, the chokepoint has merely moved out of view.

A resilient network needs diversity by function, not just flags on a map.

That is why strategic-project lists, financing mechanisms, and bilateral agreements should be treated as scaffolding. Some will become operating plants. Some will become press releases preserved after the economics changed. Some will survive because public support carried them across the dangerous early years. Others will fail because geology disappointed, costs rose, the process did not scale, or commodity prices collapsed.

Friend-shoring does not need to replace China everywhere to work.

It needs to make the next unit of supply available from somewhere else often enough that a licensing restriction, political dispute, or plant outage loses some of its power.

That is a lower bar than independence and a higher bar than an alliance announcement.

The distance between those bars is measured in operating mines, qualified chemicals, bankable offtake, working refineries, trained people, and years.

Which raises the question behind the entire policy response.

If the United States and Europe are now spending billions trying to recreate pieces of an industrial system that concentrated elsewhere, how did that concentration happen in the first place?

The tempting answer is a famous line about the Middle East having oil and China having rare earths.

The next chapter asks whether that line can carry as much history as people have loaded onto it.
