# Friend-Shoring and the New Alliances

A supply chain can be diversified on a map long before it is diversified in a factory.

That is the problem with friend-shoring.

The phrase sounds geographic. Move a mine here. Finance a refinery there. Sign an agreement with a country whose government is less likely to use exports as leverage. Replace one dangerous dependency with several safer ones.

Then the ore arrives.

It still needs to be concentrated, converted, separated, refined, qualified, financed, insured, transported, and sold into a market in which the incumbent supplier may be cheaper.

The alliance can be signed in an afternoon.

The plant takes years.

By 2026, the United States, the European Union, Japan, Australia, Canada, South Korea, and other partners had spent several years trying to build a mineral policy around that mismatch. The effort has produced real money, real projects, new rules, and a vocabulary that barely existed when the rare-earth shock of 2010 first made governments notice how much industrial capacity had migrated into one country.

It has not produced independence.

That is partly because independence is the wrong target.

Modern mineral supply chains are too complicated for every industrial country to reproduce every stage domestically. Australia has geology and mining expertise. Japan has materials science and manufacturing depth. Canada has deposits, hydropower, and access to the North American market. The European Union has large industrial demand and regulatory power. The United States has capital, research institutions, defense demand, and a large consumer market.

None has all of those things in the right proportions for every mineral.

Friend-shoring is therefore less like bringing production home than building a club whose members agree that dependence on one another is preferable to dependence on a potential adversary.

That sounds modest.

It is a profound change from the economic logic that built the old system.

For decades, a processor that could buy cheaper material from China had little commercial reason to pay more for geographic redundancy. A battery manufacturer cared about purity, price, reliability, and volume. The passport of the intermediate chemical was usually a secondary concern. A magnet buyer wanted magnets that met specification. A mining company wanted an offtaker willing to sign a bankable contract.

The market rewarded efficiency.

Governments are now paying for optionality.

The shift is visible in tax law.

The Inflation Reduction Act's clean-vehicle rules tried to turn the enormous American automobile market into an industrial-policy lever. To qualify under the original critical-minerals requirement, a rising share of the value of battery critical minerals had to be extracted or processed in the United States or in a country with which the United States had a qualifying free-trade agreement, or recycled in North America. The percentage stepped upward over time: forty percent in 2023, fifty in 2024, sixty in 2025, seventy in 2026, and eighty thereafter under the statutory schedule.

The details have since been altered by later tax legislation, and the consumer credit itself became a reminder that industrial policy is not politically permanent. But the underlying design remains important.

Washington was no longer merely subsidizing an electric car.

It was asking where the atoms came from.

That is friend-shoring at its most concrete. A tax form reaches backward through a battery pack into a refinery, then through the refinery into a mine, then through the mine into a trade agreement.

The rule also revealed how awkward the word friend can be.

The United States has formal free-trade agreements with some major mineral producers and not others. The European Union is an ally but not a U.S. free-trade-agreement partner in the conventional sense. Japan negotiated a critical-minerals agreement designed in part to bridge that problem. Other countries sought similar treatment.

A mineral alliance therefore becomes a legal classification exercise.

Which countries count?

Which processing steps count?

How much transformation has to occur in a qualifying country before material becomes qualifying material?

What happens when a friendly-country plant is owned by a Chinese company?

What happens when ore is mined in Australia, converted in China, incorporated into a cathode in South Korea, assembled into a cell in the United States, and installed in a vehicle in Mexico?

The supply chain does not respect the diplomatic shorthand.

This is one reason the Minerals Security Partnership was created in 2022. The partnership brought together the United States and a group of allied governments and the European Union around a simple problem: mining and processing projects outside the dominant Chinese system often struggle to obtain the financing, offtake, infrastructure, and political coordination required to become real.

A project can have excellent geology and still fail.

It may need a road.

It may need a port.

It may need power at a price that keeps the refinery competitive.

It may need a customer willing to sign a ten-year purchase agreement before a bank will lend against construction.

It may need a government to insure political risk.

It may need environmental and community agreements that survive a change in administration.

The partnership model tries to assemble those pieces across borders rather than pretending that a mine is a self-contained asset.

This is the right diagnosis.

The hard part is scale.

A diplomatic initiative can endorse projects. It cannot repeal the capital cycle from Chapter 10.

Europe's Critical Raw Materials Act attacks the same problem more numerically. The law set 2030 benchmarks for the European Union's strategic raw-material consumption: at least ten percent from EU extraction, forty percent from EU processing, and twenty-five percent from recycling, while seeking to keep dependence on any single third country below sixty-five percent at a relevant processing stage.

The targets are useful precisely because they expose the size of the gap.

In March 2025, the European Commission approved forty-seven strategic projects inside the Union. In June it added thirteen projects outside the EU. By the second call, which closed in January 2026, more than 160 applications had arrived.

Those numbers can be read two ways.

One reading is momentum. Companies are responding to policy. Governments have created a category that can bring permitting attention, financing guidance, and political support. A project that once looked like an isolated mine or recycling plant can now present itself as part of Europe's economic-security architecture.

The other reading is that a list of strategic projects is not a list of operating projects.

A designation does not build a concentrator.

It does not make local opposition disappear.

It does not guarantee a commodity price high enough to support the investment.

It does not create engineers, reagents, grid connections, or customers.

It does not make a technically difficult refinery easy because the Commission has placed it on a map.

Europe's policy has continued to evolve for that reason. In 2026 the Commission launched a Raw Materials Mechanism designed to aggregate demand and connect buyers with suppliers, financiers, storage providers, and strategic projects. The first diversification round focused on all seventeen strategic raw materials, with particular attention to rare earths, battery materials, and defense inputs.

This sounds less dramatic than opening a mine.

It may be closer to the actual bottleneck.

A new supplier needs a buyer.

An incumbent supplier already has one.

The most difficult years for a new mineral project often occur before the first commercial tonne, when capital has been spent but operating history does not yet exist. A long-term offtake agreement can turn future demand into present financing. Demand aggregation can make small buyers legible to a large project. Government-backed finance can absorb risks private lenders will not.

Friend-shoring becomes credible when it reaches this level of boring detail.

The United States has moved there too.

By 2026 the Department of Energy was funding projects across several points in the chain rather than treating mining as the whole problem. In June it announced $134 million for two projects intended to recover and refine rare earths from unconventional feedstocks such as mine tailings and electronic waste. In August it selected seven projects for $500 million across critical-mineral processing, battery manufacturing, and recycling. Another August selection put $162 million toward recovery from industrial feedstocks.

The amounts matter less than the pattern.

The policy has begun to resemble the supply chain.

Mining.

Waste recovery.

Separation.

Refining.

Magnets.

Batteries.

Recycling.

The old mistake was to point at a domestic deposit and call the problem solved. The newer approach at least recognizes that ore is not a magnet and spodumene is not a battery.

There is a second mistake waiting behind the first.

Government support can create capacity that is strategically desirable and commercially fragile.

China's mineral dominance was not built only by discovering deposits. It was built inside an industrial ecosystem with customers, suppliers, technical knowledge, infrastructure, and a willingness to survive periods of low prices. A new Western refinery may face an incumbent able to lower prices enough to make the newcomer's economics painful.

The policy response is becoming correspondingly less shy.

The Pentagon's 2025 agreement with MP Materials included a ten-year price floor for neodymium-praseodymium oxide and long-term support for magnet output. Whatever one thinks of the specific terms, the mechanism admits something markets often prefer not to say aloud: redundancy has a cost, and somebody has to pay it.

If a country wants a second supplier only during emergencies, the second supplier has to survive during normal times.

That is not free-market failure so much as an insurance problem.

Fire insurance looks wasteful during years without a fire.

A redundant refinery can look the same way during years of abundant imports.

The difference is that industrial insurance employs people, consumes capital, competes in commodity markets, and can go bankrupt before the emergency arrives.

Governments have several ways to keep it alive. Grants reduce construction cost. Loans lower financing cost. Tax credits improve operating economics. Procurement guarantees demand. Price floors protect revenue. Tariffs raise the price of competing imports. Stockpiles create a buyer of last resort. Regulations can require geographic diversification.

Every instrument moves the cost somewhere else.

The taxpayer pays.

The consumer pays.

The downstream manufacturer pays.

Or the supplier absorbs the risk and may not build.

Friend-shoring is therefore not a slogan about allies.

It is a decision about who pays for spare capacity.

That question becomes politically difficult when the threat is abstract. A factory manager can see the higher price of a non-Chinese input today. The avoided shortage three years from now is invisible. A finance ministry can count the subsidy. It cannot book the value of an export restriction that never bites because the supply chain was diversified in time.

This is why mineral policy tends to accelerate after a shock.

Japan diversified rare-earth supply after 2010 because the risk had become tangible. Europe rebuilt its energy strategy after Russia's invasion of Ukraine because dependency stopped being theoretical. The United States moved more aggressively on magnets after Chinese export controls showed how quickly licensing could become leverage.

Crisis makes insurance easier to sell.

Then prices fall.

The urgency fades.

Projects struggle.

This cycle may be the greatest enemy of friend-shoring. Mineral projects require policy consistency across electoral cycles that are shorter than mine-development cycles. A refinery approved under one administration may reach commissioning under another. A tax credit can disappear before a project reaches full production. A strategic partnership can survive on paper while its funding priorities change.

China's advantage is often described as patience.

Some of that patience is simply institutional continuity.

There are also limits to the friend-shoring concept that have nothing to do with Western inconsistency.

Allies have interests of their own.

Indonesia wants downstream industry, not a return to exporting cheap ore. Australia wants more processing at home. African governments increasingly want local value addition rather than another century of raw-material exports. European communities may support strategic autonomy in principle and oppose a mine near them in practice. The United States wants secure supply and domestic jobs, objectives that can conflict with the economic development goals of partner countries.

A durable alliance cannot mean that one country keeps the clean manufacturing while another keeps the pit and the waste.

The bargain has to distribute value as well as risk.

That is especially important because the language of security can become a convenient way to ignore old grievances. Many mineral-rich countries remember an earlier order in which foreign companies extracted resources, profits left, environmental liabilities remained, and promises of development arrived unevenly.

Calling the new arrangement a partnership does not erase that history.

The strongest friend-shoring projects will probably be the ones in which the host country can point to something beyond export volume: processing capacity, infrastructure, tax revenue, skilled jobs, technology transfer, or an industry that survives after the first mine declines.

That makes the projects slower and more complicated.

It may also make them politically durable.

There is a final paradox.

Friend-shoring is meant to reduce concentration.

Done badly, it can create a new concentration inside the alliance.

If every Western country subsidizes the same type of battery plant but nobody builds precursor capacity, the chain remains exposed. If every government wants a domestic magnet factory but all depend on the same separator, the apparent redundancy is downstream theater. If every project relies on the same equipment supplier, port, reagent, or financing institution, the chokepoint has merely moved out of view.

A resilient network needs diversity by function, not just flags on a map.

This is why the 2030 targets, strategic-project lists, financing mechanisms, and bilateral agreements should be treated as evidence of movement rather than evidence of completion.

They are scaffolding.

Some will become plants.

Some will become press releases preserved on government websites after the economics changed.

Some will succeed because a subsidy carried them across the dangerous early years. Some will fail despite excellent policy support because the geology disappointed, the process did not scale, costs rose, or commodity prices collapsed.

That is normal industrial development.

The strategic question is whether enough projects survive to make the network meaningfully harder to coerce.

Friend-shoring does not need to replace China everywhere to work.

It needs to make the next unit of supply available from somewhere else often enough that a licensing restriction, political dispute, or plant outage loses some of its power.

That is a lower bar than independence and a higher bar than an alliance announcement.

The distance between those bars is measured in operating mines, qualified chemicals, bankable offtake, working refineries, trained people, and years.

Which raises the uncomfortable question behind the entire policy response.

If the United States and Europe are now spending billions trying to recreate pieces of an industrial system they allowed to concentrate elsewhere, how did that concentration happen in the first place?

It is tempting to answer with a single quotation about the Middle East having oil and China having rare earths.

The next chapter begins by asking whether the quotation is even the right place to start.
