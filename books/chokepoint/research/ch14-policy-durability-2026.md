# Chapter 14 — Policy durability boundary (2026)

## Why this note exists

Chapter 14’s friend-shoring argument depends on a tension that deserves to be stated more precisely: mineral projects need policy support that lasts longer than ordinary political cycles, but the instruments governments use can change abruptly even while the strategic objective survives.

The clean-vehicle tax credit is a useful live test because it did both.

## Primary-source check: the U.S. clean-vehicle credit ended earlier than the original IRA schedule implied

The chapter correctly explains the Inflation Reduction Act’s original critical-minerals schedule for the Section 30D New Clean Vehicle Credit: the required qualifying share rose from 40% in 2023 to 50% in 2024, 60% in 2025, 70% in 2026, and 80% thereafter.

But by 2026 the consumer credit itself was no longer an operating policy lever for new vehicle purchases.

IRS guidance implementing Public Law 119-21 states that the Section 30D New Clean Vehicle Credit is not allowed for vehicles acquired after **September 30, 2025**. The same termination date applies to the Section 25E previously owned clean-vehicle credit and the Section 45W qualified commercial clean-vehicle credit.

Primary sources:

- IRS, “Clean vehicle tax credits”: https://www.irs.gov/clean-vehicle-tax-credits
- IRS, “FAQs for modification of sections 25C, 25D, 25E, 30C, 30D, 45L, 45W, and 179D under Public Law 119-21”: https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/faqs-for-modification-of-sections-25c-25d-25e-30c-30d-45l-45w-and-179d-under-public-law-119-21-139-stat-72-july-4-2025-commonly-known-as-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-obbb

### Editorial implication

Do not describe the IRA mineral-content schedule as though it remained a live consumer-credit escalator through 2026 and beyond. The useful historical point is stronger if the reversal is explicit: Washington built a sourcing rule intended to reach backward from vehicle demand into mines and refineries, then Congress terminated the consumer credit before the statutory mineral-content schedule had finished climbing.

That is direct evidence for the chapter’s claim that industrial policy can operate on a shorter political clock than the assets it is meant to finance.

## Counterevidence: the strategic objective did not disappear with the consumer credit

The strongest counterargument is that policy durability should not be measured by whether one subsidy survives unchanged.

On August 20, 2026, the U.S. Department of Energy announced **$500 million for seven selected projects** spanning critical-mineral and material processing, battery manufacturing, and recycling. The announcement explicitly framed the projects as a way to reduce reliance on foreign sources and strengthen domestic critical-material supply chains.

Primary source:

- U.S. Department of Energy, “Energy Department Announces $500 Million To Secure America’s Critical Mineral and Battery Supply Chains,” August 20, 2026: https://www.energy.gov/articles/energy-department-announces-500-million-secure-americas-critical-mineral-and-battery

This matters because it complicates a simple “policy reversal kills friend-shoring” story. One high-profile demand-side instrument ended, while direct supply-chain support continued through other programs.

### Stronger formulation for Chapter 14

The durable claim is not:

> Mineral policy disappears whenever governments change course.

It is:

> Mineral policy is vulnerable to instrument churn. Projects whose economics depend on a particular tax credit, procurement rule, grant program, tariff, price floor, or eligibility definition may face a political clock shorter than their construction and qualification cycle, even when the larger strategic objective survives under a different instrument.

That distinction is important because a refinery cannot finance itself against a generic national-security consensus. It finances against actual contracts, tax treatment, grants, loans, offtake agreements, and expected cash flows.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The chapter should explicitly acknowledge three reasons not to overstate political instability:

1. **Objectives can outlive instruments.** The 2025 clean-vehicle-credit termination did not end all federal support for critical-mineral processing, batteries, or recycling.
2. **Projects can stack support.** A project may rely on state incentives, federal loans, grants, private offtake, defense procurement, tariffs, or customer contracts rather than a single consumer subsidy.
3. **Some policy changes can improve durability.** Moving from a broad consumer credit to targeted support for a processing bottleneck may, in some cases, direct scarce public money closer to the actual chokepoint.

The empirical question is therefore not whether “friend-shoring policy” survives in the abstract. It is whether the specific revenue and risk-sharing mechanisms that make a project bankable remain credible long enough for the project to reach operation and customer qualification.

## What would make this argument false?

This boundary should weaken if evidence shows that critical-mineral projects can routinely withstand abrupt changes in tax credits, grants, tariffs, eligibility rules, or procurement without affecting financing, construction timing, or final investment decisions.

It should also weaken if successive governments converge on stable, multi-instrument support structures whose effective economics remain predictable across election cycles even when individual program names and statutes change.

## Suggested use in the manuscript

The highest-value insertion is small. Replace the chapter’s vague sentence that the clean-vehicle-credit details “have since been altered” with the exact September 30, 2025 termination date, then use the 2026 DOE awards as the immediate counterexample: the consumer lever disappeared, but supply-chain support continued elsewhere.

That pair of facts would sharpen the chapter’s argument without turning it into a catalogue of programs.
