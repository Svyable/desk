## CHAPTER 8 — "Rare Earths and the Refining Monopoly"

### Key facts and sources

**Mountain Pass — history**

- The Mountain Pass deposit was discovered in 1949 by prospectors searching for uranium; commercial rare-earth mining began in 1952. Source: *Mountain Pass Rare Earth Mine* overview, synthesized from Wikipedia/mining-technology.com via search (original Wikipedia page blocked for direct fetch this session — re-verify at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_Pass_Rare_Earth_Mine). Use: opening anecdote — irony that the world's most important rare-earth mine was found by accident, looking for a Cold War fuel (uranium), not rare earths.
- Mountain Pass supplied roughly **70% of world rare-earth output through the early 1980s**, and was essentially the only major global source from the 1960s into the 1980s. Source: geologyforinvestors.com, "Mountain Pass: America's Once and Future Rare Earth King," via search synthesis (https://www.geologyforinvestors.com/mountain-pass-americas-once-and-future-rare-earth-king/). Limitation: a secondary/investor-oriented source, not a primary USGS historical series — corroborate against USGS historical minerals yearbooks if possible.
- By 1993, China's share of world rare-earth production had reached **38%**, versus **33%** for the US and **12%** for Australia — evidence of how fast China displaced Mountain Pass in a single decade. Source: cited via search synthesis of a mining-history summary (attributed in search results without a single clean URL; treat as needing re-verification against USGS Minerals Yearbook 1993 rare earths chapter).
- A **series of wastewater pipeline ruptures leaked radioactive thorium and radium onto the desert floor at Mountain Pass at least 60 times between 1984 and 1998**, piped to evaporation ponds at Ivanpah Dry Lake. These spills, combined with weak prices from Chinese competition, forced the mine to halt production in **2002**. Source: search synthesis citing Wikipedia's Mountain Pass Rare Earth Mine article and Defense News commentary "The collapse of American rare earth mining — and lessons learned" (Nov 12, 2019), https://www.defensenews.com/opinion/commentary/2019/11/12/the-collapse-of-american-rare-earth-mining-and-lessons-learned/. Use: this is the environmental-plus-price-war one-two punch that killed the US rare earth industry — good narrative pivot into Ch. 15's China price-war material.
- Ownership chain after closure: Unocal → **Chevron acquired the site in 2005** → sold to **Molycorp Minerals in 2008** → Molycorp restarted mining in **2010**, built a ~$1.25 billion state-of-the-art processing facility, then **filed for bankruptcy in June 2015** (Bloomberg, "Molycorp Files for Bankruptcy as Rare-Earth Prices Drop," June 25 2015, https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2015-06-25/molycorp-files-for-bankruptcy-proposes-debt-restructuring-plan) as Chinese producers flooded the market and crushed the 2010–2011 price spike. **MP Materials bought the mine out of bankruptcy in 2017** and resumed mining. Sources: Motley Fool "Bankruptcy Lessons: What We Can Learn From Molycorp's Rise and Fall" (2015), High Country News "The U.S.'s only rare-earth mine files for bankruptcy," both via search synthesis.

**Mountain Pass / MP Materials — current status and 2025 US government deal**

- MP Materials is now **the only rare-earth producer in the United States**, headquartered in Las Vegas, Nevada. Source: search synthesis of company/press coverage, re-verify at investors.mpmaterials.com (blocked this session).
- Production: MP Materials produced a record **45,455 metric tons of rare-earth oxide (REO) in concentrate in 2024** (up from prior years) and a record **1,294 metric tons of separated NdPr oxide in 2024**; for **2025**, the company reported record **50,692 metric tons of REO in concentrate** (+12% YoY) and record **2,599 metric tons of NdPr oxide** (+101% YoY). MP Materials produced its **first NdFeB permanent magnets on commercial equipment at its "Independence" facility in 2025**. Source: MP Materials Q4/FY2025 results release, synthesized via search (https://investors.mpmaterials.com/investor-news/news-details/2026/MP-Materials-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Results/default.aspx — site blocked for direct fetch, re-verify).
- **July 9, 2025: MP Materials and the US Department of Defense (DoD) announced a "transformational public-private partnership."** Terms reported: DoD agreed to purchase **$400 million of a newly created series of MP Materials preferred stock**, convertible into common stock, plus a warrant to buy additional shares at an initial price of **$30.03/share**; on an as-converted/as-exercised basis this gave DoD roughly a **15% equity stake**, positioning DoD to become MP Materials' **largest shareholder**. DoD also committed a **$150 million loan** for MP Materials to expand **heavy rare-earth separation capability at Mountain Pass**. Sources: Payne Institute for Public Policy explainer, https://payneinstitute.mines.edu/explainer-on-the-mp-materials-department-of-defense-partnership/; MINING.com, "MP Materials lands multi-billion Pentagon deal," https://www.mining.com/mp-materials-lands-multi-billion-pentagon-deal/; CNBC, "Pentagon to become largest shareholder in rare earth miner MP Materials; shares surge 50%," July 10 2025, https://www.cnbc.com/2025/07/10/pentagon-to-become-largest-shareholder-in-rare-earth-magnet-maker-mp-materials.html. **This is REAL, not rumor** — confirmed via MP Materials' own SEC 424B5 filings (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1801368/000119312525160635/d923027d424b5.htm and .../000119312525159973/d923027d424b5.htm) referenced in search results, though direct SEC fetch was blocked this session — re-verify against the filing text directly.
- **Price floor mechanism**: the deal establishes a **10-year price floor of $110/kg for NdPr** (neodymium-praseodymium oxide, the key magnet input). If the market price falls below $110/kg, the US government pays MP Materials the shortfall via **quarterly cash payments**; if the price rises above $110/kg after MP's second magnet facility is operating, DoD receives **30% of the upside**. Sources: Nasdaq, "DoD Backs MP Materials (MP) in $110/kg Rare Earth Pricing Deal," https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/dod-backs-mp-materials-mp-110-kg-rare-earth-pricing-deal; Rare Earth Exchanges forum post citing the same terms, https://forum.rareearthexchanges.com/threads/dod-sets-a-floor-price-of-us-110-kg-for-ndpr.922/. Use: this price-floor structure is a striking, very citable "the US government is now underwriting a commodity price" fact for the book — good for illustrating how far Washington has moved from free-market orthodoxy on this one mineral.
- DoD additionally committed to **purchase 100% of magnets produced at MP's new "10X" facility for 10 years following commissioning**, and separately to **buy 7,000 metric tons/year of rare-earth magnets for 10 years**. The 10X magnet campus (a **$1.25 billion, ~1,500-job** manufacturing complex) is planned for **Northlake/North Texas**, expected to begin commissioning around **2028** with a target output of **7,000 metric tons of magnets/year**. Sources: Dallas Innovates, "MP Materials To Build '10X,' a $1.25B Rare Earth Magnet Manufacturing Campus in North Texas," https://dallasinnovates.com/mp-materials-to-build-10x-a-1-25b-rare-earth-magnet-manufacturing-campus-in-north-texas/; MP Materials DoD partnership release (search synthesis).
- **Counterintuitive ownership wrinkle**: as of 2025, roughly **7.7% of MP Materials is owned by Shenghe Resources, a Chinese company partly owned by China's Ministry of Natural Resources** — meaning a Beijing-linked entity holds a meaningful stake in America's flagship "de-China-ify rare earths" champion even as the Pentagon becomes the largest shareholder. Other major shareholders as of 2025: **Hancock Prospecting (Gina Rinehart), ~8.7%; BlackRock, ~8.7%; Vanguard, ~8.3%**. Sources: search synthesis citing fintel.io/tickergate.com ownership data and Yahoo Finance "Gina Rinehart becomes largest shareholder in MP Materials." Limitation: ownership percentages are self-reported/aggregator data (fintel, tickergate) — re-verify against MP Materials' most recent DEF 14A proxy (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001801368/000180136826000020/mp-20260423.htm, blocked this session). **This is strong narrative/irony material — flag for the writer.**

**Lynas Rare Earths (Malaysia) — the only significant separator outside China**

- Lynas was **founded in 1983 and listed on the ASX in 1986**; it acquired the **Mount Weld rare-earth deposit in Western Australia in 2001** and began mining there in **2007**. Source: search synthesis of Mining Technology's "Vision becoming reality: the history of Lynas's Mount Weld mine" (2023), https://www.mining-technology.com/features/vision-becoming-reality-the-history-of-lynass-mount-weld-mine/.
- Lynas built the **Lynas Advanced Materials Plant (LAMP) in Gebeng, Kuantan, Malaysia**, which began **commercial production in 2013**, processing concentrate shipped from Mount Weld. Full **Phase 2 design capacity is up to 22,000 tonnes/year of separated rare-earth oxide products**; recent operating capacity is cited around **11,000 tonnes/year**, making it one of the largest non-Chinese rare-earth separation operations in the world. Sources: search synthesis of Lynas quarterly reports (lynasrareearths.com PDFs) and rare-earth-mining.com profile.
- In **1H FY26 (second half of 2025), Lynas became the first commercial producer outside China of separated dysprosium and terbium** — the two most important heavy rare earths for high-performance permanent magnets in EV motors, wind turbines, and defense systems. Its current Malaysian heavy-rare-earth separation circuit handles up to **1,500 tonnes/year**; in **October 2025**, Lynas announced a **new facility (~$180 million AUD investment, per Capital Brief) to expand heavy-rare-earth separation capacity to 5,000 tonnes/year**. Sources: Bloomberg, "Lynas to Expand Only Heavy Rare Earths Facility Outside China," Oct 28 2025, https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-28/lynas-to-expand-only-heavy-rare-earths-facility-outside-china; Capital Brief, "Lynas Rare Earths announces $180m rare earths separation facility expansion," https://www.capitalbrief.com/briefing/lynas-rare-earths-announces-180m-rare-earths-separation-facility-expansion-e0c130f3-320e-4fe3-a612-416b22cdbedb/; MINING.com, "Lynas becomes first producer of heavy rare earths outside China," https://www.mining.com/lynas-becomes-first-producer-of-heavy-rare-earths-outside-china/.
- Lynas is also building a **US facility in Seadrift, Texas** (149-acre site), co-locating light and heavy rare-earth separation, with feedstock from Mount Weld and its Kalgoorlie, Australia processing facility, **targeted operational in FY2026**. The **US Department of Defense contract funding this facility was increased from an original $120 million to $258 million** (expenditure-based/cost-reimbursement contract). Sources: Mining Engineering Online/SME, "Lynas awarded US$120 million contract to build rare earths separation facility in Texas," https://me.smenet.org/lynas-awarded-us120-million-contract-to-build-rare-earths-separation-facility-in-texas/; search synthesis citing the updated $258M figure and Seadrift site details (SEC filings by USA Rare Earth referenced in search results for cross-context — note USA Rare Earth is a *different*, competing company, do not conflate).
- **Kuantan radioactive-waste controversy**: Lynas's "cracking and leaching" process at LAMP produces **Water Leach Purification (WLP) residue**, a radioactive byproduct containing **thorium (half-life ~14 billion years)**; the company has generated **millions of tonnes of this waste** since operations began in 2012/2013. Malaysia's government required Lynas to relocate or eliminate the waste; Lynas had originally promised to ship waste back to Australia, but **the Australian government refused to accept it**, leaving it stockpiled in Malaysia. When Malaysia renewed Lynas's operating license in 2023, it required the company to develop technology to **extract thorium from the residue and eliminate its radioactivity before the license expires in March 2026**. Sources: search synthesis of Greenpeace Malaysia, "A Radioactive Ruse" report, https://www.greenpeace.org/malaysia/report/1083/a-radioactive-ruse-report-on-lynas-malaysia/; Al Jazeera, "Malaysia tells Lynas to remove rare earths radioactive waste" (Dec 4 2018), https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2018/12/4/malaysia-tells-lynas-to-remove-rare-earths-radioactive-waste; and general coverage synthesis. **Caveat: the "no progress on license renewal" claim for 2026 is from a secondary aggregator and should be re-checked against contemporaneous Reuters/Bloomberg/Malay Mail reporting closer to the March 2026 deadline.**
- The **"Save Malaysia, Stop Lynas" (SMSL)** and **Himpunan Hijau ("Green Assembly," founded March 2011, led by Wong Tack)** protest movements opposed the LAMP plant from construction onward; **1.2 million Malaysians reportedly signed a protest petition**. Sources: search synthesis of Wikipedia's "Himpunan Hijau" entry and Borneo Project, "Green Movement Takes Root in Malaysia," https://borneoproject.org/green-movement-takes-root-in-malaysia/. Use: strong scene material — mass local protest against the only non-Chinese heavy rare-earth separator, illustrating that refining capacity isn't just capital-constrained but politically/environmentally constrained everywhere, not just in China.

**China's share: mining vs. refining — the central Chapter 8 stat**

- **Mining**: China accounted for **69.2% of world rare-earth mine production in 2024** (with a domestic mining quota of 270,000 metric tons REO-equivalent); the US was a distant second at **11.5%** (~45,000 metric tons). Source: search synthesis attributed to USGS-derived reporting (re-verify directly against USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025, "Rare Earths" chapter, PDF at https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-rare-earths.pdf — blocked for direct fetch this session, strongly recommend the writer/fact-checker pull this PDF directly, it is the single best primary source for this entire chapter).
- USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 (via search synthesis): **global 2024 mine production ~390,000 tons REO-equivalent**; **US domestic mine production ~45,000 tons REO in concentrate, valued at $260 million**; China holds the world's largest reserves (cited elsewhere as **44 million tonnes REO-equivalent** in a Feb 2026 USGS update). Source: farmonaut.com summaries of USGS data and USGS data catalog page https://data.usgs.gov/datacatalog/data/USGS:6798f088d34ea8c18376e7f9. **Re-verify exact figures against the primary PDF.**
- **Refining/separation (the more important stat for the book's argument)**: estimates cluster between **85% and 92%** for China's share of global rare-earth refining/separation capacity or output. Specific figures found: "China controls about **85%** of global processing capacity" (general secondary synthesis); IEA reportedly estimated China accounted for about **91% of global separation and refining production in 2024** (IEA "Rare Earth Elements" report executive summary — direct fetch blocked, re-verify at https://www.iea.org/reports/rare-earth-elements/executive-summary); a Rare Earth Exchanges/industry analysis cites China at **92% of 2024 refined output**, projected to fall to **78% by 2040** as new refining capacity comes online elsewhere. Sources (via search synthesis, need direct re-verification): Oxford Institute for Energy Studies, "China's rare earths dominance and policy responses" (2023 PDF), https://www.oxfordenergy.org/wpcms/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/CE7-Chinas-rare-earths-dominance-and-policy-responses.pdf; Al Jazeera, "Despite US push, China poised to dominate rare earths for years," Oct 21 2025, https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2025/10/21/despite-us-push-china-poised-to-dominate-rare-earths-for-years. **Use for the book's core thesis**: mining share (~69%) vs. refining share (~85-92%) is exactly the gap the chapter should hammer — China's dominance is concentrated *downstream*, not at the mine mouth. **Caveat: cite a single authoritative figure (ideally the IEA report itself, fetched directly, or Adamas Intelligence) rather than averaging across secondary sources — the range 85-92% reflects real methodological differences (refining capacity vs. actual refined output vs. separated-oxide output) that a careful nonfiction book should specify precisely.**

### Narrative material (Ch. 8)

- The origin-story irony: Mountain Pass was found by **uranium prospectors in 1949**, not rare-earth hunters — a Cold War-fuel-security story accidentally becoming a 21st-century magnet-security story.
- The **60 documented radioactive pipeline leaks (1984-1998)** into the Mojave desert floor is a vivid, specific, checkable data point for describing why Mountain Pass actually closed — not (only) Chinese competition, but self-inflicted environmental failure.
- Molycorp's collapse is a genuine cautionary tale with real numbers: it built a **~$1.25 billion state-of-the-art plant** betting on the 2010-2011 China-driven price spike, then Chinese producers "flooded the market," prices crashed, and Molycorp went bankrupt in 2015 — a textbook demonstration of China's ability to use price as a weapon against Western re-entrants.
- The **$110/kg DoD price floor** for MP Materials is an almost cinematic detail: the U.S. government now directly subsidizes a commodity price on a quarterly cash-payment basis, and takes 30% of the upside — a level of industrial-policy intervention that would have been unthinkable in US mineral markets a decade ago. Good material for a scene contrasting free-market rhetoric with the reality of critical-minerals policy.
- The **Shenghe Resources 7.7% stake** (a China Ministry of Natural Resources–linked company) inside MP Materials, even as the Pentagon becomes the largest shareholder, is rich, ironic, underused material — "China owns a piece of America's flagship anti-China rare earth company."
- Lynas's Kuantan plant and the **Himpunan Hijau protest movement** (1.2 million petition signatures, led by activist Wong Tack) offer a scene-ready story: the only significant rare-earth separator outside China nearly got shut down by its own host country's citizens over radioactive waste — dramatizing why refining capacity is so hard to build anywhere, including democracies.
- The **thorium byproduct's 14-billion-year half-life** is a strikingly citable (if slightly rhetorical — thorium-232's actual half-life is ~14.05 billion years, comparable to the age of the universe) detail that lands well in prose.

### Counterevidence / complications (Ch. 8)

- **Lynas's October 2025 heavy-rare-earth expansion** (1,500 → 5,000 tonnes/year) and its status as **first commercial non-Chinese producer of separated dysprosium and terbium (1H FY26)** is real, concrete evidence that the "China refining monopoly" is beginning to erode — not hypothetically, but with shipped product.
- **MP Materials' own build-out of heavy rare-earth separation at Mountain Pass**, funded by the DoD's $150 million loan, is a second concrete counter-example — US onshoring of the separation step specifically, not just mining.
- **Lynas's Seadrift, Texas facility** (DoD-funded, now $258 million) represents the first significant heavy-rare-earth separation capacity to be built on US soil.
- Structural counterpoint from S&P Global analysis (Jan 2026, "Rare earth supply bottlenecks set to persist in 2026," https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/metals/012726-rare-earth-supply-bottlenecks-set-to-persist-in-2026, via search synthesis): even with this real progress, **Western/allied nations have made the most headway at the mining stage; separation, refining, alloying, and magnet manufacturing remain "structurally underdeveloped" outside China's integrated ecosystem**, and **ex-China heavy-rare-earth bottlenecks are expected to persist through 2026-2027**. Estimated **3-4 years for new mines, separation plants, and magnet plants to meaningfully close the gap**. Good balancing material — the book's thesis (refining is the chokepoint) is validated by industry analysts, but the "West is now fixing this" narrative should not be overstated on the current timeline.
- **October-November 2025 China export-control escalation and rapid de-escalation**: on **Oct 9, 2025**, China's Ministry of Commerce announced sweeping new export controls requiring licenses for rare-earth-related items re-exported between third countries, and extended controls to **7 additional medium/heavy rare earths (terbium, dysprosium, samarium, gadolinium, lutetium, scandium, yttrium)** with extraterritorial reach (any foreign product with ≥0.1% Chinese-origin rare-earth content, or made using Chinese processing tech, required a license). At the **APEC summit in Busan (Oct 30, 2025)**, the US and China reached a "mutual stand-down": **China suspended the October measures until November 2026**, and the US suspended its own retaliatory measures for the same period. Sources: Mayer Brown, "PRC Announces New Export Controls on Rare Earth and Battery Materials and Technology," https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/10/prc-announces-new-export-controls-on-rare-earth-and-battery-materials-and-technology; FDD, "China Pauses Some Rare Earth Export Curbs While Retaining Levers of Control," Nov 12 2025, https://www.fdd.org/analysis/2025/11/12/china-pauses-some-rare-earth-export-curbs-while-retaining-levers-of-control/. **This is extremely current and directly relevant — a live, unresolved geopolitical flashpoint the book should treat as an open question rather than settled history, since the suspension expires November 2026.**

## CHAPTER 15 — "China's Long Game"

### Key facts and sources

**The Deng Xiaoping quote — origin and reliability (handle carefully)**

- The quote most commonly rendered as **"The Middle East has oil, China has rare earths"** is attributed to Deng Xiaoping during his **"Southern Tour" in January 1992**, with several accounts placing the specific remark at or near **Bayan Obo / Baotou, Inner Mongolia**, during a stop where he was reviving support for market reforms in the wake of the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown's political fallout. Source: China National Radio's website is cited (via search synthesis) as dating the remark to January 1992; the quote is also physically inscribed in stone/calligraphy at an industrial park in Baotou. **Caveat: this is a translated, secondhand paraphrase, not a directly cited primary transcript** — no single search result produced a verifiable primary Chinese-language source document, speech transcript, or page number from Deng's collected works (邓小平文选) pinning the exact wording and venue.
- **Dating is disputed even in the secondary literature**: most sources converge on **January 1992**, but some cite **1987**, and at least one outlet flagged an editorial correction acknowledging uncertainty over **1991 vs. 1992**. Source: search synthesis of multiple outlets, cross-referenced against the Domino Theory piece (URL below, could not be directly fetched this session — **recommend the writer read this piece directly before using the quote**, as it appears to be the single best English-language investigation of the quote's provenance: https://dominotheory.com/how-a-deng-xiaoping-quote-became-china-watchers-favorite-anecdote/).
- Search results indicate the quote **"first appeared in The New York Times in August 2009"**, meaning its widespread circulation in Western China-watching discourse post-dates the alleged 1992 utterance by **17 years**, and specifically post-dates it in a way that coincides with growing Western alarm about Chinese rare-earth export restrictions (2009-2010) — i.e., the quote became popular retroactively, as a way to narrate a "China planned this all along" story, not because it was well-documented at the time it was supposedly said.
- **Key scholarly caveat — Julie Klinger (University of Delaware; author of *Rare Earth Frontiers*, Cornell University Press, 2017)**: Klinger has explicitly **cautioned against reading the quote as evidence of "a fifty-year masterplan drawn up on a napkin,"** arguing that treating the 2010 rare-earths crisis as "some intended endpoint of long-term Chinese strategy" is **"ahistorical."** She describes the quote as functioning as **"a Rorschach test"** — what people claim it means (and which printed source they cite) reveals more about contemporary Western anxieties than about verified 1992 Chinese state intent. Sources: search synthesis attributing this analysis to Klinger's book and to the Domino Theory article; also referenced via a Foreign Affairs piece, "How China Found Its Most Potent Weapon" (Xiong/Weiss), https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/how-china-found-most-potent-weapon-xiong-weiss (not directly fetched — re-verify). **Recommendation for the book: quote Deng's line only with an explicit, upfront caveat along these lines — it is a widely repeated, likely genuine but imprecisely sourced paraphrase, popularized in the West nearly two decades after it was allegedly said, and its use as proof of "decades-long strategic intent" is contested by at least one serious historian of the subject (Klinger).**

**China's rare earth industrial policy, 1980s-1990s**

- China's **"863 Program," launched in 1986**, is cited as having institutionalized state rare-earth R&D funding, supporting advances in separation technology and downstream applications. Source: search synthesis (needs re-verification against a primary Chinese science-policy source; the 863 Program is real and well-documented broadly as a national high-tech initiative, but its specific rare-earth funding role should be checked against a dedicated academic source, e.g., the East Asia Young Scholars Association symposium paper referenced at https://eaysa.org/aa-symposiumproceedings-nltc/).
- In the **early 1990s, China officially classified rare earths as "strategic minerals,"** triggering state protection, investment incentives, and a **rare-earth export tax rebate of up to 17%** designed to draw new domestic producers into the industry and undercut foreign competitors on price. Source: search synthesis, cross-referenced with Congressional Research Service report, "China's Rare Earth Industry and Export Regime: Economic and Trade Implications for the United States" (CRS R42510), https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R42510.html — a strong, citable primary-adjacent government source; recommend direct fetch/read for the writer.
- **Production growth, 1985-1995**: China's rare-earth mine output grew from **~8,500 metric tons (1985) to ~48,000 metric tons (1995)**, and its share of global mining output rose from **21.4% to 60.1%** over the same decade — the single clearest quantification of how fast China displaced Mountain Pass and other Western producers. Source: search synthesis (original attribution unclear — likely derived from a USGS historical series or the CRS report above; **recommend re-verifying this specific figure against the CRS R42510 report or USGS Minerals Yearbook historical statistics before using in the book**, as it is an important, specific, and highly quotable data point).
- China's rare-earth exports benefited from **preferential government policy and lax/weakly enforced environmental standards**, which is the standard economic explanation (alongside subsidies) for how China underpriced Western rare-earth producers, including Mountain Pass, out of the market by the late 1990s. Source: search synthesis of FPRI, "China's Monopoly on Rare Earth Elements—and Why We Should Care," https://www.fpri.org/article/2020/10/chinas-monopoly-on-rare-earth-elements-and-why-we-should-care/.

**The environmental cost: Baotou / Bayan Obo**

- **Bayan Obo, in Baotou, Inner Mongolia**, is the **world's largest rare-earth deposit**, discovered as an **iron deposit in 1927** by geologist Ding Daoheng of Peking University; rare-earth minerals in the deposit were identified in **1936**, and niobium-bearing ore in the **late 1950s**. Cumulative exploration has identified **1,160 million tonnes of iron ore and 112 million tonnes of rare-earth oxides**, representing **more than two-thirds of all known rare-earth resources in the world**. Rare earths and niobium are extracted as **byproducts of iron-ore mining** at Bayan Obo. Sources: search synthesis of academic/industry summaries; original scientific characterization available in *Economic Geology* journal article, "Mineralization of the Bayan Obo Rare Earth Element Deposit by Recrystallization and Decarbonation," https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/segweb/economicgeology/article/117/6/1327/612483 (peer-reviewed primary source — strong citation for the book).
- The **Baogang (Weikuang) tailings dam**, ~10-12 km west/outside Baotou city, covering roughly **10-11.5 square kilometers**, holds tailings and radioactive waste slurry from the rare-earth and iron refineries; **Bayan Obo produces approximately 8 million tons of tailings per year**. Sources: EJAtlas, "Bayan Obo world biggest rare earths mine," https://www.ejatlas.org/conflict/bayan-obo-world-biggest-rare-earths-mine-baogang-group-baotou-inner-mongolia-china; Wikipedia's "Baogang Tailings Dam" entry (blocked for direct fetch, re-verify at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baogang_Tailings_Dam).
- **Radioactivity measurements**: a peer-reviewed in-situ gamma-ray survey found mean concentrations in the tailings dam of **238U at 3.0 ± 1.0 mg/kg and 232Th at 321 ± 31 mg/kg** — the thorium concentration was **34.6 times greater than in local background soil**; the highest estimated effective radiation dose from gamma exposure was **1.15 mSv/year**. Source: peer-reviewed study, "In-situ gamma-ray survey of rare-earth tailings dams – A case study in Baotou and Bayan Obo Districts, China," *Science of the Total Environment* via ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0265931X15301430. **This is a strong, checkable primary/peer-reviewed source — use this over looser journalistic figures where precision matters.**
- Narrative/journalistic detail: reporting (attributed via search synthesis to The Guardian, likely a Jonathan Watts-era Guardian China environment piece, and to a widely syndicated account, possibly Tim Maughan's 2015 BBC Future piece "The dystopian lake filled by the world's tech lust") describes **"dozens of pipes" churning "thick, black, chemical waste"** into the lake, with a resident/farmer named **Li Guirong** quoted: **"Before the factories were built, there were just fields here as far as the eye can see... there were watermelons, aubergines and tomatoes."** Farmers near the dam reportedly said they'd **lost teeth and their hair had turned white**, with soil/water testing showing elevated cancer-linked radioactive contamination. Sources: search synthesis citing Weather Channel, "Toxic Lake in Inner Mongolia Created By World's Addiction To High-Tech Gadgets," https://weather.com/science/environment/news/baotou-artificial-lake-toxic-sludge; ABC News, "This Toxic Lake of Black Sludge Is the Result of Mining to Create Our Tech Gadgets," https://abcnews.com/Technology/toxic-lake-black-sludge-result-mining-create-tech/story?id=30122911. **Important caveat: I could not directly confirm which single outlet (Guardian vs. BBC Future vs. a wire service) is the true first-source of the "Li Guirong" quote and the "watermelons, aubergines and tomatoes" line — several outlets recirculate what appears to be the same original reporting (most likely Jonathan Watts of The Guardian, and/or Tim Maughan's BBC Future feature, both circa 2012-2015). **Chinese state consolidation of the industry**

- **China Rare Earth Group Co. Ltd. was formally established on December 23, 2021, in Ganzhou, Jiangxi Province**, merging **China Minmetals Rare Earth, Chinalco Rare Earth & Metals, and China Southern Rare Earth Group**, plus two smaller entities (Ganzhou Zhonglan Rare Earth New Material Technology and the Jiangxi Ganzhou Rare Metal Exchange). It is **directly supervised by China's state-asset regulator, SASAC**, which holds a **31.2% stake**, with the three main merged SOEs each holding **~20.3%**. The new entity became **the world's second-largest rare-earth producer**, controlling roughly **30% of China's total rare-earth metal production and 60-70% of its heavy rare-earth metal production**. Sources: China Briefing, "China Merges Three Rare Earths State-Owned Entities to Increase Pricing Power and Efficiency," https://www.china-briefing.com/news/china-merges-three-rare-earths-state-owned-entities-to-increase-pricing-power-and-efficiency/; South China Morning Post, "China's new rare earth merger hands it 'trump card' in global fight for resources," https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3160995/chinas-new-rare-earth-merger-hands-it-trump-card-global-fight; S&P Global Market Intelligence, "China creates new state-owned rare earths giant," https://spglobal.com/marketintelligence/en/news-insights/latest-news-headlines/china-creates-new-state-owned-rare-earths-giant-68185761. This complements the pre-existing **China Northern Rare Earth Group (Baotou)**, the largest single rare-earth producer, headquartered at Bayan Obo/Baotou itself (not separately verified in depth this session — flag for follow-up if the chapter needs Northern Rare Earth Group specifics).

**Export quota / tariff history and the WTO case**

- China **restricted rare-earth exports starting in 2006**, and in **July 2010 announced a 40% cut in export quotas**; the **2010 total export quota was 24,280 tons**. Source: search synthesis (cross-reference against CRS R42510 and/or the CEPR piece below for exact figures).
- The **September 2010 Senkaku/Diaoyu boat-collision incident** (a Chinese trawler collided with Japanese coast guard vessels) triggered widely reported claims that China **halted rare-earth exports to Japan** to pressure Tokyo into releasing the detained captain; the *New York Times* reported the halt on **Sept 22, 2010**, and Japan released the captain on **Sept 24, 2010**. **Important counterevidence/caveat**: subsequent academic analysis of Japanese Ministry of Finance port-level import data found **no uniform, verifiable drop in Japanese rare-earth imports** tied to the collision — i.e., whether a formal embargo actually occurred (versus informal customs slow-walking, or a coincidental market effect) is **disputed in the scholarly literature**. Sources: CEPR VoxEU, "Revisiting the China–Japan Rare Earths dispute of 2010," https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/revisiting-china-japan-rare-earths-dispute-2010; East Asia Forum, "Did China really ban rare earth metals exports to Japan?" Aug 18 2013, https://eastasiaforum.org/2013/08/18/did-china-really-ban-rare-earth-metals-exports-to-japan/; CSIS, "China's Rare Earth Campaign Against Japan," https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-rare-earth-campaign-against-japan. **This is important and underused: the 2010 "embargo" is the canonical origin story of Western rare-earth panic, but serious scholars dispute that it happened as commonly described — the book should present this as contested, not settled fact.**
- Rare-earth prices **spiked sharply between November 2010 and summer 2011** in the aftermath, which is well-documented and less disputed than the embargo-mechanism question itself.
- China also **imposed a resource tax on rare-earth ore (0.4 to 60 yuan/ton) starting in 2011**, alongside rising export tariffs since 2006. Source: search synthesis (re-verify against CRS R42510).
- **March 2014: a WTO dispute panel ruled that China's export duties, export quotas, minimum export price rules, and export licensing requirements on rare earths (plus tungsten and molybdenum) violated its WTO obligations** (specifically GATT Article XI's prohibition on quantitative restrictions), following a complaint brought by the **United States, European Union, and Japan** (with the US formally requesting consultations on **March 13, 2012**). China's appeal was rejected and the ruling upheld. Source: Office of the US Trade Representative, official press release, "United States Wins Victory in Rare Earths Dispute with China," March 2014, https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2014/March/US-wins-victory-in-rare-earths-dispute-with-China — **this is a strong primary/government source, directly citable**. China subsequently **scrapped its formal rare-earth export quota system** to comply. Source: IndustryWeek, "China Scraps Rare Earths Export Quota After WTO Ruling," https://www.industryweek.com/the-economy/trade/article/21965206/china-scraps-rare-earths-export-quota-after-wto-ruling.
- **Note for the "long game" thesis**: China's shift away from *formal export quotas* (ruled illegal in 2014) toward *export licensing, entity-list controls, and technology-transfer restrictions* (used heavily in 2023-2025, including the October 2025 measures described in the Ch.8 section above) can be read as a direct, traceable policy evolution — Beijing adapted its control mechanism to work around the WTO ruling rather than abandoning control altogether. This is a good throughline for Chapter 15's "decades of adaptive strategy" argument, and it is well-documented across primary/government sources (USTR 2014 ruling + 2023-2025 MOFCOM notices, e.g., CSET's translation of MOFCOM Notice 2025 No. 61, https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/mofcom-notice-2025-61/ — a strong primary-adjacent source, a Georgetown-affiliated translation of the actual Chinese regulatory text).

### Narrative material (Ch. 15)

- The Deng Xiaoping quote itself, told **with its caveats intact**, is a compelling narrative device precisely *because* of its contested status — the book can use the quote's own afterlife (barely noticed in 1992, ubiquitous after 2009-2010) as a case study in how the West constructs "China planned this" narratives after the fact. Klinger's "Rorschach test" framing is a ready-made analytical hook.
- The Baotou tailings lake — "dozens of pipes," black chemical sludge, thorium-laced dust blowing onto villages, a farmer's memory of watermelon and aubergine fields — is strong, scene-ready material, assuming the writer runs down and confirms the original source (see caveat above) before quoting.
- The 8-million-tons-of-tailings-per-year figure at Bayan Obo, alongside the 112-million-tonne cumulative rare-earth-oxide resource, gives a clean "here is the actual scale of the bargain China made" contrast for the chapter's environmental-cost argument.
- The China Rare Earth Group formation (Dec 23, 2021, in Ganzhou) — five entities folded into one SASAC-controlled giant — is a clean, well-documented "state consolidates the commanding heights" scene with real corporate names, ownership percentages, and a specific date and place.
- The 2010 Japan "embargo" episode, especially its **disputed factual basis**, is excellent material for a chapter about strategic ambiguity — China may have gained enormous coercive reputation from an event that, per the trade-data record, might not have been a formal embargo at all. That gap between perception and (uncertain) reality is itself a "long game" story worth telling.
- The 2014 WTO loss → China dropping formal quotas → China pivoting to export licensing/entity lists → the October 2025 extraterritorial control regime → the Nov 2025 Busan stand-down is a clean 11-year throughline of adaptive Chinese trade-policy strategy, with primary sources at both ends (USTR 2014, CSET/MOFCOM 2025).

### Counterevidence / complications (Ch. 15)

- **Julie Klinger's direct scholarly pushback** against the "Deng masterplan" narrative is itself the single most important complication for this chapter: a serious historian argues that reading 1992-era China as executing a deliberate 30-year plan to weaponize rare earths is **"ahistorical"** — the industry's rise had more to do with decentralized provincial industrial policy, price competition, and environmental arbitrage than a unified top-down strategic vision. The book should not present the "long game" as an uncontested fact — it should show its work, cite Klinger, and let the reader weigh the "deliberate strategy" vs. "emergent/opportunistic outcome" interpretations.
- **The disputed 2010 Japan embargo** (above) is direct counterevidence against a too-clean "China has always successfully wielded rare earths as a weapon" narrative — the founding incident of that narrative is contested by trade-data analysis.
- **The 2014 WTO loss** is itself counterevidence that China's rare-earth dominance has not gone unchecked — international law forced a real policy reversal (dropping the formal quota system), even if China adapted its methods afterward.
- **China Rare Earth Group and China Northern Rare Earth Group compete with each other domestically** (they are separate SOE giants, not a single monopoly), and provincial/local government incentives historically fostered significant illegal/unlicensed "black market" mining that Beijing has struggled to fully control — worth a caveat that "China Inc." is not perfectly unified even in this strategically vital sector (referenced in passing in FPRI's "China's Rare Earth Metals Consolidation and Market Power," https://www.fpri.org/article/2022/03/chinas-rare-earth-metals-consolidation-and-market-power/, via search synthesis; recommend direct read).
