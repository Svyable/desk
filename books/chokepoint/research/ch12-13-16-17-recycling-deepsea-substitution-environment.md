## CHAPTER 12 — "The Recycling Illusion and Reality" (battery recycling / urban mining)

### Key facts and sources

- IEA models the "excess" battery-recycling capacity buildout: in Europe and the US, announced recycling capacity dissipates its overcapacity after 2030 and covers only ~30% of feedstock needs by 2040; in India, coverage is just ~10% by 2040. — "Recycling of Critical Minerals," IEA, executive summary, https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary — Use: shows recycling capacity is being built well ahead of the actual scrap supply. Limitation: exact publication date not confirmed from this pull; check the report's cover page for the release year before citing.
- IEA: by 2040, secondary (recycled) production could supply up to ~12% of total cobalt demand, ~7% of nickel, and ~5% of lithium and copper — rising to 20–30% of lithium/nickel/cobalt demand by 2050 if collection rates improve. In an ambitious climate-aligned scenario, recycling could cut 2050 primary demand by 25% for lithium and nickel and 40% for cobalt. — "The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions," IEA, https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/reliable-supply-of-minerals and executive summary page — Use: the core "how much can recycling realistically deliver" figure set for the chapter's spine argument. Limitation: figures are model-scenario outputs, not measured outcomes; different IEA reports (2023 "Role of Critical Minerals" vs. 2025 "Global Critical Minerals Outlook") give somewhat different numbers — reconcile which report year underlies which number before publishing.
- IEA's newer 2025 outlook: recycling could supply 10–15% of critical minerals demand by 2035, with cobalt recycling rates reaching ~30% under ambitious scenarios. — "Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025," IEA, published May 21, 2025, https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025 (referenced via https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/beyond-nmc-batteries-supply-chain-issues-for-emerging-battery-technologies) — Use: most recent IEA baseline figure to cite as "as of 2025." Limitation: confirm exact wording/number directly on IEA site — this was surfaced via a LinkedIn summary layer as well as the IEA site itself; go to the primary IEA page before quoting a number.
- Redwood Materials (founded by former Tesla CTO/co-founder JB Straubel in 2017) recovered/processed over 20 GWh of lithium-ion batteries a year at its Nevada campus and produced more than 60,000 metric tons of critical materials in 2025; held an estimated ~70% share of US battery recycling volume in 2024. Expected 2025 feedstock mix: ~15 GWh of battery-factory production scrap, ~5 GWh of EV batteries, ~1 GWh of consumer electronics. — "2025: A defining year for Redwood," Redwood Materials company blog, https://www.redwoodmaterials.com/news/2025-a-defining-year-for-redwood/; also Washington Post, "The quest to make American EV batteries without destructive mining," 2025, https://www.washingtonpost.com/climate-solutions/interactive/2025/battery-recycling-ev-mining-redwood-materials/ — Use: central company profile/anecdote for the chapter (see Narrative section). Limitation: company-sourced production/market-share figures — cross-check the 70% market-share claim against an independent source (e.g., trade press) before using as a hard stat; note the founder-narrative angle (ex-Tesla insider) is itself a useful frame but is company PR, not neutral analysis.
- Redwood's origin case: began by recycling battery-factory scrap from Panasonic's cell production at the Tesla Gigafactory Nevada — starting with a pilot of "more than 400 pounds" of scrap, scaled to 2 tons, then to a full supply relationship in which Panasonic began using Redwood-recovered copper foil in new cells — described as the first "closed-loop" battery production process of its kind. — Electrek, "Panasonic turns to JB Straubel's Redwood for recycled battery materials to supply Tesla Giga Nevada," Jan 4, 2022, https://electrek.co/2022/01/04/panasonic-jb-straubels-redwood-for-recycled-battery-materials-supply-tesla-giga-nevada/; also Forbes, same date, https://www.forbes.com/sites/alanohnsman/2022/01/04/panasonic-to-make-tesla-battery-cells-with-recycled-material-from-jb-straubels-redwood/ — Use: strong concrete anecdote showing recycling starting with manufacturing scrap, not end-of-life batteries — directly supports the "too young to recycle" argument.
- Li-Cycle (once a leading North American lithium-ion battery recycler, Toronto-listed, backed by Glencore with a $275M investment) filed for bankruptcy protection: Canadian entity under the CCAA (Companies' Creditors Arrangement Act) and US subsidiaries under Chapter 15 (not Chapter 11) in the US Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, in May 2025 — after having halted construction of its flagship Rochester, NY "Hub" facility roughly two years earlier and failing to find a buyer. Glencore then entered a "stalking horse" credit bid of at least $40 million for Li-Cycle's equity and assets, including the partly-built Rochester hub. — Canary Media, "Li-Cycle's quest to recycle lithium-ion batteries ends in bankruptcy," 2025, https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/recycling-renewables/li-cycles-quest-to-recycle-lithium-ion-batteries-ends-in-bankruptcy; Resource Recycling, "Li-Cycle files for bi-national bankruptcy, seeks buyer," June 26, 2025, https://resource-recycling.com/e-scrap/2025/06/26/li-cycle-files-for-bi-national-bankruptcy-seeks-buyer/; SEC 8-K filing, Li-Cycle Holdings Corp, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1828811/000119312525122542/d892872dex991.htm — Use: central cautionary case for the chapter — a well-funded, high-profile "spoke and hub" recycler that failed. Get the exact Rochester construction-halt date and Glencore's total prior investment figure directly from SEC filings or Reuters/Bloomberg before publishing the number "$275 million," which appeared only in a secondary AI-summarized search result and needs primary confirmation.
- Umicore (Belgium) currently recycles about 7,000 tonnes of batteries per year at its existing Hoboken, Belgium facility, but delayed its planned ~$525 million, 150,000-tonne/year greenfield battery-recycling plant (originally targeted for 2026 startup) to no earlier than 2032, citing weaker EV demand growth and market conditions (mid-2024 announcement). — Recycling Today, "Umicore retains recycling as key activity," https://www.recyclingtoday.com/news/umicore-2030-plan-ev-battery-precious-metals-recycling-europe/; Resource Recycling, "Market factors combine to shift Umicore's capital plans," April 10, 2025, https://resource-recycling.com/e-scrap/2025/04/10/market-factors-combine-to-shift-umicores-capital-plans/ — Use: important counterevidence/complication — even Europe's most established battery recycler is scaling back because there isn't enough feedstock yet. Verify exact dollar figure and delay date against Umicore's own investor releases.
- UN's Global E-waste Monitor 2024: the world generated 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022 (up 82% from 34 million tonnes in 2010); e-waste is growing by about 2.6 million tonnes/year and is projected to reach 82 million tonnes by 2030; only 22.3% of 2022's e-waste mass was documented as formally collected and recycled; e-waste generation is rising five times faster than documented e-waste recycling; an estimated $62 billion worth of recoverable natural resources goes unaccounted for annually. — "Global e-Waste Monitor 2024," UNITAR/ITU press release, https://unitar.org/about/news-stories/press/global-e-waste-monitor-2024-electronic-waste-rising-five-times-faster-documented-e-waste-recycling; full report landing page: https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-D/Environment/Pages/Publications/The-Global-E-waste-Monitor-2024.aspx; also https://ewastemonitor.info/the-global-e-waste-monitor-2024/ — Use: the primary "e-waste as mineral source" statistics for the chapter — go to the ITU/UNITAR PDF itself for the battery-specific and critical-minerals-specific breakdown (the topline figures above are for all e-waste, not batteries alone; the report does contain more granular data on lithium-ion batteries and critical raw materials in e-waste that should be pulled directly).
- Chemistry variability and degradation differences are a structural obstacle to recycling economics: different lithium-ion cathode chemistries fail differently (e.g., LCO degrades mainly via irreversible lithium loss from the lattice; NMC — especially high-nickel formulations — degrades via more complex transition-metal dissolution), which complicates uniform, high-value "direct recycling" approaches and pushes much of the industry toward cruder pyrometallurgical/hydrometallurgical smelting recovery instead. — "Understanding materials failure mechanisms for the optimization of lithium-ion battery recycling," Nature Reviews Materials, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41578-025-00783-5 — Use: technical grounding for the "chemistry variability" constraint named in the assignment.
- Feedstock timing problem: recyclers face a "battery drought" likely lasting until at least the early 2030s because most lithium-ion battery mass currently entering recycling streams is manufacturing scrap, not end-of-life packs; EV battery packs (e.g., Tesla Model 3) are engineered for 300,000–500,000 miles of life, and fewer than 10% of EVs reach end-of-life before 10 years old. Circular Energy Storage projections cited: North American recycling capacity could be 183% overcapacity by 2030, and if all globally announced recycling projects come online as scheduled, global recycling capacity in 2030 could be seven times the available feedstock. — Washington Post, "EV Battery Recycling Has Boomed Too Soon," Feb 22, 2023, https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/energy/ev-battery-recycling-has-boomed-too-soon/2023/02/22/ea5e75a6-b2b7-11ed-94a0-512954d75716_story.html — Use: this is the single best explicit source for the "current battery stock is too young to recycle at scale" argument the assignment specifically asked for. Limitation: 2023 article — check for an updated 2025/2026 restatement of the Circular Energy Storage overcapacity numbers, since capacity plans have shifted (several projects, including Umicore's and Li-Cycle's, have since been delayed or collapsed, which would move these ratios).

### Narrative material
- JB Straubel: Tesla co-founder and former CTO leaves Tesla to found Redwood Materials (2017), builds it into the dominant US recycler by physically co-locating next to the Gigafactory Nevada — a literal "closed loop" between battery maker and battery recycler, starting from a scrap-recovery pilot of a few hundred pounds and scaling to tens of thousands of tons. Strong "insider turns skeptic/circular-economy evangelist" character arc for a scene.
- Li-Cycle's Rochester, NY "Spoke & Hub" facility: construction halted roughly two years before the May 2025 bankruptcy filing; a half-built industrial plant as a physical symbol of overpromised recycling capacity. Glencore — the same commodities trader with deep ties to primary cobalt/copper mining — ends up bidding to acquire the recycler's carcass, a resonant image of the "recycling doesn't replace mining, mining just buys the recycler" dynamic.
- Umicore's Hoboken, Belgium plant — Europe's oldest/most established battery and precious-metals recycling site — still capped at 7,000 tonnes/year even as the company shelves a 150,000-tonne "world's largest" battery recycling plant until at least 2032.
- The UN e-waste figure of "$62 billion in recoverable resources unaccounted for annually" is a vivid one-line statistic for framing the scale of the missed opportunity in general e-waste (note: this figure is for e-waste broadly, not lithium-ion batteries specifically — do not conflate).

### Counterevidence / complications
- Even the best-resourced, most vertically-integrated recycler (Umicore) is delaying capacity by six years, and a well-capitalized, Glencore-backed pure-play recycler (Li-Cycle) went bankrupt — direct evidence against a simple "recycling will solve the minerals shortage" narrative.
- IEA's own modeling shows recycling capping out around 5–12% of demand by 2040 for most minerals even in reasonably optimistic scenarios — recycling is a supplement, not a substitute, for primary mining in the medium term.
- The overcapacity data (recycling capacity potentially 7x available feedstock by 2030 per Circular Energy Storage estimates cited in the Washington Post) is itself a counter-counter-argument: it suggests the "recycling gap" narrative may be overstated in the *near* term because too much capacity was built too early, not too little — a useful complication showing the industry mistimed itself in both directions (overbuilt capacity relative to feedstock in 2023-2025, while still not being enough relative to 2040+ demand).

## CHAPTER 13 — "Deep Sea and Desperate Measures" (polymetallic nodule mining)

### Key facts and sources

- The Metals Company (TMC), via US subsidiary TMC USA, applied to NOAA in April 2025 for exploration licenses and a commercial recovery permit to begin mining polymetallic nodules in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (CCZ) — initially focused on a ~25,000 km² area (NORI-D). — Mongabay, "The Metals Company applied to the U.S. for a deep-sea mining license," April 2025, https://news.mongabay.com/2025/04/the-metals-company-applied-to-the-u-s-for-a-deep-sea-mining-license/; TMC investor press release, https://investors.metals.co/news-releases/news-release-details/world-first-tmc-usa-submits-application-commercial-recovery-deep — Use: the inciting event for the chapter.
- TMC subsequently filed a consolidated application expanding the requested commercial recovery area to roughly 65,000 km², citing an estimated resource of 619 million tonnes of wet nodules plus a potential exploration upside of another 200 million tonnes. — TMC investor press release, "TMC USA Files First Consolidated Deep-Seabed Mining Application, Increasing Expected Commercial Recovery Permit Area to 65,000 km²," https://investors.metals.co/news-releases/news-release-details/tmc-usa-files-first-consolidated-deep-seabed-mining-application — Use: scale figure; note this is company-reported resource data, not independently verified reserves — flag as such if quoted.
- Legal mechanism: TMC applied under the US Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA) of 1980, which lets US entities explore/recover deep-sea minerals in international waters under domestic law. The US never ratified UNCLOS and is not an ISA member — which is precisely why TMC is able to route around the ISA via NOAA/US domestic law instead. — Wikipedia summary corroborated by CIMSEC, "Strategic Minerals and the False Promise of Seabed Mining," https://cimsec.org/strategic-minerals-and-the-false-promise-of-seabed-mining/; EJIL:Talk!, "Reclaiming Authority: Forcing Seabed Mining Contractors to Choose between the ISA and the USA," https://www.ejiltalk.org/reclaiming-authority-forcing-seabed-mining-contractors-to-choose-between-the-isa-and-the-usa/ — Use: legal/geopolitical crux of the chapter. Get primary text of DSHMRA and UNCLOS Part XI for direct citation.
- On April 24, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order "Unleashing America's Offshore Critical Minerals and Resources," directing federal agencies (Commerce, Defense, Energy, Interior, State) to expedite permitting and cooperation to support deep-sea mining under DSHMRA, in both US and international waters, with a 60-day (June 23, 2025) deadline for initial agency reports. NOAA characterized this as "the next gold rush." — NOAA press release, "'The next gold rush': President Trump unlocks access to critical deep seabed minerals," https://www.noaa.gov/news-release/next-gold-rush-president-trump-unlocks-access-to-critical-deep-seabed-minerals; CSIS analysis, "Trump's Deep-Sea Mining Executive Order: The Race for Critical Minerals Enters Uncharted Waters," https://www.csis.org/analysis/trumps-deep-sea-mining-executive-order-race-critical-minerals-enters-uncharted-waters; Inside Climate News, "Trump's New Executive Order Promotes Deep Sea Mining in US and International Waters While Bypassing International Law," May 18, 2025, https://insideclimatenews.org/news/18052025/trump-promotes-deep-sea-mining-bypassing-international-law/ — Use: the direct-quote NOAA "gold rush" framing is a strong narrative hook; verify exact EO title/number against the Federal Register or govinfo.gov (source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/DCPD-202500523/html/DCPD-202500523.htm).
- ISA Secretary-General Leticia Carvalho publicly stated that TMC's move to seek a US permit "violate[s] international law and undermine[s] the principle of the seabed as the common heritage of humankind" under UNCLOS. — Referenced in search aggregation of ISA statements; corroborate directly via ISA press releases at https://isa.org.jm or Reuters/AP wire coverage before quoting Carvalho verbatim — treat this as a paraphrase to be verified against a direct primary quote, not a confirmed direct quotation.
- As of mid-2025, dozens of countries have called for a moratorium or precautionary pause on deep-sea mining — reported counts vary by source and date: 24 countries (as of a 2023 ISA meeting), 37 countries (mid-2025, including the UK, per Pew and DLA Piper), 46 countries (per one aggregation), and approximately 40 countries as of December 2025. France, Germany, Chile, and Palau are named repeatedly as leading moratorium advocates. — Pew Charitable Trusts, "Why Deep-Seabed Mining Needs a Moratorium," June 2025, https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/fact-sheets/2025/06/why-deep-seabed-mining-needs-a-moratorium; Mongabay, "'Madness': World leaders call for deep-sea mining moratorium at UN ocean summit," June 2025, https://news.mongabay.com/2025/06/madness-world-leaders-call-for-deep-sea-mining-moratorium-at-un-ocean-summit/; Greenpeace UK ISA briefing, https://www.greenpeace.uk/resources/briefing-international-seabed-authority-deep-sea-mining/ — Use: important to reconcile the exact country count and date for each figure before publishing — the number has been rising over time (24 → 37/46 → ~40), so pin the count to a specific named event/date (e.g., "as of the UN Ocean Conference in Nice, June 2025").
- Sixty companies — including Panasonic, BMW, Renault, and others — have publicly committed not to source minerals from deep-sea mining, backing a moratorium. — Greenpeace/aggregated reporting; verify directly via a WWF or Business & Human Rights Resource Centre corporate-commitment tracker before citing as fact. — Use: shows industry-side skepticism, useful counterweight to "the world needs these minerals" framing.
- Independent scientific criticism: Alex Rogers, professor of biology at Oxford University and Science Director at Ocean Census, has criticized TMC for trying to "subvert a democratic process" and push governments toward accepting commercial-scale mining ahead of adequate science. — Referenced via aggregated search result citing reporting on TMC; verify direct quote against original outlet (likely Guardian/Mongabay/Reuters coverage of ISA meetings) before using as a direct quotation.
- Environmental research on the CCZ: sediment plumes from nodule collection could bury benthic organisms, clog filter-feeders' respiratory structures, and dilute/pollute the natural particulate food supply; researchers estimate 53% of zooplankton taxa and 60% of micronekton taxa at proposed discharge depths are particle feeders vulnerable to plume disruption. Most prior environmental-impact research focused on the seafloor (benthic) ecosystem and largely ignored the mid-water (pelagic) environment through which discharge plumes pass. — "Deep-sea mining discharge can disrupt midwater food webs," Nature Communications, 2025, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65411-w; "Extent of impact of deep-sea nodule mining midwater plumes is influenced by sediment loading, turbulence and thresholds," Communications Earth & Environment, https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-021-00213-8 — Use: primary peer-reviewed environmental-risk evidence base for the chapter.
- Long-term field evidence: a 1979 test-mining disturbance in the CCZ was revisited by researchers and found still visible in seafloor sediment 44 years later; however, a separate long-term plume study found no significant reduction in animal abundance in plume-affected areas versus control sites, though geochemical analysis showed altered sediment composition (reduced organic carbon, elevated trace metals) — nuanced, mixed findings rather than a simple "catastrophic and permanent" story. — The Watchers, "Long-term impacts of deep-sea mining remain visible 44 years after test in the Pacific," May 2025, https://watchers.news/2025/05/04/deep-sea-mining-impacts-clarion-clipperton-zone/; underlying research summarized in Frontiers in Marine Science review, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2025.1598584/full — Use: this is genuine, important COUNTEREVIDENCE — not all findings support "irreversible catastrophe"; some plume impacts appear more limited/localized than originally feared, even as seafloor disturbance tracks (mining scars) persist for decades. Good material for a nuanced treatment rather than a one-sided "deep sea mining = ecocide" narrative.

### Narrative material
- Gerard Barron, Australian CEO of The Metals Company: coined/popularized the phrase "a battery in a rock" for polymetallic nodules, reportedly first used at a mining investment conference in Cape Town in 2019, holding up a black potato-sized nodule for a journalist. Calls nodule mining "the easiest way to solve climate change." — Referenced across multiple outlets (Time, Pulitzer Center, Forbes); pin down and verify the exact original quote/date via a primary interview or conference video before using as a direct quotation in prose. Strong scene material: an executive selling deep-ocean extraction using the same techno-optimist rhetoric as EV/climate advocates.
- Greenpeace activists scaled and occupied a TMC-contracted mining/exploration vessel in the Pacific in protest; TMC responded by threatening Greenpeace with an injunction. — Greenpeace International press release, "The Metals Company threatens Greenpeace with injunction but activists continue protest and scale their ship in the Pacific," https://www.greenpeace.org/international/press-release/63856/the-metals-company-threatens-greenpeace-with-injunction-but-activists-continue-protest-and-scale-their-ship-in-the-pacific/ — Use: vivid direct-action scene for the chapter; verify the date and location details against the primary release.
- Nodules themselves have an evocative origin story: they form over millions of years as minerals accrete around a nucleus that is sometimes a shark's tooth or fossilized fish bone — a striking image (ancient deep-ocean detritus slowly becoming a "battery").
- TMC is a publicly traded, cash-burning company (SEC 10-K filings show ongoing losses/going-concern-type disclosures); a March 2024 Forbes headline framed the company bluntly: "This Deep-Sea Mining Company Will Sweep The Ocean Floor For Battery Materials—If It Doesn't Go Broke First," https://www.forbes.com/sites/christopherhelman/2024/03/26/this-deep-sea-mining-company-will-sweep-the-ocean-floor-for-battery-materials-if-it-doesnt-go-broke-first/ — Use: financial precarity as a narrative thread — TMC's regulatory end-run may be driven as much by corporate survival pressure as by mineral strategy; check TMC's 10-K (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1798562/000110465926037863/tmc-20251231x10k.htm) for exact cash-runway figures.

### Counterevidence / complications
- The "no significant reduction in animal numbers" finding at some plume-affected CCZ sites (Communications Earth & Environment / Frontiers review, above) complicates a simple environmental-catastrophe narrative, even as it confirms lasting geochemical and seafloor-scarring effects.
- Sixty major manufacturers (Panasonic, BMW, Renault, etc.) refusing to buy seabed minerals is evidence that the market itself does not (yet) consider deep-sea nodules necessary or acceptable — undercutting the "we desperately need this to meet demand" framing TMC uses.
- The US bypass strategy is legally contested and may not even secure TMC a bankable, internationally-recognized title — the ISA and most nations dispute the legitimacy of a US-only permit for international waters, meaning TMC could win a domestic permit but still be unable to sell nodules into markets that require ISA-recognized sourcing (e.g., EU-based buyers under emerging due-diligence rules). This is an important complication to flag for the chapter's ending — "approval" doesn't necessarily equal a workable business.

## CHAPTER 16 — "The Substitution Race" (materials science responses to scarcity)

### Key facts and sources

- CATL launched its Naxtra sodium-ion battery brand in April 2025 and announced it would begin mass production of Naxtra sodium-ion EV battery packs by the end of 2025, with customer deliveries starting in 2026 and confirmed large-scale, multi-sector deployment for 2026. Specs: ~175 Wh/kg specific energy (versus up to ~185–205 Wh/kg for competing LFP), ~500 km range for the passenger EV version, 10,000+ cycle life, and an operating range of -40°C to +70°C. — CATL, "Naxtra Battery Breakthrough & Dual-Power Architecture," https://www.catl.com/en/news/6401.html; Charged EVs, "CATL plans to mass-produce Naxtra sodium-ion EV battery packs by the end of 2025," https://chargedevs.com/newswire/catl-plans-to-mass-produce-naxtra-sodium-ion-ev-battery-packs-by-the-end-of-2025/; CarNewsChina, "CATL confirms 2026 large-scale sodium-ion battery deployment in multiple sectors," Dec 28, 2025, https://carnewschina.com/2025/12/28/catl-confirms-2026-large-scale-sodium-ion-battery-deployment-in-multiple-sectors/ — Use: the central sodium-ion commercialization case study for the chapter — this is real, verifiable, and dated, unlike many earlier "sodium-ion is coming soon" claims that didn't pan out on schedule.
- IEA analysis on sodium-ion: energy density is at least ~30% lower than mainstream lithium-ion; the latest sodium-ion cells reach up to ~175 Wh/kg, versus up to ~205 Wh/kg for LFP and ~255 Wh/kg for NMC; sodium-ion vehicles currently see roughly 350 km range versus 400–600 km for lithium-ion equivalents; sodium's larger ionic radius creates more mechanical stress and faster electrode degradation; production currently emits more greenhouse gases per unit of stored energy than lithium-ion because more material must be processed for equivalent capacity; supply chains for battery-grade sodium materials remain underdeveloped. — IEA, "Sodium-ion battery momentum grows, but challenges remain," https://www.iea.org/commentaries/sodium-ion-battery-momentum-grows-but-challenges-remain — Use: the essential counterweight/complication to the CATL hype — an authoritative source quantifying sodium-ion's real limitations. Confirm exact publication date on the IEA site.
- LFP (lithium iron phosphate — cobalt- and nickel-free) batteries reached roughly 40% of the global EV battery market by GWh in 2024 (up from about 10% in 2020), and various 2025 estimates put global share above 50–55%. Regional variation is stark: LFP hit ~64% of China's EV battery deployment in Q4 2024 and over 50% share in Southeast Asia, Brazil, and India in 2024, while in the US, LFP deployment share hit a new low of just ~5% of total GWh in a recent quarter, and in the EU LFP was just over 10% of demand. LFP cells run roughly 30% cheaper per kWh than NMC (nickel-cobalt-manganese) cells. — Adamas Intelligence, "LFP now commands 40% of the global EV battery market," https://www.adamasintel.com/lfp-now-commands-40-of-the-global-ev-battery-market/; IEA, "Electric vehicle batteries," Global EV Outlook 2025, https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-batteries and Global EV Outlook 2026, https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries — Use: this is the strongest, most concrete "substitution is already happening at scale" data point in the whole brief — cobalt- and nickel-free chemistry displacing nickel/cobalt chemistries at real, measured market-share levels, with a clear China-vs-US-vs-EU divergence worth exploring (why has the US lagged so far behind on LFP adoption?). Reconcile the 40% (Adamas, 2024) vs. "over 55%" (2025, unclear original source) figures directly against the IEA Global EV Outlook 2025/2026 reports before publishing a single headline number.
- Tesla's 2023 Investor Day (March 1, 2023): VP of Powertrain Engineering Colin Campbell announced that Tesla's next-generation permanent-magnet drive unit would use "no rare earth materials at all," and disclosed that Tesla had already cut rare-earth use in the Model 3 powertrain by 25% since 2017. Tesla did not disclose the intended substitute material at the time (analysts speculated ferrite magnets), nor a firm production start date. — Electrek, "Tesla is going (back) to EV motors with no rare earth elements," March 1, 2023, https://electrek.co/2023/03/01/tesla-is-going-back-to-ev-motors-with-no-rare-earth-elements/; Adamas Intelligence, "Implications: Tesla Announces Next Generation Rare-Earth-Free PMSM," https://www.adamasintel.com/tesla-rare-earth-free-motor/; Tesla 2023 Investor Day SEC 8-K exhibits, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1318605/000156459023000002/tsla-ex992_7.htm — Use: this is the exact fact requested in the assignment — verify the direct Campbell quote against the Tesla Investor Day webcast transcript/SEC exhibit rather than relying solely on secondary press paraphrase.
- GM Ventures partnered with Niron Magnetics (announced November 2023) to co-develop "Clean Earth Magnet" motor technology — an iron-nitride-based permanent magnet technology from Niron claimed to be free of rare-earth and other "critical materials" entirely. A separate, associated downstream US magnet-and-metals manufacturing plant (in Texas, tied to a different company — USA Rare Earth — under a GM sales agreement) targeted 1,000 tonnes/year capacity live in 2025, commercial-scale production by early 2026, and eventual capacity around 4,800 tons/year. — GM news release, "GM Ventures and Niron Magnets pursue sustainable EV motor magnets," Nov 8, 2023, https://news.gm.com/home.detail.html/Pages/news/us/en/2023/nov/1108-niron.html; USA Rare Earth SEC filings (10-K FY2025), https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1970622/000197062226000021/usar-20251231.htm — Use: GM's rare-earth-substitution case study. Limitation: be careful not to conflate the GM/Niron iron-nitride magnet partnership with the separate GM/USA Rare Earth conventional (still rare-earth-based) magnet supply deal — these are two different GM strategies (substitute chemistry vs. secure domestic rare-earth supply) and should be described as distinct in the book text.

### Narrative material
- CATL's shift from "sodium-ion is a lab curiosity" (early-2020s framing) to a named commercial product line ("Naxtra") with confirmed 2025-2026 mass production and multi-sector deployment is a good structural beat: an idea moving from press-release vaporware to an actual production timeline within about three years.
- Tesla's Colin Campbell delivering the rare-earth-free motor announcement live at the March 2023 Investor Day — a scripted, stage-managed reveal with essentially no technical specifics disclosed — is a useful scene of "the announcement as strategy," particularly given China's dominance of rare-earth processing and the geopolitical subtext (Tesla signaling supply-chain independence from China without committing to how).
- GM's dual-track hedge — investing in both a genuinely rare-earth-free magnet technology (Niron) and a separate domestic conventional rare-earth-magnet supply chain (USA Rare Earth/Texas plant) — is a good illustration of how automakers are hedging across multiple, sometimes contradictory strategies rather than betting on one clean substitution story.
- The sharp US/China/EU divergence in LFP adoption (64% China vs. ~5% recent-quarter US share) is itself a strong narrative device: substitution is not just a materials-science story, it's a story about which country's supply chains and consumer preferences (US buyers historically favor longer range, hence NMC) shape which chemistry wins.

### Counterevidence / complications
- Sodium-ion's real, quantified shortcomings (lower energy density, shorter range, higher current manufacturing emissions, immature battery-grade material supply chains per IEA) are a direct, sourced counterweight to any narrative that sodium-ion is an imminent, frictionless lithium replacement — it is scaling into a specific niche (low-cost/stationary storage/short-range vehicles), not yet displacing lithium chemistries broadly.
- Tesla's 2023 rare-earth-free motor announcement was explicitly light on technical and timeline detail — as of the assignment's date (2026), verify from Tesla's subsequent filings/teardown reporting (e.g., independent teardown analyses of the Tesla 2023 Model 3/Y "Highland" or newer drive units) whether the rare-earth-free motor has actually shipped in volume, or whether the 2023 announcement has proven partly aspirational — this is an important overhype-check the book should run down with a follow-up search closer to drafting, since our search results above did not surface confirmation of shipped rare-earth-free Tesla motors at scale.
- GM/Niron: as of this research pass, no confirmed setback or delay was found specifically for the Niron partnership (unlike Umicore's recycling delay or Li-Cycle's collapse) — but note that a 2023 Niron executive projection suggested EV-ready magnets "as soon as 2025," and no 2025/2026 confirmation of actual GM vehicle deployment was found in this search; flag for a follow-up search closer to drafting to check whether this timeline slipped (a plausible pattern given how often "critical minerals substitute" timelines have slipped elsewhere in this research, e.g., Umicore, Li-Cycle, GM/LG Ultium delays reported elsewhere in industry press).

## CHAPTER 17 — "Water, Waste, and the Hidden Costs" (environmental/social costs of mining)

### Key facts and sources

- Brumadinho dam disaster: On January 25, 2019, a tailings dam at Vale S.A.'s Córrego do Feijão iron-ore mine near Brumadinho, Minas Gerais, Brazil, collapsed, releasing roughly 10 million cubic meters of liquefied mine waste. The disaster killed 272 people (including two stillbirths); the dam had originally been built in 1976 by Ferteco Mineração and was acquired by Vale in 2001. — "The 2019 Brumadinho tailings dam collapse: Possible cause and impacts of the worst human and environmental disaster in Brazil," peer-reviewed study, ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0303243420300192; Wikipedia summary corroborating figures, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumadinho_dam_disaster — Use: the chapter's central case study, exactly as specified in the assignment. Note: death-toll figures varied slightly across early news reports (259 confirmed vs. 270 vs. 272 including stillbirths) as search-and-recovery continued for months — use the peer-reviewed, retrospective academic figure (272, including 2 stillbirths) as the authoritative number, and note in-text that the toll rose over time from initial reports.
- Technical cause: the primary cause was seepage erosion ("piping") — water gradually penetrating the tailings fill, causing internal erosion and a "liquefaction" failure of the dam structure; satellite radar (InSAR) measurements showed the dam had subsided up to 30 cm in the 12 months before collapse, a detectable warning sign in hindsight. — Same ScienceDirect study as above, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0303243420300192; also Mongabay retrospective, "Scientists now know how the Brumadinho dam disaster happened, and the lessons to learn," Feb 2024, https://news.mongabay.com/2024/02/scientists-now-know-how-the-brumadinho-dam-disaster-happened-and-the-lessons-to-learn/ — Use: technical mechanism explanation; the pre-collapse satellite subsidence data is a strong "the warning signs were there" narrative element.
- Financial settlement: Vale agreed (signed February 4, 2021) to pay 37.7 billion reais (approximately US $7 billion) to the state of Minas Gerais and affected communities, to be disbursed over 10 years — R$6.3 billion already disbursed by February 2021, R$11.4 billion earmarked for implementation projects, and R$19.9 billion to public authorities; reporting noted only about 30% of the total was designated to go directly to Brumadinho itself, and affected communities/victims protested being excluded from the settlement negotiations, later petitioning Brazil's Federal Supreme Court to overturn the deal. — MercoPress, "Mining giant Vale signs US$7bn settlement deal over Brumadinho disaster," Feb 5, 2021, https://en.mercopress.com/2021/02/05/mining-giant-vale-signs-us-7bn-settlement-deal-over-brumadinho-disaster-that-killed-more-than-270-people; CNN Business, same date, https://www.cnn.com/2021/02/05/business/brazil-vale-brumadinho-settlement-intl-hnk/index.html — Use: shows both scale of corporate liability and the contested, imperfect nature of settlement/justice for affected communities — good complication material.
- Criminal accountability: In January 2020, Minas Gerais state prosecutors filed criminal charges — including aggravated homicide (270 counts) — against 16 individuals (former Vale executives and employees, plus TÜV SÜD auditors who had certified the dam's stability) and separate environmental-crime charges against Vale S.A. itself. As of 2024-2025, the criminal case was still in pre-trial/early hearing stages (hearings reported starting Feb 23, with proceedings expected to continue until 2027); separately, in Brazilian Securities Commission (CVM) administrative proceedings, one former Vale director (Poppinga) was voted to be fined roughly R$27 million (~US$4.97 million) while former CEO Fábio Schvartsman was voted to be acquitted on grounds that dam oversight fell outside his direct executive responsibilities — that CVM proceeding was still unresolved/suspended pending additional votes as of the most recent reporting found. — Business & Human Rights Resource Centre, multiple articles, https://www.business-humanrights.org/en/latest-news/brasil-audi%C3%AAncias-criminais-sobre-desastre-de-brumadinho-come%C3%A7am-com-17-r%C3%A9us-e-seguem-at%C3%A9-2027/ and https://www.business-humanrights.org/en/latest-news/brazil-state-judge-accepts-homicide-charges-against-vale-and-t%C3%BCv-s%C3%BCd-employees-including-former-vale-ceo-over-dam-collapse/; Both ENDS, "CVM trial of Vale Executives over Brumadinho Dam Collapse brings new momentum," https://www.bothends.org/en/Whats-new/News/CVM-trial-of-Vale-Executives-over-Brumadinho-Dam-Collapse-brings-new-momentum/ — Use: this is important, nuanced, and still-unfolding — six-plus years after the disaster, criminal accountability remains unresolved, a strong complication/theme for the chapter (corporate accountability moves far slower than the disaster's human toll).
- Global tailings dam failure tracking: an academic global database compiled records of 505 tailings dam incidents from 1915 to 2022 (cause, time, location, and other factors coded). Separately, the Global Tailings Portal (built by GRID-Arendal with UN Environment Programme support, part of the Church of England Pensions Board/Swedish Council on Ethics-led Investor Mining and Tailings Safety Initiative launched after Brumadinho) is the first publicly accessible global database of individual mine tailings storage facilities, covering 1,938 tailings dams worldwide (self-reported by mining companies). — Phys.org, "Public database of mine tailings storage facilities launched to prevent dam failures," Jan 2020, https://phys.org/news/2020-01-database-tailings-storage-facilities-failures.html; "Regional Distribution and Causes of Global Mine Tailings Dam Failures," academic paper referenced via ResearchGate, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360915025_Regional_Distribution_and_Causes_of_Global_Mine_Tailings_Dam_Failures — Use: gives the chapter a "this is a recurring, tracked global pattern, not a one-off Brazilian tragedy" frame, exactly as the assignment requested. Limitation: the Global Tailings Portal is built on company self-disclosure, which is itself worth noting as a limitation of transparency in the industry; go to https://tailing.grida.no (or successor URL) directly to confirm current record counts, since these numbers grow as more disclosures are added.
- Lithium brine water use in Chile's Atacama: an oft-cited figure (attributed to Dr. Ingrid Garcés, University of Antofagasta) is that roughly 2 million liters of water evaporate per tonne of lithium produced; more recent/technical peer-reviewed estimates put water intensity for battery-grade lithium carbonate production in the Salar de Atacama in the range of roughly 500,000 to ~2,000 m³ (i.e., up to ~2 million liters) per tonne, depending on methodology and system boundaries. The two dominant lithium miners in the Salar de Atacama together extract more than 63 billion liters of brine per year, at a rate of nearly 2,000 liters per second; local groundwater levels have reportedly fallen more than 10 meters over the past 15 years. — Mongabay, "As lithium mining bleeds Atacama salt flat dry, Indigenous communities hit back," Dec 2024, https://news.mongabay.com/2024/12/as-lithium-mining-bleeds-atacama-salt-flat-dry-indigenous-communities-hit-back/; peer-reviewed source, "Water footprint of battery-grade lithium production in the Salar de Atacama, Chile," ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959652624040848; NRDC commentary, "Lithium Mining Must Not Dry Up the Atacama Desert," https://www.nrdc.org/bio/amanda-maxwell/lithium-mining-must-not-dry-atacama-desert — Use: central water-conflict case study requested in the assignment. Limitation: water-use-per-tonne figures for lithium brine operations vary widely across sources (500,000 L to 2 million L per tonne) depending on whether "water" means freshwater consumption specifically or total brine extraction/evaporation — the peer-reviewed ScienceDirect paper is the most defensible number to lead with; treat the "2 million liters" figure as an upper-bound/illustrative figure attributed to a named academic, not a universal constant.
- A 2025 Mongabay report notes newer, less water-intensive lithium extraction methods (e.g., direct lithium extraction, DLE) exist and are being piloted, which "leaves severe impacts" framing alongside "but new methods exist" — useful nuance. — Mongabay, "Lithium mining leaves severe impacts in Chile, but new methods exist: Report," Sept 2025, https://news.mongabay.com/2025/09/lithium-mining-leaves-severe-impacts-in-chile-but-new-methods-exist-report/ — Use: counterevidence/complication — the water-intensity problem is not necessarily permanent or chemistry-locked; DLE technology could reduce the water footprint substantially if it scales (a useful parallel to the Chapter 16 substitution theme).
- Indigenous land/critical minerals overlap, global: a mapping of 5,097 critical mineral projects worldwide found that 69% of existing or planned projects are located on or near Indigenous peoples' lands, and researchers separately estimate that over half of the world's known critical mineral reserves sit on or near Indigenous lands — with projected demand for critical minerals rising roughly 500% by 2050. — NPR, "Demand for minerals sparks fear of mining abuses on indigenous peoples lands," Jan 29, 2024, https://www.npr.org/2024/01/29/1226125617/demand-for-minerals-sparks-fear-of-mining-abuses-on-indigenous-peoples-lands — Use: the global-scale statistic the assignment asked for on land/indigenous rights. Limitation: confirm the "69%" figure and its underlying dataset/methodology directly (it is attributed in secondary reporting to an academic mapping study, likely published in Nature Sustainability or similar — track down and cite the primary paper rather than the NPR paraphrase for the book's endnotes).
- Australia case study: a peer-reviewed mapping study found 57.8% of critical mineral projects in Australia lie within formally recognized Indigenous lands, rising to 79.2% if land under undetermined native title claims is included; the study covered 14 commodities including cobalt and lithium, and found Indigenous communities were negatively affected by land dispossession, biodiversity loss, water pollution, soil degradation, and lost livelihoods; historically Australian First Nations have often not received fair compensation or benefit-sharing from resource extraction on their territories. — "Mapping critical minerals projects and their intersection with Indigenous peoples' land rights in Australia," Energy Research & Social Science / ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214629624001476; Mongabay, "Study: More than half of Australia's clean energy mines lie on Indigenous land," June 2024, https://news.mongabay.com/2024/06/study-more-than-half-of-australias-clean-energy-mines-lie-on-indigenous-land/ — Use: a good national-level case study with an actual peer-reviewed source (PDF available at https://assets.nationbuilder.com/fncen/pages/5365/attachments/original/1736469981/1-s2.0-S2214629624001476-main.pdf) — stronger sourcing than the global 69% NPR figure, so consider leading with the Australia study and citing the global figure as supporting context.

### Narrative material
- Brumadinho: the physical scale detail — 10 million cubic meters of liquefied waste, enough to bury a company cafeteria full of workers eating lunch at the time of collapse (many victims were Vale employees on their lunch break in an on-site cafeteria when the wave hit) — is a devastating, concrete scene; verify the cafeteria detail directly against primary reporting (e.g., Reuters/AP contemporaneous coverage, or the Wikipedia article's cited sources) before using, since it wasn't independently re-confirmed in this search pass.
- The InSAR satellite data showing 30 cm of dam subsidence in the year before collapse is a strong "the evidence was in the sky, and no one acted" narrative device — pairs well with a broader "chokepoint" book theme about visible warning signs ignored under commercial pressure.
- Atacama: groundwater decline of "more than 10 meters in 15 years" and brine extraction of "nearly 2,000 liters per second" (63 billion liters/year) give the chapter vivid, comparable-to-everyday-life numbers (roughly the volume of tens of thousands of Olympic swimming pools per year) for a resource most readers have never seen.
- The parallel between Chapter 16 (substitution optimism) and Chapter 17 (DLE as a possible lower-water lithium extraction method) offers a natural cross-chapter throughline: is technology solving these problems, or just moving where the costs land?

### Counterevidence / complications
- Vale's criminal accountability process remains unresolved 6+ years after the disaster (hearings extending to 2027, a CVM administrative vote left suspended), while the $7B civil settlement was itself contested by victims as inadequate and negotiated without their full participation — complicates any simple "justice was done" or "justice was denied" framing; the reality is a slow, partial, contested process.
- IRMA (Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance) certification is a real, growing counterexample to "all mining is equally destructive": major automakers (Mercedes-Benz, since 2021, requiring IRMA-audited sourcing for cobalt/lithium/nickel/graphite/manganese/copper in new supply contracts; Volkswagen, citing completed IRMA audits of Albemarle and SQM lithium operations in Chile) are using third-party responsible-mining certification as a procurement requirement, and the EU has moved to recognize IRMA as a leading standard. — IRMA, "Recognition of IRMA," https://responsiblemining.net/about/recognition/; IUCN NL, "EU recognizes IRMA as best standard for responsible mining," https://www.iucn.nl/en/news/eu-recognizes-irma-as-best-standard-for-responsible-mining/ — Use: this is exactly the "successful mine remediation / responsible mining certification" counterevidence the assignment asked for — note, though, that IRMA is a voluntary, auditor-based certification, not a guarantee, and its Albemarle/SQM Chile audits are specifically relevant to the Atacama lithium case above (worth a direct cross-reference: are the same operations criticized for water depletion also IRMA-audited? — a rich complication to chase down at drafting time).
- The Mongabay report on newer, lower-water DLE lithium extraction methods (Sept 2025, above) is direct evidence that the water-intensity problem, while real and currently severe, is not necessarily a permanent feature of lithium extraction — a useful complication against over-generalizing from current Atacama brine-pond methods to all future lithium production.
