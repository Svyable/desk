# Chapter 18 research — mineral wealth is a conversion problem, not a verdict

## Claim under test

Chapter 18 argues that the useful question is not whether a country has been "blessed" or "cursed" by mineral wealth. The better test is what happens to a depleting natural asset after extraction: how much value is captured, whether boom revenue is mistaken for permanent income, what durable assets or capabilities are created, and who bears costs that never appear in the national fiscal balance.

This framing should survive two hard cases: a country widely treated as a resource-curse success story that remains vulnerable to its dominant commodity, and a country with sophisticated fiscal institutions whose rules still do not eliminate political and macroeconomic pressure.

## Botswana: strong institutions did not abolish concentration risk

Botswana remains an important counterexample to deterministic versions of the resource curse. Diamond revenues supported decades of unusually strong growth and state-building, and the country's management of its mineral rents has long compared favorably with many commodity exporters.

But the latest IMF evidence makes the chapter's narrower point clearer: institutional competence can improve the conversion of rents without making the underlying export concentration disappear.

The IMF's 2025 Article IV staff report says Botswana's economy contracted 3% in 2024 as mining output fell 24% and diamond trading fell 34%. The report attributes the deterioration to weak natural-diamond demand, record inventories, competition from lab-grown diamonds, and delayed diversification. It also notes that fiscal and external buffers had already been eroding before the latest shock.

The same report treats lab-grown diamonds as a possible structural rather than merely cyclical challenge. It says their share exceeded one-fifth of global diamond jewellery sales in 2024 and notes that retail prices can be far below comparable natural stones. The important analytical point is not the exact future market share. It is that a technological substitute can attack the revenue base of a country that managed the original resource unusually well.

### What this does and does not show

Botswana is not evidence that good institutions do not matter. It is evidence against the stronger claim that good institutions can make commodity dependence cease to be dependence. Fiscal prudence, bargaining capacity, equity ownership, and public investment can determine how much social value a boom creates. They cannot guarantee that the commodity's demand curve will cooperate indefinitely.

That distinction strengthens the manuscript's existing line: "Good institutions can convert resource rents into public wealth. They cannot make a concentrated export base cease to be concentrated."

## Chile: a stabilization fund is a timing machine, not a permanent shield

Chile provides a second useful boundary because the Economic and Social Stabilization Fund can be checked directly against Ministry of Finance data.

As of June 2026, Chile's Ministry of Finance reported an ESSF market value of US$3.884 billion. Since the fund's creation in 2007 it had received US$27.763 billion in contributions and recorded US$28.293 billion in withdrawals, while net investment results generated roughly US$4.413 billion.

The withdrawal history matters more than the ending balance. The fund absorbed large drawdowns during the global financial crisis, the pandemic period, and later fiscal shocks. That is exactly what a stabilization fund is for: it lets revenue earned in one year finance needs in another.

But Chile's 2026 IMF Article IV consultation is useful counterevidence to any triumphalist version of this story. The IMF still calls for rebuilding fiscal buffers and projects structural fiscal deficits and rising public debt over the medium term. A fiscal rule and a sovereign fund can discipline commodity revenue; neither removes the politics of spending, revisions to structural assumptions, or the need to replenish buffers after they are used.

## Strongest counterargument

A skeptic could argue that this "conversion" framing risks becoming unfalsifiable: every success can be called good conversion and every failure bad conversion. That would merely replace "resource curse" with another catch-all phrase.

The way to avoid that is to name observable mechanisms before judging outcomes. For a mineral boom, ask separately:

- how much rent the state actually captures after costs and allowances;
- whether revenue rules distinguish temporary commodity income from permanent tax capacity;
- whether debt rises against optimistic price assumptions;
- whether public investment creates productive assets that remain useful after the boom;
- whether local environmental liabilities are carried on anyone's balance sheet;
- whether export and fiscal concentration decline over time rather than only being discussed as policy goals.

Those tests can fail even in countries with respected institutions, and they can improve in countries with weak starting conditions.

## Strongest defensible formulation

Mineral wealth is neither destiny nor free money. Extraction converts one asset into another stream of claims: wages, taxes, royalties, profits, foreign exchange, infrastructure, debt service, environmental liabilities, and sometimes institutional capability. The development question is whether the durable assets and capabilities left behind are worth more than the natural asset depleted and the liabilities created.

Botswana shows why successful rent management should not be confused with successful diversification. Chile shows why a credible fiscal framework should not be confused with a permanently full buffer. Together they make the resource-curse chapter more precise: the relevant mechanisms are observable, reversible, and vulnerable to new prices, technologies, and political choices.

## What would make this framing wrong?

Revise this boundary if Botswana materially diversifies its export and fiscal base while diamond dependence falls without sacrificing growth, or if Chile's fiscal framework rebuilds buffers durably enough that commodity-price swings cease to be a meaningful source of budget pressure. More broadly, the conversion framing would be weakened if cross-country evidence showed that institutional and balance-sheet mechanisms explain little once geology itself is controlled for.
