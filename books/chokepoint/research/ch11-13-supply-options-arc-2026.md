# Chapters 11–13 — Supply Options, Clocks, and the Cost of Escape

Checked 2026-09-04 for a future revision of Chapters 11–13: “Boom, Bust, Ghost Town,” “The Recycling Illusion and Reality,” and “Deep Sea and Desperate Measures.”

## Shared argument under test

These chapters work best as one escalating sequence rather than three independent warnings:

1. Chapter 11: new primary supply is slow because fixed capital is built against volatile future prices.
2. Chapter 12: recycling is a real alternative supply source, but its feedstock arrives on the product-lifetime clock rather than the mine-development clock.
3. Chapter 13: when both terrestrial development and secondary supply seem too slow, deep-sea nodules offer another possible route — but one whose legal, ecological, processing, and financing infrastructure is not yet equivalent to commercial supply.

The shared claim should therefore be narrower than “every solution creates another problem.” The stronger version is: **each supply option runs on a different clock, and resilience depends on having options whose constraints are not perfectly correlated.**

That formulation preserves the book’s skepticism without making every hedge sound futile.

## Chapter 11 — Let Kitsault carry more of the argument

The Kitsault material is unusually good because the same deposit demonstrates two different vetoes across time: the early-1980s mine/town arrived into a collapsing molybdenum market; decades later a permitted redevelopment still stalled when price and financing did not support construction.

The chapter currently explains the capital-cycle lesson several times after the example has already established it. A future prose pass can probably cut some of the abstract restatement around:

- “high prices contain the seed of low prices”;
- post-bust discipline becoming future scarcity;
- dormant projects as options;
- government support as strategic insurance.

Keep the countercase: good deposits can survive downturns, brownfield infrastructure retains option value, and a bust does not prove the original investment decision was irrational. The point is timing mismatch, not managerial stupidity.

### Handoff to Chapter 12

End closer to the practical question: if new primary capacity repeatedly arrives on the wrong side of a commodity cycle, can already-mined material provide a supply response on a different clock?

## Chapter 12 — Recycling is a timing hedge, not an escape hatch

The existing chapter’s strongest empirical structure remains sound.

IEA’s *Recycling of Critical Minerals* reports that manufacturing scrap still supplies about two-thirds of available battery-recycling feedstock in 2030. End-of-life EV and storage batteries become the largest source only from around 2035 and exceed 90% of available feedstock by 2050.

The capacity mismatch is especially useful because it resembles Chapter 11 without simply repeating it: if all announced projects arrive, global battery-recycling capacity in 2030 could be about seven times available feedstock. By 2040, available feedstock could instead exceed announced capacity by about 60%. In Europe and the United States, announced capacity covers only about 30% of 2040 feedstock in that project-pipeline view.

Source: IEA, *Recycling of Critical Minerals*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary

A 2026 IEA update strengthens the long-run case rather than weakening it. *Battery Circularity* estimates roughly 1.2 million EV batteries could reach end of life in 2030 and about 14 million in 2040. The relevant above-ground resource is therefore becoming materially larger; it is simply delayed.

Source: IEA, *Battery Circularity*, 29 April 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/battery-circularity

### Strongest counterargument

Do not let “feedstock arrives late” become an argument that recycling is marginal. IEA’s 2026 Critical Minerals Outlook says secondary supply could roughly double its contribution by 2040, while recycling rates across key energy minerals could rise from about 10% today toward 20% under current policy settings.

The harder boundary is industrial concentration. The same 2026 outlook puts China at more than three-quarters of global battery pretreatment capacity and about 90% of material-recovery capacity. Recycling can diversify the source of atoms while leaving the conversion step concentrated.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

### Editorial implication

The chapter does not need to keep proving that recycling is neither magic nor useless. State the stock-and-flow constraint once, let the seven-times/60%-short numbers do the work, and move sooner to the strategic question: **where will the returned material be processed when it finally arrives?**

That question also improves the handoff to Chapter 13. Both chapters concern potential resources that are physically present but not yet equivalent to usable supply.

## Chapter 13 — Keep “application is not permit,” and sharpen “resource is not route”

The chapter’s fast-moving U.S. regulatory paragraph is current as of this check.

NOAA’s Deep Seabed Hard Minerals Mining page, last updated 19 August 2026, lists no existing commercial recovery permits. It lists The Metals Company USA’s consolidated application for an exploration license and commercial recovery permit, with Federal Register notice posted 19 August 2026 and public comment running through 19 October 2026. NOAA separately lists other exploration applications and existing Lockheed Martin exploration licenses.

Source: NOAA Ocean Service, *Deep Seabed Hard Minerals Mining*.
https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/deep-seabed-mineral-resources/deep-seabed-mining/

So the manuscript’s distinction remains important: a commercial-recovery application is evidence of institutional movement, not evidence of operating commercial supply.

The ISA description is also current. In March 2026, the Council discussed 29 of 32 outstanding Mining Code issues and reached agreement in principle on several, including an Equalisation Mechanism and Compliance Committee. ISA still describes completion of the Mining Code within the multilateral framework as a prerequisite for future commercial activity in the Area.

Sources:
- ISA, 31 March 2026 Council update: https://isa.org.jm/news/the-council-of-the-international-seabed-authority-advanced-negotiations-on-the-mining-code/
- ISA media Q&A: https://isa.org.jm/faq-for-media/

### Strongest counterargument

The case for nodules should not be caricatured as “the abyss is empty.” Avoiding some terrestrial overburden, tailings, freshwater use, land clearing, and community displacement is a real comparative argument. The correct comparison is between portfolios of impacts, not between deep-sea disturbance and an imaginary zero-mining world.

But the strategic case also needs the same discipline applied to recycling and terrestrial projects: material on a map is not qualified supply. Collection vessels, processing, permits, environmental monitoring, capital, customers, and downstream qualification all sit between a nodule and a factory.

### Editorial implication

Chapter 13 currently accumulates many short aphoristic restatements of that point. Preserve the concrete regulatory and ecological evidence, but trust it sooner. The line to carry forward is not “there is no frictionless frontier” as a universal moral. It is more specific:

**A new resource matters strategically only when it creates a second usable route rather than another potential source waiting on the same missing capabilities.**

## Three-chapter architecture for the prose pass

A future direct edit should make the clocks increasingly visible:

- **Chapter 11 — capital clock:** price changes in weeks; mines and towns are built over years.
- **Chapter 12 — product-life clock:** recycling plants can be built before enough products have died; later the feedstock can outrun announced capacity.
- **Chapter 13 — institutional/ecological clock:** policy can accelerate applications faster than commercial proof, environmental baselines, or multilateral rules can mature.

This gives the middle-late book a stronger narrative escalation. The reader sees three ways to obtain supply and three different reasons each can arrive late.

The payoff is not pessimism. Different clocks are precisely why a portfolio can be resilient. A brownfield mine, secondary feedstock, substitution, stockpiles, and a genuinely new primary source do not need to solve the whole problem individually. Their value is greatest when one can respond while another is still waiting.

## What would make this false?

- Mine development and financing could become materially faster, reducing the capital-clock mismatch.
- Battery lifetimes, collection systems, manufacturing scrap, or policy could produce usable secondary feedstock faster than current IEA assumptions.
- Recycling processing could diversify faster than the current concentration data imply.
- NOAA or the ISA could move from application/rulemaking to approved commercial recovery, and operating evidence could demonstrate that deep-sea supply scales faster and with more manageable impacts than current uncertainty suggests.
- Substitution could reduce demand for some of the minerals discussed enough that none of these supply clocks becomes binding.

Those are not edge cases. They are the developments the manuscript should invite the reader to watch.
