# Chapters 1–3 — Foundation arc research and editorial boundaries (2026)

## Purpose

The opening three chapters already contain the right facts and most of the right counterevidence. The risk is architectural rather than conceptual: Chapter 1 establishes that strategic vulnerability can sit in processing and licensing, Chapter 2 re-explains that criticality is not the same thing as scarcity, and Chapter 3 again explains that mineral dependence differs from oil. A future manuscript pass should make these chapters feel cumulative rather than recursive.

The cleanest sequence is:

1. **Chapter 1 — classification by interruption:** a small, obscure input becomes strategically important when permission can be inserted into a concentrated supply chain.
2. **Chapter 2 — classification by system dependence:** official “critical” lists are policy judgments about what the economy cannot easily replace, and overlapping demand makes those judgments dynamic.
3. **Chapter 3 — classification by transmission mechanism:** oil shocks mainly raise the cost of using existing capital; mineral shocks mainly raise the cost of building new capital. The analogy survives, but on a different clock.

That gives the opening a spine of **permission → dependence → transmission**.

## Chapter 1 — The List of Seventeen

### What is already strong

The April 4, 2025 Chinese export-control announcement is an excellent opening because it lets the reader encounter the supply chain as paperwork rather than as an abstract concentration statistic. The distinction between a licensing regime and a blanket embargo is especially important and should remain explicit.

The chapter also earns its strongest analytical move: mining share and downstream share are different measurements. The book should continue to resist any generic sentence such as “China controls X percent of rare earths” when the underlying figure could mean mining, refining, separation, metal/alloy production, or magnets.

### 2026 boundary to sharpen

The IEA’s 2026 outlook strengthens the chapter’s processing argument while also giving it better counterevidence. It reports that refining concentration rose for most key minerals in 2025, but rare earths were the notable exception: new U.S. projects and higher Malaysian production produced a modest decline in rare-earth refining concentration.

That is useful because it turns diversification from a slogan into a falsifiable proposition. Concentration can fall when operating capacity appears outside the incumbent system.

At the same time, the IEA says planned diversified rare-earth capacity remains structurally uneven: by 2035, announced refining/separation outside dominant suppliers is equivalent to only about two-thirds of expected mined supply, while planned magnet capacity is about one-third. Another mine is therefore not the same thing as another complete route.

The chapter should not imply that China’s current industrial position is chemically inevitable. A better boundary is:

> processing concentration is durable because capability compounds, but the 2025–2026 record already shows that targeted investment can move the number.

That is stronger than saying dominance is permanent and more useful than saying diversification is impossible.

### Editorial cut to consider later

Chapter 1 currently proves several versions of the same point: a chokepoint can exist without a shortage; concentration is not coercion; licensing is not prohibition; reserves are not usable products. All four distinctions matter, but they do not need equal rhetorical weight.

The chapter can probably trust the April 2025 episode to carry more of the argument and reduce some of the later abstract restatement. The strongest sentence-level principle is not “power sits at the difficult step” by itself; it is the concrete fact that a license can interrupt a factory even when ore remains abundant in the ground.

### What would make this false?

- Rare-earth refining and magnet capacity outside China scales faster than current project pipelines imply.
- New separation plants consistently reach commercial yields, customer qualification, and survivable costs without long-lived policy support.
- Export licensing proves operationally routine enough that firms stop treating it as a material financing or inventory risk.

## Chapter 2 — Everything Runs on Rock

### Official criticality is dynamic, not geological

The U.S. Geological Survey’s final 2025 list contains 60 critical mineral commodities. Fifty were carried over from the 2022 list and ten were new; copper was among the additions.

That is one of the chapter’s best pieces of evidence because the element did not change. The administrative judgment did.

The safest formulation is that “critical mineral” is a policy classification built from economic importance and disruption risk, not a natural category of the periodic table. The chapter should preserve this because it inoculates the whole book against scarcity theater.

### Update the demand argument with 2026 evidence

The IEA’s 2026 outlook says demand for critical minerals continues to grow strongly across scenarios, almost doubling to 2040 under stated policies. Lithium grows more than threefold; nickel, graphite, and rare-earth demand rise roughly 50%–90%; copper adds about 7 million tonnes and remains the largest absolute volume increase.

The more important update is that the supply picture is not uniformly worsening. Copper’s projected 2035 deficit narrowed from about 30% in the 2025 outlook to about 25% in the 2026 outlook as projects advanced. Lithium’s balance also improved. Cobalt moved the opposite way after the DRC introduced export quotas.

This is excellent counterevidence for the chapter’s central claim. “Critical” does not mean “permanently getting scarcer.” The system changes because projects, policy, chemistry, and demand respond.

A useful sentence for a later prose pass would be:

> A critical-mineral list is not a prophecy of shortage. It is a map of where the cost of being wrong has become politically important.

### Shared demand needs one deeper mechanism

Chapter 2 already has many examples of EVs, grids, data centers, wind, defense, appliances, and industrial systems meeting upstream in the same material markets. The risk is montage.

Rather than adding more sectors, a future edit should deepen one recurring mechanism: **electrification converts unrelated end uses into competitors for electrical infrastructure and its materials.**

That is why copper is the most useful bridge into Chapter 3 and Chapter 4. AI and EVs need not compete for the same finished product to compete for the same grid expansion, transformer capacity, cabling, and copper pipeline.

The IEA’s 2026 market overview also gives a useful recent boundary: the energy sector accounted for around 75% of demand growth across key energy minerals in 2025, up from 70% in 2024. That supports the overlap thesis without implying that every tonne is consumed by climate or AI applications.

### What would make this false?

- Demand growth is revised materially downward across several mineral systems at once.
- Substitution sharply reduces mineral intensity faster than infrastructure demand expands.
- Recycling contributes enough secondary supply soon enough to prevent new-build demand from tightening primary markets.
- New capacity is geographically and industrially diversified rather than merely larger.

## Chapter 3 — The Oil Analogy, Examined

### Keep the analogy, change the clock

The chapter’s stock-versus-flow distinction is the strongest conceptual bridge in the first three chapters. The 2026 MIT CEEPR paper by Adrien Concordel, Phuong Ho, and Christopher Knittel gives it a formal mechanism.

Their model distinguishes oil-price shocks, which raise the cost of using existing capital, from critical-mineral-price shocks, which raise the cost of creating new capital. Both reduce output and welfare in the model, but oil shocks are systematically more contractionary; mineral shocks produce larger adjustments in investment, capital formation, and external borrowing, with smaller and more gradual aggregate losses.

This should not be inflated into a universal empirical law. It is a model result under stated assumptions, not proof that every mineral shock will be mild. A rare-earth licensing interruption can still be acute for a firm whose qualified inventory runs out.

The useful boundary is:

> oil scarcity tends to attack the installed machine; mineral scarcity often attacks the next machine.

That sentence is memorable only if the chapter has already shown the investment mechanism.

### Strategic reserves: preserve the form problem

The U.S. Department of Energy’s history confirms the direct line from the 1973–74 embargo to the Energy Policy and Conservation Act signed on December 22, 1975, which authorized the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. Oil could be stored in Gulf Coast salt caverns near refineries and distribution infrastructure.

Mineral stockpiles are harder because the bottleneck may be specification, qualification, or processing stage rather than raw tonnage. This is where the oil analogy should remain practical rather than rhetorical: the correct reserve is the form the downstream user can actually consume during a disruption.

The IEA’s 2026 work on strategic minor minerals reinforces this point. Small markets can create disproportionate economic risk, and diversification may require relatively modest absolute investment compared with bulk commodities. That means “too small for the market to duplicate” can itself be a strategic vulnerability.

### Strongest counterargument

The chapter should continue to resist the temptation to make minerals sound like a gentler version of oil. The MIT result is economy-wide. Distribution matters.

A mineral shock that produces a smaller aggregate contraction can still halt one aerospace line, delay one grid build, or strand one factory investment. If the constrained item is tiny relative to final-product value and difficult to qualify quickly, the local effect can be severe even when GDP barely moves.

That is the bridge into the rest of the book: aggregate macroeconomic severity and strategic industrial severity are not the same measurement.

### What would make this false?

- Empirical evidence from actual mineral disruptions shows broad operating-cost shocks much closer to oil than the investment-cost mechanism suggests.
- Recycling, inventories, substitution, and alternative qualification make mineral supply shocks consistently short-lived enough that capital formation is barely affected.
- Conversely, highly concentrated processing failures propagate through existing capital so broadly that the stock-versus-flow distinction stops being useful in practice.

## Shared editorial implication

The opening three chapters should avoid making the reader learn the same caveat three times.

Chapter 1 should own **processing and administrative leverage**.

Chapter 2 should own **dynamic criticality and overlapping demand**.

Chapter 3 should own **the transmission mechanism and the limits of the oil analogy**.

Where a later chapter needs an earlier caveat, a short callback is stronger than a full re-explanation.

The opening run is already most human when it begins with an object or institutional act—the April export list, the changing U.S. list, Baghdad in 1960—and only then widens toward theory. A future prose pass should preserve those concrete anchors and cut repeated abstract morals before adding any new rhetorical machinery.
