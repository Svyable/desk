## CHAPTER 4: "Copper: The Original Chokepoint"

### Key facts and sources

- Chile's copper mine production in 2025 was approximately 5.3 million metric tons, roughly 23–24% of world mined copper output, making it the world's largest copper-producing country. Chile has held this rank "uninterruptedly since 1983." — USGS, *Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026* (copper chapter), pub. ~Jan/Feb 2026, https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-copper.pdf ; cross-referenced via search snippet citing USGS's Chile country page, https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/chile. **[search-snippet only; pull the PDF directly for the exact table figure — snippets gave 23% and 24% in different searches, likely 2024 vs. 2025 data, so verify the precise year-by-year %.]** Use for: the "roughly a quarter of world supply" opening stat of the chapter.

- Chile's 1971 copper nationalization: Law 17,450, enacted July 11, 1971, under President Salvador Allende, unanimously approved by the Chilean Congress, transferring full ownership of the country's large-scale copper mines (previously majority US-owned, principally Kennecott and Anaconda) to the state. Allende called it "the wage of Chile" ("el sueldo de Chile"). — *Wikipedia*, "Chilean nationalization of copper," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilean_nationalization_of_copper — cross-check against a peer-reviewed/primary source: Sebastian Edwards (NBER), "Copper in Chile, 1970–1973," NBER Working Paper w33572, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33572/w33572.pdf. Use the NBER paper as the citable academic source for the book; Wikipedia is fine for orientation only. Note: Codelco itself (as a formal corporate entity) was established from Law 16,425 (Jan 25, 1966, under Eduardo Frei Montalva, "Chileanization") and reorganized/consolidated as Codelco proper in 1976 after the nationalized firms were merged — so the "1971 nationalization" and "Codelco's founding" are related but not identical events; the chapter should get this sequencing right.

- Compensation dispute: Chile's 1971 nationalization is cited as "one of the earliest cases of 'nationalization without compensation'" in the broader sense (Allende's government controversially deducted "excess profits" from compensation owed to US firms, effectively zeroing out payments to Anaconda and Kennecott), though Chile had earlier paid Kennecott $92.9 million for a partial stake in El Teniente under the preceding "Chileanization" deal. — synthesized from Wikipedia/NBER search snippets; **verify exact compensation mechanics against Edwards NBER paper before using as a specific claim**, since this is a legally/historically contested point (Allende-era "excess profits" deduction vs. Pinochet-era settlement).

- Codelco's 2025 copper production came in at roughly 1.33–1.34 million metric tons (1.332–1.334 Mt cited across sources), a shortfall against Codelco's long-term target of 1.7 million tonnes by 2030, which the company has said is now unlikely to be met on schedule. — Reuters/Mining.com, "Codelco to lower 2025 copper target after El Teniente accident," https://www.mining.com/web/codelco-pegs-el-teniente-output-at-316000t-after-deadly-collapse/ ; corroborating figure via search snippet citing Chilean media, https://discoveryalert.com.au/chile-copper-production-2026-strategic-landscape/. Use for: Codelco's underperformance narrative.

- Codelco's gross debt reached approximately $26 billion (net debt ~$25.1 billion), with a liabilities-to-equity ratio of 3.27 versus roughly 1.0 for major international mining peers, and net debt/EBITDA of 3.8x versus 0.7x for global mining peers and 0.5x for the Chilean mining industry generally. Direct cash costs (C1) were reported as 57% higher than major international mining companies and 72% higher than other Chilean copper operations. — Kitco News (wire), "Codelco debt and high costs hurt competitiveness, document says," July 3, 2026, https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2026-07-03/codelco-debt-and-high-costs-hurt-competitiveness-document-says. **[search-snippet only — this is a strong, specific claim; fetch the article directly (accessible via normal browser, blocked only in this sandbox) before quoting numbers in the book.]** Use for: the "state company in financial trouble" thread.

- In August 2026, Chile's government (under President José Antonio Kast) allowed Codelco to retain and reinvest 100% of its 2025 profits — approximately $2.4–2.42 billion — for the first time in roughly 50 years, rather than transferring them to the national treasury, as an emergency capitalization measure. — Bloomberg, "Chile Gives Struggling Copper Giant Codelco a $2.4 Billion Boost," Aug 10, 2026, https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-10/chile-gives-struggling-copper-giant-codelco-a-2-4-billion-boost ; UPI, "Chile lets state copper miner keep profits for first time in 50 years," Aug 12, 2026, https://www.upi.com/Top_News/World-News/2026/08/12/Chile-lets-state-copper-miner-keep-profits-for-first-time-in-50-years/2711786551029/. Use for: the state-company-in-crisis scene; strong, concrete, recent.

- Escondida (BHP, 57.5% ownership; Chile, Atacama Desert) is the world's largest copper mine and largest producer of copper concentrate/cathode. FY2025 (BHP fiscal year ending June 2025) production rose 16% year-on-year to its highest level in 17 years, driven by record concentrator throughput and higher feed grade (1.02% vs. 0.88% in FY2024) plus first production from the "Full SaL" leaching project in Q4 FY2025. H1 FY2025 output rose 22%, contributing to a first-half total of 987,000 tonnes. — BHP SEC filings (Form 6-K/20-F, FY2025), https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/811809/000119312525183071/d35528d6k.htm and https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/811809/000119312525185641/d941636d20f.htm ; corroborated by Mining.com, "BHP now expects nearly 2M tonnes copper production after record Escondida throughput," https://www.mining.com/bhp-now-expects-nearly-2m-tonnes-copper-production-after-record-escondida-throughput/. Use for: the BHP/Escondida scale narrative — these SEC filings are primary sources and directly citable.

- BHP announced a $2 billion investment to expand/optimize the Escondida concentrator, the first phase of a decade-long investment strategy for the mine announced in November 2024. — Mining-Technology, "BHP to invest $2bn in Chile's Escondida copper mine," https://www.mining-technology.com/news/bhp-chile-escondida-copper-mine/. - **El Teniente 2025 fatal accident (verified via multiple independent outlets):** On the afternoon of July 31, 2025, a magnitude 4.2 (Chilean national seismological center) / 5.0 (USGS) seismic event centered roughly 37 km northeast of Rancagua triggered a localized rock-burst/collapse in the "Andesita" sector of Codelco's El Teniente mine, roughly 400–500 meters from the epicenter's underground position. One worker died immediately; five others were trapped roughly 70 hours before all were confirmed dead — bringing the total death toll to six, with nine additional workers injured. Chile's government declared three days of national mourning. — ABC News (Australia), Aug 4, 2025, https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-08-04/chile-national-mourning-declared-after-el-teniente-mine-collapse/105610652 ; PBS NewsHour, "Collapse at Chile's major copper mine kills 1 worker and leaves 5 missing," https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/collapse-at-chiles-major-copper-mine-kills-1-worker-and-leaves-5-missing ; Reuters (via TradingView aggregation), "Codelco ends El Teniente rescue effort after five miners found dead," https://tr.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com%2C2025%3Anewsml_L6N3TV01L%3A0-codelco-ends-el-teniente-rescue-effort-after-five-miners-found-dead ; Wikipedia entry (orientation only, corroborates dates/figures), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_El_Teniente_mining_accident. This is a strong, multiply-corroborated, dramatic scene for the chapter — recommend pulling the PBS and Reuters wire copy directly for exact quotes and rescue-effort chronology.

- The accident cut Codelco's 2025 output guidance: El Teniente lost an estimated 33,000 metric tons of copper production, equivalent to roughly $340 million in lost revenue/value; Codelco's total 2025 guidance was revised down from 1.37–1.40 million tonnes (March 2025 estimate) to 1.34–1.37 million tonnes, with El Teniente's 2025 output re-forecast at 316,000 tonnes. — Reuters (via Mining.com), "Codelco to lower 2025 copper target after El Teniente accident," https://www.mining.com/web/codelco-pegs-el-teniente-output-at-316000t-after-deadly-collapse/. Use for: quantifying the human-cost-to-balance-sheet link.

- El Teniente is described by multiple sources (Guinness World Records category cited, mining trade press) as the world's largest underground copper mine: in operation since 1905 (originally developed by the U.S.-based Braden Copper Company), located at ~2,300 m elevation in the Andes, with a tunnel network exceeding 2,400–3,000 km, yielding historically more than 400,000 metric tons of refined copper per year, and Codelco's largest single division. — search snippets citing Britannica ("El Teniente | Copper Mine, Mining, Andes"), https://www.britannica.com/place/El-Teniente ; Mining.com feature, https://www.mining.com/worlds-largest-underground-mine-looks-like-38052/. - Copper prices hit record highs in 2026: Comex/LME copper futures reached an all-time record around $6.71/lb (roughly $14,500+/tonne on the three-month LME contract) in late August 2026, with spot prices around $6.51–6.59/lb in early September 2026 — up roughly 43% year-over-year and about 14% since the start of 2026. — TradingEconomics commodity page (search-snippet), https://tradingeconomics.com/commodity/copper ; Mining.com, "Copper price holds near record as London warehouse bidding war looms," https://www.mining.com/copper-price-holds-near-record-as-london-warehouse-bidding-war-looms/. **[search-snippet only — these are exactly the kind of volatile, fast-moving figures that need a same-day re-check before print; treat as directional/illustrative, re-verify exact price at time of writing.]**

- U.S. Section 232 copper tariffs: In July 2025, the Commerce Department, under a Section 232 national-security investigation, found copper imports threaten U.S. national security; in August 2025, President Trump imposed a 50% tariff on imports of semi-finished copper products (pipes, wire, rods, sheets, tubes) and copper-intensive derivatives (cables, connectors) — refined/cathode copper itself was initially exempted. Effective April 6, 2026, the tariff structure was modified to apply 50% to the full customs value of semi-finished copper products and 25% to the full value of copper-intensive derivative products. A further phased universal tariff on refined copper has reportedly been under consideration, starting at 15% on January 1, 2027 and rising to 30% on January 1, 2028. — Congressional Research Service, "Section 232 National Security Tariffs on Copper Imports," https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12614 (primary/authoritative — use this as the anchor citation) ; White & Case client alert, "President Trump orders 50 percent Section 232 tariff on copper imports," https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/president-trump-orders-50-percent-section-232-tariff-copper-imports ; White & Case follow-up, "United States modifies steel, aluminum, and copper Section 232 tariffs," https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/united-states-modifies-steel-aluminum-and-copper-section-232-tariffs. Use the CRS product as the most citable non-law-firm source; note CRS "IN" documents are short "Insights" memos, official and nonpartisan.

- Copper demand from AI data centers and electrification: S&P Global forecasts global copper demand rising from ~28 million tonnes (2025) to more than 42 million tonnes by 2040; data centers alone consumed an estimated 1.1 million metric tons of copper in 2025, projected to reach 2.5 million tons/year by 2040. A single large AI data center can require up to 50,000 tonnes of copper; total global data-center copper demand was projected to reach ~475,000 tonnes annually by 2026. The IEA has separately warned of a global copper supply deficit that could reach as high as 30% by 2035. — S&P Global, "Copper in the Age of AI: Challenges of Electrification," full report Jan 2026, https://www.spglobal.com/content/dam/spglobal/global-assets/en/special-reports/copper-in-the-age-of-ai/Copper%20in%20the%20Age%20of%20AI_Full%20Report_January%202026.pdf (this is the primary S&P Global report — get the PDF directly; strongest single source for the chapter's "AI needs copper" argument) ; Forbes, "Data Centers Could Gobble Half A Million Tons Of Copper A Year By 2030," Dec 8, 2025, https://www.forbes.com/sites/greatspeculations/2025/12/08/data-centers-could-gobble-half-a-million-tons-of-copper-a-year-by-2030/. Also: IEA, "Data centre electricity use surged in 2025..." news item, https://www.iea.org/news/data-centre-electricity-use-surged-in-2025-even-with-tightening-bottlenecks-driving-a-scramble-for-solutions — electricity demand from data centers rose 17% in 2025 (vs. 3% global electricity demand growth), with AI-focused data-center demand growing even faster; overall data-center electricity use projected to double by 2030 and AI-focused power use to triple.

- Copper content by end use (for narrative texture): an onshore wind turbine requires roughly 9 tonnes of copper (offshore more than double); a conventional gasoline car uses ~18 kg of copper vs. ~91 kg for a comparable battery-electric vehicle; EV-related copper demand is projected to rise from 396,000 tonnes (2023) to 2.6 million tonnes by 2035 (a 555% increase), reaching roughly 8% of global copper consumption by then. — search snippets citing Copper.org ("Driving the Future of Transportation with Copper"), https://www.copper.org/copperage/driving-the-future-of-transportation-with-copper.php, and a Discovery Alert summary of unspecified underlying research, https://discoveryalert.com.au/copper-demand-surge-electrification-energy-2025/. **Copper.org is an industry trade association (Copper Development Association) — treat its figures as industry-interested and cross-check the EV-copper-content number against an independent source (e.g., IEA Critical Minerals or a DOE/national-lab study) before using as a hard figure.**

### Narrative material

- The El Teniente collapse is a genuinely dramatic, well-documented scene: an earthquake-magnitude seismic event 400–500 meters from miners working underground, a localized rock-burst, six dead, nine injured, a ~70-hour rescue/recovery effort, three days of national mourning declared by the Chilean government, and a direct, quantifiable hit to Codelco's production guidance and to the country's flagship state company's already-strained finances (~$340 million). This is a strong opening or mid-chapter scene tying "copper is dangerous and hard to get" to the macro story of Codelco's decline.
- Codelco's 2026 story is a striking "sovereign company in crisis" narrative: a 50-year-old state monopoly, born of Allende's 1971 nationalization and the symbolic "wage of Chile," now so indebted (liabilities-to-equity of 3.27 vs. ~1.0 for peers) that the government had to let it keep its own profits for the first time in half a century just to keep it capitalized — even as world copper prices sit near all-time highs. The juxtaposition (record prices, struggling state producer) is a strong chapter thesis.
- Escondida is the counterpoint: a private, BHP-majority-owned mine posting its best output in 17 years and getting a fresh $2 billion investment, while the state company down the road (El Teniente/Codelco) is in a debt-and-safety crisis. Good structural contrast for "who benefits from the copper boom."
- The AI data-center copper numbers (a single large AI data center needing up to 50,000 tonnes of copper — roughly the annual output of a mid-sized mine) is a vivid, quotable statistic linking the book's AI framing directly to Chapter 4's subject.

### Counterevidence / complications

- **Who is actually #1 in copper production is contested and shifting.** Some 2025 reporting says Codelco "lost its spot as world's largest copper producer in 2025" to BHP, while other snippets (from earlier in 2025, before the El Teniente accident) said Codelco "extended its reign" over BHP "by the slimmest of margins." This needs to be resolved with hard 2025 full-year figures (Codelco ~1.33 Mt vs. BHP Group total, not just Escondida) before the book states a definitive rank — it's a moving target and a good spot to show real-time uncertainty/complexity rather than a clean claim.
- **Copper's near-term price direction is genuinely disputed among major banks/analysts, cutting against a simple "structural shortage forever" narrative.** Goldman Sachs projects prices declining to roughly $11,000/tonne by end-2026 on an expected large global surplus once tariff-driven distortions pass and Chinese demand softens; a bear case cited puts prices at $9,000–10,000 if China's economy weakens further, U.S. data-center capex cools, scrap supply floods in, and Freeport's operations restart faster than expected. Meanwhile ICSG forecasts a ~150,000-tonne deficit for 2026 and JPMorgan a ~330,000-tonne shortfall — i.e., credible institutions disagree by hundreds of thousands of tonnes on whether 2026 is a surplus or deficit year. — Goldman Sachs Research, "Why Record-High Copper Prices Aren't Forecast to Last," https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/why-record-high-copper-prices-arent-forecast-to-last, and "Copper Prices Are Forecast to Decline Somewhat from Record Highs in 2026," https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/copper-prices-forecast-to-decline-from-record-highs-in-2026. Use this explicitly as the "not everyone agrees the chokepoint is permanent" counterweight.
- Section 232 tariffs on copper are on semi-finished/derivative products, not (initially) on refined copper cathode itself — an important nuance against any simplistic "US taxes all copper imports" framing; a further universal refined-copper tariff was only "under consideration" as of the most recent search results, phased in from 2027, not yet in force. ## CHAPTER 5: "The Lithium Triangle"

### Key facts and sources

- Chile holds roughly half of the world's identified lithium reserves, concentrated in the Salar de Atacama; SQM and Albemarle are the two companies currently operating brine extraction there. SQM's operating contract with Chile's state (via CORFO) runs through 2030; Albemarle's runs through 2043. — search snippet citing Fastmarkets, "Negotiations for world's largest lithium reserve in brine can be win-win for SQM and Chile," https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/negotiations-for-worlds-largest-lithium-reserve-in-brine-can-be-win-win-for-sqm-and-chile/ ; Albemarle's own corporate site, https://www.albemarle.com/cl/en/what-we-offer/reliable-supply/salar-de-atacama (primary/company source for the operational description) ; Albemarle SEC exhibits (Form 10-K/8-K technical report summaries on Salar de Atacama), https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/915913/000091591326000018/ex963salardeatacama2025t.htm — **this SEC exhibit is the strongest, most citable primary source for exact reserve/resource figures; pull it directly rather than relying on the Fastmarkets summary.**

- Salar de Atacama brine extraction process: brine is pumped from wells at a natural lithium concentration of about 0.2%, then concentrated via solar evaporation ponds to roughly 6% lithium before processing. SQM's lithium carbonate equivalent (LCE) production roughly tripled from 80,417 tonnes to 240,000 tonnes by 2022. — search snippet citing ScienceDirect academic article, "Uncertainties in the debate on the environmental impact of lithium brine extraction in the Salar de Atacama, Chile," https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2950117223000249, and a related ScienceDirect piece, "The off-sites of lithium production in the Atacama Desert," https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214790X23000990. These are peer-reviewed academic sources — good for citing the extraction mechanics and environmental-impact framing; pull full text to verify exact production figures and get the precise citation (author/journal/year).

- Bolivia holds the largest single-country lithium resource base — an estimated 23 million tonnes of lithium resources, of which roughly 21 million tonnes are in the Salar de Uyuni alone — but produces well under 1% of global supply. The state company Yacimientos de Litio Bolivianos (YLB) commissioned its first industrial-scale extraction plant at Uyuni in late 2023, but it has suffered design flaws and operational problems and has produced only a fraction of intended output. In 2024, Bolivia signed contracts worth a combined $2 billion with Russian and Chinese firms/consortia (including a CATL-BRUNP-CMOC consortium, "CBC") to develop direct lithium extraction (DLE) at Uyuni, including a planned 2,500-tonnes-per-year lithium carbonate facility. Local communities have raised concerns about freshwater diversion from agriculture and a lack of environmental impact assessments/community consultation as legally required. — Space Daily / AFP-style feature (search snippet), https://spacedaily.com/d-beneath-a-vast-white-salt-flat-in-bolivia-lies-the-largest-single-basin-deposit-of-lithium-on-earth... ; Mongabay, "Bolivian communities push back against foreign-backed lithium projects," April 2025, https://news.mongabay.com/2025/04/bolivian-communities-push-back-against-foreign-backed-lithium-projects/ (Mongabay is a reputable environmental-journalism outlet with named reporting — good secondary source) ; Mining.com, "Bolivia Uyuni plant to yield first lithium by 2025-end," https://www.mining.com/bolivia-uyuni-plant-to-yield-first-lithium-by-2025-end/. Use for: the "Bolivia has the resource but not the production" paradox that is central to the Lithium Triangle story.

- Australia is the world's largest lithium-producing country by volume, primarily via hard-rock spodumene mining rather than brine. USGS (as of the Jan 2025 Mineral Commodity Summaries) confirms this ranking; one search snippet cites Australian 2025 estimated production around 92,000 metric tons of lithium content (down from ~88,000 t in 2024, a reported 4% drop). Australia produces roughly 47% of world spodumene concentrate, about 400 kilotons/year. Key mines: Greenbushes (WA) produced nearly 798,000 tonnes of spodumene concentrate in FY2024–25; Kathleen Valley (WA), a new underground mine, opened mid-2024 and reached 500,000 t/year spodumene concentrate capacity by Q1 2025. — USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries (search-snippet reference; **pull the actual PDF** https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-lithium.pdf for exact 2024 tonnage table) ; CSIRO (Australia's national science agency — strong primary/institutional source), "Spodumene: Australia's lithium mineral hero," March 2025, https://www.csiro.au/en/news/all/articles/2025/march/spodumene-australias-lithium-mineral-hero`. Use CSIRO as the most citable non-USGS source for the "Australia = largest producer, via hard rock" claim.

- **Lithium price boom, 2021–2022:** Benchmark Mineral Intelligence's China lithium carbonate assessment reportedly reached about $81,375/tonne by December 2022 — an all-time high cited elsewhere as roughly $77,000/tonne in "late 2022," more than 1,000% above 2020 levels. — search snippet citing Visual Capitalist, "The Boom, Bust, and Rebound of Battery Material Prices," https://www.visualcapitalist.com/the-boom-bust-and-rebound-of-battery-material-prices/ ; also referenced via Benchmark Mineral Intelligence's own site (Wikipedia entry on the firm, orientation only), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benchmark_Mineral_Intelligence. **Recommend going directly to Benchmark Mineral Intelligence's or Fastmarkets' published price series/press releases for the exact assessment figure and date, since two different peak numbers ($77k vs. $81k) appeared in search snippets — reconcile before using in print.**

- **Lithium price bust, 2023–2024:** Fastmarkets' lithium carbonate (China) assessment fell 71.8% from its December 2022 peak to $22,950/tonne by its October 11, 2023 assessment. From the late-2022 peak to the end of 2024, prices fell by nearly 90% overall. Fastmarkets estimated a lithium market surplus of ~175,000 tonnes in 2023 and ~154,000 tonnes in 2024. — Fastmarkets, "OEMs and battery makers on alert as lower lithium prices to push into 2024," https://source.benchmarkminerals.com/article/oems-and-battery-makers-on-alert-as-lower-lithium-prices-to-push-into-2024-benchmark [note: URL domain is benchmarkminerals.com though content attributed to Fastmarkets in the snippet — verify correct attribution/outlet when pulling full text] ; corroborating figures via Fastmarkets insight, "Facing the tightening lithium supply challenge in 2025," https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/facing-the-tightening-lithium-supply-challenge-in-2025/. This is the core "bust" data point for the chapter — Fastmarkets and Benchmark Mineral Intelligence are the two industry-standard price-assessment firms cited throughout mining/battery journalism; either is a legitimate primary-ish source, but pull the actual assessment publication/press release rather than a secondary summary for an exact quotable figure.

- **Partial recovery, 2025–2026:** Battery-grade lithium carbonate prices reportedly rose from roughly $8/kg in May 2025 to more than $25/kg by May 2026. Lithium prices at the start of 2026 were reported as more than double the same period in 2025, driven partly by battery energy storage system (BESS) demand growing faster than anticipated. — search snippet citing Metal Tech News, "Finding direction in a lithium price storm," Aug 25, 2026, https://www.metaltechnews.com/story/2026/08/25/critical-minerals-alliances-2026/finding-direction-in-a-lithium-price-storm/2898.html ; IEA Global EV Outlook 2026 executive summary (search snippet), https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/executive-summary. Use for closing the boom-bust-recovery arc through to the book's present (2026).

- EV demand and lithium: Electric vehicles now account for nearly 90% of global lithium demand, up from 64% in 2020. Worldwide EV sales reached 20.7 million passenger/light-duty units in 2025 (global EV share of new car sales exceeded 25% for the first time); global EV production rose more than 25% year-on-year in 2025 to almost 22 million units. Under the IEA's Stated Policies Scenario, lithium demand for clean-energy technologies is projected to rise more than 5x by 2040, driven mainly by EVs. — IEA, *Global EV Outlook 2025* and *2026*, executive summaries and "Electric vehicle batteries" analysis pages: https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/executive-summary , https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/executive-summary , https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-batteries , https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries , and full PDF https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/7ea38b60-3033-42a6-9589-71134f4229f4/GlobalEVOutlook2025.pdf. **These IEA reports are primary, authoritative, directly citable — the strongest sourcing block in this brief. Use liberally.**

- Global EV battery demand grew to over 950 GWh in 2024, 25% more than 2023; grid-scale battery deployment exceeded 90 GWh in 2024, with lithium-based storage system costs expected to fall up to 40% by 2030. — IEA Global EV Outlook (same reports as above, search-snippet synthesis — verify exact GWh figures against the report PDF directly).

### Narrative material

- The "Lithium Triangle" itself is a strong organizing device: three neighboring countries (Chile, Argentina, Bolivia) sit atop most of the world's lithium brine, yet have wildly different production outcomes — Chile and Argentina are established brine producers with decades of infrastructure and long-term foreign operator contracts (SQM to 2030, Albemarle to 2043), while Bolivia, despite having the single largest resource (21 Mt at Uyuni alone), produces almost nothing due to state-control ambitions, technical failures at its DLE pilot plant, and now a pivot to Chinese/Russian joint ventures amid community pushback over water use. This is a clean natural-resource-curse/state-capacity contrast to build a chapter around.
- The price chart is a genuinely dramatic boom-bust arc worth rendering almost as a mini-narrative in itself: ~$7,000/tonne pre-2021 baseline → ~$77,000–81,000/tonne peak (Dec 2022) → a 90% collapse by end-2024 → a fresh doubling-plus recovery into 2026 (from ~$8/kg to $25+/kg battery-grade carbonate). This maps almost exactly onto Chapter 10's capital-cycle argument (a price spike triggers investment that arrives just as the spike ends, causing the crash) and is worth cross-referencing.
- Concrete mine-level casualties of the bust: Australian producers Pilbara Minerals (formerly PLS Group) placed its Ngungaju plant on care and maintenance in December 2024; Mineral Resources (MinRes) suspended its Bald Hill operation in November 2024 — real, named, dateable corporate decisions that dramatize the bust for named companies rather than abstract price lines.

### Counterevidence / complications

- **The lithium "glut" is not over as of the most recent search results — some analysts (UBS) expect oversupply to persist until 2027**, despite the price recovery narrative above; Fastmarkets projected only a modest ~10,000-tonne oversupply in 2025 tipping to a small ~1,500-tonne deficit in 2026 — i.e., a much less dramatic "shortage is coming" story than the copper chapter's IEA-cited 30%-deficit-by-2035 claim. This is useful complicating texture: lithium's supply response to the 2021–22 price spike may have been *effective* (a genuine bear case that new supply did arrive and overshot), unlike copper where the more common claim is chronic underinvestment. — Oilprice.com, "The Lithium Glut Could Persist Until 2027," https://oilprice.com/Energy/Energy-General/The-Lithium-Glut-Could-Persist-Until-2027.html ; Fastmarkets insight cited above.
- **Kathleen Valley (Liontown Resources, Western Australia) is a strong counterexample to the "10–20 year lead time" thesis** central to Chapter 10 — see full writeup under Chapter 10 below; it is arguably even more relevant to Chapter 5's lithium narrative, since it shows hard-rock lithium mines *can* be built fast when permitting and financing align, directly countering any "lithium supply structurally can't respond to price signals" claim.
- Some sources describe Chile's 2024–25 lithium output as still expanding (capacity expansions in Argentina, Chile, China, and Zimbabwe in 2024 despite low prices) even as "many lithium projects were postponed or cancelled due to low prices" elsewhere — a useful nuance showing the bust hit marginal/high-cost projects (mostly hard-rock, mostly Australian) much harder than low-cost incumbent brine operations in the Atacama.

## CHAPTER 10: "The Capital Cycle, Underground"

### Key facts and sources — this is the best-sourced chapter in this brief; S&P Global Market Intelligence has published a specific, well-defined, citable study series

- **Core finding:** S&P Global Market Intelligence's Global Metals & Mining database study calculated the average lead time from discovery to first commercial production for 127 precious- and base-metals mines (gold, copper, nickel, and lithium) that began production between 2002 and 2023, and were discovered from 1980 onward, at **15.7 years**, with a range of 6 to 32 years across individual projects. Gold mines specifically averaged 15.2 years (about six months faster than the all-commodity average, and roughly a year slower than copper mines specifically). — S&P Global Market Intelligence, "Discovery to production averages 15.7 years for 127 mines," https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/discovery-to-production-averages-15-7-years-for-127-mines. **This is a primary industry-research source (S&P Global's own Metals & Mining database) — the single best citation for the chapter's core "10-20+ years" claim. It is currently blocked from direct WebFetch in this sandbox but should be fetchable normally; pull the full article/report for exact charts and methodology notes before writing.**

- **Trend over time — lead times are getting longer, not shorter:** for mines that became operational in the more recent 2020–2023/2020–2024 window, the average lead time rose to nearly 18 years (17.8–18 years cited across snippets), compared with roughly 6 years for mines that started production in the 1990s — i.e., lead times have roughly **tripled** since the 1990s. — S&P Global Market Intelligence, "Average lead time almost 18 years for mines started in 2020–23," https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/average-lead-time-almost-18-years-for-mines-started-in-2020-23 ; related framing piece, "From 6 years to 18 years: The increasing trend of mine lead times," https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/from-6years-to-18years-the-increasing-trend-of-mine-lead-times. This is a superb, quotable, single-sentence statistic for the chapter's thesis: **lead times nearly tripled from the 1990s to the 2020s.**

- **A second, more recent S&P Global report (July 2026) found that for nonoperating projects that have already completed feasibility studies, the discovery-to-production timeline has stretched to nearly 30 years** — about five times longer than lead times observed in the 1990s — attributing the increase largely to permitting delays. — S&P Global Market Intelligence, "Mine Permitting Delays Stretch Timelines to 30 Years," July 2026, https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/2026/07/mine-permitting-delays-discovery-to-production-timeline. This is the most current data point available and shows the trend accelerating even further — good for a chapter written in 2026.

- **US-specific finding:** a companion S&P Global report ("Mine Development Times: The U.S. in Perspective," June/July 2024, commissioned in partnership with/discussed alongside the National Mining Association) found it takes an average of **29 years** for US mines to go from discovery to production — longer than every other country studied except Zambia (34 years) — ranking the US "next to last" globally in mine development speed, despite the U.S. holding significant reserves of critical minerals needed for the energy transition. — S&P Global press release, "United States Ranks Next to Last in Development Time for New Mines that Produce Critical Minerals for Energy Transition," July 18, 2024, https://press.spglobal.com/2024-07-18-United-States-Ranks-Next-to-Last-in-Development-Time-for-New-Mines-that-Produce-Critical-Minerals-for-Energy-Transition,-S-P-Global-Finds ; full PDF report, https://cdn.ihsmarkit.com/www/pdf/0724/SPGlobal_NMA_DevelopmentTimesUSinPerspective_June_2024.pdf. This PDF is likely to be directly fetchable (ihsmarkit.com/cdn subdomain, not the blocked spglobal.com main site) — **try pulling this PDF directly**, it is likely the single most citable document for the whole chapter.

- **IEA corroboration (independent of S&P Global):** IEA analysis of major mines that became operational between 2010 and 2019 found it takes more than 16 years on average to develop a project from discovery to first production — broken down as more than 12 years for exploration and feasibility studies and 4–5 years for the construction phase. The IEA explicitly frames this as a risk: if companies wait for supply deficits to emerge before committing to new projects, long lead times "could lead to a prolonged period of market tightness and price volatility" — i.e., the mechanism by which high prices do *not* quickly summon new supply, unlike (say) shale oil. — IEA, *The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions*, "Reliable supply of minerals" chapter, https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/reliable-supply-of-minerals ; also IEA *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025* and *2026* executive summaries, https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary and https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary. This IEA report is a second, independent, authoritative primary source confirming the S&P Global finding with a similar (16-year) number using different methodology — very strong for the chapter, since it shows the ~16-year figure is not an artifact of one database.

### Narrative material

- The core "story" of this chapter is essentially already written by the data: lead times have gone from ~6 years (1990s) to ~15.7–18 years (2000s–2020s) to a possible ~30 years for the newest crop of not-yet-built projects — a near-exponential lengthening trend, mostly attributed to permitting delays rather than exploration/technical difficulty (per S&P Global's July 2026 report). This is a strong "the chokepoint is getting worse, not better" throughline.
- The IEA's explicit mechanism — "if companies wait for deficits to emerge before committing to new projects, this could lead to prolonged tightness and price volatility" — is essentially the textbook definition of a mining "capital cycle" (long lag between price signal and supply response, producing boom-bust price cycles) and can likely be used almost as a direct paraphrase of the chapter's thesis, grounded in an IEA citation.
- The US ranking "next to last" globally (29 years, behind only Zambia's 34) is a strong, surprising, quotable fact for an American audience — worth a scene/example (e.g., Resolution Copper in Arizona, which has been in permitting/litigation for over a decade — see counterevidence below).

### Counterevidence / complications

- **Kathleen Valley lithium mine (Liontown Resources, Western Australia) went from discovery to production in six years — roughly a quarter of the ~15.7–18-year average** — with 2.5 years of planning/permitting and an 18-month construction build. It began production at an initial 2.8 Mt/year rate (with a resource base of 156 Mt), is Australia's first underground lithium mine, and secured offtake agreements with Tesla, Ford, LG Energy Solution, and Beijing Sinomine before construction was even complete — i.e., the market financed it fast once it looked viable. — Mining-Technology and Australian Mining trade coverage (search snippets), https://www.mining-technology.com/news/liontown-lithium-kathleen-wa/ , https://www.australianmining.com.au/kathleen-valley-on-track-to-reach-first-production/ ; Liontown's own investor/company pages, https://www.liontown.com/project/kathleen-valley/ and https://www.liontown.com/latest-news/kathleen-valley-lithium-operation-officially-opened-in-landmark-ceremony/ (primary/company source — use for exact dates). **This is the single best "counterevidence" case in this entire brief** — a real, named, recent, well-documented mine that defied the chapter's central statistic. Strongly recommend using it explicitly as a counterpoint/complication within Chapter 10, not just burying it in this section — it makes the chapter more credible, not less, to show the exception.

- **Indonesia's nickel-smelting buildout is an even more dramatic (though structurally different — processing capacity, not primary mine discovery-to-production) counterexample of extremely fast critical-minerals capacity growth.** Following a government ban on raw nickel ore exports effective January 2020 (intended to force domestic downstream processing), more than 50 new smelters were built within a few years, mostly by Chinese-backed joint ventures in industrial parks in Sulawesi and North Maluku; Indonesia's total nickel smelting capacity reached an estimated 2.5 million tonnes/year by 2025, and nickel-related exports grew from roughly $6 billion (2013) to $30 billion (2022) — a fivefold increase in the broader nickel-industry economy in under a decade. This shows that *state industrial policy plus concentrated FDI* can compress "supply response" timelines dramatically, at the cost of significant environmental and labor concerns and, by 2023–24, early signs of ore-supply shortages feeding those very smelters. — International Institute for Environment and Development (IIED), "Indonesia's nickel refining boom: lessons for other mineral-rich countries," https://www.iied.org/indonesias-nickel-refining-boom-lessons-for-other-mineral-rich-countries (a reputable policy-research institute — good citable source) ; East Asia Forum, "Smelters squeeze Indonesia's nickel ore supply," Sept 2024, https://eastasiaforum.org/2024/09/14/smelters-squeeze-indonesias-nickel-ore-supply/. Use this to show the capital-cycle thesis is not universal — command economies/state-directed capital can move much faster than Western permitting-constrained systems, with real tradeoffs.

- **Resolution Copper (Arizona, Rio Tinto/BHP joint venture)** is the flip side — a real, current example of the extreme end of the US permitting-delay problem the S&P Global "29 years" and "next-to-last" findings describe: despite a 2025 Trump executive order and Defense Production Act invocation aimed at compressing permitting review to "as little as 28 days," Resolution Copper still faces active litigation over a required federal land swap even after receiving an expedited environmental review. Useful as a live, ongoing case study of the chapter's core problem, and also of the limits of political will to override the legal/permitting system. — search snippet citing White & Case, "Shovel-ready? Building US critical mineral resilience," https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/shovel-ready-building-us-critical-mineral-resilience. Also note Panama's Cobre Panamá copper mine, where the Supreme Court twice invalidated the operating-contract legislation following public protest — a case of a mine actually built and operating, then shut down by political/legal reversal rather than never being built at all — a different but related capital-cycle risk (expropriation/reversal risk after the capital is already sunk) that could also suit this chapter or Chapter 4.

## CHAPTER 11: "Boom, Bust, Ghost Town"

### Recommended case: Humberstone and Santa Laura Saltpeter (Nitrate) Works, Chile — UNESCO World Heritage Site

This is the strongest-sourced option found (UNESCO official listing plus multiple corroborating popular/travel-journalism accounts), and it fits the book's Chile/Atacama geography naturally (bookending nicely with Chapters 4–5, also set in Chile's mining north).

### Key facts and sources

- Humberstone was founded in 1862 as a nitrate mining/processing center originally called "Oficina La Palma," renamed Humberstone in 1925 in honor of British mine manager (chemical engineer) James Humberstone. Santa Laura is a neighboring, smaller nitrate works. Both are located in the Atacama Desert (Tarapacá Region), northern Chile. — UNESCO World Heritage Centre, official listing #1178, "Humberstone and Santa Laura Saltpeter Works," https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1178/ (this is the primary, most authoritative source — **strongly recommend fetching this page directly outside the sandbox**, as it will contain UNESCO's official statement of Outstanding Universal Value, exact inscription criteria, and precise dates) ; corroborating popular accounts: Rough Guides, "The Atacama's Ghost Towns | Humberstone, Chile," https://www.roughguides.com/articles/ghost-town-humberstone-chile/.

- The nitrate ("saltpeter") boom: thousands of workers (called "pampinos") lived and worked in the towns from roughly 1880 through the industry's peak, processing what is described as the largest sodium nitrate (used as agricultural fertilizer, and as an input for explosives/gunpowder) deposit in the world — nitrate that "transformed agricultural lands" across North America, South America, and Europe before the invention of synthetic nitrogen fixation. Humberstone's population grew from 123 inhabitants in 1877 to 3,700 by 1940. — search snippet citing Rough Guides and Google Arts & Culture ("Humberstone and Santa Laura Saltpeter Works, Chile"), https://artsandculture.google.com/story/humberstone-and-santa-laura-saltpeter-works-chile-unesco/XQVRfkn12rzafA. **Verify exact population figures against the UNESCO nomination dossier or a Chilean national archive/INE historical census source before using as a hard number in print** — travel-journalism sources are not ideal primary sources for precise historical demographics.

- The bust: the nitrate industry's "bubble of prosperity" burst after Germany developed synthetic (Haber-Bosch-process) nitrogen fixation, industrialized during and after World War I — Chile's nitrate export monopoly collapsed as a result. By the 1950s, Chile's share of the world nitrate market had fallen to roughly 3%. The towns underwent a slow, roughly three-decade decline; the Humberstone works' factory/offices finally closed completely in 1961.
- Preservation/UNESCO status: after closure, the site passed through private hands, including an owner who reportedly planned to sell the remaining infrastructure for scrap metal before going bankrupt — inadvertently helping to preserve the site by halting demolition. A non-profit organization later restored/maintained the site, which was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2005. — search snippets citing Rough Guides and Google Arts & Culture as above. Again, **cross-check against the UNESCO official listing page** (https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1178/) for the authoritative inscription date/criteria and any UNESCO-published historical summary, which is likely to correct or refine some of these secondary-source details.

### Narrative material

- Humberstone offers vivid physical/sensory detail for a scene: an abandoned company town in the Atacama Desert — one of the driest places on Earth, which is itself why the buildings (theater, hotel, market, swimming pool, workers' housing) have survived so well rather than rotting or being reclaimed by vegetation — with a still-standing theater and other company-town infrastructure, now a tourist and UNESCO site. This is highly visual and would translate well into descriptive prose about what a "chokepoint economy" boomtown looks like fifty years after the bust.
- The town's name itself is a small human-scale detail worth a sentence: renamed after a real British engineer, James Humberstone, in 1925 — i.e., named at the height of the boom for the man who ran it, only 36 years before the whole enterprise shut down completely (1961).
- The precise mechanism of collapse — a *technological substitution* (German synthetic nitrate fixation replacing natural Chilean nitrate as an input) rather than a price crash, ore exhaustion, or geopolitical event — is a distinct and useful "type" of chokepoint-industry collapse for the book to name explicitly, and could be set up as a live open question for the present-day critical-minerals chapters (i.e., "could synthetic/recycled/substitute materials do to lithium or cobalt what Haber-Bosch did to Chilean nitrate?").
- The "bankrupt scrap dealer accidentally preserves a UNESCO site" detail, if verified, is a nice, ironic historical beat for the book's ending or a chapter close.

### Alternative/backup cases considered (if Humberstone's sourcing proves insufficient on full verification, or as secondary comparanda)

- **Bodie, California** (gold/silver, not copper/lithium, but extremely well-documented via the National Park Service/California State Parks): gold discovered 1859; the town stayed small (fewer than 20 buildings) until the Bunker Hill Mine's 1876 major gold/silver discovery triggered a boom; by 1879 the town had over 250 buildings and roughly 8,000–10,000 residents (sources vary between these two figures — reconcile), earning a reputation as "the wildest town in the West." Population collapsed to ~1,500 by 1886 (just seven years after peak) and to 120 by 1920; the last mine closed in 1942; declared California's official state gold rush ghost town in 2002. Total historical output was over $35 million in gold and silver. — National Park Service page, https://www.nps.gov/places/bodie-historic-district.htm (primary government source — use this for the authoritative version) ; Wikipedia (orientation), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodie,_California. Good if the book wants a US-based, non-Chile example, or a second boom-bust case for contrast/comparison, though it is a precious-metals rather than industrial/battery-metals story.

- **Butte, Montana** (copper — thematically closer to Chapter 4): silver discovered 1872; copper demand exploded with 1880s electrification; Marcus Daly bought what became the Anaconda mine for $30,000 in 1881 and built the Anaconda Copper Mining Company, which extracted an estimated 18 billion pounds of copper over roughly a century of operation, at one point making Butte "the largest city between Chicago and San Francisco." The 1917 Speculator Mine disaster (a major underground mining disaster — worth checking exact death toll before use) is a dark historical parallel to the 2025 El Teniente accident in Chapter 4. The end: after a shift to open-pit mining and declining ore grades/demand, ARCO (which had acquired Anaconda) closed its Berkeley Pit and all Montana operations in 1982; the abandoned pit filled with acidic, heavy-metal-laden water and Butte became one of the largest EPA Superfund sites in the country. — Wikipedia, "History of Butte, Montana" (orientation), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Butte,_Montana ; CBS News, "Rechanneling the boom-and-bust history of Butte, Montana," https://www.cbsnews.com/news/rechanneling-the-boom-and-bust-history-of-butte-montana/ ; NPS, "Butte-Anaconda Historic District," https://www.nps.gov/places/butte-anaconda-historic-district.htm (primary source). **This is arguably the single best-fitting case thematically** (it is a copper story, directly parallel to Codelco/El Teniente in Chapter 4, with a clean "boom → environmental catastrophe → Superfund" arc that mirrors current worries about lithium/nickel/cobalt mining regions) — recommend the writer weigh Butte against Humberstone directly; Butte has the advantage of being a copper case (thematic echo of Ch. 4) and having a federal (EPA Superfund, NPS Historic District) documentary record, while Humberstone has the advantage of UNESCO's international documentary/preservation apparatus and geographic proximity to the book's other Chile chapters.

### Counterevidence / complications

- None of these three cases is a "critical mineral for the energy transition" in the modern sense (nitrate was a fertilizer/explosives input; gold/silver at Bodie were currency/store-of-value metals; Butte's copper is the closest analog but its bust was driven by ore depletion and cost, not by substitution or a green-energy demand shift). If the chapter wants a boom-bust case in a mineral literally discussed elsewhere in the book (copper, lithium, cobalt, nickel, rare earths), Butte, Montana is the best available match found in this research pass; a further research pass specifically on 20th-century cobalt (Katanga/DRC) or historical tin (Bolivia's own tin bust, 1980s — notably in the same country as the Lithium Triangle's Bolivia thread) busts might be worth a follow-up search if the writer wants a tighter thematic match. **Flagging Bolivia's 1980s tin collapse (the "Tin Crash" of October 1985, when the London Metal Exchange tin price cartel collapsed) as a promising unexplored lead** — it directly parallels Bolivia's present-day lithium ambitions in the same country, was a real price-cartel collapse (unlike the others found here), and devastated Bolivian mining towns (e.g., Llallagua/Siglo XX) — but it was not fully researched in this pass and would need dedicated searches before being used.
