# Chapter 15 research boundary: China's long game

Compiled 2026-09-04. This note is deliberately narrow. It does not rewrite Chapter 15. It tightens the chapter's most consequential claims around the shape of China's rare-earth advantage, the 2025 export-control episode, and the U.S. policy response using current primary or authoritative sources.

## Editorial proposition

Chapter 15 is strongest when it argues that China's leverage sits less in geology than in the industrial middle and downstream of the chain. The freshest evidence makes that argument sharper: China is important in mining, more dominant in refining, and still more dominant in finished permanent magnets.

The chapter should therefore resist the loose phrase "China controls rare earths." It obscures where the concentration actually rises.

A better hierarchy is:

- mining concentration matters;
- separation and refining matter more;
- magnet manufacturing is the deepest chokepoint;
- downstream demand is what makes the upstream system durable.

## 1. The concentration grows as the material moves downstream

The International Energy Agency's 2026 rare-earth report gives unusually clean, stage-specific numbers for magnet rare earths in 2024:

- China accounted for about **60% of global mined production**;
- **91% of global refined output**;
- **94% of sintered permanent-magnet production**.

Source: IEA, *Rare Earth Elements*, executive summary, 2026: https://www.iea.org/reports/rare-earth-elements/executive-summary

The IEA also notes that China's share of sintered permanent-magnet production was only around 50% in 2005. That makes the downstream buildout the dynamic part of the story rather than a fixed geological inheritance.

**Use in Chapter 15:** this is a stronger empirical backbone for "a country can import ore and export leverage." The sentence is not merely rhetorical. The concentration visibly increases after extraction.

**Boundary:** these percentages are for magnet rare earths and the corresponding refining/magnet chain, not for every rare-earth element and every end use. Do not generalize them into one universal "China controls X% of rare earths" statistic.

## 2. The 2025 controls show why the downstream chokepoint matters

On April 4, 2025, China's Ministry of Commerce and General Administration of Customs imposed export controls on specified samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium-related items. The list included metals, alloys, oxides, compounds, and in some cases permanent-magnet materials. Exporters were required to obtain licenses for controlled items.

Primary source: Ministry of Commerce, Announcement No. 18 of 2025: https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/Policies/AnnouncementsOrders/art/2025/art_0dd87cbee7b045bf93fabe6ab2faceee.html

The legal framing matters. The announcement describes the controls as measures to safeguard national security and interests and fulfill non-proliferation obligations. It does not describe them as a blanket embargo on all rare-earth exports.

**Use in Chapter 15:** if the chapter references 2025 controls, describe them as licensing controls on specified rare-earth-related items, not as "China stopped rare-earth exports."

**Counterevidence:** controls can create leverage without producing a clean, permanent cutoff. Licensing systems allow exemptions, approvals, suspensions, and diplomatic adjustment. The existence of a control regime should not be conflated with zero trade.

## 3. China's official rationale complicates the simple coercion story

China's Ministry of Commerce has repeatedly described rare-earth export controls as dual-use and national-security measures rather than country-specific economic coercion. In October 2025, a ministry spokesperson said compliant exports intended for civil use would be approved and said the measures were not targeted at a specific country or region.

Source: Ministry of Commerce/Xinhua repost, October 16–17, 2025: https://is.mofcom.gov.cn/AboutChinaAndInvestmentEnvironment/art/2025/art_b5aaba3201cb44b3aceadcbec20c747b.html

That claim should not simply be adopted at face value, but it is the strongest official counterargument to a purely weaponization-based reading.

**Editorial use:** distinguish three things that are often collapsed:

1. industrial concentration created by decades of investment and clustering;
2. export-control law justified by Beijing as national-security policy;
3. the strategic leverage that concentration gives the state regardless of stated motive.

The chapter is stronger if it does not require proof of malign intent to establish vulnerability.

## 4. The U.S. response now explicitly buys industrial resilience

The U.S. Department of Defense and MP Materials moved well beyond grantmaking in 2025.

MP Materials announced on July 10, 2025 that the partnership included:

- a **10-year NdPr price-floor commitment of $110/kg** for covered products;
- a 10-year arrangement intended to ensure purchase of **100% of magnets** produced at the planned 10X facility;
- a **$150 million DoD loan** to expand heavy-rare-earth separation at Mountain Pass;
- a **$400 million preferred-equity investment** plus warrants, positioning DoD to become the company's largest shareholder on an as-converted/as-exercised basis.

Company primary source: https://investors.mpmaterials.com/investor-news/news-details/2025/MP-Materials-Announces-Transformational-Public-Private-Partnership-with-the-Department-of-Defense-to-Accelerate-U-S--Rare-Earth-Magnet-Independence/default.aspx

The Department of Defense separately confirmed the $150 million loan on August 10, 2025 and said it was intended to add heavy-rare-earth separation capabilities at Mountain Pass.

Government source: https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/4270722/office-of-strategic-capital-announces-first-loan-through-dod-agreement-with-mp/

**Use in Chapter 15:** the chapter's line that price floors, strategic designations, government-backed offtake, and defense procurement are "admissions" that strategic capacity has value beyond normal short-term returns is now directly supportable.

The stronger, less slogan-like formulation is that Washington is paying explicitly for capabilities the market may not maintain at acceptable scale through low-price cycles.

## 5. The strongest counterargument: diversification is real, not theatrical

China's position is dominant, but the chapter should not treat it as permanent by definition.

The IEA's 2026 project pipeline shows meaningful planned growth outside China in mining, refining, and downstream processing. But the pipeline is uneven: by 2035, diversified mining capacity is much larger than diversified refining capacity, and announced metals/alloys/magnet capacity is smaller still.

IEA source: https://www.iea.org/reports/rare-earth-elements/executive-summary

This supports a more precise claim than "the West is decades behind." The bottleneck is not that nothing is being built. It is that diversification thins as the chain moves toward finished magnets.

**Disconfirming evidence to keep:** if non-Chinese refining and magnet projects commission successfully, reach commercial yields, survive price cycles, and secure customers, China's share can fall materially. The chapter should not imply that industrial learning is irreversible.

## 6. A useful falsification test for the chapter's thesis

The chapter's thesis would weaken if, over the next decade, all three of the following happen:

- non-Chinese refining capacity scales roughly in line with non-Chinese mining;
- non-Chinese magnet production grows enough to absorb that refined output;
- those plants remain commercially viable without extraordinary state support through at least one low-price cycle.

If that happens, China's current industrial depth will have been shown to be replicable faster than the chapter assumes.

The thesis would strengthen if mining diversifies while refining and magnet manufacturing remain highly concentrated, because that would confirm that geological diversification alone does not remove the chokepoint.

## 7. Suggested surgical revisions for the manuscript

Do not turn Chapter 15 into a data dump. The current argument and cadence are stronger than the older research brief's long sequence of percentages and anecdotes.

The highest-value manuscript revisions are small:

- replace the vague "China mines most of the world's rare earths and processes an even larger share" passage with one compact stage-by-stage 2024 comparison from the IEA;
- add one sentence making clear that the April 2025 action was a licensing regime for specified items, not a blanket halt to all rare-earth exports;
- anchor the U.S. industrial-policy paragraph to the concrete 2025 MP Materials structure: equity, loan, price floor, and offtake;
- preserve the existing counterargument that leverage creates incentives to diversify;
- avoid implying that every low price or foreign plant closure was deliberately engineered by Beijing unless evidence supports intent.

## 8. Opening-quote boundary

The chapter is right to be cautious about the polished English version of the Deng Xiaoping rare-earth quotation. Unless a contemporaneous transcript or authoritative archival source is found, do not upgrade the attribution beyond the existing careful language.

The argument does not need the quote to be exact. The historical evidence should carry the claim.

## Bottom line

The chapter's strongest current proposition is not that China "owns the rare earths." It is that industrial power compounds downstream. In 2024 China's share rose from about 60% of magnet-rare-earth mining to 91% of refining and 94% of sintered permanent magnets, while the 2025 export-control episode and the U.S. government's unusually interventionist response demonstrated how much strategic value sits in those middle and downstream capabilities.

That is a more precise, more falsifiable, and less myth-dependent version of "China's long game."
