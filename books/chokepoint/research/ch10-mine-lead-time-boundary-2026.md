# Chapter 10 — Mine lead-time boundary (2026)

Checked September 3, 2026 for Chapter 10, “The Capital Cycle, Underground.”

## Claim under test

The chapter brief asks why new mines can take 10–20+ years from discovery to production and what that means for a market trying to respond to a shortage. The useful version of this claim needs one important correction before it reaches prose: **“mine lead time” is not one clock.**

Discovery, exploration, feasibility, financing, permitting, construction, commissioning, and ramp-up are different stages. Some overlap. Some can be compressed. Some projects begin with an already-known deposit or existing infrastructure and therefore skip years that appear inside a discovery-to-production average.

Treating every mine as a sixteen-year permitting problem would be wrong.

## IEA: the famous sixteen-year number is discovery to first production

The International Energy Agency’s critical-minerals analysis of major mines that entered production between 2010 and 2019 found an average of **more than 16 years from discovery to first production**.

The IEA breaks that observed interval into more than twelve years for exploration and feasibility work, followed by roughly four to five years for construction.

Source: International Energy Agency, *The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions — Reliable supply of minerals*.
https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/reliable-supply-of-minerals

The boundary matters. The statistic does **not** mean every permitted mine requires sixteen years to build, nor that government review alone consumes sixteen years. Much of the observed clock begins before a company knows whether it has a technically and economically mineable project.

That makes the statistic more interesting, not less. High prices cannot instantly create an orebody that has already been discovered, drilled, modeled, metallurgically tested, financed, permitted, and engineered. The fastest supply response often comes from projects that have already paid part of this time cost.

## Why price signals arrive late

A mineral shortage can raise prices this week. A greenfield mine responds through a sequence of irreversible commitments.

Exploration has to establish where the ore is and how continuous it is. Feasibility work has to determine whether the grade, recovery rate, waste movement, water, power, transport, labor, environmental controls, and capital cost can support a business. Financing becomes easier or harder with the commodity cycle. Construction then has its own schedule even after permits and money exist.

The IEA’s 2025 outlook adds another pressure: for mature commodities such as copper, declining ore quality is increasing the capital intensity of new projects. It estimates roughly USD 500 billion of new mining investment is required through 2040 under its Stated Policies Scenario, with copper singled out as difficult because of declining grades, higher project costs, and a slowdown in major discoveries.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 — Overview of outlook for key minerals*.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/overview-of-outlook-for-key-minerals

This gives Chapter 10 a stronger mechanism than “bureaucracy is slow.” The long cycle is partly geological search, partly engineering proof, partly finance, partly politics, and partly construction.

## Strongest counterargument: some clocks really can be shortened

The United States supplied an unusually sharp counterexample in 2025. The Department of the Interior used emergency procedures to complete the federal environmental review for the Velvet-Wood uranium-vanadium mine in Utah in fourteen days.

Source: U.S. Department of the Interior, “Interior Department Approves Utah Uranium-Vanadium Mine to Strengthen U.S. Mineral Security,” May 23, 2025.
https://www.doi.gov/pressreleases/interior-department-approves-utah-uranium-vanadium-mine-strengthen-us-mineral

Interior’s broader emergency-permitting announcement said environmental assessments normally taking up to one year could be compressed to roughly fourteen days, while environmental impact statements normally taking about two years could be handled in roughly twenty-eight days under the emergency process.

Source: U.S. Department of the Interior, “Department of the Interior Implements Emergency Permitting Procedures to Strengthen Domestic Energy Supply,” April 23, 2025.
https://www.doi.gov/pressreleases/department-interior-implements-emergency-permitting-procedures-strengthen-domestic

This is strong evidence against writing permitting delay as a law of nature. A government can change procedure and move one approval stage dramatically faster.

It is not evidence that a greenfield mine can move from geological discovery to dependable commercial output in fourteen days. The review clock and the mine-development clock are different things.

## A second counterargument: supply response differs by mineral

The IEA’s 2025 market review notes that recent battery-mineral production expanded quickly enough to push prices down, especially in markets led by China, Indonesia, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It explicitly says battery-metal supply demonstrated an ability to scale faster than traditional metals such as copper and zinc.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 — Executive summary*.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary

That should stay in the chapter. “Mining is slow” is not a universal coefficient. Nickel capacity in Indonesia, lithium projects with favorable geology and existing infrastructure, brownfield expansions, restarts, and by-product recovery can move on different timelines from a giant greenfield copper project.

The 2026 IEA outlook reinforces the point. Announced project pipelines have narrowed projected supply gaps for copper and lithium, while early-stage nickel and graphite projects could cover much more demand if they arrive on schedule. The problem is therefore not that supply never responds. It is that the speed and credibility of response vary sharply by mineral, project maturity, processing route, jurisdiction, and stage of development.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 — Outlook*, July 16, 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook

## Editorial implication

Chapter 10 should resist using “sixteen years” as a magic number. A better frame is a **portfolio of clocks**.

A shortage first asks what can respond fastest: inventories, higher recovery at existing operations, restarts, brownfield expansions, substitution, recycling, or a project already far down the development pipeline. Greenfield discovery sits at the slow end because the market cannot buy back years of geological and engineering knowledge that nobody accumulated in advance.

That is the capital-cycle chokepoint worth emphasizing.

An export rule can change overnight. A price can move in seconds. The physical supply response depends on how much uncertainty was already retired before the shock arrived.

That formulation keeps the chapter’s central asymmetry while preserving the strongest evidence against a simplistic claim that all mining delay is permitting delay or that every new tonne requires the same number of years.
