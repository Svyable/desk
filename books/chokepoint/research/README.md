# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Chokepoint* asks whether the mineral inputs to AI infrastructure, electric vehicles, renewable power, and defense systems are becoming a twenty-first-century analog to twentieth-century oil — a small set of physical inputs whose control confers geopolitical leverage — and, if so, where that analogy holds and where it breaks.

The strong version of the argument — that a mineral cartel is about to seize the global economy the way OPEC did in 1973 — is too brittle to be useful and is not well supported: no OPEC-equivalent price-setting cartel currently exists for lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, or rare earths, and Chinese dominance in several of these markets looks more like accumulated industrial-policy advantage in processing than coordinated price control. The version worth testing is more durable:

> A short list of minerals has become simultaneously necessary across AI infrastructure, electrification, and defense; supply and especially refining capacity for several of them are geographically concentrated to a degree that creates real chokepoints; and the response to that concentration — friend-shoring, recycling, substitution, deep-sea mining, new alliances — is still early, uneven, and itself full of costs and unresolved trade-offs.

The book should survive multiple outcomes. If diversification and substitution proceed faster than expected and today's chokepoints dissolve over the 2030s, the evidence should force the argument to change. If a genuine supply shock or a coordinated export restriction occurs, the book should explain the mechanism without claiming it predicted the date.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Concentration:** How concentrated is mining, and separately, refining/processing capacity for copper, lithium, cobalt, nickel, and rare earths? Mining and refining concentration are different questions and should not be conflated.
2. **Demand overlap:** How much do AI data centers, EV/battery manufacturing, grid buildout, wind/solar, and defense systems actually compete for the same specific minerals, and how fast is that demand projected to grow?
3. **Weaponization:** When has mineral supply actually been used as diplomatic or economic leverage (2010 China-Japan rare earth incident; 2023-2026 Chinese export licensing), what happened to prices and alternative supply afterward, and how effective was the leverage in each case?
4. **The oil analogy:** Where does the 1973 OPEC embargo genuinely illuminate mineral geopolitics (stockpile behavior, price shock dynamics, strategic reserves) and where does it mislead (minerals accumulate as stock rather than burn as flow; no unified mineral-exporter cartel exists; recycling has no oil equivalent)?
5. **The capital cycle:** Why do new mines take 10-20+ years from discovery to production even at high prices, and what does that imply about how fast supply can respond to a shortage or a policy push?
6. **The resource curse:** Do mineral-rich, mining-dependent economies (DRC, Zambia, historically Bolivia) systematically underperform mineral-poor economies, and what do the exceptions (Chile, Botswana, Norway) show about which institutions matter?
7. **Substitution and recycling:** How much of projected mineral demand could realistically be met by chemistry substitution (LFP, sodium-ion, rare-earth-free motors) or by recycling/urban mining by 2030 and 2040, and how much is that a plausible offset versus a hopeful narrative?
8. **The policy response:** How far have friend-shoring efforts (US IRA sourcing rules, the Minerals Security Partnership, the EU Critical Raw Materials Act) actually gone in building non-Chinese capacity, and on what timeline could they plausibly matter?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should look for evidence against the strong version of the thesis.

- Several "critical mineral" supply chains are less concentrated than the popular narrative suggests, or concentration is easing faster than expected (e.g., non-Chinese rare earth separation capacity coming online).
- Recycling and substitution may progress faster than pessimists assume, especially in battery chemistry, where LFP and sodium-ion adoption has already measurably reduced cobalt and nickel intensity per vehicle.
- Export controls have sometimes proven less effective than intended — alternative suppliers, stockpiles, or substitution blunted their impact — and some "weaponization" episodes are better explained by ordinary regulatory friction than by strategic intent.
- High mineral prices can summon real new supply over a long enough horizon; the capital cycle argument should not be treated as proof that scarcity is permanent.
- Some mineral-rich countries and regions have used resource wealth well; the "curse" is not deterministic, and the book should show the institutional conditions under which it is avoided.
- Deep-sea mining, recycling, and substitution are not mutually exclusive hedges; a mature response likely combines several imperfect solutions rather than one clean fix.

## Evidence lanes

### I. The mineral-oil analogy

Comparative history: the 1973 OPEC embargo and 1979 oil shock against the structure of contemporary mineral markets. Distinguish stock-versus-flow economics, cartel versus concentrated-but-uncoordinated supply, and the different roles of strategic reserves.

### II. Mining and refining concentration

Country- and company-level data on mine production and, separately, refining/separation capacity for copper (Chile, Peru), lithium (Chile, Argentina, Australia, China processing), cobalt (DRC mining, Chinese refining), nickel (Indonesia), and rare earths (Chinese mining and especially Chinese separation dominance).

### III. The demand side

AI data-center, EV, wind/solar, grid, and defense demand projections from IEA, USGS, and company disclosures, tracking overlap across sectors for the same minerals.

### IV. Weaponization and statecraft

The 2010 China-Japan rare earth incident, 2023-2026 Chinese export licensing on gallium, germanium, graphite, and rare earths, and the Western/allied policy response (IRA sourcing rules, Minerals Security Partnership, EU Critical Raw Materials Act, stockpiling).

### V. The capital cycle underground

Mine lead times, financing, permitting, and the historical pattern of price spikes failing to summon fast new supply; at least one deeply researched historical mining boom-bust case.

### VI. The human and environmental ledger

Artisanal cobalt mining and labor conditions in the DRC, Indonesian nickel smelting's environmental costs, tailings dam failures (Brumadinho as the central case), water conflicts around lithium brine extraction, and the resource curse literature.

### VII. The hedges

Battery recycling economics, deep-sea polymetallic nodule mining and the ISA regulatory fight, and materials substitution (LFP, sodium-ion, rare-earth-free motors) as partial, imperfect responses to concentration.

## Source discipline

- Prefer USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries, IEA reports, company filings and earnings materials, government press releases and regulatory filings, and original academic/NBER research over secondary blogs.
- Separate mine production share from refining/processing share; they are frequently different numbers and conflating them is a common and misleading simplification in popular writing on this subject.
- Record the vintage of every dataset; mineral prices and export-control regimes move quickly and a 2023 figure should not be presented as current without a date attached.
- Distinguish verified quotations and well-documented policy actions from repeated but poorly sourced claims (for example, the widely circulated Deng Xiaoping rare-earths remark); flag confidence level explicitly where sourcing is thin.
- Preserve counterevidence rather than arguing it away. Include cases where high prices did summon supply, where substitution or recycling outperformed expectations, and where resource wealth co-existed with good governance.
- Never quote a historical or contemporary figure without verifying the original source and, where possible, the original language and translation history.
- For every major claim, keep a note titled "What would make this false?"
