# Chapter 13 research note — Deep-sea mining after the 2026 U.S. permitting shift

Research checked September 3, 2026 for Chapter 13, “Deep Sea and Desperate Measures.”

## Claim under test

Chapter 13 should not be written as a simple race between a heroic new mineral frontier and an untouched ocean. The stronger argument is that deep-sea mining concentrates three unresolved questions in one place: who has authority to permit extraction beyond national jurisdiction, how much ecological damage can be measured before commercial-scale disturbance exists, and whether avoiding some land-mining harms is enough to justify creating a new industrial footprint on the abyssal plain.

The chapter should distinguish clearly between exploration, test mining, permit applications, and actual commercial recovery. As of September 3, 2026, NOAA lists no existing U.S. commercial recovery permits under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act.

## Primary regulatory spine: NOAA’s 2026 process is real, but commercial recovery has not been authorized

NOAA’s current Deep Seabed Hard Minerals Mining page says the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA) authorizes NOAA to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits to U.S. companies for polymetallic nodules beyond national jurisdiction. It also states explicitly that the United States is not a party to the Law of the Sea Convention, while the International Seabed Authority regulates activities in the international seabed Area for states that are parties to that convention.

Source: NOAA, `Deep Seabed Hard Minerals Mining`
https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/deep-seabed-mineral-resources/deep-seabed-mining/

On January 21, 2026, NOAA issued a final rule allowing qualified applicants to use a consolidated exploration-license and commercial-recovery-permit application process. NOAA describes the change as procedural streamlining rather than a removal of substantive statutory obligations.

Sources:
- NOAA, `NOAA accelerates permitting timeline for deep seabed mining applications`, January 21, 2026
https://www.noaa.gov/news-release/noaa-accelerates-permitting-timeline-for-deep-seabed-mining-applications
- NOAA Small Business Compliance Guide for the January 21, 2026 DSHMRA final rule
https://cdn.oceanservice.noaa.gov/oceanserviceprod/deep-sea-mining/noaa-small-business-compliance-guide-DSHMRA-Jan-2026.pdf

The most current procedural fact matters for the chapter’s opening. NOAA lists The Metals Company USA’s consolidated exploration-license and commercial-recovery-permit application as publicly noticed on August 19, 2026, with public comment open through October 19, 2026. NOAA still lists `N/A` under existing commercial recovery permits.

Source: NOAA, `Deep Seabed Hard Minerals Mining`, applications and permits section, checked September 3, 2026.

This means the chapter should not write that TMC “has permission to mine” or that U.S.-authorized commercial seabed mining has begun. The sharper description is that a U.S. domestic permitting route is now active and materially further along than it was a year earlier.

## The international route remains unresolved

The International Seabed Authority says it has been developing exploitation regulations since 2014. Its March 2026 Council session discussed 29 of 32 identified outstanding issues in the draft regulations and advanced several matters in principle, but did not complete the Mining Code.

Sources:
- International Seabed Authority, `The Mining Code`
https://isa.org.jm/the-mining-code/
- International Seabed Authority, `The Council of the International Seabed Authority advanced negotiations on the Mining Code`, March 31, 2026
https://isa.org.jm/news/the-council-of-the-international-seabed-authority-advanced-negotiations-on-the-mining-code/

The legal conflict sharpened in 2026. Nauru Ocean Resources Inc. and Tonga Offshore Mining Ltd. brought proceedings against the ISA before the Seabed Disputes Chamber of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea concerning ISA inquiries into contractor conduct. The Chamber delivered provisional-measures orders on July 18, 2026. The ISA emphasized the next day that those orders did not decide jurisdiction or the merits and that its inquiry process remained in force.

Sources:
- ITLOS Case 34, Nauru Ocean Resources Inc. v. International Seabed Authority
https://www.itlos.org/en/main/cases/list-of-cases/case-concerning-an-inquiry-by-the-international-seabed-authority-nauru-ocean-resources-inc-v-international-seabed-authority/
- ITLOS Case 35, Tonga Offshore Mining Ltd. v. International Seabed Authority
https://www.itlos.org/en/main/cases/list-of-cases/case-concerning-an-inquiry-by-the-international-seabed-authority-tonga-offshore-mining-ltd-v-international-seabed-authority/
- International Seabed Authority statement, July 19, 2026
https://isa.org.jm/news/statement-on-the-orders-delivered-by-the-seabed-disputes-chamber-of-the-international-tribunal-for-the-law-of-the-sea/

Editorial boundary: do not reduce this to `the U.S. bypassed international law` or `the ISA blocked mining`. The relevant fact is that two legal-regulatory systems now claim meaningful authority over overlapping practical ambitions, and the disagreement is still being litigated and negotiated.

## Ecological evidence: disturbance is persistent, but impact is not uniform

A 2025 Nature study revisited a 44-year-old experimental mining disturbance and found that directly disturbed areas remained physically altered and biological communities remained changed after four decades, although several organism groups had begun to re-establish. Areas affected mainly by sediment plumes showed more recovery than the directly disturbed tracks.

Source: `Long-term impact and biological recovery in a deep-sea mining track`, Nature, 2025
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08921-3

This is more useful than saying vaguely that `the ocean may never recover`. Some effects persist for decades; some assemblages recover substantially; direct nodule removal and plume exposure are not equivalent disturbances.

A 2025 Nature Communications field study of a collector test found that much of the benthic sediment plume stayed close to the seafloor, with highest concentrations near the bottom and background concentrations reached at roughly 50 metres monitoring altitude. That cuts against the most dramatic versions of an ocean-wide plume argument, while still documenting centimetre-scale redeposition near mining lanes and direct seafloor erosion.

Source: `Monitoring benthic plumes, sediment redeposition and seafloor imprints caused by deep-sea polymetallic nodule mining`, Nature Communications, 2025
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-56311-0

A separate 2025 Nature Communications study found a different risk in the water column: proposed discharge of nutritionally poor mining particles into lower mesopelagic and upper bathypelagic waters could dilute the particles that support local food webs and plausibly propagate effects upward through zooplankton and micronekton. The authors argue that returning discharge closer to the seafloor may reduce this particular midwater risk, though it would not remove benthic impacts.

Source: `Deep-sea mining discharge can disrupt midwater food webs`, Nature Communications, 2025
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65411-w

The chapter should therefore resist one generic `sediment plume` bucket. Collector-track disturbance, benthic redeposition, and midwater discharge have different mechanisms and different evidence.

## Strongest counterargument: land mining has real harms too

The strongest case for nodules is comparative rather than absolute. A 2022 peer-reviewed systems analysis in the Journal of Industrial Ecology found that producing battery metals from polymetallic nodules could generate less mining and processing waste than several terrestrial routes, while explicitly caveating the uncertain ecological severity of disturbed deep-sea sediment. One of the paper’s authors was affiliated with The Metals Company, so the study should not be treated as neutral proof of net environmental superiority.

Source: Paulikas et al., `Deep-sea nodules versus land ores: A comparative systems analysis of mining and processing wastes for battery-metal supply chains`, Journal of Industrial Ecology, 2022
https://doi.org/10.1111/jiec.13225

This counterargument should stay in the chapter. Land-based nickel, cobalt, copper and manganese production can involve deforestation, tailings, freshwater demand, community displacement, pollution, and substantial waste rock. A blanket claim that leaving nodules untouched is environmentally costless simply moves the comparison offstage.

But the reverse overclaim is equally weak. A lower mass of waste on land does not establish that damage to a slowly recovering abyssal ecosystem is commensurable with terrestrial impacts. The comparison depends on value judgments as well as engineering data, and the deep-sea baseline remains much less observed than mature land-mining systems.

## Editorial implication

A stronger Chapter 13 should frame deep-sea mining as an attempt to exchange one class of chokepoints and harms for another, not as a magical new source of metal.

The most durable factual sequence is:

1. nodules contain several useful metals in one ore-like object and avoid conventional overburden;
2. commercial extraction beyond national jurisdiction is still not an established industry;
3. the U.S. created a faster consolidated DSHMRA pathway in January 2026 and TMC USA now has an active consolidated application under public review;
4. the ISA Mining Code remains unfinished and contractor disputes have moved into formal international litigation;
5. field evidence shows long-lived direct disturbance, more limited plume persistence in some settings, and distinct midwater food-web risks;
6. land mining provides the strongest environmental counterfactual, but present evidence does not collapse the comparison into one obvious winner.

The chapter’s governing question should therefore become narrower and harder: when the alternative is more terrestrial mining, what level of ecological uncertainty is acceptable before industrial disturbance begins in a place where recovery can take decades and the governing rules are still being written?

That gives Chapter 13 a better handoff to Chapter 14. If the deep ocean is not a frictionless escape from concentrated supply, governments are pushed back toward the more prosaic work of building alternative mines, processors, alliances and financing systems among countries they already trade with.
