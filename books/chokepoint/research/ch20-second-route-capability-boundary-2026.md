# Chapter 20 research — a second route must be an operating capability

## Claim under test

Chapter 20 closes on the idea that resilience comes from credible second routes rather than autarky. That claim is useful only if "second route" means more than a second mine or an announced project. A route has to connect enough of the chain — processing, equipment, know-how, qualification, feedstock, logistics, and customers — to deliver usable material during the disruption it is meant to insure against.

## Fresh primary / authoritative check

The IEA's *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* makes the upstream/downstream mismatch unusually concrete. Outside dominant suppliers, announced rare-earth mining projects could provide nearly 50 kt of capacity by 2035, while planned refining and separation capacity is below 40 kt and planned downstream metals, alloys, and magnet capacity is only about 18 kt on a rare-earth-content basis. The IEA reports similar mismatches for lithium, graphite, nickel, and cobalt: mining diversification is advancing faster than midstream and downstream capacity.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, Outlook, 16 July 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook

The same report argues that diversification is an ecosystem problem, not simply a project count. Processing depends on specialised equipment and accumulated technical expertise that can themselves be concentrated. The IEA notes, for example, that outside China only a handful of suppliers provide key battery-grade-graphite processing equipment; some ultra-high-purity gallium and semiconductor processes depend on one or two specialised equipment suppliers; and rare-earth magnet grain-boundary-diffusion equipment has only one supplier outside China, with reported equipment costs and lead times substantially worse than in the incumbent ecosystem.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, Executive summary, 16 July 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

This is direct support for Chapter 20's existing line that "a redundant route that shares the same hidden dependency is not redundant." It also supplies a stricter test: count deliverable capability, not flags on a map.

## Policy check: diversification, not self-sufficiency

The European Commission's description of the Critical Raw Materials Act matches the chapter's framing. Its 2030 benchmarks are at least 10% of EU annual needs from EU extraction, 40% from EU processing, and 25% from EU recycling, while seeking to keep dependence on any single third country below 65% at a relevant processing stage for each strategic raw material. The Commission separately describes monitoring, stress tests, strategic stockpiles, investment, trade, innovation, and skills as resilience tools.

The policy architecture therefore does not define security as producing everything domestically. It combines domestic capability with diversified external supply and preparedness.

Source: European Commission, "European Critical Raw Materials Act."
https://commission.europa.eu/topics/competitiveness/green-deal-industrial-plan/european-critical-raw-materials-act_en

## Strongest counterargument

Redundancy can become an excuse for expensive capacity that would not survive without permanent support. The IEA's 2025 policy analysis estimates that mining and refining capital expenditures outside dominant producers are typically around 50% higher than within the leading producing country. A second route can therefore impose a real insurance premium, and not every proposed alternative deserves to be financed.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025*, "Policy mechanisms for diversified mineral supplies."
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/policy-mechanisms-for-diversified-mineral-supplies

The chapter already anticipates this objection with its warning that "critical" should not suspend arithmetic. The evidence suggests a useful refinement: the right comparison is not cheapest route versus expensive route in an uninterrupted market. It is the expected cost of maintaining an actually operable alternative versus the expected cost of losing access to the dominant route.

That does **not** imply every alternative passes the test. A project with no customers, no qualification path, no feedstock, no skilled operators, or the same hidden equipment dependency can be expensive and strategically useless at once.

## Disconfirming evidence and signs of progress

Concentration is not immutable. The IEA reports that rare-earth refining was a notable exception to the broader concentration trend in 2025: new US projects and higher Malaysian production reduced the leading supplier's share from above 90% in 2023 to about 85% in 2025. If planned projects arrive as scheduled, the IEA projects that share could fall to about 70% by 2035.

Source: International Energy Agency, "Supply concentration, export restrictions and declining investment put critical mineral security at risk," 16 July 2026.
https://www.iea.org/news/supply-concentration-export-restrictions-and-declining-investment-put-critical-mineral-security-at-risk

This is the strongest counterexample to fatalism in the finale. Targeted policy and investment can create a genuine second route. The difficult part is that the route has to extend far enough downstream to work.

## Strongest defensible formulation

A mature mineral system is not one with two mines on a map. It is one in which a disruption leaves at least one alternative path capable of delivering qualified material to the user who needs it. That path may involve foreign suppliers, stockpiles, recycling, substitution, or deliberately redundant processing. What matters is whether it works when called.

The final chapter should therefore treat "another route" as an operational claim, not a geographic one.

## What would make this false?

Revise this framing if upstream diversification reliably produces downstream diversification without targeted capability-building; if concentrated equipment, know-how, and qualification cease to be material constraints; or if the cost of maintaining alternative routes consistently exceeds plausible disruption losses across strategic applications.
