# Chapter 3 — oil analogy boundaries (2026 check)

## Why this note exists

Chapter 3 is strongest when the oil analogy is treated as a mechanism to test rather than a slogan. Two sources already in the book's ledger support that distinction, but they need to be read with their limits intact.

## 1973: what the historical record actually supports

Federal Reserve History's account of the 1973–74 shock says OAPEC's U.S. embargo began on October 19, 1973, alongside production cuts by Arab exporters. It records the oil price rising from about $2.90 a barrel before the embargo to $11.65 by January 1974, with the embargo lifted in March while the higher price persisted.

Source: https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/oil-shock-of-1973-74

The same source is useful counterevidence against a monocausal retelling. It emphasizes that the United States entered the embargo with industrial commodity inflation already running hot, plant utilization near capacity, major-material shortages, weak domestic spare oil-production capacity, and recent dollar devaluations. The embargo intensified an already vulnerable macroeconomic setting; it should not be described as a clean single-cause explanation for the decade's inflation.

## 2026 MIT result: model implication, not observed historical estimate

Adrien Concordel, Phuong Ho, and Christopher R. Knittel's January 2026 MIT CEEPR working paper compares oil-price and critical-mineral-price shocks in a unified neoclassical growth model. Its central mechanism matches the manuscript well: oil prices raise the cost of using existing capital, while critical-mineral prices mainly raise the cost of creating new capital.

Source: https://ceepr.mit.edu/workingpaper/the-differential-impacts-of-critical-mineral-prices-and-oil-prices-on-the-economy/

Within that model, both shocks reduce output and welfare, but oil-price increases are systematically more contractionary. Mineral-price shocks produce relatively larger adjustments in investment, capital, and external borrowing, with smaller and more gradual aggregate output and welfare losses.

The boundary matters: this is a modeled comparison, not an empirical estimate showing that every real mineral shock will be milder than every oil shock. A highly specific mineral shortage can still stop a factory or weapons line quickly when inventories are thin and qualified substitutes do not exist. The paper supports the chapter's aggregate stock-versus-flow distinction; it does not license a universal claim about the severity of every firm-level disruption.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter

The oil analogy may still understate mineral risk in narrow sectors. Oil is globally traded at enormous scale, whereas some strategically important mineral products are tiny markets with few qualified suppliers, long customer-qualification cycles, and little redundant capacity. A small market can therefore create a sharp operational chokepoint even when its macroeconomic footprint is modest.

That does not overturn the chapter's argument. It suggests the cleanest formulation is two clocks at once: oil shocks are unusually capable of hitting the existing capital stock across the whole economy, while mineral shocks may be slower in aggregate yet immediate inside particular industrial chains.

## Editorial implication

When Chapter 3 is next revised, preserve three distinctions explicitly:

- OAPEC's 1973 embargo was a major shock, but it hit an economy already under inflationary and capacity pressure.
- The 2026 MIT result is a model result about transmission mechanisms, not a universal empirical ranking of future crises.
- "Slower in aggregate" must not be allowed to read as "harmless at the factory gate."

Those qualifications make the oil comparison harder to misuse without weakening the chapter's central claim that mineral leverage often works through the time needed to build, qualify, and finance replacement capacity.
