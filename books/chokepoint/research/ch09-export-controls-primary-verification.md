# Chapter 9 — export-control primary verification

Checked September 3, 2026.

## Claim under test

Chapter 9 traces the shift from episodic mineral leverage to a formal licensing regime around rare earths and other dual-use materials. The key factual risk is to collapse several different Chinese measures into one permanent "ban." The primary record shows a sequence of controls with materially different scopes, implementation dates, and later suspensions.

## April 2025: seven medium and heavy rare earth categories

On April 4, 2025, China's Ministry of Commerce and General Administration of Customs issued Announcement No. 18 of 2025. It placed export controls on items related to samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium. The measure covered metals, alloys, oxides, compounds, targets, and—where specified—permanent-magnet materials. Exporters were required to apply for licenses under China's dual-use export-control regime.

Primary source: Ministry of Commerce and General Administration of Customs, Announcement No. 18 of 2025, April 4, 2025.
https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/Policies/AnnouncementsOrders/art/2025/art_0dd87cbee7b045bf93fabe6ab2faceee.html

This is better described as a licensing control than as a blanket export ban. MOFCOM's own May 15, 2025 press conference stated that some Chinese rare-earth magnet makers had already obtained export licenses. That distinction matters for the chapter's argument: leverage can arise from administrative discretion, delay, uncertainty, and end-use screening even when compliant trade is legally possible.

Primary source: MOFCOM Regular Press Conference, May 15, 2025.
https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/News/PressConference/art/2025/art_2822201e74a844859550521c782253b6.html

## October 2025 expansion—and the part later suspended

On October 9, 2025, China announced additional controls covering five more medium and heavy rare earth categories—holmium, erbium, thulium, europium, and ytterbium—along with selected rare-earth equipment and inputs, superhard materials, lithium-battery items, and artificial-graphite anode materials. MOFCOM described these as dual-use export controls and said compliant applications could be licensed.

Primary sources:
- MOFCOM spokesperson remarks, October 9, 2025.
  https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/syxwfb/art/2025/art_9ef35b6a69f24e61a8cbd63ed0a42d16.html
- MOFCOM / General Administration of Customs Announcement No. 57 of 2025.
  https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zfxxgk/gkml/art/2025/art_b9cf403808634a649ce8b3f921f4dcf3.html

A separate October 9 measure, MOFCOM Announcement No. 61 of 2025, extended licensing requirements extraterritorially to certain foreign-made items containing specified Chinese-origin rare-earth inputs or made using covered Chinese rare-earth technology. This measure is analytically distinct from the April controls on exports from China itself.

Primary source: MOFCOM Announcement No. 61 of 2025, October 9, 2025.
https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zfxxgk/fdzdgknr/ztfl/dwmygl/art/2025/art_148254ba99284928ae6e4f84d1d6f297.html

The chapter should not present all October measures as continuously operative through 2026. In an April 9, 2026 response, MOFCOM stated that the export-control measures China announced on October 9, 2025 had been suspended under the Kuala Lumpur China-U.S. economic and trade consensus through November 10, 2026. That is the current primary-source status as of this note.

Primary source: MOFCOM/Xinhua report of the April 9, 2026 regular press conference.
https://bombay.mofcom.gov.cn/jmxw/art/2026/art_aa03380c0a93428db090cda7656d33d7.html

## What remains operative in 2026

The suspension of the October package does not erase the April 4, 2025 controls. MOFCOM's 2026 export-license materials continue to treat rare earths as goods subject to export licensing, while dual-use items must first obtain the applicable dual-use export license. The safest manuscript formulation is therefore not "China suspended rare-earth controls" in the aggregate, but that later October 2025 measures were suspended while the earlier licensing architecture remained relevant.

Primary source: MOFCOM and General Administration of Customs, 2026 Export License Administration Catalogue, announced December 30, 2025 and effective January 1, 2026.
https://wms.mofcom.gov.cn/zcfb/wmgl/art/2025/art_c7cfa341670b456c8119042be393e062.html

MOFCOM also reported on December 18, 2025 that some Chinese exporters had begun receiving general licenses for rare-earth exports after accumulating compliance experience. That is additional evidence against describing the regime as a simple embargo.

Primary source: MOFCOM Regular Press Conference, December 18, 2025.
https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/News/PressConference/art/2025/art_ab552360b92f43ccbeb0054d2715768b.html

## Boundaries for the manuscript

The chapter can accurately say that China has built a progressively more formal export-control apparatus around strategic minerals and rare-earth products. It should distinguish at least three mechanisms: licensing of specified exports from China, restrictions targeted at particular destinations or end uses, and the attempted extraterritorial reach of the October 2025 rules.

It should not turn "export control" into "export ban." Licensing can itself create leverage because approvals, end-use scrutiny, timing, and uncertainty affect supply chains, but the legal mechanism matters. Nor should the chapter imply that every control announced in 2025 remained continuously effective through 2026; the October package was suspended through November 10, 2026 under the stated bilateral consensus.

The strongest analytical point is narrower and more durable: concentrated processing capacity gives an export-licensing system strategic weight that the same paperwork would not have in a diversified market. The geopolitical mechanism is concentration plus administrative control, not the word "ban" by itself.
