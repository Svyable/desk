## CHAPTER 6 — CONGO'S COBALT

### Key facts and sources

- **DRC share of world cobalt mine production is roughly 70–76%, depending on year/source; most-cited round figure is ~70%.** IEA states "The Democratic Republic of Congo supplies 70% of cobalt" as of the *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025* (IEA, published 2025), https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary — IEA's flagship critical-minerals report; good headline citation. A search-engine summary of USGS *Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026* cobalt chapter reported DRC at "73% of world total" for the most recent year (USGS, pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-cobalt.pdf) — **I could not directly fetch/verify this PDF** because pubs.usgs.gov and usgs.gov are blocked by this session's network egress proxy; confirm the exact 2026 MCS cobalt figure directly before citing a specific percentage. Use IEA's 70% as the safe, verifiable headline number; USGS MCS (published each January, free PDF) is the standard primary source to re-check by hand.
- **World cobalt mine production in 2025 was on the order of 310,000 metric tons (rounded), Congo and Indonesia the top two producers, Indonesia ~10–12%.** Derived from a web search summarizing USGS MCS 2025 data, https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-cobalt.pdf — **primary source, could not directly fetch; re-verify numbers against the actual PDF** before publication.
- **CMOC (China Molybdenum Co.) became the world's largest cobalt producer, overtaking Glencore, with 2024 output of 114,165 tonnes — nearly double 2023's 55,526 tonnes — driven mainly by its Kisanfu mine (KFM) ramping up alongside Tenke Fungurume (TFM).** "CMOC sets cobalt production record in 2024," MINING.COM, 2025, https://www.mining.com/cmoc-sets-cobalt-production-record-in-2024/ ; corroborated by "Cobalt: Chinese Firm CMOC Doubles Output in 2024 Despite Lower Prices," Ecofin Agency, https://www.ecofinagency.com/mining/0801-46296-cobalt-chinese-firm-cmoc-doubles-output-in-2024-despite-lower-prices — strong, current, gives CMOC's global market share rising from 24% (2023) to 41% (2024). Good anchor stat for "China now produces more of the world's cobalt than any single country besides DRC itself."
- **Tenke Fungurume: Freeport-McMoRan sold its 70% interest in TF Holdings (an effective 56% interest in Tenke Fungurume Mining) to China Molybdenum Co. (CMOC) for $2.65 billion, announced May 9, 2016, deal completed November 2016.** Freeport-McMoRan Form 8-K, SEC filing, May 9, 2016, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000831259/000083125916000069/exhibit99105-09x2016.htm — primary source (SEC filing), highly reliable. Deal included contingent payments up to $120M tied to copper/cobalt prices exceeding $3.50/lb and $20/lb respectively in 2018–19. Background/financing: China Development Bank contributed $790 million via a $1.59 billion syndicated loan for the acquisition (AidData/China.AidData.org project record), https://china.aiddata.org/projects/91897/ — useful for the "Chinese state financing" angle.
- **Mutanda (Glencore): the world's largest cobalt mine at the time, produced 27,000 tonnes of cobalt hydroxide in 2018 (≈20% of global cobalt supply that year), before Glencore put it on care and maintenance in November 2019 citing falling cobalt prices, rising costs, and higher DRC taxes.** MINING.COM / S&P Global reporting on the closure and 2021–22 restart plans, https://www.mining.com/web/glencore-to-restart-operations-at-mutanda-copper-cobalt-mine-in-2022/ and https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/articles/2021/5/glencore-aims-to-restart-congo-s-mutanda-copper-cobalt-mine-in-fy-22-8211-reuters-64378768 — reputable trade press; good scene for "boom-bust of a single company's cobalt decision moving global markets."
- **Kamoto Copper Company (KCC): owned 70% Glencore / 25% Gécamines (DRC state miner) / 5% DRC state; as of 2022 the Kamoto mine complex is described as the largest active cobalt mine in the world.** Wikipedia entries on Katanga Mining and Kamoto mine, cross-checked against Glencore's own DRC operations page, https://www.glencore.cd/en/our-operations — Glencore's site is a primary company source; Wikipedia used for structure summary, verify percentages against Glencore's own filings/CSR reports before final use. Katanga Mining Ltd (the Canadian-listed operator of KCC) was fully absorbed into Glencore in 2020 and delisted from the Toronto Stock Exchange.
- **Chinese firms own or hold stakes in roughly 15 of the DRC's 19 major cobalt-producing mines; estimates of Chinese-controlled share of DRC cobalt output range from ~70% to ~80%, and China separately controls roughly 80% of global cobalt refining capacity.** Multiple corroborating secondary sources surfaced in search: Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, "How much cobalt production is owned by Chinese companies?", https://source.benchmarkminerals.com/article/how-much-cobalt-production-is-owned-by-chinese-companies (industry data provider, good for the ownership-count figure); CSIS, "A Window of Opportunity to Build Critical Mineral Security in Africa," https://www.csis.org/analysis/window-opportunity-build-critical-mineral-security-africa (think-tank analysis); CECC (Congressional-Executive Commission on China) hearing "From Cobalt to Cars: How China Exploits Child and Forced Labor in the Congo," https://www.cecc.gov/events/hearings/from-cobalt-to-cars-how-china-exploits-child-and-forced-labor-in-the-congo (US government hearing record — good primary-ish source for the political framing, but note it is advocacy-inflected, from a US congressional commission with a stated adversarial view of China). **Caution:** exact ownership percentages vary by source/year (70% vs 80% appear repeatedly but are not identical figures from identical methodologies) — pin down one consistent, dated figure before print, ideally from a mining-data provider like Benchmark, CRU, or Fastmarkets with a named report.
- **Sicomines joint venture: Chinese state-linked firms China Railway Group (CREC), Sinohydro, and Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt hold a combined 68% stake, financed via a "minerals-for-infrastructure" deal with over $7.8 billion in lending from Chinese creditors to DRC.** Surfaced via AidData research blog "Chasing copper and cobalt: China's mining operations in Peru and the DRC," https://www.aiddata.org/blog/chasing-copper-and-cobalt-chinas-mining-operations-in-peru-and-the-drc — AidData (William & Mary) is a respected academic data source on Chinese overseas lending; good primary-adjacent citation for the "infrastructure-for-minerals" narrative (this is the deal often compared to a 21st-century resource concession).
- **Amnesty International/Afrewatch report "This is what we die for: Human rights abuses in the Democratic Republic of the Congo power the global trade in cobalt," published January 2016.** Amnesty International, full report, https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/afr62/3183/2016/en/ ; summary/campaign page https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/campaigns/2016/06/drc-cobalt-child-labour/ — primary NGO investigative report, widely cited, methodologically transparent (co-authored with Congolese NGO Afrewatch). Documents children as young as seven working up to 12-hour days for $1–2/day; traces cobalt from artisanal mines through traders to Congo Dongfang Mining (CDM, a wholly owned subsidiary of Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt) and onward to battery makers supplying Apple, Microsoft, Samsung, Sony, Daimler, Volkswagen. **Companies' formal responses are compiled in a companion Amnesty document**, https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/afr62/3412/2016/en/ — useful for "what companies said when confronted."
- **Washington Post investigation "The Cobalt Pipeline" by Todd C. Frankel, published September 30, 2016.** Washington Post, https://www.washingtonpost.com/graphics/business/batteries/congo-cobalt-mining-for-lithium-ion-battery/ — major mainstream investigative piece; estimated 100,000 cobalt miners in Congo using hand tools to dig hundreds of feet underground with little oversight; traced supply chain to Congo DongFang International Mining (CDM). Follow-on WaPo pieces: companies' responses (Sept 30, 2016), https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/economy/companies-respond-to-questions-about-their-cobalt-supply-chains/2016/09/30/910f94de-7b51-11e6-bd86-b7bbd53d2b5d_story.html ; tech giants' pledge to keep children out of cobalt mines (Dec 20, 2016), https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/the-switch/wp/2016/12/20/tech-giants-pledge-to-keep-children-out-of-cobalt-mines-that-supply-smartphone-and-electric-car-batteries/ ; State Department signaling interest (Dec 23, 2016), https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/the-switch/wp/2016/12/23/state-department-signals-interest-in-how-tech-companies-address-abuses-in-cobalt-supply-chain/ — good chain of accountability-journalism follow-through for narrative arc (investigation → industry pledges → limited follow-through).
- **International Rights Advocates filed a federal lawsuit (John Doe I et al. v. Apple, Alphabet/Google, Dell, Microsoft, Tesla) on December 15, 2019, on behalf of 14 DRC parents/children, alleging the companies knowingly benefited from child labor in cobalt mines supplying Glencore, Umicore, and Huayou Cobalt.** IRAdvocates case page, https://www.internationalrightsadvocates.org/cases/cobalt ; CNN report on filing, https://www.cnn.com/2019/12/17/tech/apple-microsoft-tesla-dell-congo-cobalt-mining — **Outcome: the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals unanimously dismissed the suit on March 5, 2024**, ruling plaintiffs had standing but failed to meet the legal elements required (proximate cause / "aiding and abetting" standard under the Trafficking Victims Protection Reauthorization Act). CNN, https://www.cnn.com/2024/03/05/tech/big-tech-child-labor-congo-lawsuit-throw-out — important for the book: shows the *limits* of legal accountability mechanisms, a strong narrative beat.
- **Fatal mine collapses recur regularly in the Kolwezi/Lualaba artisanal belt.** June 2019: a landslide/collapse at the KOV open-pit (operated by Glencore's Kamoto Copper Company, with artisanal miners working illegally within the concession) killed at least 43 people. Al Jazeera, "DR Congo mine collapse death toll rises to 43," June 28, 2019, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2019/6/28/dr-congo-mine-collapse-death-toll-rises-to-43 — reputable wire-style reporting, good scene-setting anecdote. More recent: a November 2025 bridge/pit collapse at a Lualaba cobalt mine killed at least 32 informal miners (Daily Sabah / AP wire reporting), https://www.dailysabah.com/world/africa/at-least-32-killed-in-dr-congo-mine-collapse — shows the danger is ongoing, not historical. Also useful context figure: estimates of "10,000 to 15,000 hand-dug tunnels" in the region with no engineering supports, cited in African Arguments, "'We miners die a lot': The lives of cobalt miners in the DRC," Feb 2024, https://africanarguments.org/2024/02/we-miners-die-a-lot-the-lives-of-cobalt-miners-in-the-drc/ — secondary but vivid; verify the tunnel-count figure's original source before using as a hard statistic.
- **Glencore–Dan Gertler corruption scandal: Glencore partnered from 2007 with Israeli mining financier Dan Gertler (close to then-president Joseph Kabila) to access DRC copper/cobalt assets; the 2017 Paradise Papers leak revealed a $45 million loan to a Gertler-controlled entity while Gertler helped Glencore negotiate DRC mining deals. Swiss courts sentenced Glencore in August 2024 to a fine plus a CHF 150 million compensatory payment; roughly $26 million was found to have been paid to Gertler via Swiss accounts, including $10 million in cash to a Congolese official close to the president.** ICIJ (International Consortium of Investigative Journalists), Paradise Papers-linked reporting, https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/us-sanctions-mining-magnate-accused-of-corruption-in-the-congo-reversing-trump-era-move/ ; Public Eye (Swiss investigative NGO), "Glencore, Gertler and the Congo," https://www.publiceye.ch/en/topics/commodities/rohma/when-commodity-traders-get-caught/glencore-gertler-and-the-congo ; Bloomberg, "Glencore-Congo Mine Row Is Tied to Sanctioned Ex-Partner Gertler," Oct 3, 2024, https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-10-03/glencore-congo-mine-row-is-tied-to-sanctioned-ex-partner-gertler — this is a strong, well-documented corruption thread (ICIJ/Paradise Papers, US OFAC sanctions on Gertler since 2017, Swiss and Dutch criminal/bribery settlements) that sits well alongside the labor-conditions material as a second axis of "how cobalt gets extracted."
- **The battery-chemistry shift away from cobalt: LFP (lithium iron phosphate) batteries made up nearly half of the global EV battery market in 2024; in China, LFP met nearly three-quarters of domestic battery demand in 2024, reaching ~80% of batteries sold in Nov–Dec 2024.** IEA, *Global EV Outlook 2025*, "Electric vehicle batteries" section, https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-batteries — IEA primary report, best citation for this figure; corroborated by industry-analysis summaries in search (carboncredits.com, Cobalt Institute market report). Cobalt intensity (kg cobalt per kWh) reportedly dropped by roughly two-thirds from 2020 to 2024 as LFP share rose — this figure surfaced via search summary of Cobalt Institute's *Cobalt Market Report 2024* (May 2025), https://www.cobaltinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Cobalt-Market-Report-2024.pdf — **industry-association primary source (Cobalt Institute is funded by cobalt producers, so treat market narrative with appropriate skepticism, but its production/demand statistics are generally considered reliable and widely cited)**.
- **Cobalt prices collapsed to a nine-year low in early 2024 and continued down to roughly $21,000/tonne (LME) in early February 2025 — the lowest since 2016 — driven by oversupply from both DRC (CMOC's Kisanfu ramp-up) and Indonesia (nickel-byproduct cobalt).** Then, **the DRC imposed a full suspension of cobalt exports starting February 2025** to support prices, which drove a roughly 70% price spike within three weeks, cobalt exceeding $35,000/tonne by early March 2025, later rallying further to roughly $47,000/tonne by late October 2025. S&P Global, "COMMODITIES 2025: Cobalt market oversupply to ease in 2025," Dec 19, 2024, https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/metals/121924-commodities-2025-cobalt-market-oversupply-to-ease-in-2025 ; Investing News Network, "Cobalt Market 2025 Year-End Review," https://investingnews.com/daily/resource-investing/battery-metals-investing/cobalt-investing/cobalt-market-update/ ; Fastmarkets, "Dried-up feedstock pipeline sends cobalt prices soaring in 2025," https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/dried-up-feedstock-pipeline-cobalt-prices-soaring-2025-deficit/ — **the DRC export suspension is a major, book-relevant policy event** (DRC unilaterally using export controls to manage global price, analogous to Indonesia's nickel playbook in Ch.7) — worth its own paragraph/scene; verify exact suspension dates and duration (initially announced as ~4 months) against a Reuters/Bloomberg primary wire story before final draft.
- **Cobalt supply grew roughly 21% in 2024 to about 288,200 metric tons; ~45,000 of the ~50,240-tonne global increase came from the DRC (largely CMOC's Kisanfu ramp-up), most of the remainder from Indonesia.** Sourced via search summary citing Cobalt Institute/Fastmarkets-style analysis, cross-check against S&P Global and Fastmarkets links above before using precise tonnage in print — treat as a good order-of-magnitude figure pending direct-PDF verification.

### Narrative material

- **The Kolwezi "creuseurs":** individual artisanal miners (Lingala/Swahili slang "creuseurs," diggers) dig by hand with pickaxes and rebar, sometimes 60+ meters down unsupported shafts, in and immediately around industrial concessions like Glencore's KCC — vivid physical detail available from Amnesty's "This is what we die for" (2016) and WaPo's "The Cobalt Pipeline" (2016), both of which include direct interview quotes from miners, including child miners, describing wages ($1–2/day), work hours (up to 12 hrs), and health effects.
- **The Tenke Fungurume ownership handoff (2016)** is a clean, single-scene emblem of the US-to-China handover in Congolese mining: Freeport-McMoRan (Phoenix-based, publicly traded, needing to cut debt after the oil-price crash) selling to China Molybdenum for $2.65B, financed substantially by China Development Bank ($790M tranche). Good "changing of the guard" narrative beat for opening or closing Chapter 6.
- **CMOC's 2024 production doubling** (55,526 → 114,165 tonnes) is a striking single-year statistic that could anchor a "how fast is this happening" paragraph — from ~24% to 41% global market share in one year.
- **Dan Gertler**, an Israeli diamond-and-mining financier with personal ties to former president Joseph Kabila, is a genuinely colorful, book-worthy character: sanctioned by the US Treasury (OFAC) in 2017 (then briefly delisted under a Trump-era move, later reversed), the subject of Paradise Papers revelations, Swiss and Dutch bribery settlements running into hundreds of millions of dollars. A useful embodiment of the "opaque middleman" archetype in resource extraction.
- **The failed Big Tech lawsuit (2019–2024)** is a five-year narrative arc in itself: filed with named child plaintiffs and specific injury allegations, thrown out on a legal technicality (proximate cause) rather than on the facts — good material for the "supply chains are legally unaccountable" theme.
- **Mine collapses as recurring tragedy, not one-off event:** 43 dead in June 2019 (KOV/KCC), 32+ dead in November 2025 (Lualaba bridge/pit collapse) — bookending the whole period the book covers, useful to show nothing structural has changed.

### Counterevidence / complications

- **Responsible-sourcing initiatives are real and growing, though contested in scale.** The Fair Cobalt Alliance (founded August 2020) works directly with artisanal mining communities in Lualaba on child-labor referral systems, worker training, and "living income" programs. https://faircobaltalliance.org/ and https://faircobaltalliance.org/blog/launching-the-fair-cobalt-alliance/ . The Responsible Minerals Initiative (RMI) has, since 2020–22, worked with the Global Battery Alliance, FCA, and DRC government representatives to develop "ASM Cobalt Criteria," piloted at specific mine sites. https://www.responsiblemineralsinitiative.org/news/asm-cobalt/ . As of January 2025, USAID launched a partnership with FCA/RMI to formalize legal, responsibly-sourced artisanal cobalt in Lualaba province — worth checking for a 2026 update on outcomes. Good counterweight to "nothing is being done" framing, though critics (see below) argue these programs cover a small fraction of total ASM output.
- **Kara's *Cobalt Red* (2023) has been criticized on methodological/representational grounds.** openDemocracy review argues Kara "barely acknowledges or cites earlier writing on Congo's artisanal mining, such as the 2016 Amnesty/Afrewatch report," and raises concerns about how Congolese people's agency is portrayed. https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/beyond-trafficking-and-slavery/cobalt-red-siddharth-kara-democratic-republic-congo-book-review/ — useful for the book to differentiate itself methodologically and to avoid repeating criticized framings; also useful to note in any citation of Kara's on-the-ground numbers (e.g., his claim that ASM accounts for a large minority of DRC output) that they have been disputed by DRC-based researchers as overstated.
- **The child-labor lawsuit's dismissal is itself evidence of the limits (not absence) of accountability infrastructure** — worth treating as complication rather than pure vindication either direction: DRC court found standing valid but the legal theory (aiding-and-abetting under a forced-labor statute) insufficient, not that the underlying facts were false.
- **China's dominance is sometimes overstated by percentage-shopping across sources** (70% vs 80% Chinese-controlled DRC cobalt output recur inconsistently) — flag for fact-checking a single authoritative number (e.g., a specific Benchmark Mineral Intelligence or CRU report by name and date) rather than repeating an approximate range.
- **DRC government's own export-suspension move (Feb 2025)** complicates a simple "China/foreign companies extract, Congo is powerless" narrative — the Congolese state has recently used supply-side leverage (similar to Indonesia's playbook) to move global prices, suggesting growing (if still limited) state agency.

## CHAPTER 7 — NICKEL AND THE INDONESIAN GAMBIT

### Key facts and sources

- **Indonesia's nickel ore export ban took effect January 1, 2020**, applying to all grades of ore, ordered regardless of standing contracts; announced in September 2019 (giving roughly four months' transition), moved up two years earlier than a 2017 rule that had targeted a January 2022 start. Al Jazeera, "World's biggest nickel ore maker bans exports from January 2020," Sept 2, 2019, https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/economy/2019/9/2/worlds-biggest-nickel-ore-maker-bans-exports-from-january-2020 ; The Jakarta Post, "Indonesia to ban nickel exports from January 2020," Sept 2, 2019, https://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2019/09/02/indonesia-to-ban-nickel-exports-from-january-2020.html ; IEA policy database entry, "Prohibition of the export of nickel ore," https://www.iea.org/policies/16084-prohibition-of-the-export-of-nickel-ore — IEA entry is the best primary-adjacent citation for legal/policy basis; verify the specific ministerial regulation number (commonly cited as Minister of Energy and Mineral Resources Regulation, under the 2009/2020 Mining Law framework) directly against Indonesian government gazette text before print.
- **This was actually Indonesia's second nickel export ban.** An earlier ban on raw nickel ore exports began January 2014 (under the 2009 Mining Law's downstream-processing mandate); a partial relaxation allowed export of lower-grade ore (below 1.7% nickel content) from January 2017 to December 2019, before the blanket ban resumed January 2020. USITC working paper, "Indonesia's Export Ban of Nickel," https://www.usitc.gov/publications/332/working_papers/ermm_indonesia_export_ban_of_nickel.pdf — US government trade-agency working paper, strong primary-adjacent source with historical/legal detail; use this as the anchor document for the policy history section.
- **Indonesia's share of world nickel mine production rose sharply across this period: ~48% in 2022, and by some 2023 industry estimates ~70% "five years after providing only 30%."** Fastmarkets, "Oversupply sets tone for China's nickel market | 2024 preview," https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/oversupply-sets-tone-for-chinas-nickel-market-2024-preview/ — reputable metals-price-reporting agency; good trend citation, though note the "70%" figure differs from other 2024/2025 estimates below — reconcile before using a single headline number. **USGS-derived figure (via search): Indonesia accounted for 62% of global mined nickel in 2024**, world total ~3.71 million metric tons; a 2025 projection put Indonesia at roughly 2.6 million of 3.9 million tonnes (~67%). USGS MCS 2025/2024 nickel chapters, https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-nickel.pdf and https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2024/mcs2024-nickel.pdf — **primary source, could not directly fetch due to the research environment's egress block on pubs.usgs.gov; re-verify the exact percentage and both years' tonnages against the actual PDFs before publication.** Recommend citing whichever single MCS year's number you can hand-verify, e.g., "USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries [year], Nickel."
- **Tsingshan Holding Group's Indonesia Morowali Industrial Park (IMIP), Sulawesi:** as of a 2024 snapshot, 53 RKEF (rotary kiln electric furnace) production lines with installed annual capacity of roughly 4.2 million tonnes of nickel pig iron (NPI), plus 4 Mt stainless steel billet and 7 Mt carbon steel capacity; roughly 50 companies operating/under construction/planned at the park with total investment of about $34.3 billion as of December 2024. Wood Mackenzie report listing (paywalled, cite title only), "Tsingshan Morowali nickel operation Report," https://www.woodmac.com/reports/metals-tsingshan-morowali-nickel-operation-27952638/ ; Wikipedia "Morowali Industrial Park" (secondary, cross-check against IMIP's own site or Tsingshan filings) — good scale anchor for "how big is this industrial buildout" section; the $34.3B cumulative investment figure and 53-line/4.2Mt capacity figure should ideally be traced to IMIP's own investor communications or an Indonesian government (Kementerian Investasi/BKPM) release for a fully primary citation.
- **Weda Bay Industrial Park (IWIP), Halmahera island, North Maluku:** a joint venture of Tsingshan (majority, ~51.3%), French miner Eramet (~37.8%), and Indonesian state miner PT Aneka Tambang/Antam (~10%) at the mine level (PT Weda Bay Nickel); the broader industrial park (PT IWIP) is majority-controlled by Tsingshan (~46% via subsidiary Perlux Technology) alongside Chinese partners Huayou and Zhenshi. Construction began April 2018; mining operations began October 2019; metallurgical (smelting) production began April 2020. Investment target: roughly $15 billion by 2024. Wikipedia "Weda Bay Industrial Park" cross-referenced with Eramet's own "History" page, https://indonesia.eramet.com/en/eramet-indonesia/history/ (Eramet is a primary corporate source, French-listed, generally reliable for its own JV stake disclosures) and CTRM Center trade-press report on production start, https://www.ctrmcenter.com/news/tsingshan-and-eramets-indonesia-weda-bay-nickel-project-starts-production/ — good for establishing IWIP as the second mega-hub alongside Morowali, and its Chinese-majority ownership structure.
- **Nickel export-value transformation: processed nickel exports rose from about $1.5 billion in 2014 to nearly $20 billion in 2022, and by one 2025 estimate to about $38.5 billion — a roughly 26-fold increase since 2014.** Export composition shifted from ~18% processed (pre-2014) to 99.9% processed (post-2020 ban). World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11249, "Nickel, Steel and Cars: Export Ban and Domestic Value-Added in Indonesia," https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099457511102525232/pdf/IDU-dd5457e6-f5fe-419c-b0ef-ae05bd244854.pdf — **primary source, World Bank research paper, strong citation**; this is probably the single best document to build the "did the ban work economically" section around, since it's an independent multilateral-institution empirical study rather than an Indonesian government press release.
- **Official Indonesian government figures: value-added from nickel production rose from $1.4 billion to $34.8 billion between 2020 and 2023; mining now accounts for just under 10% of Indonesia's GDP.** Surfaced via search summarizing Australian Institute of International Affairs / CSIS-linked commentary — **these appear to be Indonesian government (likely Ministry of Investment or Coordinating Ministry for Maritime and Investment Affairs) self-reported figures; treat as a claim to attribute explicitly to the Indonesian government rather than as independently verified**, and pair with the more independent World Bank paper above.
- **WTO ruling: a WTO dispute panel ruled Indonesia's nickel-ore export ban and domestic-processing requirement violate WTO rules; the panel report was circulated November 30, 2022, in case DS592 (Indonesia — Measures Relating to Raw Materials), brought by the European Union (consultations requested November 2019, panel established January 2021).** WTO official case page, https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/cases_e/ds592_e.htm — primary source (WTO itself), authoritative. European Commission press release welcoming the ruling, https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_22_7314 — primary EU source. President Joko Widodo publicly vowed to appeal and stated the ruling would not change his government's resource-processing policy; Indonesia formally appealed, and as of reporting found in this search (2024), the appeal remained unresolved (WTO's Appellate Body has been non-functional/paralyzed since 2019 due to the US blocking judge appointments, which is itself a relevant footnote for why the appeal has stalled — verify this AB-paralysis point independently before using). The Diplomat, "Indonesia to Appeal WTO Ruling on Nickel Export Ban," Dec 2022, https://thediplomat.com/2022/12/indonesia-to-appeal-wto-ruling-on-nickel-export-ban/ — good for capturing Jokowi's defiant public stance, a strong narrative/quote-adjacent moment (find the direct Jokowi quote from primary Indonesian wire coverage, e.g. Antara or Reuters, before drafting).
- **The March 2022 LME nickel short squeeze: Tsingshan founder Xiang Guangda ("Big Shot") had built a short position of 150,000+ tonnes of nickel — about five times the LME's own visible inventory — much of it via undisclosed OTC trades with banks including JPMorgan Chase, BNP Paribas, Standard Chartered, and United Overseas Bank. ### Narrative material

- **Xiang Guangda and the March 2022 nickel short squeeze** is an obvious, cinematic centerpiece for Chapter 7 — a single Chinese industrialist's massive, partly-hidden short bet on his own company's Indonesian nickel supply nearly broke the 147-year-old London Metal Exchange in three days, and a Wall Street bank consortium bailed him out rather than force a default that might have cascaded through the financial system. Multiple detailed accounts (CNN, MINING.COM, Bloomberg, the OFR working paper) allow reconstruction of the night-by-night timeline.
- **The Morowali/Weda Bay "instant industrial cities"**: both mega-parks went from open jungle/coastline to multi-billion-dollar integrated smelting complexes within roughly five to seven years (Weda Bay: construction began 2018, metallurgical production by April 2020; Morowali similarly rapid) — a strong "speed of the energy transition made physical" visual/narrative device. Good pairing with the December 2023 double-fire tragedy to show the human cost of that speed.
- **Jokowi's defiance of the WTO ruling** — a developing-country head of state publicly telling a global trade body he'll keep breaking its rules because the policy is working domestically — is a clean geopolitical set piece for the chapter's framing of "resource nationalism vs. free trade orthodoxy." Track down his exact quoted words from a primary Indonesian or wire source (Reuters/Antara) for direct quotation.
- **Before/after export value chart**: nickel product exports rising from roughly $1.5B (2014) to $20B (2022) to a reported $38.5B (2025) is an extremely clean, quotable trajectory for illustrating the policy's economic effect, backed by the World Bank's independent research paper.
- **The Obi Island fishing community and chromium-6 drinking water** (Mongabay's "Red seas and no fish") offers ground-level, human-scale reporting — named villages (Kawasi), specific pollutants, specific before/after fishing-yield claims from local sources — good texture for an on-the-ground scene contrasting with the CMOC/Tsingshan boardroom-level material.

### Counterevidence / complications

- **The policy has delivered large, independently-verified economic gains, not just extractive-industry talking points.** The World Bank's own working paper (Nickel, Steel and Cars, 2025) — an independent multilateral assessment, not an Indonesian government press release — documents substantial domestic value-added growth from the export ban and processing mandate. This is a legitimate complication to any simple "resource curse" or "China exploits Indonesia" framing: Indonesia's government made a calculated, WTO-rule-breaking bet, and by most economic metrics it appears to have worked domestically, even as environmental and Chinese-ownership costs mounted. Worth treating this as a genuine two-sided policy debate rather than a clear villain story.
- **Indonesia reached upper-middle-income status in 2023**, a milestone its government and independent commentary link partly to downstreaming-driven export/trade-balance improvements — verify the World Bank income-classification date and language directly (World Bank publishes country classifications annually) before citing as caused-by-nickel, since correlation-vs-causation should be handled carefully in the manuscript.
- **The WTO's Appellate Body has been non-functional since 2019** (a separate, US-driven crisis in WTO dispute-settlement machinery, unrelated to Indonesia) which may partly explain why Indonesia's appeal has never been finally resolved — this is worth an independent check, since it complicates a simple "Indonesia is a rule-breaker who got away with it" reading; the deeper truth may be "the enforcement mechanism itself is broken," a bigger and more interesting geopolitical point for the book. Verify with a WTO or academic trade-law source directly before using.
- **Company and government responses to safety/environmental criticism exist and should be represented.** The Business & Human Rights Resource Centre aggregation pages (used above) systematically include company non-responses and occasional responses — worth checking each specific incident page for whether Tsingshan, ITSS, Harita, or the Indonesian government issued rebuttals, safety-improvement pledges, or investigation results, to avoid one-sided sourcing.
- **Nickel price collapse arguably benefits the broader energy transition** (cheaper battery inputs) even as it devastates Western/Australian miners and, some argue, pressures Indonesian smelters' own environmental compliance spending — a genuine both-sides economic tension worth surfacing rather than flattening into pure "Indonesia won" or "Indonesia's workers/environment lost."
