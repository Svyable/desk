# Chapter 20 research note — what “after the rush” could actually mean

Research checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 20, “After the Rush.”

## Why the ending needs a harder boundary

The book should not end by declaring that mineral chokepoints disappear, nor by implying that the current pattern of extraction and refining persists unchanged forever.

A more defensible endpoint is a mineral economy in which primary mining still matters, but no single mechanism has to carry the whole system. New extraction, recycling, substitution, product redesign, stockpiles, geographic diversification, processing redundancy, and demand growth all interact. Maturity means the system has more ways to absorb a shock.

That is different from self-sufficiency.

The final chapter should therefore avoid a victory-lap ending about “solving” critical minerals. The more interesting claim is that chokepoints can become less absolute as industries acquire alternative suppliers, larger secondary-material streams, different chemistries, and enough accumulated infrastructure that every disruption does not begin from year zero.

## Current evidence: recycling becomes material, but not dominant

The International Energy Agency’s 2026 *Global Critical Minerals Outlook* says average recycling rates across key energy minerals could rise from about 10% today to close to 20% by 2040 under current policy settings. Secondary supply could roughly double its contribution by then.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

The IEA’s dedicated recycling analysis makes the longer-run battery case more concrete. With improving collection rates, recycled batteries could supply roughly 20–30% of lithium, nickel, and cobalt demand by 2050. The plausible range moves significantly with collection performance: higher collection can push the contribution toward 30–40%, while weaker collection can leave it nearer 15–25%.

Source: IEA, *Recycling of Critical Minerals*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary

This supports a useful closing distinction.

A mature mineral economy is not a closed loop in which yesterday’s batteries supply all of tomorrow’s batteries. Demand can still grow faster than the available end-of-life stock. Some materials are dissipated, difficult to collect, uneconomic to recover, or locked in long-lived infrastructure. Primary mining therefore remains necessary even in a much more circular system.

But secondary material can become large enough to change marginal dependence on new mines, especially once the first large generations of EVs, storage systems, motors, and other equipment reach end of life.

That is a genuine structural change.

## The stock-and-flow constraint survives into the finale

Chapter 12’s key constraint should carry forward: recycling cannot recover material that has not yet completed its useful life.

The IEA expects end-of-life battery feedstock to grow sharply after 2030 as early EV fleets retire. Before that point, manufacturing scrap remains unusually important. The implication for Chapter 20 is that circularity is partly a demographic property of an industrial system.

Young mineral-consuming industries are hungry because most of their material is still in service.

Older ones begin to own an above-ground orebody.

That is a useful image for the closing chapter, but it should remain metaphor rather than overclaim. The “orebody” is dispersed among millions of products and only has economic value if collection, transport, sorting, pretreatment, recovery, and re-entry into manufacturing actually work.

## Recycling can create its own chokepoint

The strongest counterargument to a circularity-heavy ending is that the recycling industry is itself concentrated.

The IEA reports that China currently accounts for more than three-quarters of global battery pre-treatment capacity and about 90% of material-recovery capacity. Korea is the largest recovery player outside China.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

So a country can reduce dependence on newly mined material while remaining dependent on somebody else to process its scrap.

Circularity does not automatically produce geographic resilience.

The same industrial logic that created refining chokepoints can create recycling chokepoints: scale, chemistry expertise, equipment, permitting, feedstock aggregation, capital intensity, qualified customers, and the ability to survive volatile commodity prices.

Chapter 20 should make that explicit. Recycling is one layer of resilience, not a moral category that sits outside the political economy described in the rest of the book.

## Policy is beginning to hard-code secondary supply

The European Union’s Batteries Regulation provides a concrete example of circularity moving from aspiration to procurement constraint.

From August 18, 2031, covered industrial, EV, and SLI batteries must contain minimum recovered-material shares of 16% cobalt, 6% lithium, 6% nickel, and 85% lead. From August 18, 2036, the minimums rise to 26% cobalt, 12% lithium, 15% nickel, with lead remaining at 85%.

Source: Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, Article 8.
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32023R1542

The significance is larger than the percentages.

A recycled-content mandate creates a customer for secondary material even when newly mined material becomes cheap. That matters because one of recycling’s recurring economic problems is precisely that commodity prices can fall below the level needed to support expensive recovery systems.

Policy can therefore preserve industrial capability through the down-cycle.

The counterevidence is equally important: a mandate does not create feedstock. If insufficient end-of-life material exists, policymakers may have to revise targets, allow flexibility, or accept higher costs. The EU regulation itself contains review mechanisms tied to material availability and technological change.

This is a good example of what a mature system looks like in practice: rules that create demand for resilience, but remain constrained by physical stocks and industrial reality.

## Diversification is not autarky

The EU Critical Raw Materials Act is useful because its numerical benchmarks implicitly reject total self-sufficiency.

By 2030, the EU aims for domestic capacity equal to at least 10% of annual consumption in extraction, 40% in processing, and 25% in recycling, while seeking to limit dependence on any single third country to no more than 65% for a strategic raw material at a relevant processing stage.

Source: European Commission, Critical Raw Materials Act overview.
https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/raw-materials/areas-specific-interest/critical-raw-materials/critical-raw-materials-act_en

Those benchmarks describe resilience as a portfolio.

Some material is mined domestically. More is processed domestically. A growing share is recycled. Imports remain essential. The objective is not to eliminate trade but to make one supplier’s failure less catastrophic.

That is probably the right conceptual destination for the book.

A mature mineral economy still trades.

It simply has fewer single points of failure.

## Primary mining still matters, and producer countries should capture more of the chain

The IEA’s 2026 Latin America analysis offers a useful corrective to an ending centered only on consumer-country circularity.

In 2025, Latin American countries refined only about 20% of the key energy minerals they extracted on average, excluding lithium. The IEA estimates that expanding local refining could raise the region’s economic value from the critical-mineral pipeline while also improving global diversification.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 — Latin America*.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/latin-america

This matters because “after the rush” should not mean wealthy consuming countries recycle more while producer countries remain exporters of low-value raw material.

The book’s resource-curse argument points toward a better endpoint: producer regions retain more processing, technical knowledge, infrastructure, fiscal value, and bargaining power from extraction.

That is not automatically efficient in every project. Local processing can be uneconomic where power, logistics, skills, water, environmental controls, or scale are insufficient. The IEA itself identifies financing costs, infrastructure, technology, regulatory uncertainty, environmental management, and local engagement as constraints.

The stronger conclusion is conditional: where downstream capacity can operate competitively, moving more of it toward producing regions can both diversify global supply and leave more durable value behind.

## Substitution belongs in the mature system too

The ending should preserve Chapter 16’s lesson that mineral demand is partly an engineering choice.

The IEA’s 2024 outlook estimated that a combination of smaller EV batteries, alternative chemistries, and recycling could reduce lithium demand in 2030 by around 25% in its net-zero scenario relative to a less resource-efficient pathway. It also estimated that recycling could reduce 2040 primary supply requirements for copper and cobalt by about 30%, and for lithium and nickel by around 15%.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2024*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2024/executive-summary

The chapter should not reuse those figures as a forecast for what will happen. They are scenario evidence that the demand side has enough leverage to matter.

A mature mineral system therefore contains engineers as well as miners and recyclers. When one input becomes expensive, strategically exposed, environmentally burdensome, or simply inconvenient, product design can move the chokepoint.

Sometimes that creates a new one.

That is still progress if the new dependency is easier to diversify, recycle, substitute, or tolerate.

## What should “mature” mean in the manuscript?

The ending can define maturity through properties rather than a utopian destination.

A mature mineral economy has more than one credible supplier for strategically important processing steps. It has enough project development underway that a shock does not force every response to begin with exploration. It treats recycling as industrial infrastructure rather than waste management alone. It designs products with material intensity, recoverability, and substitution in mind. It maintains some inventory or spare capacity where the cost of interruption justifies it. It expects producer countries and communities to capture durable value rather than merely hosting extraction. It has environmental rules strong enough that resilience is not purchased by hiding costs somewhere politically weak.

Most importantly, it can tolerate inefficiency at the margin when the alternative is fragility.

That is the conceptual reversal running through the second half of the book.

The system that produced today’s chokepoints optimized hard for cost, scale, and concentration. A more resilient system will sometimes pay for duplicate capacity, higher inventories, smaller suppliers, less concentrated processing, recoverable design, or projects that look redundant until the dominant route fails.

The price of resilience is that some assets will look underused in normal years.

The value appears during abnormal ones.

## Strongest counterargument

The obvious danger is that “resilience” becomes a blank check.

Governments can use strategic language to subsidize uneconomic mines, politically connected processors, unnecessary stockpiles, or recycling plants with no feedstock. Firms can describe ordinary commercial risk as national-security risk. Local processing mandates can destroy value if they force activity into places where it cannot operate competitively. Duplicate supply chains can raise costs without meaningfully reducing vulnerability if they still depend on the same equipment, reagents, technology, or financing source.

A mature mineral economy therefore cannot be defined by how much money it spends on redundancy.

It should be judged by whether the redundancy is real.

Can a second supplier actually deliver qualified material at useful scale? Can a recycler obtain feedstock and sell recovered product through a downturn? Can a stockpile be released into the right processing chain? Does a substitute work in serial production? Does a producer country retain productive assets after extraction slows? Can the system lose one major supplier without losing the entire service the mineral supports?

Those are harder tests than counting announcements.

They are also the right ones.

## Suggested narrative function for Chapter 20

Chapter 20 should not introduce another parade of programs, targets, or technologies. Most of the evidence is already in the book.

The research above can support a quieter synthesis.

Open with something ordinary that now contains the whole argument: a battery, motor, transformer, transmission line, or piece of industrial equipment reaching the end of its first useful life. Instead of seeing waste, the mature system sees feedstock—but only because collection, processing, markets, and regulation were built beforehand.

Then widen.

The first half of the book made the chokepoints visible. The second half showed all the forces trying to route around them: capital, new mines, allied policy, substitution, recycling, environmental limits, producer-country bargaining, and changed product design.

None wins permanently.

That should be the point.

Chokepoints are not geological commandments. They are temporary concentrations of capability, capital, infrastructure, policy, and demand. Some endure for decades. Some disappear when a chemistry changes. Some move downstream. Some move into recycling. Some become less dangerous because the system finally acquires a second route.

The book can end without predicting which route wins.

It only needs to leave the reader better able to see where leverage sits next.
