# Chapter 12 research note — recycling can move the chokepoint

Checked September 4, 2026 for future revision of Chapter 12, “The Recycling Illusion and Reality.”

## Claim under test

Chapter 12 correctly argues that recycling can reduce long-run dependence on new extraction without eliminating the near-term need for mines. The 2026 evidence suggests a second boundary worth making explicit: recycling can reduce dependence on primary geology while leaving a country dependent on concentrated processing, collection, and battery-manufacturing ecosystems.

The useful distinction is therefore not `mined versus recycled`, but `where the material is recovered, refined, qualified, and returned to manufacturing`.

## 2026 IEA evidence: the stock-and-flow lag is longer and more concrete than the chapter currently states

The International Energy Agency’s 2026 *Global EV Outlook* says nearly all EV and stationary-storage batteries deployed in recent years remain in service and that most will continue operating until the mid-2030s or later. It describes a structural lag of roughly fifteen years between rapid growth in EV battery demand and comparable volumes of batteries reaching end of life.

Source: IEA, `Global EV Outlook 2026 — Electric vehicle batteries`
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries

This strengthens the chapter’s opening mechanism. The recycler’s shortage of dead batteries is not merely an early-industry anecdote. It is a predictable consequence of durable products and fleet age.

## Recycling can reproduce industrial concentration

The same 2026 IEA analysis says China hosts more than 85% of global battery-recycling capacity. Its *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* separates the chain more finely: China accounts for more than three-quarters of global battery pre-treatment capacity and about 90% of material-recovery capacity, with Korea the leading material-recovery player outside China.

Sources:
- IEA, `Global EV Outlook 2026 — Electric vehicle batteries`
- IEA, `Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 — Executive summary`
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

This is important for the book’s chokepoint thesis. Recycling does not automatically dissolve the processing problem described in Chapters 7 and 8. A country can accumulate millions of used batteries and still export black mass or intermediate material to a concentrated overseas processor if it lacks local hydrometallurgy, refining, precursor/cathode production, qualification, or customers.

A circular supply chain can therefore be circular and geopolitically concentrated at the same time.

## Feedstock access is partly an industrial-organization advantage

IEA notes that some Chinese recyclers, including CATL-affiliated Brunp, benefit from direct relationships with major battery manufacturers and therefore have preferential access to manufacturing scrap and more predictable feedstock. Other recyclers face underutilization because global recycling capacity currently exceeds the material available to process.

Source: IEA, `Global EV Outlook 2026 — Electric vehicle batteries`.

This sharpens the chapter’s claim that `collection` is strategic infrastructure. The issue is not only whether households and scrapyards return batteries. Manufacturing integration can itself be a feedstock advantage. A recycler tied into cell production receives clean scrap years before the first large end-of-life vehicle wave arrives.

The strongest firms may therefore win the waiting period through contracts and integration rather than superior recovery chemistry alone.

## Counterargument: recycling still has a geographic diversification advantage

The strongest argument against treating current Chinese recycling concentration as destiny is that end-of-life batteries are not geologically fixed in China. They accumulate wherever vehicles, storage systems, electronics, factories, and collection networks exist.

The IEA’s April 2026 *Battery Circularity* report estimates around 1.2 million EV batteries could reach end of life in 2030 and around 14 million in 2040. It also reports very rapid patenting growth across collection, sorting, mechanical processing, and metal-recovery technologies.

Source: IEA, `Battery Circularity`, April 29, 2026
https://www.iea.org/reports/battery-circularity

That means the raw material for a future recycling industry can emerge in countries with no lithium or cobalt mine at all. Unlike an orebody, the above-ground stock moves with trade and consumption.

Policy can reinforce this advantage. The European Union’s Batteries Regulation requires covered EV and large industrial batteries from August 18, 2031 to contain minimum recycled shares of 16% cobalt, 6% lithium, and 6% nickel, alongside 85% recycled lead. These mandates create a regulatory source of demand for recovered material even when virgin material is temporarily cheap.

Source: European Union, Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, Article 8, consolidated text
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02023R1542-20250731

The United States is also still funding process innovation rather than treating recycling as a solved technology. On July 28, 2026, the U.S. Department of Energy advanced eight teams in its Lithium-Ion Battery Recycling Prize for work on scalable recycling infrastructure.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, `DOE’s Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation Announces Winners of Lithium-Ion Recycling Prize Breakthrough Contest`, July 28, 2026
https://www.energy.gov/cmei/articles/does-office-critical-minerals-and-energy-innovation-announces-winners-lithium-ion

These facts do not prove diversified recycling capacity will arrive on time. They do show why current concentration should be treated as an industrial lead, not a geological inevitability.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter’s optimistic long-run frame

A critic could accept that recycled material eventually becomes abundant and still argue that circularity may fail to create strategic independence.

The reason is the same one the book identifies elsewhere: material availability and conversion capability are different assets. Used batteries can exist domestically while collection remains poor, plants run below capacity, black mass is exported, midstream cathode capacity remains abroad, or local recovered material lacks a qualified buyer.

IEA’s 2024 recycling work made this point directly: by 2030 nearly 90% of precursor cathode active material and cathode active material capacity was expected to remain concentrated in China, limiting the security benefit of recycling for regions whose recyclers still need downstream off-takers.

Source: IEA, `Recycling of Critical Minerals — Executive summary`
https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary

The chapter should therefore avoid a neat progression from `imports today` to `domestic mineral endowment tomorrow`. The more defensible sequence is conditional:

Imported products create a future above-ground stock. Collection can turn that stock into feedstock. Processing can turn feedstock into recovered material. Midstream manufacturing and qualification are still required before recovered material becomes strategic supply.

Each arrow can fail.

## Editorial implication

The chapter already has the line that “industrial history can become mineral endowment.” Keep it, but add the missing boundary: industrial history becomes strategic mineral capacity only when the country also retains or builds the competence to close the loop.

That would connect Chapter 12 more tightly to the book’s broader distinction between geology and processing. Recycling is not the end of the chokepoint story. It is one way the chokepoint can migrate from the mine to collection, contracts, hydrometallurgy, cathode production, and qualification.

A useful future sentence is:

> Recycling can make yesterday’s imports into tomorrow’s feedstock. It does not guarantee that tomorrow’s refinery is yours.

That formulation preserves the chapter’s optimism about material circularity while resisting a new version of the same mistake the book criticizes in mining: confusing possession of raw material with control of the chain.
