# Chapter 11 research note — Kitsault and the mine built for a price that vanished

Research checked September 3, 2026 for Chapter 11, “Boom, Bust, Ghost Town.”

## Why Kitsault fits the chapter

Kitsault, British Columbia is a useful case because it compresses the mining capital cycle into one place. A remote molybdenum deposit justified a mine, a power line, road access, and a purpose-built town. The town arrived close to the top of the commodity cycle. The mine then shut after roughly eighteen months of renewed operation when molybdenum prices collapsed. Decades later, the same brownfield asset was permitted for redevelopment but still failed to restart when prices and financing did not cooperate.

The chapter should use Kitsault not as proof that every mining boom creates a ghost town, but as a case where fixed capital outlived the price assumption that made it rational.

## Primary/authoritative factual spine

British Columbia Geological Survey material records two operating periods at Kitsault: 1967–1972 and 1981–1982. The province’s 2005 molybdenum overview says the second operation ran at about 10,900 tonnes per day, involved roughly 500 employees, and followed capital spending of about US$200 million in late-1970s dollars. It also records an extreme molybdenum-price collapse in 1982.

Source: British Columbia Ministry of Energy and Mines, *Molybdenum, the British Columbia Perspective*, Information Circular 2005-3.
https://cmscontent.nrs.gov.bc.ca/geoscience/PublicationCatalogue/InformationCircular/BCGS_IC2005-03.pdf

A British Columbia Property File account gives the economic mechanism more precisely. It says molybdenum consumption was falling while production continued rising, creating surplus inventories and sharply lower prices. The same account notes that the average U.S. spot price in 1979, when construction began, was US$23.86 per pound; by the November 1982 shutdown the market price had fallen below Kitsault’s reported operating cost. The useful point is not the exact peak-to-trough ratio but the timing: the project committed capital under one price regime and opened into another.

Source: British Columbia Property File, historical Kitsault account, PF011728.
https://propertyfile.gov.bc.ca/reports/PF011728.pdf

Peer-reviewed work on Kitsault’s marine-tailings history independently confirms that the mine reopened in 1981–1982 after the earlier 1967–1972 operating period.

Source: Pedersen, Ellis, Poling, and Pelletier, “Effects of changing environmental rules: Kitsault molybdenum mine, Canada,” *Marine Georesources & Geotechnology* 13 (1995).
https://doi.org/10.1080/10641199509388281

## The town is part of the capital cycle, not scenery

The mine’s remoteness meant that labor could not simply commute from an established city. Historical descriptions of Kitsault record a purpose-built community intended to keep workers and families near the operation, with housing and civic amenities rather than a temporary camp alone. That makes the town economically relevant to the chapter: labor retention itself became fixed capital tied to the expected mine life.

For the prose draft, archival reporting should be used before adding resident-level scenes or quotations. Secondary histories describe homes, a school, recreation facilities, and families who expected a long-lived community, but those details should be anchored to contemporaneous newspapers, company material, or oral-history sources before being narrated at close range.

## A second boom-bust cycle strengthens the case

Kitsault is more interesting than an abandoned-town anecdote because the deposit did not stop existing when the first economics failed.

British Columbia granted a provincial Environmental Assessment Certificate in March 2013 for a proposed redevelopment. In June 2014 the province issued a Mines Act permit for an approximately C$812 million project, then expected to support about 700 construction jobs and 300 operating jobs. Federal approval followed in 2014 for a project expected to operate for roughly sixteen years.

- British Columbia, “Kitsault Mine project granted conditional environmental approval,” March 19, 2013.
https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2013ENV0030-000542
- British Columbia, “Kitsault mine receives permit,” June 2014.
https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2014MEM0016-000829
- Government of Canada, “Kitsault Mine Project: Release of Environmental Assessment Decision Statement,” June 2014.
https://www.canada.ca/en/news/archive/2014/06/kitsault-mine-project-release-environmental-assessment-decision-statement.html

Yet later British Columbia environmental-assessment documentation records that, although the project had obtained its permits by the end of 2014 and a camp had been established, lower metal prices and inability to secure financing put construction on hold.

Source: British Columbia Environmental Assessment Office project documentation, discussion of the Kitsault project in the Red Mountain cumulative-effects assessment.
https://projects.eao.gov.bc.ca/api/document/59fa119d6839010019a029df/fetch/Red_Mountain_Volume3_Chapter6_Effects_Assessment_Methodology.pdf

This is unusually useful counterevidence to a simplistic permitting story. The redevelopment reached the point where governments had approved it, but approval did not manufacture a financeable commodity price.

## Strongest counterargument

Kitsault should not be written as “boom, then permanent death.” The asset retained option value.

The brownfield site still had a known orebody, road and power infrastructure, decades of geological information, and a previously developed mine footprint. By 2011 British Columbia’s geological survey was again describing a large defined resource and a proposed sixteen-year mine life. The later permitting process shows that a bust can leave behind information and infrastructure that make a future restart more plausible than a new greenfield project.

Source: British Columbia Geological Survey, *Exploration and Mining in British Columbia 2011*.
https://cmscontent.nrs.gov.bc.ca/geoscience/PublicationCatalogue/GeologyExplorationMining/BCGS_EXP2011-01.pdf

The chapter therefore should distinguish three outcomes:

- capital that is physically stranded because the project never becomes competitive again;
- capital that becomes a long-dated option and can be reused if prices, technology, or infrastructure change;
- communities whose costs cannot be reduced to the resale value of the mine assets.

That last distinction is the human reason to use Kitsault. A company can put a project into care and maintenance. A family cannot put five years of a life into care and maintenance in quite the same way.

## What would make the chapter’s Kitsault argument false or misleading?

It would be misleading if the chapter implied that the 1982 closure resulted from one variable alone. Molybdenum prices were central, but environmental rules, market structure, by-product supply from copper mines, recession, operating costs, financing expectations, and the economics of a remote site all belong in the causal background.

It would also be misleading to generalize Kitsault into a law that mining towns inevitably become ghost towns. Many mines operate for decades, existing towns diversify, workers commute rather than live in company towns, and brownfield projects can reopen after long closures.

The narrower claim is stronger: commodity projects commit large, location-specific capital against uncertain future prices, and the social infrastructure built around them can be just as exposed to the cycle as the mine itself.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 11

A strong opening would begin with the dissonance of a nearly new town becoming unnecessary, then move backward into the decision to build it. The reader should learn what the company and workers reasonably believed before learning how quickly the market invalidated the assumption.

Avoid a generic “greed caused a ghost town” moral. The more interesting story is timing. The investment was not irrational simply because hindsight knows the price collapse. Mines are built precisely because somebody believes today’s scarcity will last long enough to repay tomorrow’s concrete.

The chapter can then widen from Kitsault to the capital-cycle mechanism established in Chapter 10: price is the fastest variable, supply is slower, and communities are often slower still. The handoff to Chapter 12 should come from the obvious question left by the abandoned capital: if new extraction repeatedly overshoots and undershoots demand, how much of the next cycle can be met from material already above ground?
