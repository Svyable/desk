# Chapters 14–15 research — diversification is a capability stack

## Claim under test

Chapters 14 and 15 meet at the same question from opposite directions. Chapter 14 asks whether friend-shoring can build credible alternatives to concentrated mineral supply. Chapter 15 asks what, exactly, China accumulated that makes those alternatives difficult to reproduce.

The strongest formulation is not simply that China has more plants or that allied governments need to spend more money. Diversification requires a stack of capabilities — feedstock, separation and refining, metals and alloys, qualified downstream manufacturing, specialised equipment, operating know-how, customers, and enough financial durability to survive low-price periods. A second mine can therefore coexist with a first-route dependency.

## Primary and authoritative evidence

The IEA's *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* gives the clearest quantitative test. For magnet rare earths, announced mining capacity outside the dominant supplier approaches 50 kt by 2035, while planned refining and separation capacity remains below 40 kt. Planned metals, alloys, and magnet production is only about 18 kt on a rare-earth-content basis. The project pipeline therefore becomes thinner as material moves downstream.

The IEA's dedicated 2026 rare-earth analysis puts current concentration in sharper perspective. In 2024 China accounted for about 60% of mined magnet rare earths, 91% of refined output, and 94% of sintered permanent-magnet production. Those are different markets and should not be collapsed into one generic "China share."

## The hidden bottleneck: equipment and learning

The IEA also identifies constraints that a project-counting approach misses. Processing and refining depend on specialised equipment and experienced operators, and both remain concentrated. Its 2026 outlook notes that only a handful of suppliers outside China provide some key battery-grade graphite processing equipment. In rare-earth magnet manufacturing, grain-boundary-diffusion equipment is especially constrained: the IEA reports only one equipment supplier outside China, with equipment costs reportedly more than ten times higher and with longer lead times.

This is useful for Chapter 15 because it turns the "learning curve" from metaphor into an observable industrial constraint. A country can finance a factory and still depend on the incumbent ecosystem for equipment, process knowledge, maintenance, or qualification. Capability is accumulated by operating plants, not merely announcing them.

It is equally useful for Chapter 14. Friend-shoring that diversifies ownership or geography but leaves specialist equipment and technical knowledge concentrated has moved the visible chokepoint without necessarily creating an independent route.

## Cost is a real counterargument

Diversification is not automatically efficient. The IEA estimates that capital costs for refining projects outside the dominant supplier can be 20% to more than 150% higher, while operating costs average roughly 50% higher. Feedstock, energy, equipment, construction, skills, infrastructure, and permitting all contribute.

That is strong counterevidence to any chapter language implying that governments can solve concentration merely by deciding to do so. A second route carries an insurance premium. Some proposed projects will be bad projects even when the strategic objective is sound.

But the final-product economics complicate the objection. The IEA estimates rare earths at roughly 40% of permanent-magnet cost but less than 1% of a vehicle's value. Its illustrative calculation says tripling rare-earth prices would add only about 0.1% to the cost of a car. In some applications, therefore, paying materially more for a diversified input can be economically tolerable downstream even when it looks expensive at the refinery gate.

## Evidence that policy can work

The countercase to fatalism is already visible. The IEA reports rare earths as an exception to the broader 2023–2025 rise in refining concentration: new U.S. projects and higher Malaysian production produced a modest decline in the leading supplier's share. Diversification is difficult, not impossible.

The U.S. policy mix is also moving beyond grants for mines. In August 2025, the Department of Defense's Office of Strategic Capital executed a $150 million loan to MP Materials for heavy-rare-earth separation capability at Mountain Pass as part of the wider July 2025 agreement. In August 2026, the Department of Energy selected seven projects for $500 million across critical-mineral processing, battery manufacturing, and recycling, and separately selected nine projects totaling $162 million for recovery from industrial feedstocks.

Selections, loans, and announced capacity are not operating output. They are evidence that policy is targeting more of the chain, not proof that the chain has been rebuilt.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest challenge to the capability-stack framing is that it can make incumbent advantage sound self-perpetuating. It is not. Rare-earth refining concentration has already declined modestly, and enough capital, customer commitment, technical learning, and policy durability can create new industrial clusters. Japan's support for Lynas and the U.S. build-out around Mountain Pass show that learning can be rebuilt outside China.

The correct claim is therefore about time and completeness, not impossibility. New entrants have to assemble enough of the stack for material to reach a qualified customer without falling back through the incumbent route.

## Falsification test

This framing should be weakened if diversified regions begin commissioning refining, metallisation, alloy, and magnet capacity at roughly the same pace as mining; if specialist equipment and operating expertise become broadly available; if new plants consistently reach qualification and competitive yields quickly; or if diversified suppliers survive low-price cycles without continuing extraordinary support.

## Manuscript implication

For Chapter 14, the useful test of friend-shoring is: **Can the alternative route deliver qualified material during the disruption it is meant to insure against?**

For Chapter 15, the useful definition of China's accumulated advantage is: **not a stockpile of rocks, but an operating stack of plants, equipment, people, customers, and repeated production knowledge.**

Together these formulations keep the chapters from making opposite versions of the same mistake — treating industrial capacity as something that appears when a government points at a deposit or writes a cheque.
