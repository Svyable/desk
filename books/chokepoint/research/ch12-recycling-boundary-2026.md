# Chapter 12 research note — recycling is a stock-and-flow problem

Research checked September 3, 2026 for Chapter 12, “The Recycling Illusion and Reality.”

## Claim under test

Chapter 12 should resist both easy versions of the recycling story.

The optimistic version says the minerals transition can eventually mine the material already circulating through cars, batteries, electronics, buildings and scrap yards instead of continuing to dig new rock.

The dismissive version says recycling is too small, too expensive or too technically difficult to matter.

The evidence supports neither extreme. Recycling can become a strategically important secondary supply source, but its near- and medium-term contribution is constrained by the age of the installed stock, collection rates, chemistry, geography and the timing mismatch between announced recycling plants and available feedstock.

The useful analytical frame is therefore not “can batteries be recycled?” They can. The question is how much recoverable material is available, where it is, when it becomes waste, and whether recycling economics remain attractive as battery chemistries and primary-mineral prices change.

## Primary evidence: IEA Recycling of Critical Minerals (2024)

The IEA’s dedicated 2024 report is the cleanest quantitative backbone for the chapter:
https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary

The report explicitly says recycling does not eliminate the need for new mining investment. In the Announced Pledges Scenario, however, successful recycling scale-up could reduce the need for new mine development by about 40% for copper and cobalt and about 25% for lithium and nickel by 2050.

That distinction matters. Recycling is not a replacement for primary supply in the transition period; it is a growing secondary source that can reduce how much new primary capacity must be built.

The IEA also gives Chapter 12 a better timing argument than the vague phrase “there is not enough scrap yet.” Manufacturing scrap still supplies about two-thirds of available battery-recycling feedstock in 2030. End-of-life EV and storage batteries become the largest source only from 2035 onward and rise to more than 90% of available feedstock by 2050.

This makes the installed stock itself a clock. The battery fleet has to age before it can become a mine.

The same report shows why recycling capacity can look simultaneously overbuilt and insufficient. China is expected to retain excess recycling capacity relative to domestic feedstock. In Europe and the United States, excess capacity dissipates after 2030, and announced capacity covers only about 30% of available feedstock by 2040. In India, the figure is around 10%.

This is a much stronger boundary than saying simply that “recycling plants are being built too early.” Some regions can have too much plant relative to feedstock in one decade and too little in the next.

The IEA estimates that battery recycling could supply roughly 20–30% of lithium, nickel and cobalt demand by 2050 under improving collection rates. The range moves materially with collection performance: around 30–40% under higher collection assumptions and around 15–25% under lower ones.

The bottleneck is therefore not only process efficiency. It is getting the battery back.

## Chemistry changes recycling economics

The IEA notes that lithium iron phosphate batteries accounted for around 40% of EV batteries deployed in 2023. Their lower material value weakens the economics of recycling relative to nickel-rich chemistries.

That is an important counterweight to a simple “more batteries means better recycling economics” story. Battery chemistry can improve supply security by reducing dependence on nickel and cobalt while simultaneously making the waste stream less valuable to recyclers.

The same technological substitution can therefore help one chokepoint while worsening another business model.

This should appear in the chapter because it links Chapter 12 to the later substitution argument rather than treating recycling as an independent lever.

## Current industrial reality: Redwood proves technical scale is real

Redwood Materials currently reports receiving more than 20 GWh of lithium-ion batteries annually and recovering more than 95% of lithium, nickel, cobalt and copper from incoming material:
https://www.redwoodmaterials.com/materials/

Its 2025 year-end update says its Nevada campus produced 60,000 metric tons of critical minerals that year, while its South Carolina operation added an initial 20,000 metric tons of annual processing capacity:
https://www.redwoodmaterials.com/news/2025-a-defining-year-for-redwood/

These are company-reported numbers and should be labeled as such if used in prose. They are useful not as neutral market-share evidence but as proof that commercial-scale recovery and refining are no longer laboratory concepts.

Redwood’s business model also complicates the word “recycling.” The company now combines recycling, refining, materials production and second-life stationary storage. Its April 2026 partnership with Rivian uses more than 100 second-life EV packs in a 10 MWh storage system rather than immediately shredding them for minerals:
https://www.redwoodmaterials.com/news/rivian-and-redwood-materials-announce-energy-storage-partnership-for-manufacturing/

That distinction matters for material-flow accounting. Reuse can delay recycling while extracting more service from the same battery. A battery that remains useful in stationary storage is not failed recycling; it is feedstock deferred.

## Failure case: Li-Cycle shows that technical need does not guarantee a financeable recycler

Li-Cycle’s SEC filings give Chapter 12 a primary-source cautionary case.

On May 14, 2025, Li-Cycle and its North American subsidiaries entered restructuring proceedings under Canada’s Companies’ Creditors Arrangement Act. Its U.S. subsidiaries, including the entity that owns the Rochester Hub project, entered Chapter 15 proceedings in the Southern District of New York.

Source:
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1828811/000119312525122542/d892872d8k.htm

The same filing records a stalking-horse agreement under which Glencore agreed to purchase specified Li-Cycle assets and subsidiaries, including the Rochester Hub project, for aggregate consideration of at least $40 million, subject to court approval.

An April 30, 2025 board decision had already suspended operations at Li-Cycle’s Arizona and Alabama Spoke facilities as the company tried to preserve liquidity and find a buyer.

Source:
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1828811/000119312525111697/d944053d8k.htm

The safe conclusion is not “battery recycling does not work.” The company’s failure involved capital structure, project execution, liquidity and market conditions. The useful point is narrower: a strategically desirable recycling technology can still be mistimed, overcapitalized or poorly financed.

A recycling plant has its own capital cycle.

## Policy can manufacture demand for recycled material

The European Union’s Batteries Regulation creates a direct counterargument to the idea that recycling economics depend only on commodity prices.

Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 requires specified industrial, EV and SLI batteries placed on the EU market from August 18, 2031 to contain minimum recycled shares of 16% cobalt, 85% lead, 6% lithium and 6% nickel. From August 18, 2036 the requirements rise to 26% cobalt, 85% lead, 12% lithium and 15% nickel, with light-means-of-transport batteries included at that stage.

Primary text:
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1542/oj

The regulation also sets material-recovery targets for recycling. By the end of 2027, recovery must reach 90% for cobalt, copper, lead and nickel and 50% for lithium; by the end of 2031 the targets rise to 95% for cobalt, copper, lead and nickel and 80% for lithium.

This is significant because regulation can create a floor under demand for secondary material even when virgin material is cheap. Recycling is therefore not merely an engineering or commodity-price story. It is partly a market-design story.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest case against treating recycling as the answer to mineral scarcity is temporal.

The transition needs large volumes of minerals before the first large wave of EVs and stationary batteries reaches end of life. The IEA therefore still expects substantial new mine and refining investment even in scenarios with aggressive recycling.

The strongest case against dismissing recycling is cumulative.

Primary minerals placed into durable products do not disappear after first use. As the installed stock grows, it becomes an increasingly large above-ground resource. Collection systems, recycled-content mandates, improved processing and second-life use can turn part of yesterday’s imports into tomorrow’s domestic supply.

Both statements can be true at once.

Recycling is too late to remove the need for mining during the buildout, yet important enough to change the scale and geography of mining later.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 12

The chapter should avoid framing “urban mining” as either a miracle or a fraud.

A stronger opening would start with the apparent absurdity that a battery recycler can have too much factory and too little waste even while forecasters warn of future mineral shortages. That contradiction is the chapter.

The story can then move through four clocks:

- the lifetime of the product before it becomes feedstock;
- the collection system that determines whether the material comes back;
- the economics of the chemistry inside the battery;
- the capital cycle of the recycling plant itself.

The conclusion should be narrower and more durable than “recycling will solve it.”

A mine extracts from geology once. A circular system tries to keep the same atoms economically useful through several lives. The strategic advantage grows only after enough material has entered the loop to circulate.

That makes recycling a stock-and-flow problem before it becomes a technological one.
