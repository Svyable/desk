# Chapters 17–19 late-book evidence and flow boundaries

Checked September 4, 2026 for a coherent revision pass across Chapters 17–19 of *Chokepoint*.

## Why these chapters belong in one pass

The late-book sequence already has the right architecture:

- Chapter 17 asks what mineral production costs physically and who lives with those costs.
- Chapter 18 asks what producing countries keep after the ore leaves.
- Chapter 19 asks how price and investment decisions determine which supply options exist later.

The main editorial risk is not weak argument. It is repetition. Each chapter sometimes pauses after strong evidence to restate a general lesson in several parallel forms. A later manuscript pass should let more of the evidence carry the transition: **cost becomes distribution; distribution becomes balance-sheet exposure; balance-sheet exposure becomes investment behavior.**

This note adds current evidence and explicit counterarguments so that tightening prose does not flatten the intellectual boundaries.

## Chapter 17 — Water, Waste, and the Hidden Costs

### Current Chile water evidence

Cochilco's 2026 projection for Chilean copper mining expects total sector water use to rise from 18.5 cubic metres per second in 2024 to 20.6 cubic metres per second in 2034. The more important change is the source mix: seawater rises from 40.7% of total use in 2024 to 67.6% in 2034.

Source: Comisión Chilena del Cobre, `Proyección de la Demanda de Agua en la Minería del Cobre 2025-2034`, 2026.
https://www.cochilco.cl/web/https-www-cochilco-cl-web-download-1014-2026-15581-informe-proyeccion-de-la-demanda-de-agua-en-la-mineria-del-cobre-en-chile-periodo-2025-2034-pdf/

This is unusually useful counterevidence to a static `more copper means proportionally more scarce freshwater` story. The industry can change the engineering boundary by changing the source.

The stronger claim is narrower: environmental constraints often **migrate into infrastructure** rather than vanish. Seawater use reduces pressure on continental sources while increasing dependence on coastal intake, desalination or direct-use systems, pipelines, pumping, electricity, rights of way, maintenance, and redundancy.

### Editorial implication

Chapter 17 should preserve the line that improvement can be real without being magical. The best transition is not a long catalogue of trade-offs. It is the concrete Chilean example followed by one compact principle: solving a water constraint can create an energy-and-infrastructure bill somewhere else.

That keeps the chapter from sounding anti-mining or anti-mitigation. It also sets up Chapter 18 more cleanly: once a producing country accepts the physical burden and infrastructure bill, the obvious question is what durable value remains locally.

### Strongest counterargument

It would be wrong to treat every migrated burden as equivalent to the original one. Replacing scarce continental water with seawater can be a substantial environmental improvement even if pumping consumes electricity. The chapter should compare dimensions rather than imply that every solution merely moves an unchanged quantity of harm.

## Chapter 18 — Who Gets Rich, Who Gets Left

### Botswana as a stronger anti-destiny case

Botswana remains valuable precisely because the recent evidence makes the success story less neat.

IMF reporting says the economy contracted by about 3% in 2024 as the diamond sector weakened, with mining output down roughly 24%. Its 2025 Article IV work describes a sharper and more persistent decline in natural-diamond demand than expected and stresses the need for diversification. The IMF also notes that diamond exports had accounted for close to 90% of Botswana's goods exports over the previous decade.

Sources:
- International Monetary Fund, `Botswana: 2025 Article IV Consultation`, December 2025.
https://www.imf.org/en/publications/cr/issues/2025/12/08/botswana-2025-article-iv-consultation-press-release-staff-report-and-statement-by-the-572347
- International Monetary Fund, `Botswana: 2024 Article IV Consultation`, September 2024.
https://www.imf.org/en/publications/cr/issues/2024/09/09/botswana-2024-article-iv-consultation-press-release-and-staff-report-554633

This is stronger than a generic `even Botswana still needs diversification` sentence. It shows the exact boundary of institutional success: good bargaining and fiscal management can convert resource rents into public assets and buffers, but they cannot make a concentrated export base immune to a structural demand shock.

### Editorial implication

Chapter 18 can tighten several explanatory passages by making Botswana do more work. The case should carry three ideas at once:

1. resource wealth is not destiny;
2. institutional design changes what a country retains;
3. even competent management cannot abolish concentration risk.

That is more interesting than placing `curse` and `blessing` in symmetrical opposition.

The handoff to Chapter 19 should become explicit through the balance sheet. A state can manage ownership, taxes, saving, spending, and debt intelligently, but all of those choices still depend on assumptions about future commodity prices and demand. Botswana's diamond shock makes that transition concrete rather than theoretical.

### Strongest counterargument

Do not use the recent contraction to retroactively discredit Botswana's long record. The country accumulated unusually strong institutions and buffers relative to many resource exporters. The point is not `the model failed`; it is that institutional quality changes resilience while leaving some external price and technology risks intact.

## Chapter 19 — The Price of the Future

### Current investment evidence

The IEA's 2026 critical-minerals outlook reports that total critical-mineral investment fell 9% in 2025, ending several years of growth. Capital spending by battery-material companies fell by more than 20%, lithium-focused investment by around 40%, and exploration spending by more than 10%. Copper-focused investment rose about 8%.

Source: International Energy Agency, `Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 — Executive summary`.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

The same report says refining concentration increased further: over the previous two years, Indonesia for nickel and China for other major energy minerals accounted for more than three-quarters of refined-supply growth.

This strengthens the chapter's most useful idea: a low price can be the visible surface of a future shortage if it suppresses the projects and exploration needed later, while a high price can finance the response that makes the spike temporary.

### A sharper counterargument

Low investment is not automatically evidence of dangerous underinvestment. Prices also convey legitimate information about oversupply, technology shifts, project quality, and expected demand. Keeping every marginal mine or refinery alive in the name of resilience can destroy capital and turn `critical` into a subsidy category rather than an engineering judgment.

The chapter's test should therefore remain operational: are technically credible projects, alternative processors, inventories, recycling routes, and qualified suppliers being preserved at enough stages that a disruption has somewhere else to go?

### Editorial implication

The strongest phrase in Chapter 19 is already `the future keeps changing because people are trying to prepare for it.` A later prose pass should trust that sentence more and reduce the number of subsequent restatements.

The chapter can gain momentum by moving faster through this sequence:

**price signal → capital response → project pipeline → industrial topology → option value.**

That sequence also prepares Chapter 20 without repeating Chapter 20's conclusion. Chapter 19 should end on the uncertainty of price and the value of keeping routes alive; Chapter 20 can then own the broader synthesis about a mature system accumulating options.

## Cross-chapter revision rule

When editing Chapters 17–19, preserve the concrete facts and counterevidence but cut repeated explanatory scaffolding where two adjacent paragraphs make the same move.

Prefer one earned sentence after evidence to a ladder of slogans. In particular, watch for repeated patterns such as:

- `That does not mean... It means...`
- `The mistake would be...`
- repeated `A government can... A company can... A community can...` sequences
- strings of short declarative sentences after the point has already landed
- restating the chapter's thesis immediately before and after an example

The late-book voice is strongest when it is specific, dry, and willing to stop one sentence earlier.

## What would make this note wrong?

Revise these boundaries if later data show that Chilean seawater substitution fails to reduce pressure on continental water sources in practice; if Botswana materially diversifies its export and fiscal base enough that diamond concentration ceases to be a central macroeconomic risk; or if the 2025 investment decline reverses quickly enough that the implied option-value problem does not persist.

The point is not to preserve a pessimistic ending. It is to preserve a mechanism that can survive better outcomes.
