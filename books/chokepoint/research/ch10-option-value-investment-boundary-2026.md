# Chapter 10 — project-pipeline option value boundary

Checked September 4, 2026.

## Claim under test

Chapter 10's strongest idea is not that every mine takes sixteen years. It is that a shortage is much easier to answer when exploration, feasibility, processing development, qualification, and financing work have already retired uncertainty before the shortage arrives. The manuscript calls this "time already spent." This note tests that proposition against the latest IEA evidence and records the counterargument that a mature pipeline can still be a bad investment pipeline.

## Current evidence

The IEA's July 2026 *Global Critical Minerals Outlook* reports that critical-mineral investment fell 9% in 2025, ending several years of growth. The aggregate hides a sharper divergence: capital spending by battery-material companies fell more than 20%, lithium-focused companies cut investment by around 40%, while copper-focused companies increased spending by 8%.

Exploration moved in the same direction. The IEA reports exploration spending down by more than 10% overall, with lithium and nickel exploration falling by roughly 40–45% while copper was broadly steady. That matters for Chapter 10 because exploration is not today's supply. It is information and project maturity that can become tomorrow's supply. A low-price period can therefore improve affordability for consumers while simultaneously thinning the set of projects available to accelerate later.

The IEA's older mine-lead-time analysis provides the mechanism behind that lag. For major mines entering production from 2010 through 2019, it found more than sixteen years on average from discovery to first production, including more than twelve years for exploration and feasibility and another four to five for construction. The IEA's actual warning is conditional: waiting for visible deficits before committing projects can prolong tightness because much of the response cannot be created instantly by a higher price.

The 2026 outlook adds an important downstream boundary. Diversification requires more than mines. The IEA says processing and refining depend on specialized equipment and expertise that remain concentrated geographically; outside dominant suppliers, equipment vendors can be scarce and lead times lengthy. Its examples include battery-grade graphite processing equipment, ultra-high-purity gallium refining, and rare-earth magnet manufacturing. A geographically diversified mine pipeline can therefore leave the commercially relevant chokepoint intact if processing, equipment, know-how, or customer qualification remains concentrated.

## Strongest counterargument

"Time already spent" has option value only if the option is credible. Advancing every marginal deposit through studies and permitting would destroy capital rather than preserve resilience. Commodity prices change, technologies substitute materials, orebody assumptions disappoint, costs inflate, communities oppose projects, and processing routes fail to reach commercial yields. A shelf full of feasibility studies is not strategic capacity if none of the projects can survive ordinary prices.

The 2025 investment pullback itself supports this objection. Lithium companies did not cut spending because lithium ceased to matter; the IEA attributes the retreat to oversupply-driven price weakness, changing battery-chemistry preferences, and policy uncertainty. Those are exactly the signals capital is supposed to process. Keeping every boom-era project alive would preserve optionality by socializing a great deal of bad economics.

The defensible claim is therefore narrower: resilience benefits from a portfolio of technically credible, economically legible projects at different stages of maturity, not from maximizing the number of announced projects. Public policy can reasonably value optionality that spot markets underprice, but security does not turn a poor orebody or uncompetitive process into a good asset.

## Editorial implication

Chapter 10 can make "time already spent" more concrete by pairing it with the 2025–2026 investment cycle. Falling prices are not merely the end of a boom; they can also erase exploration budgets and defer projects whose absence will only become visible years later. That is the capital cycle's delayed feedback loop.

The sharper formulation is:

> A cheap mineral market can be doing two things at once: rewarding consumers for yesterday's investment and cancelling some of tomorrow's options.

Use that idea only if it improves the chapter's existing rhythm. Do not turn the section into a recital of investment percentages; one or two figures are enough to establish the mechanism.

## Falsification test

The option-value thesis should weaken if sustained low-price periods do not materially reduce exploration, project advancement, or future supply responsiveness; if mothballed and early-stage projects can routinely be restarted or advanced fast enough to answer shocks; or if substitution and recycling consistently respond faster and more cheaply than maintaining a mature primary-supply pipeline. The chapter should leave those routes open rather than treating mine development as the only form of preparedness.
