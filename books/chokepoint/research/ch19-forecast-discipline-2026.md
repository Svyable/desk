# Chapter 19 research — forecasts are moving inventories of decisions

## Claim under test

Chapter 19 argues that long-range mineral balances are most useful as conditional maps, not as point predictions. The important question is not whether a 2035 deficit number is “right,” but which projects, policies, technologies, and demand responses would move it.

The 2026 IEA outlook provides unusually clean evidence for that framing because several headline balances moved materially in a single annual revision.

## Primary-source check

The International Energy Agency’s *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* says expected project pipelines improved the projected 2035 balances for copper and lithium relative to its 2025 outlook. For copper, the base-case projected supply gap narrowed from around 30% to 25% as more projects advanced.

Cobalt moved in the opposite direction. The IEA says its projected 2035 cobalt gap widened from just over 15% to more than 25%, reflecting expected lower available production after the Democratic Republic of the Congo introduced export quotas.

These are not trivial revisions. They show why the manuscript should resist treating a deficit estimate as a geological constant. A project reaching a more credible development stage can shrink a projected gap; a producer-country policy can widen one.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, “Outlook,” July 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook

## The investment signal

The same report gives a second reason to watch mechanisms rather than prices alone. Critical-mineral investment fell 9% in 2025, the first substantial decline since 2020. Capital spending by battery-material companies fell by more than 20%, and lithium-focused companies cut investment by around 40%. Copper-focused companies moved the other way, increasing investment by 8%.

Exploration spending also fell by more than 10% overall. The IEA reports declines of roughly 45% for lithium and nickel exploration while copper remained broadly steady.

This matters because current production and future optionality run on different clocks. A mine already operating can continue shipping through a weak investment year. The effect of cancelled exploration, deferred studies, and missing final investment decisions appears later, when replacement supply fails to arrive.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, “Executive summary” and “Market overview,” July 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/market-overview

## Announced tonnes are not interchangeable tonnes

The IEA’s 2026 project pipeline also reinforces the manuscript’s distinction between mine diversification and supply-chain diversification. Outside the dominant refining countries, announced rare-earth mining projects could provide nearly 50 kt of capacity by 2035. Planned refining and separation capacity is under 40 kt, while planned downstream metals, alloys, and magnet capacity is only about 18 kt on a rare-earth-content basis.

The mismatch is the point. Counting mine output alone can make diversification look farther advanced than it is. The ore still has to become the chemical, metal, alloy, magnet, cathode, anode, or other qualified form a customer can actually use.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, “Outlook,” July 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook

## Public money: commitment versus delivery

The IEA reports that public-finance commitments for critical-mineral projects in advanced economies reached about USD 65 billion in 2025, more than four times the 2023 level. It simultaneously warns that a considerable gap remains between commitments and actual disbursements.

That is a useful discipline for Chapter 19. Policy announcements should not be counted as supply. The stronger indicators are disbursement, construction, commissioning, specification-grade output, customer qualification, and survival through an adverse commodity-price cycle.

## Strongest counterargument

Scenario humility can become an excuse for refusing to say anything useful. If every forecast is conditional and every number can move, a writer can protect himself from being wrong by never making a testable claim.

Chapter 19 should avoid that escape hatch. The IEA revisions do not show that forecasting is futile. They show which variables deserve monitoring. A conditional forecast can still fail: projects can advance without closing the gap; investment can recover without producing qualified supply; substitution can disappoint; demand can outrun plausible response.

The chapter’s strongest position is therefore not “nobody can predict mineral markets.” It is that the useful prediction is causal and updateable. State the mechanism, name the indicators, and say what evidence would force the view to change.

## What would make this framing false?

This framing would weaken if long-range balances proved insensitive to project development, policy changes, technology, recycling, and investment cycles; if announced capacity consistently translated into usable supply on schedule; or if spot prices alone reliably predicted later physical shortages. The 2025-to-2026 IEA revisions point in the opposite direction.
