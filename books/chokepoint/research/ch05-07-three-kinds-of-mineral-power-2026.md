# Chapters 5–7 — Three kinds of mineral power (2026)

## Purpose

Chapters 5–7 already contain strong individual stories: lithium’s boom-and-bust cycle, the DRC’s cobalt concentration and export policy, and Indonesia’s nickel downstreaming strategy. Read together, though, they can do more than supply three commodity case studies.

The cleaner shared argument is that **price power, policy power, and industrial power are different forms of leverage**.

- Chapter 5 — lithium: a market can look strategically scarce and then punish the scarcity narrative with supply growth and a price collapse.
- Chapter 6 — cobalt: a producer state can hold extraordinary geological concentration yet still struggle to convert that position into durable bargaining power.
- Chapter 7 — nickel: a state can use trade policy to force capital and processing capacity toward the ore, creating industrial power even while prices fall.

That gives the run a cumulative spine of **price → policy → installed capability**.

## Chapter 5 — The Lithium Triangle

### What is already strong

The chapter’s best move is to treat the lithium bust as evidence rather than embarrassment. High prices did summon supply. Argentina and other newer producers expanded, project pipelines thickened, and the shortage narrative weakened before long-run demand disappeared.

The chapter also correctly separates geology from conversion. A geographically diversified mine base is not the same thing as a diversified battery-grade chemical supply chain.

### 2026 evidence boundary

The IEA’s 2026 outlook materially changes the forward picture relative to 2025. It still projects lithium demand growing more than threefold by 2040 under stated policies, but the expected 2035 supply deficit has narrowed as more projects advance.

The report’s broader market update makes the short-run reversal even sharper. Lithium demand grew at around 25% per year on average over the previous two years, yet the market moved from the deep 2023–2024 downturn into a strong rebound: lithium prices more than doubled between January 2025 and April 2026 as battery-storage demand strengthened and supply conditions tightened.

The useful point is not that the 2024 glut was wrong or that the 2026 rebound proves scarcity. It is that both can occur inside the same strategic story.

A future prose pass should resist treating either the boom or the bust as the verdict.

### Strongest counterargument

Lithium is the chapter in which the book should give markets the most credit.

The supply response was real. Mining concentration is diversifying faster than several other mineral systems. Announced projects have narrowed the projected 2035 gap. Sodium-ion batteries and continued gains in material efficiency could reduce pressure further.

If those trends persist, lithium may prove to be a poor example of permanent geological leverage.

That does not make it a weak chokepoint case. It makes it a strong case for **financing-cycle vulnerability**: low prices can improve consumer economics while simultaneously killing projects needed for later diversification.

### Editorial implication

The chapter currently contains several versions of the same boom/bust lesson. The stronger later edit would keep one cycle as the carrier of the argument and use the 2026 rebound as the final proof that spot price is a poor proxy for structural security.

The sentence worth earning is not “lithium is scarce” or “lithium is abundant.” It is:

> lithium’s strategic problem is that the market can move from fear of shortage to fear of oversupply faster than mines, converters, governments, and communities can reorganize around either story.

### What would make this false?

- The current project pipeline consistently reaches production and keeps lithium structurally well supplied through the 2030s.
- Sodium-ion or other chemistries materially reduce lithium demand growth across several large use cases.
- Conversion capacity diversifies fast enough that mine diversification translates into genuine end-to-end resilience.

## Chapter 6 — Congo’s Cobalt

### Geological concentration does not equal complete market control

The chapter’s opening question remains excellent: if the DRC produces most mined cobalt, why did it need to stop exports to defend price?

The 2025–2026 record now makes the answer unusually concrete.

The DRC’s regulator, ARECOMS, shifted from a temporary export suspension to a quota system beginning 16 October 2025. The 2026 total quota is 96,600 tonnes: 87,000 tonnes of base quota plus 9,600 tonnes of strategic quota under ARECOMS discretion.

The IEA estimates that cobalt prices subsequently rose by around 130%, largely because of the DRC restrictions.

That is real producer-state power.

But the same IEA outlook also shows why the power is not absolute. Cobalt demand growth to 2040 has moderated because LFP batteries expanded rapidly. At the same time, the DRC quota widened the projected 2035 cobalt supply gap from just over 15% to over 25%.

The producer can tighten the market.

The customer can change the product.

Both mechanisms operate at once.

### The chapter’s sharper boundary

A future prose pass should make clearer that the DRC is not simply choosing between “exercise leverage” and “do nothing.” It is balancing four different objectives:

1. raise the price of a by-product whose oversupply weakened national bargaining power;
2. preserve export revenue and copper production;
3. capture more value domestically rather than remaining the geological starting point of a foreign-controlled chain;
4. avoid accelerating substitution away from cobalt so quickly that today’s market power shrinks tomorrow’s market.

That is a more interesting resource-state problem than a generic resource-nationalism story.

### Strongest counterargument

The strongest argument against the chapter’s geopolitical emphasis is that cobalt may become less strategically central precisely because the concentration was so uncomfortable.

LFP already removed nickel and cobalt from a large and growing share of battery demand. Cobalt intensity in nickel-rich chemistries has also changed. If those trends continue, the DRC may have considerable leverage over a smaller future battery market than earlier forecasts assumed.

The book should not treat this as a failure of the chokepoint thesis. It is evidence of one of its main escape mechanisms: enough leverage can change engineering.

### Ethical implication

The chapter’s strongest ethical line is still the asymmetry of options. Automakers can change chemistry. Traders can move inventory. Governments can change export rules. Households dependent on artisanal mining have fewer ways to move the cost elsewhere.

That should remain the human center of the chapter. The quota story is most useful when it makes visible who can react quickly and who cannot.

### What would make this false?

- Cobalt demand proves more resilient than the LFP shift suggests and alternative chemistries fail to scale.
- The quota system raises revenue and domestic value capture without materially accelerating substitution or informal leakage.
- New cobalt production outside the DRC diversifies mine supply faster than current pipelines imply.

## Chapter 7 — Nickel and the Indonesian Gambit

### The 2026 evidence strengthens the industrial-policy case

Indonesia’s nickel story is now better understood as a conversion of border policy into installed capital.

The IEA says that, over 2023–2025, Indonesia as the top nickel refiner and China as the top refiner for most other key energy minerals accounted for more than three-quarters of total refined-supply growth. For nickel specifically, virtually all recent supply growth came from the dominant supplier.

That is an important distinction from the cobalt case.

The DRC used export restrictions to reduce material leaving the country.

Indonesia used an ore-export ban to make much larger volumes of processed material leave the country.

One strategy seeks price support.

The other seeks industrial relocation.

### Downstreaming is no longer a one-commodity experiment

Indonesia’s Ministry of Investment and Downstream Industry reported that downstream investment reached IDR 584.1 trillion in 2025, up 43.3% year on year and equal to 30.2% of total investment realization.

By the first half of 2026, downstream investment reached another IDR 300.1 trillion, about 29.7% of total investment. Mineral downstreaming remained dominant, but bauxite displaced nickel as the largest individual downstream commodity for the first time.

That is useful counterevidence to the idea that Indonesia’s strategy is merely a nickel anomaly. The state is trying to generalize the model.

It is also a warning against declaring victory from one commodity cluster. A policy that successfully attracts furnaces still has to prove that domestic capability, productivity, cleaner power, skills, and local value capture deepen after the first wave of foreign capital arrives.

### Midstream is the next test

A June 2026 BKPM announcement about a proposed precursor cathode active material facility is revealing because it shows the policy moving beyond ore and first-stage nickel intermediates. The stated goal is to use domestically produced mixed hydroxide precipitate from HPAL plants as feedstock for pCAM production in Indonesia.

A July 2026 BKPM update on the Sambalagi HPAL project similarly framed the arrival of a major autoclave as evidence that the country is building a more integrated battery-material ecosystem.

Government announcements are not proof that every project will operate competitively or on schedule. But they show what the next industrial-policy question has become.

The benchmark is no longer “did a smelter arrive?”

It is “how much of the qualified battery-material chain remains in-country after the smelter?”

### Strongest counterargument

Indonesia’s success can be overstated in three ways.

First, ownership and operating capability remain deeply international, especially Chinese.

Second, nickel’s explosive supply growth helped weaken prices and damage higher-cost competitors elsewhere, which is industrial power but can also create domestic overcapacity risk.

Third, the environmental cost of coal-heavy processing can become a commercial constraint as buyers and regulators care more about embedded emissions and due-diligence rules.

The country has already demonstrated that policy can move capital.

It has not yet proved that every layer of the resulting industrial ecosystem will remain competitive when ore restrictions, cheap coal, tax incentives, and Chinese capital are no longer sufficient advantages by themselves.

### What would make this false?

- Downstream plants continue moving into higher-value cathode and battery-material stages while domestic firms, workers, and institutions capture a larger share of capability.
- Cleaner power materially lowers the carbon intensity of nickel processing without destroying cost competitiveness.
- LFP growth reduces battery nickel demand but stainless steel and other markets keep the industrial cluster economically durable.

## Shared editorial implication

These chapters should stop behaving like three separate warnings about concentration.

The stronger sequence is:

- **Lithium:** price can destroy a scarcity narrative before long-run demand disappears.
- **Cobalt:** geological concentration can create policy power without guaranteeing industrial or technological control.
- **Nickel:** trade policy can create industrial power even while abundant supply destroys scarcity rents.

That gives the reader three different answers to the question “what does mineral power actually look like?”

It can be a high price.

It can be the ability to withhold.

It can be the ability to make everyone build where you are.

Those are not interchangeable forms of leverage, and the rest of the book becomes easier to follow once the distinction is explicit.
