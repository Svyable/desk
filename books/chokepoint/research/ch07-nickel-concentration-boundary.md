# Chapter 7 research boundary: Indonesian nickel after the gambit

## Why this note exists

Chapter 7 already makes the important argument that Indonesia's nickel strategy is not a conventional scarcity play: Jakarta restricted raw ore exports while enabling a large expansion in domestic processing. Fresh 2026 evidence makes that argument stronger, but it also supplies a useful boundary. Indonesia's success has made the nickel chain more geographically concentrated, while ownership, technology, capital, and demand remain substantially transnational.

The chapter should therefore resist two easy conclusions: that Indonesia now simply "controls nickel," or that rapid Indonesian supply growth proves mineral chokepoints are self-correcting. The more precise story is that policy moved the chokepoint downstream and concentrated it in an industrial ecosystem whose resilience depends on power, foreign-owned capacity, customers, and competing battery chemistries.

## Current primary / authoritative evidence

### 1. Indonesia's mine share is now roughly two-thirds of world output

USGS's 2026 Mineral Commodity Summaries estimates 2025 world nickel mine production at 3.9 million tonnes and Indonesian production at 2.6 million tonnes, up about 13% as new operations ramped. That implies roughly 67% of global mined output.

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, *Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 — Nickel*, February 6, 2026.
https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-nickel.pdf

Useful boundary: attach a year to any share. Indonesia's position has changed quickly enough that an undated "more than half" understates current concentration and will age badly.

### 2. The supply response has been real enough to close higher-cost mines elsewhere

The same USGS summary estimates Australian nickel mine production fell 54% in 2025 after multiple companies placed mines into care and maintenance because of low prices. This is unusually clean evidence for the chapter's argument that Indonesian abundance can exercise strategic power through competitors' economics rather than through deliberate withholding.

This should not be written as proof that Indonesia intentionally set out to close Australian mines. The causal claim USGS supports is narrower: low prices coincided with, and were associated with, care-and-maintenance decisions while Indonesian production continued to expand.

### 3. Concentration continued to rise in refining too

The IEA's *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* reports that refining concentration reached record levels in 2025. Across key energy minerals, the dominant suppliers — Indonesia for nickel and China for most others — accounted for more than three-quarters of refined-supply growth between 2023 and 2025. The IEA says virtually all recent supply growth in nickel refining came from the dominant supplier.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, market overview and executive summary, 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/market-overview
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

This is useful because the chapter's thesis is not merely that Indonesia mines a lot of ore. Its industrial-policy achievement is precisely that processing followed the ore.

### 4. Geography is not ownership

The IEA's 2024 ownership analysis remains an important guardrail. Although Indonesia had become the leading location for nickel mining, Indonesian-headquartered companies accounted for less than 10% of nickel mine production in the IEA dataset, while Chinese companies accounted for around 40%.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2024*, market review.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2024/market-review

A newer IEA regional investment assessment adds a downstream version of the same point: Chinese investment in Indonesia's nickel refinery sector reached about USD 30 billion in 2024, and Chinese companies accounted for around 75% of refining capacity in the country.

Source: International Energy Agency, *World Energy Investment 2025 — Southeast Asia*.
https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/southeast-asia

These figures should not be collapsed into a claim that "China owns Indonesian nickel." Ownership differs by mine, park, refinery, joint venture, and corporate structure. The defensible claim is that Indonesian jurisdiction over the physical resource coexists with substantial foreign ownership and financing, especially Chinese participation in refining.

## Strongest counterargument

Indonesia may be evidence that concentrated mineral markets are more contestable than the book's chokepoint framing suggests. Its rise was astonishingly fast. High prices, policy pressure, capital, and processing technology brought millions of tonnes of new supply to market; the resulting glut damaged incumbents. A reader could reasonably conclude that commodity markets work: scarcity raises returns, investment arrives, supply expands, prices fall, and yesterday's chokepoint loses force.

The chapter should concede the part that is true. Nickel is powerful counterevidence to any deterministic scarcity thesis.

But the supply response did not simply diversify the market. It shifted production toward one country and, downstream, toward a cluster with heavy foreign participation. The IEA's 2026 concentration data therefore makes the more interesting point: markets can cure a shortage while increasing concentration. Resilience and abundance are different variables.

## What would make the chapter's argument weaker?

The argument that Indonesia has become a durable nickel chokepoint should weaken if several of the following happen:

- meaningful new mine and refining capacity reaches commercial scale outside Indonesia;
- non-Indonesian projects remain economic through a low-price cycle rather than repeatedly entering care and maintenance;
- battery chemistries materially reduce nickel demand faster than stainless-steel and other uses absorb supply growth;
- Indonesian processing loses its cost advantage as carbon intensity, power costs, regulation, or ore quality become more binding;
- ownership and technical capability inside the Indonesian chain become materially more domestic, reducing the chapter's current distinction between jurisdiction and corporate control.

## Editorial use

If this evidence is incorporated into the manuscript later, the highest-value move is probably not another paragraph of market-share statistics. Use the 2025 Australia-versus-Indonesia production contrast to dramatize the unusual mechanism: Indonesia gained leverage by making nickel plentiful enough to strand competitors.

Then qualify it immediately. The lesson is not that Jakarta can dictate a monopoly price. It is that a country able to add marginal supply faster and cheaper than its rivals can shape which rival mines get financed, maintained, or mothballed.

A durable formulation for the chapter is:

> A commodity market can become more abundant and more concentrated at the same time.

That sentence earns the paradox already latent in the chapter without turning it into a prediction that today's Indonesian dominance is permanent.
