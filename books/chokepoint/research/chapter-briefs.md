# Chapter Briefs

These are research and argument briefs, not prose outlines. Each chapter should still discover its own narrative shape from the evidence available when it is written.

## Chapter 1 — The List of Seventeen

**Job:** Open the book with a concrete, dramatic episode of rare-earth export control and make the reader feel how a handful of unfamiliar elements became a geopolitical weapon.

**Questions to answer:** What exactly did China restrict, when, and how? Who was affected first? What is a rare earth element, technically, and why seventeen of them? Why does a number most readers cannot name suddenly matter to a car factory or a defense contractor on the other side of the world?

**Evidence to seek:** First-party Chinese Ministry of Commerce announcements or reliable reporting on them; market and industry reaction; a specific affected company or sector.

**Counterevidence:** Note where the controls had limited effect, were quickly worked around, or were narrower than initial reporting suggested.

**Handoff:** End by widening from rare earths to the full list of minerals the book will cover, and the claim that they now function the way oil once did.

## Chapter 2 — Everything Runs on Rock

**Job:** Establish the demand-side scope: what "critical mineral" means officially, and why AI data centers, EVs, wind turbines, grid buildout, and defense systems compete for the same short list.

**Questions to answer:** What are the official critical-minerals lists (USGS, IEA, EU) and how are they compiled? How much copper, lithium, cobalt, nickel, and rare-earth demand is actually projected from each of these sectors through 2030 and beyond? Where does demand overlap and where does it diverge by sector?

**Evidence to seek:** USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries; IEA Critical Minerals reports; EU Critical Raw Materials Act mineral list; sector-specific demand studies.

**Counterevidence:** Some demand projections are speculative and have been revised down before; avoid treating any single forecast as certain.

**Handoff:** Once the scope is set, ask how well the twentieth century's experience with a different critical input — oil — predicts what happens next.

## Chapter 3 — The Oil Analogy, Examined

**Job:** Build the comparison to twentieth-century oil geopolitics carefully enough to show where it illuminates and where it misleads.

**Questions to answer:** What actually happened in the 1973 OPEC embargo and the 1979 shock? How did oil geopolitics reshape strategic reserves, alliances, and foreign policy? Which structural features of minerals differ from oil — stock versus flow, absence of a unified cartel, recyclability?

**Evidence to seek:** Primary/historical accounts of 1973 and 1979; OPEC founding history; contemporary mineral-market structure analysis from economists and policy researchers.

**Counterevidence:** Analysts who argue the analogy is overused or actively misleading; specific ways mineral markets behave unlike oil markets.

**Handoff:** With the framework in place, the book turns to the individual minerals, starting with copper.

## Chapter 4 — Copper: The Original Chokepoint

**Job:** Ground the book in the oldest and most electrification-critical of the minerals, using Chile as the central case.

**Questions to answer:** How concentrated is global copper mine supply? What is Codelco's history and current condition? What does a mine like Escondida or El Teniente actually look like as an operation? How does copper demand connect specifically to grid and data-center buildout?

**Evidence to seek:** USGS copper data; Codelco and BHP disclosures; recent copper price and supply reporting.

**Counterevidence:** Copper is more geographically diversified than cobalt or rare earths; note where supply is not as concentrated as the popular narrative suggests.

**Handoff:** From the oldest electrification metal to the newest: lithium and the battery race.

## Chapter 5 — The Lithium Triangle

**Job:** Tell the story of lithium's extreme price cycle and geographic concentration.

**Questions to answer:** How is lithium extracted differently in the Chile/Argentina/Bolivia brine triangle versus Australian hard-rock mining? What actually happened in the 2021-2023 price boom and the subsequent bust? How does EV demand growth interact with this volatility?

**Evidence to seek:** Real price data (Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, Fastmarkets, USGS, EIA); production-share data by country.

**Counterevidence:** The bust shows that high prices did eventually summon supply and that lithium may be less structurally scarce than feared.

**Handoff:** Lithium's volatility raises the question of what happens when a mineral supply chain runs through one of the world's least stable regions — the DRC's cobalt.

## Chapter 6 — Congo's Cobalt

**Job:** Examine the most ethically fraught chokepoint in the book: DRC cobalt mining and Chinese ownership of much of its supply chain.

**Questions to answer:** How dominant is the DRC in global cobalt mine supply? Who owns the major mines, and how did Chinese companies acquire that position? What do credible investigations say about artisanal and small-scale mining conditions, including child labor? How is the shift to LFP batteries changing cobalt's importance?

**Evidence to seek:** USGS/IEA cobalt data; company ownership history (CMOC, Glencore); credible investigative reporting (Amnesty International, Washington Post, and similar).

**Counterevidence:** Responsible-sourcing initiatives, formalization efforts, and the economic dependence of Congolese communities on artisanal mining income.

**Handoff:** From cobalt's ethical weight to nickel's environmental one, in Indonesia.

## Chapter 7 — Nickel and the Indonesian Gambit

**Job:** Show how a single policy decision — Indonesia's ore export ban — remade an entire global market in a few years.

**Questions to answer:** What was the export ban, when, and why? How did Chinese capital (Tsingshan and others) build Indonesian smelting capacity so fast? What is the environmental cost, and what happened to global nickel prices as a result?

**Evidence to seek:** Indonesian policy documents/reporting; USGS/LME nickel data; environmental reporting on captive coal power and smelter pollution.

**Counterevidence:** Indonesian government and industry defenses of the policy; real downstream economic development it produced.

**Handoff:** Nickel and cobalt show mining chokepoints. The next two chapters show that the deeper chokepoint is refining — and rare earths make that clearest of all.

## Chapter 8 — Rare Earths and the Refining Monopoly

**Job:** Make the book's sharpest analytical point: mining rare earths is not the scarce step, separating them is, and China dominates that step far more than it dominates mining.

**Questions to answer:** What happened to Mountain Pass, and why did the world's dominant rare-earth mine close and reopen? What is Lynas's role as the one significant separator outside China? What is China's actual share of separation/refining capacity, as distinct from its mining share?

**Evidence to seek:** Mountain Pass and MP Materials history; Lynas disclosures; USGS/IEA/Adamas Intelligence processing-capacity data.

**Counterevidence:** New Western/allied separation capacity coming online; cases where the mining-versus-refining distinction is narrower than claimed.

**Handoff:** Ask how China arrived at this position — not by accident, but by decades of deliberate policy.

## Chapter 9 — The Weaponization of Supply Chains

**Job:** Trace the history of mineral supply used explicitly as leverage.

**Questions to answer:** What happened in the 2010 China-Japan rare earth incident, and how did Japan respond? What is the fuller 2023-2026 history of Chinese export licensing on gallium, germanium, graphite, and rare earths? How effective has this leverage actually been?

**Evidence to seek:** Contemporaneous reporting on 2010; Chinese Ministry of Commerce announcements 2023-2026; market and diplomatic reactions.

**Counterevidence:** Cases where controls were circumvented, had limited practical effect, or provoked responses that reduced future Chinese leverage.

**Handoff:** If mineral supply is a slow-moving weapon, the next question is why supply itself cannot respond quickly — the capital cycle.

## Chapter 10 — The Capital Cycle, Underground

**Job:** Explain why mining is uniquely slow to respond to price signals.

**Questions to answer:** Why do new mines take 10-20+ years from discovery to production? What role do permitting, financing, ore-grade decline, and construction lead times play? How does this compare to capital cycles in other industries?

**Evidence to seek:** Mine lead-time studies (S&P Global, IEA, academic sources); specific project timelines as illustrations.

**Counterevidence:** Cases of unusually fast project development, and reforms (permitting streamlining) that could shorten timelines.

**Handoff:** A slow capital cycle means booms and busts play out over decades, not quarters — illustrated by history.

## Chapter 11 — Boom, Bust, Ghost Town

**Job:** Tell one deeply researched historical mining boom-bust story to make the capital cycle concrete and human.

**Questions to answer:** What mine, company, or town best illustrates the pattern — rapid boom, price collapse, abandonment or reinvention? What happened to the people who worked there?

**Evidence to seek:** Historical/archival sources on the chosen case; production and price data; what remains of the site today.

**Counterevidence:** Note booms that did not bust, or busts that were followed by durable recovery.

**Handoff:** If busts are inevitable, is there a way to reduce dependence on any single new mine — recycling?

## Chapter 12 — The Recycling Illusion and Reality

**Job:** Assess how much "urban mining" can realistically contribute, without either dismissing it or overselling it.

**Questions to answer:** How much lithium/cobalt/nickel could recycled batteries realistically supply by 2030 and 2040? What are the physical and logistical constraints? Which companies are trying to scale this, and how are they doing?

**Evidence to seek:** IEA/academic recycling-supply estimates; company case studies (including any real financial distress); e-waste data.

**Counterevidence:** Cases where recycling economics worked better than expected, or where policy mandates accelerated adoption.

**Handoff:** If recycling can't close the gap alone, what about mining somewhere nobody has mined before — the ocean floor?

## Chapter 13 — Deep Sea and Desperate Measures

**Job:** Examine the most contested new frontier: deep-sea polymetallic nodule mining.

**Questions to answer:** What is The Metals Company actually proposing, and where does the International Seabed Authority dispute stand? What do marine scientists say about the ecological risk? Why would a company try to bypass the ISA process?

**Evidence to seek:** ISA and TMC primary materials; credible reporting on the regulatory fight; peer-reviewed deep-sea ecology research.

**Counterevidence:** Arguments that deep-sea mining's environmental footprint could be smaller than land-based mining in some respects.

**Handoff:** From an entirely new mining frontier to an entirely different kind of response: not finding more of a mineral, but needing less of it.

## Chapter 14 — Friend-Shoring and the New Alliances

**Job:** Cover the Western and allied policy response to mineral dependence.

**Questions to answer:** What do the US IRA sourcing rules, the Minerals Security Partnership, and the EU Critical Raw Materials Act actually require and fund? What real projects have resulted? How fast is this moving relative to the scale of the problem?

**Evidence to seek:** Primary government/EU documents; MSP project announcements; critical assessments of pace and funding.

**Counterevidence:** Genuine progress and specific successes should be represented alongside the critiques.

**Handoff:** Ask how China got so far ahead in the first place — the subject of the next chapter.

## Chapter 15 — China's Long Game

**Job:** Tell the history of how Chinese rare-earth and mineral-processing dominance was built deliberately, not accidentally.

**Questions to answer:** What is the real, sourced history behind the industrial policy — including honest treatment of the widely repeated Deng Xiaoping quotation, verifying or flagging its sourcing? What environmental costs did China accept to build this position (Baotou)? How has the industry consolidated more recently?

**Evidence to seek:** Historical scholarship and reporting on Chinese industrial policy; Baotou pollution reporting; China Rare Earth Group formation.

**Counterevidence:** Ways Chinese dominance is now being eroded or contested from within (environmental crackdowns, overcapacity, price wars).

**Handoff:** With both the Chinese strategy and the Western response established, the book turns to whether either side even needs as much of these minerals as projected — substitution.

## Chapter 16 — The Substitution Race

**Job:** Cover the materials-science response: using less of the scarce inputs, or none at all.

**Questions to answer:** How real and how fast is the shift to LFP and sodium-ion batteries? What is the status of rare-earth-free or reduced motor designs? How much could substitution plausibly reduce demand growth?

**Evidence to seek:** Battery-chemistry market-share data; company announcements (Tesla, CATL, GM) with real, verifiable specifics.

**Counterevidence:** Substitution claims that proved slower or less complete than announced.

**Handoff:** Every solution in this book — new mines, recycling, deep-sea nodules, substitution — has a cost somewhere. The next chapter names it.

## Chapter 17 — Water, Waste, and the Hidden Costs

**Job:** Confront the environmental and social costs of mining directly, anchored in one well-documented disaster.

**Questions to answer:** What happened at Brumadinho in January 2019, and why? What do tailings dam failures reveal about the industry's risk management? What are the water conflicts around lithium brine extraction, and what indigenous and local community concerns recur across these cases?

**Evidence to seek:** Brumadinho investigation and reporting; tailings dam failure data/tracking; Atacama water-use research.

**Counterevidence:** Cases of genuine remediation, stronger regulation after disasters, and responsible-mining certification progress.

**Handoff:** Having traced costs and benefits at the mine level, the book widens to the national level: who actually gets rich from mineral wealth.

## Chapter 18 — Who Gets Rich, Who Gets Left

**Job:** Examine the resource curse and its exceptions.

**Questions to answer:** What does the foundational Sachs and Warner research argue, and how has it held up? What do the DRC, Zambia, and historical Bolivia show about resource wealth without broad-based benefit? What do Chile, Botswana, and Norway show about avoiding the curse?

**Evidence to seek:** Sachs and Warner and follow-up academic literature; country-specific economic and institutional histories.

**Counterevidence:** Scholars who dispute or complicate the resource curse thesis; cases where the curse's supposed mechanism did not hold.

**Handoff:** With the human and institutional stakes established, the book turns to where prices and geopolitics are actually headed.

## Chapter 19 — The Price of the Future

**Job:** Synthesize the economics into a scenario-based, non-deterministic view of what happens as demand compounds.

**Questions to answer:** What would distinguish a well-managed transition from a genuine crisis? Which indicators (utilization, financing terms, project cancellations, price trends, diversification progress) would show which path is playing out?

**Evidence to seek:** Synthesis of prior chapters' data; any long-range IEA/USGS scenario work.

**Counterevidence:** The chapter should present multiple plausible futures, not one prediction.

**Handoff:** Close by asking what a mature mineral economy could look like on the other side.

## Chapter 20 — After the Rush

**Job:** Resolve the book without declaring victory for any single outcome.

**Questions to answer:** What would a diversified, partly recycled, partly substituted mineral economy look like in the 2040s? What should policymakers, companies, and individuals take from the preceding chapters?

**Evidence to seek:** Forward-looking material already gathered; avoid introducing new unsourced speculation.

**Final note:** The chokepoints in this book are not permanent facts of nature. They are the current shape of a system that is still being built, contested, and slowly diversified. The durable skill is recognizing where leverage actually sits — in the refining step more than the mine, in the decade-long project more than the quarterly headline — rather than in whichever chokepoint made the news this week.
