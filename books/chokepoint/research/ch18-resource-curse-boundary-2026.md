# Chapter 18 research note — the resource curse is a mechanism, not a mineral destiny

Research checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 18, “Who Gets Rich, Who Gets Left.”

## Why this note exists

The existing Chapter 18 brief contains useful leads, but much of it was assembled from search-result snippets during an earlier session that could not fetch source pages directly. Before drafting the chapter, the book needs a cleaner factual boundary built from sources that can now be read directly.

The most important correction is conceptual. “Resource curse” should not be used as a synonym for “country has valuable minerals and remains poor.” The original Sachs-Warner result was a cross-country growth correlation. Later work challenged its specification and proposed several different mechanisms. The strongest chapter will therefore ask what institutions and balance-sheet choices convert a volatile mineral rent into broad public wealth — and what happens when they do not.

## The foundational claim, stated narrowly

Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner’s 1995 NBER paper found that countries with high natural-resource exports relative to GDP in 1971 tended to grow more slowly from 1971 to 1989, even after controls for initial income, trade policy, government efficiency, investment, and other variables.

Source: Jeffrey D. Sachs and Andrew M. Warner, “Natural Resource Abundance and Economic Growth,” NBER Working Paper 5398, December 1995.
https://www.nber.org/papers/w5398

The chapter should not inflate this into a law of nature. The paper measures a statistical relationship across countries and a particular historical period. It does not establish that every new mineral discovery lowers growth, nor that geology by itself causes weak institutions.

## Strongest econometric counterargument

Osmel Manzano and Roberto Rigobon challenged the canonical interpretation in 2001. Their NBER paper argues that the apparent resource effect can disappear after accounting for debt overhang: commodity-rich countries borrowed heavily against high 1970s prices, then entered debt crises when prices collapsed in the 1980s.

Source: Osmel Manzano and Roberto Rigobon, “Resource Curse or Debt Overhang?” NBER Working Paper 8390, July 2001.
https://www.nber.org/papers/w8390

This is valuable because it turns the chapter away from mystical explanations. A mineral boom can become destructive through ordinary financial plumbing: collateral, borrowing, spending commitments, currency appreciation, and a commodity price that later refuses to cooperate.

A later survey by Frederick van der Ploeg reaches the more useful synthesis: natural resources can be either a curse or a blessing, with outcomes depending on volatility, institutions, rule of law, corruption, financial development, conflict risk, and whether a country converts an exhaustible asset into other productive assets.

Source: Frederick van der Ploeg, “Natural Resources: Curse or Blessing?” Journal of Economic Literature 49(2), 2011.
https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.49.2.366

That should be the chapter’s intellectual boundary: mineral wealth creates unusually large rents and unusually difficult policy problems. It does not predetermine the answer.

## DRC — enormous export importance, little automatic inclusion

The Democratic Republic of the Congo is useful because the mining sector is simultaneously macroeconomically dominant and socially narrow.

The World Bank’s current country overview says the DRC remains among the five poorest countries in the world. Its 2024 household survey puts roughly 69% of the population below the national poverty line, while the Bank also notes limited job creation in mining as a constraint on inclusive growth.

Source: World Bank Group, Democratic Republic of Congo country overview, current as checked September 2026.
https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/drc

EITI’s DRC profile reports that the extractive sector accounted for 46% of government revenue and nearly all exports in 2021. That coexistence is more informative than a generic “resource curse” label: a sector can dominate foreign exchange and public revenue without employing enough people, linking deeply enough into the domestic economy, or converting proceeds efficiently enough to generate broad prosperity.

Source: Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative, Democratic Republic of the Congo country profile.
https://eiti.org/countries/democratic-republic-congo

The chapter should avoid implying that mining alone created the DRC’s poverty. Conflict, colonial extraction, weak infrastructure, governance failures, state capacity, regional security, and the structure of the wider economy are causal background, not footnotes.

## Botswana — a success case that is no longer a victory lap

Botswana remains one of the strongest counterexamples to mineral fatalism.

Its diamond structure matters. Debswana is owned 50/50 by the Government of Botswana and De Beers, giving the state an equity claim as well as tax and royalty channels. Botswana also created the wholly state-owned Okavango Diamond Company as a direct route to market for part of Debswana’s production.

Sources:
- Debswana, “Our Shareholders.”
  https://www.debswana.com/our-shareholders/
- Government of Botswana, DailyNews/BOPA reporting on the Debswana-De Beers partnership and ODC allocations.
  https://dailynews.gov.bw/news-detail/73947

Government historical material records that Botswana initially received 15% equity in the Orapa venture, then renegotiated to a 50/50 Debswana shareholding in 1975. It also describes diamond revenue financing later mine development. That is the institutional story worth emphasizing: the state did not merely possess diamonds; it repeatedly negotiated how rents, ownership, and market access were distributed.

Source: Government of Botswana, DailyNews/BOPA, June 25, 2023.
https://dailynews.gov.bw/news-detail/73711

But Botswana should not be presented as proof that the problem has been permanently solved. The IMF’s 2025 Article IV work reports that the economy contracted in 2024 as diamond output fell, with fiscal deficits widening and reserves declining; it specifically stresses diversification as necessary for more sustainable growth.

Sources:
- IMF, “Staff Completes 2025 Article IV Mission to Botswana,” September 26, 2025.
  https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2025/09/26/pr-25317-botswana-imf-staff-completes-2025-article-iv-mission-to-botswana
- IMF, Botswana 2025 Article IV Consultation, Country Report No. 2025/325.
  https://www.imf.org/en/publications/cr/issues/2025/12/08/botswana-2025-article-iv-consultation-press-release-staff-report-and-statement-by-the-572347

The modern Botswana lesson is therefore better than “good institutions beat the curse.” Good institutions can capture and compound rents for decades, while commodity dependence still leaves the economy exposed when demand changes — including competition from lab-grown diamonds.

## Chile — turning copper volatility into a fiscal engineering problem

Chile offers a different escape mechanism: rules that try to stop a commodity boom from becoming a permanent spending baseline.

Chile’s Ministry of Finance says the Economic and Social Stabilization Fund was created in 2007, largely from the former Copper Stabilization Fund, to help finance the central government when the economic cycle turns or extraordinary events occur.

Source: Chile Ministry of Finance, Economic and Social Stabilization Fund.
https://www.hacienda.cl/english/work-areas/international-finance/sovereign-wealth-funds/economic-and-social-stabilization-fund

The structural-balance framework explicitly removes cyclical copper-price effects from the budget calculation. For the 2026 budget, independent experts supplied a long-term copper reference price used in calculating the cyclically adjusted balance.

Source: Chile Ministry of Finance and Budget Directorate, August 29, 2025.
https://www.hacienda.cl/noticias-y-eventos/noticias/ministerio-de-hacienda-y-dipres-publican-proyecciones-del-pib-no-minero

The mechanism can be seen in the fund’s cash history. The Ministry reports large contributions in the boom years and large withdrawals during 2009, the social unrest/pandemic period, and later fiscal needs. As of June 2026 the fund’s market value was about US$3.88 billion after cumulative contributions of roughly US$27.8 billion and withdrawals of roughly US$28.3 billion.

Source: Chile Ministry of Finance, ESSF market value, June 2026.
https://www.hacienda.cl/english/work-areas/international-finance/sovereign-wealth-funds/economic-and-social-stabilization-fund/financial-situation/market-value

This is not a claim that Chile has escaped every mining problem. The IMF’s 2026 Article IV still projects structural fiscal deficits and rising public debt. The narrower lesson is stronger: copper-price volatility can be treated as something to design institutions around rather than something a government simply experiences.

Source: IMF, Chile 2026 Article IV Consultation, Country Report 2026/169.
https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2026/169/article-A001-en.xml

## Zambia — revenue capture is not the same as development

Zambia is useful as a middle case because copper is economically central but the policy problem is neither simple expropriation nor simple laissez-faire.

The World Bank’s 2024 Public Finance Review notes that Zambia’s tax collection remains constrained by over-reliance on mining, tax expenditures, and weaknesses in VAT and customs administration. It also frames better governance, public investment execution, and revenue mobilization as necessary if natural-resource income is to finance infrastructure, education, and health.

Source: World Bank, “New World Bank Report Emphasizes the Urgent Need for Zambia to Boost Revenue Mobilization,” September 23, 2024.
https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2024/09/23/new-world-bank-report-emphasizes-the-urgent-need-for-afe-zambia-to-boost-revenue-mobilization

The editorial implication is important: the chapter should not measure success solely by whether a government raises the mining tax rate. The meaningful chain is rent capture → stable public revenue → competent allocation → productive assets and services. A break anywhere in that chain can leave a mineral-rich country with little durable wealth.

## What the chapter should not say

Avoid “minerals make countries poor.” The evidence does not support that deterministic claim.

Avoid treating Botswana, Chile, or Norway as frictionless model answers. Their institutions have costs, political histories, distributional disputes, and continuing commodity exposure.

Avoid using GDP growth alone as the scorecard. A country can grow quickly while failing to broaden employment, reduce poverty, build state capacity, or accumulate productive assets.

Avoid using government revenue alone as the scorecard for the same reason. Revenue can be captured and still be spent badly, borrowed against, stolen, or used to support a permanently higher cost structure that becomes painful when prices fall.

Avoid implying that every mechanism travels together. Dutch disease, debt overhang, corruption, conflict, enclave employment, fiscal volatility, and rent-seeking are distinct channels and should be named only where evidence supports them.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter’s likely thesis

The strongest objection is that “institutions matter” can become a tautology: successful countries are said to have good institutions because they succeeded, while failures are retroactively blamed on bad institutions.

The chapter should answer by staying concrete. Botswana’s equity negotiations, Chile’s structural copper reference price and stabilization fund, transparent revenue reporting, debt rules, and direct market access are observable institutional choices. They do not guarantee prosperity, but they change who receives rents, when governments spend them, and what happens when prices fall.

Likewise, Manzano and Rigobon’s debt-overhang critique matters because it provides a testable alternative to vague institutional storytelling. A boom financed by borrowing can produce a very different bust from a boom saved into liquid public assets.

## Editorial spine for Chapter 18

A stronger chapter can be organized around one question: when a tonne of ore leaves the country, what remains after the ship sails?

The answer might be tax revenue, dividends, foreign exchange, local wages, roads, schools, engineering capability, a stabilization fund, debt, environmental liabilities, or almost nothing beyond a hole in the ground.

That is more useful than calling one country blessed and another cursed.

The durable distinction is between extracting a resource and converting an exhaustible rent into assets that survive the resource cycle.

The handoff to Chapter 19 then becomes natural. Even well-designed institutions still have to make decisions under uncertain future prices. The next problem is not simply who captures the rent, but what price assumptions those decisions are built on.
