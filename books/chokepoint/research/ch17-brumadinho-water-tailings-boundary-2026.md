# Chapter 17 research note — Brumadinho, tailings, and the water-cost boundary

Research checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 17, “Water, Waste, and the Hidden Costs.”

## Why this note exists

The existing Chapter 17 research has the right central case but one technical claim is too neat: it describes seepage erosion or “piping” as the primary cause of the 2019 Brumadinho failure. The stronger record is more complicated. The chapter should distinguish the dam’s physical failure mechanism, the conditions that made the tailings susceptible to liquefaction, and the governance failures that allowed warning signs and safety-relevant information to coexist with continued operation.

That distinction improves the book’s larger argument. Mining’s environmental costs are not just a list of bad outcomes. They arise from engineering choices, hydrology, monitoring, governance, and tradeoffs that can sometimes be changed — but not wished away.

## Brumadinho: authoritative factual spine

On January 25, 2019, Dam I at Vale’s Córrego do Feijão iron-ore mine near Brumadinho, Minas Gerais, failed suddenly. The technical expert panel commissioned after the disaster reported that the collapse released about 9.7 million cubic metres of material, roughly three-quarters of the stored tailings, with the dam slope collapsing in less than ten seconds and most of the released material leaving the impoundment within minutes.

Source: Robertson, de Melo, Williams and Wilson, *Report of the Expert Panel on the Technical Causes of the Failure of Feijão Dam I*, December 12, 2019. Public copy hosted by the U.S. Forest Service’s Resolution Copper EIS reference library:
https://www.resolutionmineeis.us/documents/robertson-et-al-2019

The current Government of Minas Gerais Brumadinho reparation portal records 272 deaths and describes continuing social, environmental, and economic impacts in the Paraopeba River basin and across the state.

Source: Government of Minas Gerais, Pró-Brumadinho, updated August 11, 2026:
https://www.mg.gov.br/pro-brumadinho/pagina/entenda-o-acordo-judicial-de-reparacao-ao-rompimento-em-brumadinho

Use 272 as the retrospective death toll in the chapter. Earlier official and press accounts used lower figures while recovery was ongoing; the text should not mix those interim counts with the settled retrospective total.

## Do not reduce the technical cause to “piping”

The expert-panel report describes a sudden slope failure associated with liquefaction of loose, saturated tailings in an upstream-raised dam. The event did not require an obvious external trigger such as an earthquake or extreme rainfall at the moment of collapse. The chapter should therefore avoid a single-cause sentence such as “piping caused Brumadinho.”

A better hierarchy is:

- **failure mechanism:** sudden slope failure and liquefaction of susceptible tailings;
- **physical conditions:** loose saturated tailings, the dam’s upstream construction history, drainage and water conditions, and long-term deformation;
- **warning/governance layer:** anomalies and safety-relevant information existed before failure, and the regulator later concluded that some material information in Vale’s internal records had not been reported correctly.

Brazil’s National Mining Agency said in its November 5, 2019 technical-report announcement that internal Vale records contained anomalies not reported to the agency. It specifically described solids observed during horizontal drain installation and an unexpected seepage condition; ANM stated that accurate reporting could have led to precautionary or emergency measures.

Source: Agência Nacional de Mineração, “Agência Nacional de Mineração conclui o relatório técnico sobre barragem de Brumadinho,” November 5, 2019:
https://www.gov.br/anm/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2019/agencia-nacional-de-mineracao-conclui-o-relatorio-tecnico-sobre-barragem-de-brumadinho/

This is the stronger narrative point. The disaster should not be written as a mysterious geotechnical accident with a single hidden defect. Nor should the chapter imply that every detected anomaly meant collapse was certain. The evidence instead supports a harder institutional question: how does a hazardous system treat ambiguous warning signals when production continues and the probability of catastrophe is low but the consequence is extreme?

## The aftermath changed industry practice — real counterevidence

Brumadinho was followed by a global attempt to raise the floor on tailings governance. UNEP, the Principles for Responsible Investment, and the International Council on Mining and Metals launched the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management in August 2020 after a multi-stakeholder review initiated in response to Brumadinho.

The standard covers the full lifecycle of a tailings facility and includes requirements on affected communities, human rights, environmental protection, climate risk, consequence classification, governance, independent oversight, emergency response, and public disclosure. UNEP describes 15 principles and 77 auditable requirements.

Sources:
- UNEP, Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management:
https://www.unep.org/resources/report/global-industry-standard-tailings-management
- UNEP launch announcement, August 5, 2020:
https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/new-global-industry-standard-tailings-management-aims-improve-safety

In January 2025, UNEP, PRI, and ICMM launched the Global Tailings Management Institute to oversee independent assessment and certification against the standard.

Source: UNEP, January 21, 2025:
https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/new-independent-institute-drive-and-assess-implementation-global

This belongs in the chapter because the strongest counterargument to a fatalistic “mining always externalizes catastrophe” story is that engineering standards, disclosure, regulation, and independent assurance can improve after failure. The chapter should grant that progress without treating a voluntary or certification framework as proof that catastrophic risk has disappeared.

## Reparation is substantial, but money is not a rewind button

The 2021 judicial reparation agreement between Vale, the Government of Minas Gerais, prosecutors, and the state public defender was estimated at about R$37.69 billion. The state’s 2026 implementation page makes clear that the agreement combines obligations Vale must execute directly with monetary obligations used for public projects and recovery.

Source: Government of Minas Gerais, updated July 1, 2026:
https://www.mg.gov.br/pro-brumadinho/pagina/reparacao-brumadinho-obrigacoes-de-pagar-da-vale-ao-estado

Use the agreement as evidence of real accountability and remediation effort, not as a price tag for 272 lives. The sharper distinction is between losses that can be compensated, environments that can be restored in part, and harms that are irreversible.

## Water: Chilean copper provides a useful counterexample to a static scarcity story

The chapter should not imply that mining water demand automatically means permanent competition for the same freshwater source. Chile’s copper industry is rapidly changing its water mix.

Cochilco’s 2026 water-demand outlook projects total copper-mining water use rising from 18.5 cubic metres per second in 2024 to 20.6 m³/s in 2034, while seawater becomes the dominant source. Cochilco projects seawater reaching 67.6% of total sector water use by 2034.

Sources:
- Comisión Chilena del Cobre, *Proyección de la Demanda de Agua en la Minería del Cobre en Chile, 2025–2034*:
https://www.cochilco.cl/web/informe-proyeccion-del-consumo-de-agua-en-la-mineria-del-cobre-2025-2034/
- Cochilco summary on the 67.6% seawater projection:
https://www.cochilco.cl/web/https-www-cochilco-cl-web-download-1014-2026-15581-informe-proyeccion-de-la-demanda-de-agua-en-la-mineria-del-cobre-en-chile-periodo-2025-2034-pdf/

That is genuine mitigation. It weakens a simplistic claim that more copper production must mean proportionally more continental freshwater extraction.

But the burden moves rather than vanishes. Pumping and desalinating seawater requires infrastructure and energy. Cochilco’s 2026 electricity outlook projects copper-mining electricity consumption rising from 27.6 TWh in 2025 to 33.2 TWh in 2034, faster than projected copper-output growth, with increased use of seawater among the drivers.

Source: Cochilco, *Proyección del Consumo de Energía Eléctrica en la Minería del Cobre, 2025–2034*:
https://www.cochilco.cl/web/informe-proyeccion-del-consumo-de-energia-electrica-en-la-mineria-del-cobre-periodo-2025-2034-2/

This is an ideal Chapter 17 pattern: an environmental constraint can be engineered around, but the solution creates a new infrastructure and energy bill. The right question is not whether mining has impacts. It is which impacts can be reduced, which are displaced, and which remain attached to the tonne of metal.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter’s environmental thesis

A serious version of this chapter must acknowledge three things.

First, standards and engineering do improve. Brumadinho catalyzed stronger global tailings governance, and newer projects can use different tailings designs, monitoring systems, water-management practices, and consequence planning.

Second, the relevant comparison is not “mining versus no environmental impact.” Copper, lithium, nickel, and rare earths enable systems that may avoid other environmental burdens. Refusing one mine can shift production to another jurisdiction rather than eliminate demand.

Third, some apparent externalities can be materially reduced by investment: seawater can substitute for continental water, process water can be recycled, dangerous facilities can be redesigned or decommissioned, and disclosure can improve oversight.

The counterargument fails only when mitigation is treated as elimination. Desalination still needs power and coastal infrastructure. Tailings standards still require competent implementation and enforcement. A safer dam still stores waste. A lower-water chemistry still has a material and energy chain.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 17

A strong chapter can open at Brumadinho without turning the disaster into spectacle. Start with the speed of the failure and the ordinary industrial setting it erased, then move backward into the system of tailings, water, monitoring, and decisions that existed before 12:28 p.m.

Do not write “the warning signs were obvious.” Hindsight makes ambiguous signals look cleaner than they were. The harder and more useful claim is that high-consequence systems need institutions capable of acting on uncertainty before certainty arrives.

From Brumadinho, widen to the physical reason tailings exist: ore bodies contain far more unwanted rock and finely ground material than saleable metal. Then move from waste to water, using Chile to show both the conflict and the engineering response. Seawater is a genuine improvement and a clean counterexample to fatalism; its energy requirements prevent the chapter from becoming a redemption story.

The handoff to Chapter 18 should come from distribution rather than another environmental inventory. The costs of a mine are borne in specific valleys, watersheds, towns, and balance sheets. The mineral revenue is distributed through a different set of institutions. Chapter 18 asks who captures that upside — and who is left holding the downside.
