# Chapter 12 — Recycling timing and policy boundary (2026)

## Why this note exists

Chapter 12 makes a useful claim that is easy to flatten into either recycling optimism or recycling cynicism: recycling is strategically important, but its contribution is constrained by the age of the installed stock, collection, chemistry, utilization, and policy. The most important variable is often not laboratory recovery efficiency but **when material actually returns**.

This note rechecks that boundary against authoritative sources and records the strongest counterevidence.

## 1. Feedstock arrives on a different clock from factory capacity

The IEA's *Recycling of Critical Minerals* finds that manufacturing scrap still accounts for about two-thirds of available battery-recycling feedstock in 2030. End-of-life EV and storage batteries become the largest source only from around 2035 and exceed 90% of available feedstock by 2050.

That lag is the core stock-and-flow constraint in the chapter. A battery placed into service is a mineral stock, but it is not recycling feedstock while it remains useful.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Recycling of Critical Minerals*, executive summary. https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary

## 2. Today's apparent overcapacity can become tomorrow's shortage

The same IEA analysis estimates that, if all announced projects arrive on schedule, global battery-recycling capacity in 2030 could be roughly **seven times** available feedstock. By 2040, however, available feedstock could exceed announced capacity by about **60%** as the EV fleet ages.

The regional picture is even more important than the global total. The IEA says China continues to show capacity in excess of domestic feedstock, while in Europe and the United States announced recycling capacity covers only about **30% of available feedstock by 2040**.

This supports a narrower argument than “recycling is overbuilt.” Capacity and waste mature on different schedules. A plant can be early in 2030 and scarce in 2040 without either observation being contradictory.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Recycling of Critical Minerals*, executive summary. https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary

## 3. Chemistry is a real counterweight to simple recycling economics

The IEA reports that LFP batteries represented around **40% of EV batteries deployed in 2023** and notes that their lower residual material value changes recycling economics. Avoiding nickel and cobalt can improve primary-material security while simultaneously reducing the commodity value available to pay for end-of-life recovery.

This is useful counterevidence to any assumption that a larger battery fleet automatically produces a more profitable recycling industry. The physical case for recovery and the private business case for recovery are not identical.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Recycling of Critical Minerals*, executive summary. https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary

## 4. Collection, not just metallurgical recovery, sets the ceiling

Under the IEA's central assumptions, recycled batteries could provide roughly **20–30% of lithium, nickel, and cobalt demand by 2050**. With higher collection rates that range rises to roughly 30–40%; with lower collection it falls to roughly 15–25%.

The policy implication is mundane but consequential: take-back systems, transport rules, ownership records, export rules, sorting, and collection economics can matter as much as impressive recovery percentages inside a plant.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Recycling of Critical Minerals*, executive summary. https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary

## 5. The EU is creating demand for secondary material by rule, not merely by spot-price economics

Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 requires, from **18 August 2031**, minimum recycled-content shares in covered industrial batteries over 2 kWh, EV batteries, and SLI batteries: **16% cobalt, 85% lead, 6% lithium, and 6% nickel**. It also sets end-2031 recovery targets of **95% for cobalt, copper, lead, and nickel** and **80% for lithium**.

That supports Chapter 12's argument that policy can alter the secondary-material market directly. It also needs a boundary: a mandate can create demand for recycled content, but it does not by itself prove that every recycling plant, process, or capacity announcement is economically sensible.

## Strongest counterargument

A reader could reasonably argue that Chapter 12 understates how quickly recycling can become strategically material. The IEA's own projections show that secondary supply can reach meaningful shares of lithium, nickel, and cobalt demand by mid-century, and stronger collection can push those shares materially higher. Policy can also improve weak private economics, as mature lead-acid systems demonstrate.

That counterargument should stay in the chapter. The stronger claim is not that recycling is too small to matter. It is that **recycling's strategic value grows with the accumulated stock and with the institutions that retrieve it**. During a rapid buildout, primary supply remains necessary because most of the new material is still in use. Later, the same installed base becomes a large above-ground resource.

## What would make the chapter's boundary false?

The timing argument should weaken if several things happen together:

- end-of-life batteries become available materially earlier than current fleet-age assumptions imply;
- collection rates rise rapidly enough that theoretical urban stocks reliably become actual feedstock;
- announced recycling capacity converts to operating capacity with high utilization despite changing chemistry;
- low-value chemistries such as LFP support durable recovery economics without sustained policy support or cross-subsidy; and
- recycled supply displaces primary mine demand substantially earlier than current IEA pathways imply.

If those conditions emerge, recycling would cease to be mainly a long-run hedge and become a near-term primary-supply substitute sooner than the chapter currently argues.

## Manuscript-use boundary

If this evidence is folded into Chapter 12, keep the distinction among three different quantities explicit: **material physically recoverable at a plant, material actually collected as feedstock, and recycled material as a share of total demand**. A high number in the first category does not establish a high number in the third.
