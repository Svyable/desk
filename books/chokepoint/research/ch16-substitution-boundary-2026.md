# Chapter 16 research note — substitution changes the chokepoint

Research checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 16, “The Substitution Race.”

## Editorial thesis

The strongest version of this chapter is not “engineers can invent their way out of mineral scarcity.” It is narrower and more useful: **product design can destroy demand for one constrained material surprisingly quickly, but substitution usually trades one materials system for another rather than abolishing material dependence.**

That distinction gives the chapter a clean answer to Chapter 15. China’s industrial advantage is partly built around the materials demanded by current designs. Engineers can attack that leverage from the demand side. But a successful substitute creates its own supply chain, manufacturing knowledge, performance trade-offs, and possible chokepoints.

## 1. LFP is already the proof that substitution can rewrite a mineral forecast

The IEA’s *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* says cobalt demand growth through 2040 has moderated because of the rapid rise of lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) batteries. That is a consequential real-world example: chemistry choice changed the demand outlook for a mineral that only a few years earlier was treated as one of the defining EV bottlenecks.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, “Outlook,” July 16, 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook

The IEA’s *Global EV Outlook 2026* shows how far LFP has moved from alternative chemistry to mass-market technology. In emerging-market and developing economies covered by the IEA, LFP batteries powered two-thirds of electric-car sales in 2025, twice their 2023 share. In stationary battery storage, the IEA reports LFP at roughly 90% of global deployments in 2025, up from well below half only five years earlier.

Sources:
- IEA, *Global EV Outlook 2026*, “Electric vehicle batteries.”
  https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries
- IEA, *Global Energy Review 2026*, “Technology: Battery storage.”
  https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-battery-storage

Editorial use: this is stronger than a laboratory anecdote because it demonstrates substitution at industrial scale. LFP removes nickel and cobalt from the cathode. It did not wait for those metals to disappear; cost, safety, cycle life, manufacturing scale, and acceptable performance changed the design choice.

## 2. The counterevidence is inside the same battery

LFP is not mineral-free. It still requires lithium and graphite, and its cathode supply chain requires purified phosphoric acid. The IEA’s 2025 critical-minerals outlook warned that China produced about 75% of the world’s purified phosphoric acid used for LFP batteries and that announced projects were insufficient to meet projected demand from around 2030.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025*, executive summary and “Beyond NMC batteries.”
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary

This is the chapter’s most important corrective. Substitution can reduce exposure to cobalt and nickel while increasing exposure elsewhere. The engineering victory is real; the geopolitical victory is conditional.

A useful sentence-level principle for the eventual chapter: **the periodic table does not disappear when the bill of materials changes.**

## 3. Sodium-ion is a live experiment in trading one dependency for another

The IEA says sodium-ion batteries entered a genuine scale-up phase by 2026. The first sodium-ion electric car appeared in China only in late 2023, while major producers including CATL and BYD are now scaling the chemistry. The technology has useful low-temperature performance and can reduce exposure to lithium price spikes.

But sodium-ion still loses to lithium-ion on energy density in many applications, limiting its competitiveness with LFP at current lithium prices. That makes it a particularly good case for this book: substitution is not simply a scientific question of whether a replacement exists. It is an economic question about what performance the customer will surrender, at what price, for what supply-chain advantage.

Source: IEA, *Global EV Outlook 2026*, “Electric vehicle batteries.”
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries

The upstream map also changes rather than vanishes. The IEA notes that sodium-ion can diversify some upstream inputs through soda ash, caustic soda, and biomass, but the downstream cell, cathode, and hard-carbon-anode chain remains heavily concentrated in China. High-purity manganese sulphate is another potential constraint for manganese-rich and sodium-ion chemistries; the IEA’s 2025 project pipeline covered only about 55% of expected 2035 demand.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary

## 4. Substitution potential varies radically by mineral and use

Do not let the chapter imply that every chokepoint can be designed away. The IEA’s 2026 risk assessment places gallium, magnet rare earths, yttrium, graphite, tungsten, tellurium, cobalt, and germanium among the materials most exposed to supply vulnerabilities. The agency specifically identifies limited substitution potential as part of the risk for several strategic minor minerals.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

This matters for the rare-earth-motor section. A rare-earth-free motor can be technically possible without being an interchangeable substitute for every high-performance permanent-magnet motor. Torque density, efficiency, package size, thermal behavior, control electronics, manufacturing cost, and application requirements determine whether the substitute is economically useful. The chapter should avoid treating a prototype or product announcement as proof that neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium demand can simply disappear.

Before using a specific automaker’s “rare-earth-free” motor announcement as a centerpiece, verify the exact motor architecture, production status, vehicle application, and whether the design eliminates all rare earths or only heavy rare earths. Marketing language in this area often compresses materially different claims.

## 5. The final-product economics make substitution politically interesting

The IEA’s 2026 outlook estimates that critical minerals account for roughly one-quarter of battery-cell costs but only about 3% of the price of an average EV. Rare earths represent around 40% of permanent-magnet cost but less than 1% of a vehicle’s value. Its illustrative calculation suggests that tripling rare-earth prices would add only about 0.1% to a car’s price, while tripling battery-material prices would raise final EV and storage-system prices by around 5%.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

This complicates the substitution story in a useful way. If a mineral is strategically dangerous but economically tiny in the final product, redesign may not be the cheapest response. Paying more for a diversified supplier, carrying inventory, or qualifying a second source can be cheaper than sacrificing performance to eliminate the material.

The best engineering answer to a chokepoint is therefore not automatically substitution. Sometimes redundancy wins.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest objection to a chapter celebrating substitution is that it can become a disguised cornucopian argument: whenever a mineral gets scarce, engineers will simply replace it.

The evidence does not support that universal claim.

LFP shows that substitution can move much faster than mine development when a technically mature alternative reaches scale. Sodium-ion shows that an alternative can progress rapidly while still carrying performance and manufacturing constraints. Strategic minor minerals show the opposite boundary: in some applications, substitutes are poor, costly, or unavailable. And even successful substitutions can move concentration downstream into a new chemistry dominated by the same country whose leverage the redesign was supposed to reduce.

The narrower claim is stronger: **demand is not fixed. Mineral forecasts are partly forecasts about engineering choices.** Those choices can materially alter which chokepoints matter, but they cannot guarantee that chokepoints disappear.

## What would make the chapter misleading?

- Treating LFP as a hypothetical future technology rather than a dominant chemistry in several major battery markets by 2025.
- Saying LFP eliminates “critical minerals”; it removes nickel and cobalt from the cathode but retains lithium, graphite, phosphorus inputs, copper, and other material dependencies.
- Describing sodium-ion as a drop-in replacement for all lithium-ion applications; current energy-density differences matter.
- Treating “rare-earth-free motor” as one uniform category without distinguishing architectures and performance requirements.
- Counting laboratory demonstrations or announced production as equivalent to high-volume qualified manufacturing.
- Assuming lower mineral intensity necessarily means lower geopolitical concentration.

## Narrative shape

Open with cobalt rather than a speculative invention. A few years ago, cobalt scarcity was routinely embedded in EV mineral forecasts. Then LFP gained enough share to change the forecast itself. The suspense is not whether chemistry can change; it already did.

From there, widen to the general mechanism: a mine changes supply, while an engineer can change demand. Then complicate the apparent escape. LFP’s success creates a phosphoric-acid and graphite supply chain. Sodium-ion removes lithium from the cell but introduces different materials and still depends on concentrated downstream manufacturing. Rare-earth-free motors confront performance requirements that chemistry-switch stories can obscure.

The chapter should end with a distinction rather than a slogan. Some chokepoints are worth routing around through redesign. Others are cheap enough in the final product, and difficult enough to substitute, that paying for redundancy is more rational. Mineral security is partly the art of knowing which is which.

That hands naturally into Chapter 17. Once the book has shown that engineers can change the material recipe, the next question is not only whether the substitute is available, but what environmental burdens its new recipe moves, reduces, or creates.
