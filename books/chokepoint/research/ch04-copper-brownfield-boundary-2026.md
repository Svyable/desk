# Chapter 4 research note — copper scarcity is a project-pipeline problem, not a geological-depletion story

Research checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 4, “Copper: The Original Chokepoint.”

## Claim under test

Chapter 4 is strongest when it avoids the easy claim that the world is simply “running out of copper.”

The more defensible argument is that copper supply can tighten even with a large geological resource base because the economically useful unit is not copper in the crust. It is an operating mine, an expansion, a usable concentrate stream, or refined metal available on the customer’s schedule.

That makes the constraint a project-pipeline problem: ore quality, capital intensity, project complexity, discovery rates, permitting, construction, operating performance and midstream capacity determine how quickly resources become supply.

## Current IEA evidence: the deficit estimate improved, but the structural constraints did not disappear

The IEA’s July 2026 *Global Critical Minerals Outlook* now estimates that expected copper supply from the current project pipeline could fall about 25% short of projected demand in 2035. The 2025 edition had put the implied gap at about 30%.

Source:
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

That revision is important counterevidence to any deterministic shortage narrative. Forecasts can improve when projects advance, especially in places such as the DRC and Zambia. Chapter 4 should therefore treat the 25% figure as a scenario output, not a prophecy.

The more durable evidence sits underneath the forecast. In March 2026, the IEA summarized four structural pressures on new copper supply:

- average global copper mine grade has fallen about 40% since 1991;
- average capital intensity for brownfield expansion has risen about 65% since 2020, approaching levels historically associated with greenfield projects;
- only about 5% of copper deposits discovered during the last 35 years were discovered in the most recent decade;
- typical discovery-to-production lead time remains around 17 years.

Source:
https://www.iea.org/commentaries/copper-prices-have-hit-record-highs-but-smelters-face-mounting-strategic-pressures

The useful editorial implication is not “copper is scarce.” It is that high prices have to work through increasingly difficult projects before they become new tonnes.

## Strongest counterexample: Escondida shows that mature mines can still surprise to the upside

BHP’s 2025 Form 20-F provides unusually clean counterevidence to a simple decline story.

Escondida, the world’s largest copper mine, achieved its highest production in 17 years in FY2025, up 16% year on year. BHP attributed the increase to record concentrator throughput, improved recoveries, a higher concentrator feed grade of 1.02% versus 0.88% the prior year, and first production from the Full SaL leaching project.

Primary source:
https://www.bhp.com/-/media/documents/investors/annual-reports/2025/bhpform20f2025.pdf

BHP’s operational review states the same result and records group copper production above 2 million tonnes for FY2025.

Source:
https://www.bhp.com/news/media-centre/releases/2025/07/bhp-operational-review-for-the-year-ended-30-june-2025

This matters because the fastest supply response is not always a new discovery becoming a new mine. Existing districts can add tonnes through better grades in a given phase of the mine plan, higher throughput, better recoveries, new leaching circuits, debottlenecking and brownfield expansion.

That is the strongest argument against treating declining average ore grade as destiny. A global average can worsen while an individual world-class mine improves output for operational reasons.

## But brownfield is not automatically cheap or easy

The same IEA evidence complicates the usual “brownfield will save us” response. The agency estimates that average capital intensity for copper brownfield expansion has increased about 65% since 2020 and is approaching greenfield-like levels.

That changes the chapter’s framing in a useful way. Brownfield projects can be faster because roads, power, processing plants, workforces and permits already exist, but they can still require very large amounts of capital to move deeper, process more rock, improve recovery or replace depleting production areas.

The relevant distinction is therefore not simply greenfield versus brownfield. It is how much technically credible, permitted, financeable capacity can reach production soon enough to matter.

## Current production base: Chile remains dominant without being a monopoly

The U.S. Geological Survey’s 2026 *Mineral Commodity Summaries* remains the best current baseline for mine output.

USGS reports roughly 23 million tonnes of world copper mine production in 2025 and about 5.3 million tonnes from Chile.

Primary source:
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/mcs2026

USGS separately describes Chile as the world’s leading mined-copper producer, with roughly one-quarter of world output in the most recent detailed country data.

Source:
https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/chile

This supports Chapter 4’s distinction from rare earths. Chile matters enormously, but copper mining is spread across a broader group of major producers. The chokepoint is therefore less “one country can turn off the tap” than “a very large existing system has to keep replacing and expanding tonnes across long, capital-intensive project cycles.”

## A second chokepoint is emerging downstream

Copper’s midstream deserves more attention than a mine-only account suggests.

The IEA reports that China accounted for more than 90% of the growth in global copper smelter output since 2005, raising its share of global smelting supply from about 15% to roughly half by 2025. At the same time, concentrate availability has become tight enough that annual treatment and refining charges settled at zero in January 2026, while spot charges had already turned negative.

Source:
https://www.iea.org/commentaries/copper-prices-have-hit-record-highs-but-smelters-face-mounting-strategic-pressures

This is valuable counterevidence to a chapter that might otherwise imply copper’s main constraint is mining. A world with enough nominal smelter capacity can still have too little concentrate, and a world with diversified mines can still develop a more concentrated refining stage.

The chokepoint can move.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest case against the chapter’s tight-supply thesis is not that copper demand will vanish. It is that forecasts systematically underweight adaptive response.

Higher prices can trigger brownfield investment, scrap collection, substitution toward aluminium in suitable applications, demand thrift, project acceleration and technological improvements in recovery. The 2035 IEA deficit estimate already narrowed from about 30% to 25% in one year as projects moved forward.

Escondida is the concrete warning against extrapolating decline. An old mine can post its strongest production in nearly two decades when grade, throughput, recovery and investment align.

Chapter 4 should therefore avoid treating declining average ore grade as a one-way production forecast.

## Falsification boundary

The chapter’s project-pipeline argument should weaken materially if several things happen together:

- the current project pipeline repeatedly reaches production faster than historical lead-time evidence would suggest;
- brownfield capital intensity falls materially while output rises;
- discovery rates recover enough to replenish the pipeline with large, high-quality deposits;
- recycling, efficiency and substitution reduce primary copper requirements faster than current scenarios assume;
- non-Chinese smelting remains commercially healthy enough to prevent further midstream concentration.

If those conditions emerge, copper would remain essential without remaining a durable chokepoint.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 4

The chapter already contains the right core distinction: copper is not a geological-exhaustion story.

The sharper version is this:

A resource estimate tells you how much copper may exist. A mine plan tells you how much copper can arrive, in what form, at what cost, and on what date.

The strongest narrative tension is between structural difficulty and operational adaptability. Falling average grades and rising capital intensity make the next tonne harder, but Escondida shows that mature mines are not passive wasting assets. Engineering, mine sequencing, recovery improvements and brownfield investment can still create meaningful upside.

That tension is more credible than either “we are running out” or “high prices will solve it.”
