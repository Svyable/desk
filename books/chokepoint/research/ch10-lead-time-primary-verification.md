# Chapter 10 — mine lead-time primary verification

Checked September 3, 2026.

## Claim under test

Chapter 10 argues that mining supply responds slowly to price and policy signals because discovery, exploration, feasibility work, permitting, financing, construction, and commissioning can span well over a decade. The existing research brief identifies this as a central mechanism but flags several S&P Global and IEA sources as not directly fetched. This note records direct reads of the two strongest institutional sources and narrows what they establish.

## Directly verified evidence

S&P Global Market Intelligence's June 6, 2023 study examined 127 precious- and base-metals mines that began production between 2002 and 2023 and were discovered from 1980 onward. It reported an average discovery-to-commercial-production lead time of 15.7 years, with a range of six to 32 years. The sample was not comprehensive; S&P excluded operations lacking sufficient milestone detail and satellite operations using existing infrastructure.

Source: Paul Manalo, S&P Global Market Intelligence, “Discovery to production averages 15.7 years for 127 mines,” June 6, 2023.
https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/discovery-to-production-averages-15-7-years-for-127-mines

S&P's April 10, 2024 update reported that mines entering production in 2020–2023 averaged 17.9 years from discovery to startup, compared with 12.7 years for mines entering production in 2005–2009. S&P attributes the increase to longer exploration, permitting and studies and to a longer interval between feasibility and construction, including time spent obtaining financing and construction permits.

Source: Paul Manalo, S&P Global Market Intelligence, “Average lead time almost 18 years for mines started in 2020–23,” April 10, 2024.
https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/average-lead-time-almost-18-years-for-mines-started-in-2020-23

S&P's July 8, 2026 update expands the study to 232 assets. For 203 operating mines discovered and brought into production between 1990 and 2025, the average lead time is 14 years. Adding 29 nonoperating mines that have completed feasibility studies and for which S&P estimated startup dates raises the combined average to 16 years. The nonoperating subset reaches nearly 30 years, with permitting issues or revocations identified as the primary cause of startup delays for mines scheduled for 2026 and beyond.

Source: Paul Manalo, S&P Global Market Intelligence, “From Discovery to Delay: Mine Permitting Stretches Project Timelines,” July 8, 2026.
https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/2026/07/mine-permitting-delays-discovery-to-production-timeline

The International Energy Agency independently reaches a similar historical order of magnitude. Its analysis of major mines that came online between 2010 and 2019 reports more than 16 years on average from discovery to first production: more than 12 years for exploration and feasibility work and another four to five years for construction. The IEA's policy implication is conditional rather than deterministic: if firms wait for visible deficits before committing new projects, long lead times can prolong tightness and price volatility.

Source: International Energy Agency, *The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions*, “Reliable supply of minerals.”
https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/reliable-supply-of-minerals

## Boundaries and counterevidence

These figures do not mean every mine takes 16–18 years. S&P's 2023 sample ranged from six to 32 years, and its own discussion attributes variation to commodity, deposit type, geography, regulatory environment, financing, ownership changes, infrastructure, and community opposition. A chapter sentence such as “a new mine takes 18 years” would turn an observed sample average into a physical law.

The 2026 S&P “nearly 30 years” figure needs especially careful wording. It is not the observed average for mines that have already entered production. It describes a subset of nonoperating projects that have completed feasibility studies, using estimated startup dates where owners did not provide guidance. It is useful evidence that the current pipeline contains severely delayed projects, not proof that every future mine will take three decades.

Nor should permitting carry all causal weight. S&P identifies permitting as a major current source of delay, especially in the 2026 pipeline, but earlier studies also identify exploration, feasibility work, financing, ownership changes, construction complexity, infrastructure, and local opposition. The book should resist turning a multi-stage capital-cycle problem into a single political claim about regulation.

Fast counterexamples matter. The existing chapter brief already identifies Kathleen Valley lithium in Western Australia as a roughly six-year discovery-to-production case and Indonesia's rapid nickel-processing buildout as a different kind of supply response. Those examples should remain in the manuscript because they show that the slow-capital-cycle claim is a distribution, not a destiny.

## Editorial implication

The strongest formulation for Chapter 10 is: multiple independent industry and institutional datasets put typical discovery-to-production timelines for major mines in the mid-teens, while recent S&P cohorts and delayed feasibility-stage projects show that the tail can be much longer. That lag makes mining supply unusually slow to answer a sudden shortage, but policy, financing, geology, infrastructure, and project type can compress or extend it materially.

This supports the chapter's mechanism without converting an average into a universal rule. It also gives the chapter a clean way to preserve its countercase: high prices can summon supply, but the response time differs sharply across projects and stages of the value chain.
