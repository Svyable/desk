# Chapter 13 — Deep-sea legal boundary, 2026

## Claim under test

Chapter 13 argues that polymetallic nodules are not yet commercial supply merely because the resource is physically accessible and politically salient. In 2026, the strongest evidence for that claim is institutional: the United States accelerated an existing domestic licensing path while the International Seabed Authority continued negotiating rules for commercial exploitation in the international seabed Area. Neither path had yet produced an operating commercial nodule mine.

The chapter should preserve a strict distinction among **application, exploration authorization, environmental review, commercial recovery permit, and operating mine**. Collapsing those stages turns a live regulatory process into a false statement of commercial reality.

## What changed in the United States

NOAA finalized revisions to the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act regulations on January 21, 2026. The rule permits a qualified applicant to submit a consolidated application for an exploration license and a commercial recovery permit, allowing portions of those reviews to proceed together.

Source: NOAA, “Deep Seabed Hard Minerals Mining.”
https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/deep-seabed-mineral-resources/deep-seabed-mining/

The important boundary is what the rule did **not** do. It did not itself grant a commercial recovery permit, waive the statutory eligibility requirements, or convert an application into permission to begin commercial recovery.

NOAA’s current program page lists no existing DSHMRA commercial recovery permits. It does list The Metals Company USA’s consolidated exploration-license/commercial-recovery-permit application, publicly noticed on August 19, 2026, with a public comment period running through October 19, 2026.

That makes the manuscript’s formulation defensible as of September 2026: the application is real, but the commercial permit is not yet an issued authorization.

## The multilateral route remains unfinished

The International Seabed Authority reported on March 31, 2026 that, during Part I of its 31st Session, Council members discussed 29 of 32 outstanding issues in the draft exploitation regulations and reached agreement in principle on several of them, including an Equalisation Mechanism and a Compliance Committee.

Source: International Seabed Authority, “The Council of the International Seabed Authority advanced negotiations on the Mining Code,” March 31, 2026.
https://isa.org.jm/news/the-council-of-the-international-seabed-authority-advanced-negotiations-on-the-mining-code/

The ISA’s own 2026 media guidance states that completing the Mining Code remains a prerequisite, in the prevailing view of Council members, for future commercial activities in the Area.

Source: International Seabed Authority, “Q&A on the ISA.”
https://isa.org.jm/faq-for-media/

That does not mean the regime is static. It means the correct description is **unfinished but advancing**, not either “commercial mining has been approved” or “the ISA process is frozen.”

## The 2026 litigation is evidence of institutional tension, not commercial approval

Nauru Ocean Resources Inc. and Tonga Offshore Mining Ltd. instituted proceedings against the International Seabed Authority before the Seabed Disputes Chamber of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea in June 2026. The Chamber heard requests for provisional measures in July and delivered orders on July 18, 2026.

Sources:
- ITLOS Case 34, Nauru Ocean Resources Inc. v. International Seabed Authority.
  https://www.itlos.org/en/main/cases/list-of-cases/case-concerning-an-inquiry-by-the-international-seabed-authority-nauru-ocean-resources-inc-v-international-seabed-authority/
- ITLOS Case 35, Tonga Offshore Mining Ltd. v. International Seabed Authority.
  https://www.itlos.org/en/main/cases/list-of-cases/case-concerning-an-inquiry-by-the-international-seabed-authority-tonga-offshore-mining-ltd-v-international-seabed-authority/

The evidentiary use of these cases should remain narrow. Their significance is that the dispute over the ISA’s investigative and supervisory authority has become concrete enough to reach the Seabed Disputes Chamber while the exploitation code itself remains incomplete. The provisional-measures proceedings are not evidence that a contractor obtained permission to begin commercial mining.

## Strongest counterargument

The obvious counterargument is that regulation can move faster than the chapter’s “potential supply versus actual supply” framing suggests.

The U.S. consolidated-review rule, multiple 2026 exploration applications, The Metals Company USA’s consolidated application, and continued progress at the ISA all show that legal and administrative bottlenecks are not immutable. A determined government can shorten sequencing, run reviews in parallel, and create a clearer path for applicants. If permits, financing, environmental review, processing arrangements, and collector operations begin to converge, deep-sea supply could arrive faster than a terrestrial greenfield mine.

That counterargument should stay in the book. The stronger claim is not that deep-sea mining is permanently trapped in paperwork. It is that **legal acceleration does not erase the remaining physical, ecological, financing, processing, and qualification steps between a resource and dependable supply**.

## What would make the chapter’s boundary false?

The chapter should be revised if any of the following occur:

- NOAA issues a commercial recovery permit under DSHMRA and the holder advances into genuine commercial recovery rather than testing or exploration;
- the ISA adopts a complete exploitation code and approves a plan of work for exploitation that progresses into commercial operations;
- a commercial nodule project demonstrates sustained collection, lifting, processing, product qualification, and sales at material scale;
- subsequent ecological evidence materially narrows the uncertainty around long-duration benthic disturbance or shows recovery substantially faster than the chapter implies;
- project economics show that the legal and environmental stages are no longer material schedule constraints relative to engineering and processing.

## Editorial boundary

The chapter is strongest when it treats deep-sea mining as a test of the book’s recurring resource-versus-supply distinction, not as a referendum on whether mining the seabed is inherently good or bad. The most durable line is procedural and economic: a nodule on a map is not yet a dependable metal stream, and moving the legal path faster can shift the chokepoint without eliminating it.
