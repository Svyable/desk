# Chapter 6 research — cobalt quotas move the chokepoint to the border

## Claim under test

Chapter 6 argues that the DRC's cobalt leverage does not require shutting mines. Because much Congolese cobalt is produced alongside copper, the state can constrain exports while operators keep copper circuits running and let cobalt accumulate in inventory. The chokepoint can sit at customs rather than at the pithead.

## Primary-source check

Glencore's 29 January 2026 full-year production report makes this mechanism unusually concrete. It says the DRC's cobalt quota system applies until at least the end of 2027. At KCC and Mutanda, cobalt produced above allocated export quotas is stored in-country or held as work-in-process; Glencore said those operations had enough inventory to satisfy near-term quotas and therefore planned to prioritize copper production where commercially sensible.

Glencore reported 36,100 tonnes of own-sourced cobalt production in 2025, down 5% year over year, and explicitly attributed part of that reduction to prioritizing copper during the export restrictions. Its 2026 allocation, including unused 2025 carryover, was 22,800 tonnes of cobalt exports across KCC and Mutanda. The company said uncertainty around the quota regime was high enough that it did not provide full-year 2026 cobalt production guidance.

Source: Glencore, "Full Year 2025 Production Report," 29 January 2026.
https://www.glencore.com/media-and-insights/news/full-year-2025-production-report

CMOC provides the counterintuitive other half of the picture. Despite the restriction regime, CMOC reported record 2025 cobalt production of about 117,500 tonnes while also producing about 741,100 tonnes of copper at its DRC operations. That is useful evidence against a simplistic equation of export restraint with mine shutdown.

## Market effect and counterevidence

The IEA's *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* says cobalt prices rose around 130% between January 2025 and April 2026, largely because of DRC export restrictions. It also says the DRC quota changed its medium-term supply outlook enough to widen the projected cobalt supply gap for 2035 from just over 15% to more than 25% in the base case.

But the same IEA outlook weakens any claim that producer leverage is permanent. Cobalt-demand growth to 2040 has moderated because lithium iron phosphate batteries expanded faster than older forecasts expected. The quota therefore demonstrates real state leverage while also increasing the incentive to economize on cobalt, recycle it, or choose chemistries that avoid it.

## Strongest defensible formulation

The DRC's experiment is not simply "a dominant miner withheld supply." The more precise mechanism is that a dominant producing country restricted the rate at which cobalt could leave while large copper-cobalt operations continued producing. That converted unsold cobalt into inventory, supported prices, and altered the expected global supply balance without requiring the copper industry to stop.

The strongest counterargument is technological response. The more successful the quota is at making cobalt expensive, the more valuable cobalt-thrifty or cobalt-free battery chemistries become. Producer power is therefore real but endogenous: exercising it changes the incentives that determine how much power remains later.

## What would make this false?

This framing would need revision if quotas cease to bind in practice, inventories overwhelm the intended scarcity, alternative supply expands quickly enough to neutralize the policy, or battery-chemistry substitution reduces cobalt demand faster than the quota constrains exports.
