- The petrodollar arrangement: in 1974, one year after the embargo, the US and Saudi Arabia struck an agreement (details reportedly kept partly secret until documents surfaced around 2016) under which Saudi Arabia would price its oil sales exclusively in US dollars and recycle petroleum surpluses into US Treasury securities, in exchange for US security guarantees; Treasury Secretary William Simon made a July 1974 mission to Jeddah to negotiate the financial side, following a broader June 1974 US-Saudi cooperation agreement. - The second oil shock (1979): triggered by the Iranian Revolution (1978–79) and the collapse of the Shah's government, global oil supply fell by only about 4% in volume terms, but panic buying by purchasers rebuilding inventories more than doubled the effective shortage's price impact; oil prices more than doubled over 12 months to about $39.50/barrel (roughly $175/barrel in 2025 dollars). A second leg came in 1980 with the Iran-Iraq War, which further cut Iranian and Iraqi output and helped trigger a global recession. Source: search synthesis, Wikipedia "1979 oil crisis"; Federal Reserve History, "Oil Shock of 1978-79," federalreservehistory.org/essays/oil-shock-of-1978-79 (could not fetch directly — recommend as primary citation); Middle East Institute, "The 1979 'Oil Shock': Legacy, Lessons, and Lasting Reverberations," mei.edu/publications/1979-oil-shock-legacy-lessons-and-lasting-reverberations. Use: shows that even the 1970s' second great oil shock was driven as much by market psychology/panic buying as by the actual physical shortfall — a genuinely useful analytical point for the "how do commodity panics work" through-line of the chapter, and relevant to mineral-market panic-buying dynamics (e.g., 2021-2022 lithium/nickel price spikes).

- Consumer-level 1970s scene material: gas lines, odd-even rationing days, prices roughly doubling at the pump (34 cents to 84 cents/gallon) — classic "oil shock as national experience" texture, sourceable in more depth from ADST's oral-history-based piece (adst.org) which likely contains named diplomat recollections worth pulling directly.
- China's role in mineral processing is repeatedly described by analysts (CSIS event commentary) as "hard for any country to accept living under a cartel of one" — i.e., unlike OPEC's multilateral cartel structure (multiple sovereign members coordinating output), China's dominance in mineral refining is a single-country phenomenon achieved through decades of industrial policy rather than an explicit multilateral cartel agreement. This is a sharper, more citable formulation of "there is no OPEC for minerals — there's something structurally different" than most popular treatments offer. ### Counterevidence / complications — where the oil analogy breaks down

- **Stock vs. flow (the core economic distinction):** Oil is consumed contemporaneously as a flow input — burned to release energy — so an oil-price shock acts like an immediate adverse cost/productivity shock across the whole economy. Critical minerals, by contrast, are embedded once into long-lived capital goods (an EV battery, a wind turbine, a data-center's copper wiring) and then keep providing services for years; a mineral-price shock raises the cost of building new capital without touching the productivity of capital already in place, so its effects propagate more slowly and less severely. Source: MIT Center for Energy and Environmental Policy Research (CEEPR) Working Paper WP 2026-01, "The Differential Impacts of Critical Mineral Prices and Oil Prices on the Economy," by Adrien Concordel, Phuong Ho, and Christopher R. Knittel, posted Feb. 18, 2026 (revised March 20, 2026), https://ceepr.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/MIT-CEEPR-WP-2026-01.pdf (SSRN mirror: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6246344). Finding: both shock types reduce output and welfare, but oil-price increases are "systematically more contractionary" than equivalent mineral-price increases; mineral shocks instead generate larger adjustments in investment, capital stock, and external borrowing, with smaller, more gradual welfare losses — and in capital-rich economies can even slightly raise long-run employment. Use: this is the single best, most rigorous, most recent academic source for the book's central "stock vs. flow" analytical claim — worth reading the full working paper directly, not just the abstract, before drafting. This is a primary, citable, dated academic source exactly matching the assignment's request.
- **Concentration is in processing, not (only) reserves — and it is more extreme than OPEC's peak:** OPEC countries today produce only about 35% of world oil, and even at OPEC's 1970s peak its market share never approached the concentration China now holds in mineral refining. China refines roughly three-quarters of the world's cobalt, about 60% of lithium, about 90% of rare earths, and about 95% of battery-grade graphite. For rare earth elements specifically, China's share of the separation/refining stage (as opposed to mining) is around 91%, with Malaysia a distant second. Notably, China processes minerals it barely mines — e.g., it refines about 76% of global cobalt despite the metal coming mostly from the DRC — demonstrating that its dominance is a processing/refining chokepoint rather than a resource-endowment chokepoint. Source: search synthesis attributed to CSIS analysis (csis.org, various 2025-26 critical-minerals pieces) and IEA, "Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 / 2026," Executive Summary and "Overview of outlook for key minerals," https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/overview-of-outlook-for-key-minerals ; https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary ; also IEA, "With new export controls on critical minerals, supply concentration risks become reality," https://www.iea.org/commentaries/with-new-export-controls-on-critical-minerals-supply-concentration-risks-become-reality. Use: precise, comparative figure (35% oil vs. 90%+ minerals processing) is a strong, checkable data point for the "the analogy actually understates the concentration risk in one dimension" argument. Limitation: pull the exact IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025/2026 PDF directly for exact percentages before printing — search snippets can round or conflate mining vs. refining shares, and the book's own research framework (see README.md, Evidence Lane II) explicitly warns against conflating the two.
- **Average top-3 refiner concentration trend:** across copper, lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite, and rare earths, the average market share held by the top three refining nations for each mineral rose from about 82% in 2020 to about 86% in 2024, with nearly all incremental supply growth concentrated in a single top supplier per mineral (Indonesia for nickel, China for essentially everything else). Source: IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook (2025/2026 vintage), same URLs as above. Use: shows the concentration trend is worsening, not easing, as of the most recent IEA data — a fact that should be checked against the report's actual release date and dataset vintage.
- **No cartel exists, and geology/geography argue against one forming easily:** unlike oil, where OPEC could coordinate output across a relatively homogeneous product pumped by member governments, critical-mineral deposits are geologically heterogeneous, geographically scattered across politically fragile and disparate jurisdictions, and extraction/processing for each mineral involves different technical complexities — structural obstacles to organizing anything like a multilateral producers' cartel. A 2020s academic/policy debate ("Crisis to Cooperation: What Can Mineral Exporters Learn from OPEC?", Kleinman Center for Energy Policy, University of Pennsylvania, kleinmanenergy.upenn.edu/research/publications/crisis-to-cooperation-what-can-mineral-exporters-learn-from-opec/) explicitly examines whether mineral exporters (e.g., a hypothetical DRC-Zambia-Chile-Indonesia-Peru grouping) could organize OPEC-style coordination, and finds structural reasons this is harder than it was for oil. Use: directly on-point academic/think-tank source for the "no OPEC for minerals (yet)" argument — recommend reading the full Kleinman Center piece directly.
- **Recycling potential differs sharply from oil, which cannot be recycled at all:** oil is combusted once and is permanently consumed; battery/EV-relevant metals (lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper) can in principle be recovered and reused indefinitely. A Stanford University study found recycling can produce roughly 80% fewer emissions than fresh extraction. But actual current recycling rates are low: only about 3% of lithium-ion batteries are currently recycled, with lithium recovery rates below 1%, versus a much more mature copper recycling system where roughly 30-45% of global copper supply already comes from secondary/scrap sources (global end-of-life recycling rate for copper is cited around 40%, with over a third of annual global copper demand met by scrap). Source: search synthesis, Earthworks ("Battery Circularity: The Key to an Ethical and Sustainable EV Transition," earthworks.org/blog/battery-circularity-the-key-to-an-ethical-and-sustainable-ev-transition/); International Copper Association ("Circular Copper: Building a Culture of Sustainability," internationalcopper.org); Copper Development Association (copper.org/environment/lifecycle/g_recycl.html). Use: this is a genuinely important complication for the book's "recycling as a hedge" argument (see README Evidence Lane VII) — copper's recycling infrastructure is mature and already meaningful, while battery-metal recycling is nascent despite theoretical promise, so "recycling" is not one uniform story across the different minerals in the book's scope. Limitation: recycling-rate figures vary significantly by source methodology (end-of-life rate vs. recycled content vs. old-scrap ratio); flag this imprecision explicitly if used quantitatively.
- **Daniel Yergin's framing (a major, citable trade-nonfiction precedent for this exact book's territory):** in "The New Map: Energy, Climate, and the Clash of Nations" (Penguin Press, 2020), Yergin argues the energy transition moves the world from "big oil" to "big shovels" — noting an EV uses roughly six times more minerals than a conventional car and a wind turbine roughly nine times more minerals than a gas plant, with projected demand growth for lithium as high as 4,300% and for cobalt/nickel as high as 2,500% by some scenarios; he cites concentration figures — the top three lithium producers controlling over 80% of supply, China controlling 60% of rare earths output, and the DRC controlling 70% of cobalt needed for EV batteries. Source: search synthesis of quotes/summaries via Goodreads, Medium ("Top Quotes: The New Map"), and MasterResource.org's quotation compilation of the book; danielyergin.com/books/thenewmap for the publisher page. Use: Yergin is the most prominent living oil/energy historian (Pulitzer Prize for "The Prize") writing directly in this book's comparative-history space — his own explicit oil-to-minerals analogy-making is worth engaging with directly (and possibly interviewing him, or at minimum reading the book in full) rather than only using secondhand quote compilations. ## CHAPTER 18 — "Who Gets Rich, Who Gets Left"

### Key facts and sources

- **Foundational paper:** Jeffrey D. Sachs and Andrew M. Warner, "Natural Resource Abundance and Economic Growth," NBER Working Paper No. 5398, published 1995 (also circulated via Harvard Institute for International Development). Core finding: economies with a high ratio of natural resource exports to GDP in 1971 tended to have low growth rates over 1971-1989, and this negative relationship held even after controlling for initial per-capita income, trade policy openness, government efficiency, and investment rates. Source: NBER, https://www.nber.org/papers/w5398 ; SSRN mirror, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=225459. Use: the canonical starting citation for the resource curse literature — cite the 1995 NBER working paper number directly.
- **Sachs and Warner's follow-up/synthesis paper:** "The Curse of Natural Resources," European Economic Review, vol. 45 (2001), pp. 827-838 (also titled in some listings "Natural Resources and Economic Development: The Curse of Natural Resources"). It summarizes and extends the earlier findings; a full-text version is hosted by Columbia University's Earth Institute (Sachs's home institution): https://www.earth.columbia.edu/sitefiles/file/about/director/pubs/EuroEconReview2001.pdf ; also at jeffsachs.org, https://www.jeffsachs.org/journal-articles/7y84hgmwg29z434ygxd55yzhz5s8mb. Use: this is the more polished, peer-reviewed journal version of the thesis and is likely the more citable of the two Sachs-Warner papers for a trade book footnote. Note: resource-abundant economies were found to be systematically "high-price economies" that tended to miss out on export-led manufacturing growth — an early statement of the Dutch-disease mechanism in growth-regression terms.
- **The term "resource curse" itself predates Sachs-Warner:** it was coined by geographer/economist Richard M. Auty in his 1993 book "Sustaining Development in Mineral Economies: The Resource Curse Thesis" (Routledge, London/New York). Source: search synthesis (Wikipedia "Resource curse"; multiple book-review citations via EconPapers/RePEc, e.g., https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jrpoli:v:20:y:1994:i:1:p:77-78); publisher page https://www.routledge.com/Sustaining-Development-in-Mineral-Economies-The-Resource-Curse-Thesis/Auty/p/book/9780415094825. Use: correct attribution — Auty coined the term two years before Sachs-Warner's quantitative treatment made it famous; the book should credit Auty by name rather than implying Sachs-Warner originated the phrase.
- **Institutions-matter counter-model (a major complication/refinement, not a flat rebuttal):** Halvor Mehlum, Karl Moene, and Ragnar Torvik, "Institutions and the Resource Curse," The Economic Journal, vol. 116, issue 508 (2006), pp. 1-20 (a closely related version, "Cursed by Resources or Institutions?," appeared in The World Economy, vol. 29, issue 8 (2006), pp. 1117-1131). Core finding: resource-rich countries split into growth winners and growth losers depending on institutional quality — more resources depress income where institutions are "grabber-friendly" (weak property rights, rent-seeking-permissive) but raise income where institutions are "producer-friendly." The authors explicitly note this contrasts with Sachs and Warner's own claim that institutions did not mediate the effect. Source: Economic Journal DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2006.01045.x, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1468-0297.2006.01045.x ; also summarized by the Peace Research Institute Oslo (PRIO), https://www.prio.org/publications/3514. Use: this is the single most important "complication" paper for Chapter 18 — it reframes the resource curse from a deterministic economic law into an institutionally contingent outcome, which is exactly the frame the book needs to explain Chile/Botswana/Norway as exceptions rather than anomalies.
- **Direct empirical challenge to Sachs-Warner's methodology and conclusion:** Christa N. Brunnschweiler and Erwin H. Bulte, "Linking Natural Resources to Slow Growth and More Conflict," Science, vol. 320, issue 5876 (2008), pp. 616-617 (DOI 10.1126/science.1154539). The authors (ETH Zurich / Wageningen University) argue that Sachs-Warner's resource-export-to-GDP ratio is a poor, endogenous proxy for actual resource abundance and that using better measures of subsoil mineral wealth weakens or reverses the "curse" finding; they stress institutional quality as the real mediating variable. Source: https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.1154539 ; Tilburg University research portal summary, https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/are-resource-rich-countries-cursed-linking-natural-resources-to-s. Use: a peer-reviewed Science-journal-level methodological critique — strong counterevidence citation.
- **A second, sharper rebuttal:** Michael Alexeev and Robert Conrad, "The Elusive Curse of Oil," The Review of Economics and Statistics, vol. 91, issue 3 (2009), pp. 586-598. Core finding: contrary to the prevailing "curse" literature, the authors find that large oil and mineral endowments have had, on balance, a *positive* effect on long-run economic growth, and dispute claims that resource wealth damages institutional quality, attributing much of the "curse" finding in prior literature to methodological/data misinterpretation. Source: MIT Press / Review of Economics and Statistics, https://direct.mit.edu/rest/article/91/3/586/57795/The-Elusive-Curse-of-Oil. Use: the strongest single "the resource curse is overstated/an artifact of bad measurement" citation found in this research pass — essential counterevidence to include so the chapter isn't one-sided.
- **The definitive academic survey of the whole debate:** Frederick van der Ploeg, "Natural Resources: Curse or Blessing?," Journal of Economic Literature, vol. 49, no. 2 (June 2011), pp. 366-420 (DOI 10.1257/jel.49.2.366). This survey concludes the evidence supports "either outcome is possible" — resource wealth appreciates the real exchange rate and can cause deindustrialization and weak growth, but these effects are markedly worse specifically in countries that are volatile, have weak rule of law, high corruption, non-democratic/presidential political systems, and underdeveloped financial systems. Source: American Economic Association, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjel.49.2.366 ; SSRN, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1640462. Use: this is the best single "if you can only cite one survey article" source for Chapter 18 — a top-journal literature review as of 2011 synthesizing everything above it, written by one of the field's leading scholars (Oxford/Amsterdam).
- **The Dutch disease mechanism, formally:** W. Max Corden and J. Peter Neary, 1982 (the canonical formalization — commonly cited as Corden & Neary, "Booming Sector and De-Industrialisation in a Small Open Economy," Economic Journal, 1982). The model splits the economy into three sectors: the booming resource sector, the "lagging" non-resource tradable sector (manufacturing/agriculture), and the non-tradable sector (services, construction). Two mechanisms drive de-industrialization: (1) the "resource movement effect" — labor and capital shift directly from manufacturing into the booming resource sector; (2) the "spending effect" — resource-boom income raises demand for non-tradable goods, whose prices rise domestically (since tradable-goods prices are fixed by world markets and can't rise), which amounts to a real exchange rate appreciation that makes the non-resource tradable sector internationally uncompetitive. Source: search synthesis; explained secondarily in IMF Working Paper WP/10/271, "When and Why Worry About Real Exchange Rate Appreciation?," https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2010/wp10271.pdf ; CEPR VoxEU, "Dealing with Dutch disease," https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/dealing-dutch-disease. Use: this is the standard, textbook-citable mechanical explanation of Dutch disease the book needs — recommend citing the original 1982 Economic Journal article directly (locate exact title/page numbers from a library database) alongside the IMF/CEPR secondary expositions for accessibility.

### Narrative material (case studies)

**Democratic Republic of Congo — cobalt**
- The DRC supplies roughly 70% of the world's mined cobalt. Cobalt has accounted for roughly 9% of DRC GDP on average from 2013-2018 (rising to about 10% in 2018). Despite this, the DRC ranks as the world's ninth-poorest country by per-capita GDP; nearly three-quarters of the population lives below the international poverty line of $2.15/day (a more recent 2025-vintage figure cited poverty at 81.1% using a $3/day PPP threshold). 2025 nominal GDP per capita: about $742; PPP-adjusted: about $1,880. Source: World Bank Group DRC country page, https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/drc ; World Bank, "Cobalt in the Democratic Republic of Congo Market Analysis" PDF, https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099500001312236438/pdf/P1723770a0f570093092050c1bddd6a29df.pdf ; corroborating figures via search synthesis. - Roughly 200,000 artisanal miners work directly in DRC cobalt deposits, with about 1 million people economically dependent on artisanal mining; the informal artisanal sector produces about 20% of DRC's total cobalt output. Average daily income for copper/cobalt miners is about $7.65/day; about 40% of miners earn less than the Congolese legal daily minimum wage of $4.20. Source: search synthesis, likely traceable to Delve Database (delvedatabase.org — a World Bank/Pact-affiliated artisanal-mining data initiative) and/or Harvard School of Public Health field reporting (hsph.harvard.edu/field-education-and-practice/news/student-field-post-health-in-artisanal-miners-communities-the-harsh-realities-of-cobalt-mining-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo/). Use: concrete, human-scale wage data for the chapter's ground-level scenes.
- Recent market volatility as live illustration of resource dependence: cobalt prices collapsed roughly 60% during 2023, falling from a post-pandemic peak above $77,000/metric ton to around $22,000-30,000/mt by 2024-2025 (a nine-year low), driven by oversupply as DRC and Indonesian output kept rising even as EV demand growth slowed and battery chemistries shifted toward less cobalt-intensive formulations. In response, the DRC imposed a cobalt export ban in February 2025, lifted it in October 2025 but replaced it with an export quota system meant to stabilize prices and reserve some output for domestic value-added projects; prices reportedly more than doubled, reaching around $57,000/mt by December (2025). Source: search synthesis of CarbonCredits.com "Cobalt Crash: Why Prices Hit a 7-Year Low and What's Next"; OilPrice.com, "DR Congo's Cobalt Miners Pivot To Copper Amid Price Crash" and "DRC Cobalt Export Halt Slams EV Industry"; Semafor, "DR Congo's cobalt quotas force miners to stockpile, pivot, or pause," May 4, 2026, https://www.semafor.com/article/05/04/2026/dr-congos-cobalt-quotas-force-miners-to-stockpile-pivot-or-pause ; dialogue.earth, "What does a changing cobalt market mean for the DRC?" Use: this is a live, unfolding case of a resource-dependent state attempting exactly the kind of supply-management intervention OPEC pioneered for oil — the DRC's 2025 cobalt export ban/quota is arguably the closest real-world thing to a mineral-OPEC move currently in progress, and is a strong, current, on-point connective tissue between Chapters 3 and 18. Recommend deeper reporting specifically on this DRC quota policy as it is very recent and still evolving.
- Ongoing conflict linkage: UN reporting and analysts (per Global Witness, "How race for critical minerals fuels conflict and inequality," https://globalwitness.org/en/campaigns/transition-minerals/the-critical-minerals-scramble-how-the-race-for-resources-is-fuelling-conflict-and-inequality/) suggest the M23 insurgency's Rwanda-linked backing is motivated partly by access to DRC's cobalt/mineral reserves; the UN and humanitarian agencies estimated over 25 million people in DRC in need of emergency humanitarian assistance in 2025. **Zambia — copper**
- Copper has historically driven the Zambian economy since colonial-era mining began in the 1920s; production peaked at 769,000 tons in 1969 (over 62,000 direct jobs) before the industry was nationalized in 1973 and remained state-owned for about 24 years, during which output declined steadily to a low of 250,000 tons around 2000. Privatization under President Frederick Chiluba in the early-to-mid 1990s, plus over $12 billion in subsequent investment, drove production back up to a peak of 763,000 tons in 2013 (about 90,000 direct jobs). Source: search synthesis, likely traceable to SciELO South Africa, "Copper mining in Zambia – history and future," https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S2225-62532016000600003 ; Wikipedia "Mining in Zambia." Use: a clean before/after nationalization-privatization arc, useful as a contrast case to Chile's copper nationalization (below) — same commodity, different institutional trajectory.
- Currently, copper accounts for about 70% of Zambia's total export earnings; in 2022, mining was about 72% of exports and 44% of government revenue. Source: search synthesis; African Arguments, "Zambia: Who will benefit from the global copper rush?," Feb. 2025, https://africanarguments.org/2025/02/zambia-who-will-benefit-from-the-global-copper-rush/ ; PERI (Political Economy Research Institute, UMass Amherst) Working Paper 601, "The State, Corporations, or the People: Who Benefits from Mining in Zambia?," https://peri.umass.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/WP601.pdf. Use: current-day dependence figures for framing Zambia as an active, ongoing (not just historical) resource-dependence case.
- Zambia's copper dependence has been explicitly linked in the academic literature to Dutch-disease dynamics — the AERC (African Economic Research Consortium) working paper "Copper Price and Exchange Rate Dynamics in Zambia" studies exactly this mechanism (https://aercafrica.org/old-website/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Research-Paper-364.pdf). Use: a directly on-topic, Africa-based academic source specifically modeling Dutch disease via Zambia's kwacha/copper-price relationship — worth reading in full for the chapter's Dutch-disease case study.

**Bolivia — Potosí silver (historical) and lithium (present)**
- Cerro Rico ("Rich Mountain") at Potosí began large-scale Spanish colonial silver extraction in 1545; Potosí grew to a population over 200,000 by the late 16th century (comparable to London or Paris at the time) and is estimated to have supplied around 60% of the world's silver during the peak of Spanish colonial extraction, financing the Spanish Empire and much of the European economy of the era. The mountain became known as "the mountain that eats men"; an estimated 8 million mineworkers (overwhelmingly indigenous and enslaved African laborers under the mita forced-labor system) are believed to have died in Potosí's mines over the colonial period. Source: search synthesis of CNN Travel ("'The Mountain that Eats Men': This Bolivian town is the only place in the world where you can legally buy dynamite," cnn.com/travel/potosi-bolivia-town-silver-mining-dynamite), Cultural Survival ("The Man-Eating Mines of Potosi," culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/man-eating-mines-potosi), and Historic Mysteries. Use: the paradigmatic pre-modern resource-curse narrative — a genuinely dramatic, well-documented historical case (Potosí is one of the most heavily studied sites in colonial economic history; Cerro Rico is a UNESCO World Heritage Site) that predates and prefigures the entire modern resource-curse literature by roughly 350 years. Limitation: the 8-million-death figure is a widely repeated estimate but its precise historical/demographic sourcing should be checked against academic colonial-history sources (e.g., work by Peter Bakewell, the leading historian of Potosí silver mining) rather than travel-journalism secondary sources before using as a precise figure in the book.
- By Bolivian independence in 1825, the silver mines were nearly exhausted and Potosí's population had collapsed from roughly 200,000 to about 10,000 — a direct, quantifiable illustration of a boom-bust resource cycle leaving the local economy poorer than before extraction began, relative to its peak. Source: same cluster as above. Use: a clean "the resource curse is not new" opening anecdote for Ch. 18, and a useful contrast to Chile's more successful, longer-duration management of its own major mineral (copper) discussed below.
- Bolivia today: it sits within the "lithium triangle" (with Chile and Argentina) that holds over half the world's known lithium reserves; Bolivia's Salar de Uyuni is believed to hold the single largest lithium deposit in the world (2025-vintage estimates put Bolivia's national reserves above 21 million metric tons, the world's largest by some estimates), yet Bolivia remains by far the least-developed lithium producer of the three triangle countries — Chile produced about 49,000 tonnes of lithium in 2024 and Argentina about 18,000 tonnes, versus Bolivia producing only a few hundred tonnes. Source: search synthesis, Americas Market Intelligence, "Bolivia at a Crossroads: Lithium, Gas, and the Search for Economic Stability After the 2025 Election," https://americasmi.com/insights/bolivia-2025-lithium-gas-economic-pressure/ ; International Relations Review, "Resource Nationalism in the Lithium Triangle." Use: Bolivia is the clearest live "reserves ≠ production ≠ wealth" case in the book's mineral set — vast reserves, minimal current output, due to a state-monopoly model (Yacimientos de Litio Bolivianos, YLB) that has struggled to attract capital/technology on terms it's willing to accept.
- In late 2024, Bolivia signed a $1 billion agreement with a Chinese-led consortium (including CATL, under the "CBC" grouping) to build direct lithium extraction (DLE) plants at Uyuni, with the Bolivian state retaining 51% ownership of each project, targeting roughly 35,000 tonnes/year of production. Source: search synthesis, Brasil de Fato, "US targets Bolivian lithium, but nationalization guarantees government control," June 28, 2024, https://www.brasildefato.com.br/2024/06/28/us-targets-bolivian-lithium-but-nationalization-guarantees-government-control/. Use: a current, ongoing test case of whether Bolivia's resource-nationalist model (majority state ownership, foreign technology partners) will succeed where its historical silver-extraction model catastrophically failed to build lasting local wealth — a live, unresolved thread the book can flag explicitly as "we don't yet know the ending" rather than force into a premature verdict.

**Chile — copper (a partial resource-curse-avoidance success story)**
- The Chilean state gradually acquired majority stakes in its copper mines starting under President Carlos Ibáñez del Campo (1952-58), continuing through the creation of the National Corporation of Chilean Copper (a precursor to Codelco); President Salvador Allende completed full nationalization via a constitutional amendment passed unanimously by Chile's Congress on July 11, 1971, made law on July 16, 1971 (Law 17.450), transferring the Chuquicamata, Andina, Salvador, El Teniente, and Exótica mines — previously foreign-owned — to the Chilean state. Notably, Codelco (the state copper company, today the world's largest copper producer) was formally established in 1976 under the right-wing Pinochet dictatorship that overthrew Allende in 1973 — meaning copper nationalization in Chile was a bipartisan, decades-long continuity across radically different governments rather than a single ideological event. Source: search synthesis, Wikipedia "Chilean nationalization of copper"; Chilean government retrospective, "Nationalization of copper celebrates 53 years," gob.cl/en/news/nationalization-of-copper-celebrates-53-years-this-is-how-this-historic-measure-for-chile-came-about/ ; NBER Working Paper w33572, Sebastian Edwards, "Copper in Chile, 1970-1973," https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33572/w33572.pdf (a rigorous economic-history source worth reading in full — Edwards is a leading Chilean-American economist who has written extensively on Chile's economic history, and this is an NBER working paper specifically on this exact episode). Use: the Edwards NBER paper is likely the single best rigorous academic source for a detailed Chile-copper-nationalization narrative — prioritize obtaining and reading it directly.
- Since the 1980s Chile has built multiple fiscal-stabilization mechanisms tied to copper revenue: a fiscal rule (structural balance rule), an Economic and Social Stabilization Fund, a Pension Reserve Fund, and an informal "defense fund," governed under a Fiscal Responsibility Law and complemented by a flexible exchange rate and an independent central bank. Source: UNU-WIDER Working Paper 2017/53, "The copper sector, fiscal rules, and stabilization funds in Chile: Scope and limits," https://www.wider.unu.edu/sites/default/files/wp2017-53.pdf ; IMF Working Paper WP/11/17, Teresa Dabán, "Strengthening Chile's Rule-Based Fiscal Framework," https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2011/wp1117.pdf. Use: this is the institutional-design evidence for "how Chile avoided the worst of the resource curse" — the UNU-WIDER paper explicitly cautions against a "blind endorsement" of these fiscal rules as a complete explanation, which is useful nuance (avoid an uncomplicated success narrative). Chile has been cited by multiple sources as "one of the few positive examples of managing resource-based income" while achieving relatively high income levels, macro stability, and generally good development indicators over the last ~25 years — though not spectacular overall GDP growth compared to some peers.

**Botswana — diamonds (the other classic "resource blessing" case)**
- Diamond mining constitutes roughly a third of Botswana's GDP, about half of public spending, and about 80% of exports. Botswana is the world's top diamond producer by value. Debswana — a 50/50 public-private partnership between the Botswana government and De Beers, founded in 1969 (immediately after independence) — is described as the oldest such partnership in the world and the bedrock of the economy; it directly employs about 6,300 people, 93% of them Batswana nationals. The government holds 50% of Debswana's board seats and two seats on De Beers' own board, contributing to transparency. Botswana ranks as Africa's least-corrupt country per the Corruption Perceptions Index. GDP per capita rose from about $1,344 (1950, inflation-adjusted framing per source) to about $15,015 today — a trajectory from one of the world's poorest countries at independence (1966) to upper-middle-income status. Source: search synthesis, The Boar (University of Warwick student publication, treat as secondary/lower-tier — verify claims against primary sources), "Diamonds to development: Botswana's post-independence economic growth"; International IDEA, "Mineral Resource Governance in Botswana," https://www.idea.int/publications/catalogue/html/mineral-resource-governance-botswana ; IMF Public Financial Management Blog, "Management of Botswana's Diamond Revenues," July 2024, https://blog-pfm.imf.org/en/pfmblog/2024/07/management-of-botswana-diamond-revenues. Use: prioritize the IMF PFM Blog and International IDEA sources over the student-publication source for any figures used in the book; the IMF blog in particular should directly address the fiscal-management institutions (analogous to Chile's stabilization funds) that are usually credited for Botswana's outcome.

**Norway — the "resource blessing" benchmark case (oil, not minerals, but the standard comparator)**
- Norway's Government Pension Fund Global (the sovereign wealth fund built from North Sea oil revenue) was established in 1990 specifically to give long-term-perspective management of government petroleum revenues; as of the end of 2025 it was valued around NOK 21.27-21.3 trillion (more than $2 trillion), the largest sovereign wealth fund in the world, equal to roughly 1.5% of the value of all listed companies globally and nearly four times Norway's GDP; the fund posted a return of about NOK 1.4 trillion in 2025. Source: search synthesis, Life in Norway, "Norway's Oil Fund Posts One of Its Biggest Ever Annual Gains"; Norway's own government pages, norskpetroleum.no/en/economy/management-of-revenues/ and regjeringen.no/en/topics/the-economy/the-government-pension-fund/government-pension-fund-global-gpfg/id697027/ (both official Norwegian government sources — prioritize these two for the book's citations over secondary blog aggregators). Use: the standard "what resource-wealth management done well looks like" comparator case for the chapter, though note for the book's own analytical honesty that Norway's advantage (already-wealthy, already-institutionally-strong Scandinavian democracy discovering oil, rather than a poor/fragile state discovering minerals) is a confound the literature (van der Ploeg 2011 above) explicitly flags — Norway's success may be as much a function of pre-existing strong institutions as of any specific fund design, which is exactly the Mehlum-Moene-Torvik "institutions are what matter" point.

### Counterevidence / complications

- The Mehlum-Moene-Torvik (2006) and Alexeev-Conrad (2009) papers above are the two strongest formal-academic counterweights to a simple "resources cause poverty" narrative — the former reframes the curse as institutionally contingent, the latter argues the curse effect is empirically weak or absent once measurement issues are addressed and even finds a net-positive long-run growth effect from oil/mineral endowment.
- Brunnschweiler and Bulte's 2008 Science paper directly challenges Sachs-Warner's core methodology (the resource-exports/GDP ratio as a proxy for resource abundance), arguing it's an endogenous, backward-looking trade measure rather than a clean measure of subsoil wealth — a technical but important critique that should temper any unqualified citation of the original 1995/2001 Sachs-Warner growth-regression results.
- Chile, Botswana, and Norway together demonstrate that resource wealth is not deterministically a curse — but each case also has a distinct institutional story (Chile's fiscal rules and sustained macro-institutional continuity across the Allende-Pinochet-democratic transition; Botswana's transparent 50/50 state partnership with De Beers and Africa-leading anti-corruption record; Norway's pre-existing strong Scandinavian democratic institutions before oil was even discovered in the 1960s-70s) — meaning the "exceptions" are not simply proof the curse is fictional, but evidence for the more nuanced institutions-mediate-outcomes view that Mehlum-Moene-Torvik and van der Ploeg formalize.
- Zambia is a useful within-country natural experiment for institutional-quality effects: the same copper endowment produced sharply different production and (implicitly) welfare trajectories under nationalized (1973-mid-1990s, declining output) versus privatized (mid-1990s onward, recovering output) management — though correlation with world copper prices over the same period is a confound that any serious treatment must address (the nationalization period coincided with a prolonged global copper price slump, complicating a clean "nationalization caused decline" reading).
- The DRC's 2025 cobalt export ban/quota system is explicitly a live test of whether a poor, fragile, conflict-affected resource-dependent state can extract more value via supply management (an OPEC-style intervention) — early reporting (Semafor, May 2026) suggests miners are responding by stockpiling, pivoting to copper, or pausing production, indicating the policy's on-the-ground effects are contested and not yet a clean success story either way; this should be reported as an open, unresolved case rather than folded into either a "curse confirmed" or "curse broken" narrative.
- Note a related but analytically distinct literature the book may want to flag rather than conflate: the "conflict resource" / civil-war literature (e.g., Collier & Hoeffler-style "greed and grievance" work) addresses whether resource wealth increases civil conflict risk, which is a different (though related) claim from the Sachs-Warner growth-curse claim; the DRC's M23/cobalt situation implicates both literatures simultaneously and the chapter should be precise about which claim it is making at any given moment.
