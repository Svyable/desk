# Chapter 9 — 2010 rare-earth coercion evidence boundary

Checked September 4, 2026.

## Claim under test

Chapter 9 opens with the 2010 China-Japan rare-earth episode. The manuscript already avoids claiming that Beijing issued a formal public embargo order. The remaining risk is to blur three separate questions: whether shipments were disrupted, whether the disruption was Japan-specific, and whether China's market dominance translated into large economic costs for Japan.

## Official record

Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry records that rare-earth exports bound for Japan were reportedly stalled beginning September 21, 2010 as Chinese customs procedures were enforced more strictly. China denied imposing an embargo and said the tighter procedures were aimed at unlawful exports. METI says the stagnation continued for nearly two months.

Primary source: METI, Chapter 3 Reference: Export Restrictions.
https://www.meti.go.jp/english/report/data/2016WTO/pdf/02_05.pdf

The safe formulation is a country-specific customs slowdown during the diplomatic crisis, with the embargo label and retaliatory intent disputed in real time.

## Trade data strengthen the disruption case

The OECD's 2024 study Trade impacts of economic coercion tests the episode against merchandise-trade statistics. It finds patterns consistent with shipment delays in October and November 2010: lower Chinese rare-earth export volumes to Japan, a strong rebound in December, and no recorded trade in some rare-earth categories during the disruption months. The contraction was specific to rare earths rather than a general fall in bilateral trade, and Japanese imports from other suppliers continued or expanded.

Authoritative source: OECD, Trade impacts of economic coercion, 2024.
https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/05/trade-impacts-of-economic-coercion_044bdb0a/d4ab39b9-en.pdf

This gives Chapter 9 stronger evidence than contemporary press accounts alone without pretending that a public Chinese directive explicitly tied customs treatment to the fishing-boat dispute.

## Strongest counterevidence

Eugene Gholz and Llewelyn Hughes treat the 2010 episode as a best-case test for sanctions effectiveness. Their peer-reviewed study finds that Japanese firms and markets adjusted in ways that limited the sanction's economic bite despite China's dominant position. Their broader point is that inventories, substitution, alternative suppliers, and downstream adjustment can make apparent supply power less decisive than concentration statistics suggest.
