# Chapter 9 — 2010 rare-earth coercion evidence boundary

Checked September 4, 2026.

Chapter 9 is right not to claim that Beijing publicly announced a Japan-specific rare-earth embargo in 2010. METI's retrospective says exports to Japan were reportedly stalled from September 21 as Chinese customs procedures were enforced more strictly; China denied an embargo and described the change as anti-smuggling enforcement. METI says the stagnation lasted nearly two months.

Primary source: METI, Chapter 3 Reference: Export Restrictions.
https://www.meti.go.jp/english/report/data/2016WTO/pdf/02_05.pdf

The stronger evidence comes from trade data. The OECD's 2024 study Trade impacts of economic coercion finds patterns consistent with disruption in October and November 2010: lower Chinese rare-earth export volumes to Japan, a strong rebound in December, and zero recorded trade in some rare-earth categories during the disruption months. The contraction was specific to rare earths rather than a broad fall in China-Japan trade, while Japanese imports from other suppliers continued or expanded.

Authoritative source: OECD, Trade impacts of economic coercion, 2024.
https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/05/trade-impacts-of-economic-coercion_044bdb0a/d4ab39b9-en.pdf

The strongest counterargument is that concentration did not guarantee coercive success. Eugene Gholz and Llewelyn Hughes treat the episode as a best-case sanctions test and find that Japanese firms and markets adjusted enough to limit the economic bite despite China's dominant position. Inventories, substitution, alternative suppliers, and downstream adjustment made the apparent chokepoint less decisive than market-share statistics alone implied.

Academic source: Eugene Gholz and Llewelyn Hughes, Review of International Political Economy 28(3), 2021, pp. 611-634.
https://doi.org/10.1080/09692290.2019.1693411

Editorial boundary: the chapter can say that a Japan-specific rare-earth disruption occurred during the diplomatic crisis and that later trade data are consistent with informal coercion. It should keep China's denial visible and avoid presenting the episode as proof that supply concentration automatically compels the target. The more durable lesson is that chokepoint power depends on the target's ability to draw inventory, reroute supply, substitute materials, and build alternatives before the disruption bites.
