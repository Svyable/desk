# Chapter 14 research note — Friend-shoring after the announcements

Research checked September 3, 2026 for Chapter 14, “Friend-Shoring and the New Alliances.”

## Claim under test

The weak version of the chapter is a tour of policy acronyms: IRA, MSP, CRMA, grants, strategic projects, corridors. The stronger question is whether governments can turn diplomatic alignment into physical mineral supply on a timetable that matters.

Friend-shoring is not one policy. It is an attempt to coordinate several different bottlenecks: geology, processing know-how, permitting, finance, offtake, infrastructure, and political trust. A country can be a close ally and still lack an economic deposit. A mine can be excellent and still send concentrate through the old refining chokepoint. A government can designate a project “strategic” without making its economics work.

The chapter should therefore measure policy by projects and capabilities, not communiqués.

## The Minerals Security Partnership: useful coordination, limited coercive power

The Minerals Security Partnership’s own project announcements show what the mechanism can actually do. In September 2023, MSP partners said they were working to advance 17 projects: eleven upstream mining/extraction projects, four processing projects, and two recycling/recovery projects, spread across the Americas, Africa, Europe, and Asia-Pacific.

Source: U.S. Department of State, `Joint Statement on the Minerals Security Partnership Announce Support for Mining, Processing, and Recycling Projects`, October 10, 2023.
https://2021-2025.state.gov/joint-statement-on-the-minerals-security-partnership-announce-support-for-mining-processing-and-recycling-projects/

Concrete deals matter more than the count. In May 2024, the MSP welcomed an agreement between Gécamines subsidiary STL in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Belgium’s Umicore for germanium offtake and processing. In February 2024, it announced a cooperation framework between Gécamines and Japan’s JOGMEC covering exploration, production, and processing, explicitly linked to the Lobito Corridor. In October 2024, the U.S. welcomed a $150 million investment by existing investors into Brazil’s Serra Verde rare-earth project and described the investment as an MSP/MINVEST-supported diversification step.

Editorial boundary: these examples support the claim that diplomacy can convene capital, producers, processors, and buyers. They do not establish that the MSP itself financed, built, or guaranteed all resulting capacity. Avoid giving a diplomatic forum causal credit for every project it endorses.

## The EU Critical Raw Materials Act: targets plus a project pipeline

The European Commission’s implementation of the Critical Raw Materials Act provides a different model. The Commission approved an initial set of 60 strategic projects in 2025, inside and outside the EU. Strategic-project status is meant to improve access to financing guidance, attract potential offtakers, and accelerate permitting; it is not the same thing as a grant award or a completed mine.

Sources:
- European Commission, `Selected strategic projects under CRMA`.
https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/raw-materials/areas-specific-interest/critical-raw-materials/strategic-projects-under-crma/selected-projects_en
- European Commission, `Strategic projects on critical raw materials gain momentum in second selection round for potential funding and faster permitting`, January 19, 2026.
https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/news/strategic-projects-critical-raw-materials-gain-momentum-second-selection-round-potential-funding-and-2026-01-19_en

The second selection round closed in January 2026 with more than 160 applications. That is evidence of project appetite, not evidence of delivered tonnes.

In April 2026, the Commission also launched the Raw Materials Mechanism, a voluntary market platform intended to aggregate demand and connect buyers with suppliers, financial institutions, storage providers, and strategic projects. Its first diversification round focuses particularly on rare earths, battery materials, and defence raw materials.

Source: European Commission, `Commission launches platform to aggregate demand of raw materials and boost diversification`, April 13, 2026.
https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-launches-platform-aggregate-demand-raw-materials-and-boost-diversification-2026-04-13_en

This is analytically useful because it acknowledges a problem that chapters 10–12 already establish: supply projects often need credible future buyers before lenders will finance them. Friend-shoring is partly an offtake problem.

## Strongest counterargument: “friend” is not a geological or commercial category

The strongest objection to friend-shoring is not that alliances are meaningless. It is that supply chains optimize across variables governments do not control completely.

Ore deposits do not relocate to treaty allies. Processing plants need scale, energy, reagents, expertise, customers, and acceptable margins. A politically preferred supplier can remain too expensive. A strategic project can lose financing when commodity prices fall. A friendly government can change after an election. Source countries may reasonably resist a model in which they export ore while allies capture the processing margin.

The book already has evidence for this boundary. Kitsault obtained approvals but still stalled when price and finance disagreed. Indonesia built nickel leverage partly by insisting that more processing occur locally rather than accepting the role of raw-material exporter. Congo’s mineral politics make clear that “secure supply for us” is not automatically an attractive development proposition for the country supplying it.

Friend-shoring therefore works best when it creates durable mutual economics, not when it merely redraws a geopolitical map around existing extraction.

## Counterevidence to the counterargument: coordination can change project economics

Government action can alter the variables that make a project financeable. Grants can lower capital cost. Public lenders and export-credit agencies can absorb risks private lenders reject. Faster permitting can reduce carrying costs. Long-term offtake can make revenue more bankable. Shared infrastructure can turn a stranded deposit into an economic one. Recycling and processing investments can create value without discovering new geology.

The EU’s 2026 Raw Materials Mechanism is revealing precisely because it tries to connect demand, supply, finance, and storage rather than treating mineral security as a mine-counting exercise. The MSP examples likewise show that an alliance can be useful as a matchmaking and coordination layer even when it does not own the mine.

So the chapter should not conclude that friend-shoring is diplomatic theater. The narrower conclusion is that diplomacy is most valuable when it changes a physical project’s probability of completion.

## Editorial implication

Chapter 14 can open with a deliberately prosaic problem: a mine can have ore, permits, and friendly flags on both sides of the transaction and still fail because nobody has signed a bankable purchase agreement for what it will produce five years from now.

From there, the chapter can treat alliances as machinery for retiring specific risks:

- the MSP as a coordination and deal-convening layer;
- the CRMA strategic-project system as a way to prioritize projects and accelerate permitting/finance attention;
- the EU Raw Materials Mechanism as an explicit attempt to solve the buyer-seller-offtake problem;
- U.S. grants and pilots as efforts to rebuild processing capability that markets alone allowed to concentrate elsewhere.

Avoid a catalogue of initiatives. Follow two or three projects deeply enough to ask what changed because governments became involved and what remained stubbornly commercial or geological.

The governing claim should be: a friendly flag does not create a supply chain. Friend-shoring matters when alliances convert political preference into contracts, infrastructure, processing knowledge, finance, and qualified material.

That creates a clean handoff to Chapter 15. If allied governments are now trying to assemble those capabilities deliberately, the obvious question is how China assembled so many of them in the first place — and which parts of that history were strategy, subsidy, accumulated industrial learning, environmental tolerance, market scale, or hindsight mythology.
