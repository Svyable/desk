# Chapter 1 — export controls from paperwork to production

## Why this note exists

Chapter 1 already gets the legal mechanism right: China’s 4 April 2025 measure was a licensing regime over specified medium- and heavy-rare-earth-related items, not a blanket embargo on all rare-earth exports. The chapter also explains the strategic logic well — a small input can interrupt a much larger industrial system when access becomes uncertain.

The remaining gap is empirical. The opening currently moves from the licensing rule to hypothetical manufacturers and contractors, then later to the broader argument. The chapter brief asks for a specific affected sector and for counterevidence showing where the controls were narrower or less durable than the strongest version of the story implies.

The 2026 IEA evidence supplies both.

## Primary-source boundary: what China actually controlled

MOFCOM and China’s General Administration of Customs issued Announcement No. 18 on 4 April 2025. It covered specified samarium-, gadolinium-, terbium-, dysprosium-, lutetium-, scandium-, and yttrium-related items, including enumerated metals, alloys, oxides, compounds, targets, and some permanent-magnet materials.

Exporters of covered items had to apply for a licence. They also had to identify controlled items in customs declarations, and customs authorities could hold goods while questioning whether a declaration was correct.

The document did **not** announce a blanket halt to all rare-earth exports. That distinction is important enough to preserve in the chapter’s first pages.

Primary source:
- Ministry of Commerce and General Administration of Customs, Announcement No. 18 of 2025, 4 April 2025: https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/Policies/AnnouncementsOrders/art/2025/art_0dd87cbee7b045bf93fabe6ab2faceee.html

## What happened downstream

The International Energy Agency’s later assessment shows that the administrative mechanism did become a physical production constraint.

After the April controls, export volumes of the affected rare-earth materials and permanent magnets fell sharply in April and May 2025. The IEA reports that automakers in the United States, Europe, and elsewhere struggled to source permanent magnets; some reduced utilisation rates and some temporarily halted production.

That is the concrete consequence Chapter 1 currently gestures toward but does not show.

The strongest useful version of the point is therefore not “China banned rare earths and factories stopped.” It is:

> A licensing rule over a narrow set of materials was enough to reduce exports sharply for several weeks and push the disruption all the way to automotive production lines.

Authoritative sources:
- IEA, *Rare Earth Elements*, executive summary: https://www.iea.org/reports/rare-earth-elements/executive-summary
- IEA, “With new export controls on critical minerals, supply concentration risks become reality”: https://www.iea.org/commentaries/with-new-export-controls-on-critical-minerals-supply-concentration-risks-become-reality
- IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary: https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

## The counterevidence belongs in the same paragraph

The same IEA evidence prevents the episode from being overstated.

Licences were eventually granted and export volumes recovered in subsequent months. The disruption was consequential, but it was not a permanent shutoff. That matters because it strengthens the chapter’s larger claim about administrative leverage: the tool did not need to function like an oil embargo to matter.

The harder, more defensible interpretation is that concentrated supply creates a capability to inject delay and uncertainty into downstream production. A licensing regime can be economically coercive even when it is reversible, selective, and eventually loosened.

This is also the strongest counterargument to a dramatic reading of the episode. If exports recover once licences begin flowing, then the event is evidence of vulnerability and bargaining power, not proof that one country can indefinitely turn off the world’s rare-earth supply without provoking substitution, diversification, stockpiling, or political response.

## Suggested manuscript insertion

The cleanest location is immediately after the chapter’s early hypothetical examples of the European magnet manufacturer, aerospace supplier, and defense contractor, before the explanation of why the seven listed elements were not “the seven rare earths.”

A restrained insertion could read:

> The disruption did not remain hypothetical. The International Energy Agency later reported that exports of the affected rare-earth materials and permanent magnets fell sharply in April and May 2025. Automakers in the United States, Europe, and elsewhere struggled to source magnets; some cut utilisation and some temporarily stopped production. Licences were eventually granted and export volumes recovered. The episode was disruptive without becoming a permanent embargo — which is exactly why the distinction between permission and prohibition matters.

This adds a concrete factory-floor consequence without turning the opening into a chronology dump or repeating the later sections on bargaining power.

## What this would improve

The opening currently asks the reader to imagine how licensing uncertainty might reach a factory. The IEA evidence means the chapter no longer has to rely on imagination. The mechanism can be shown before it is explained.

It also gives the book a better evidentiary rhythm:

1. the official rule;
2. the physical downstream consequence;
3. the recovery that limits the claim;
4. the broader argument about chokepoint leverage.

That sequence is stronger than either sensationalism (“ban”) or abstraction (“a supplier might face delays”).

## What would make this interpretation false?

This reading would weaken if later trade data showed that the April–May decline was unrelated to the controls, if the IEA’s reported automotive disruptions were materially revised, or if subsequent evidence established that the affected factories were constrained primarily by unrelated shortages.

It would also need qualification if licensing proved so routine over time that the April–May disruption was a one-off implementation shock rather than an enduring source of leverage.

For now, the evidence supports a narrower conclusion: a targeted licensing regime over a small set of rare-earth-related items was sufficient to create a short, real downstream production shock, while subsequent licence approvals show why the episode should not be described as a permanent blanket embargo.
