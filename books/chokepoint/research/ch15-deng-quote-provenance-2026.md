# Chapter 15 — Deng rare-earth quote provenance boundary

Checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 15, “China's Long Game.”

## Claim under test

Chapter 15 opens with the familiar line usually rendered in English as “The Middle East has oil; China has rare earths,” attributed to Deng Xiaoping in 1992. The chapter already treats the line cautiously. This note records why that caution is warranted and what can be said with higher confidence.

## What the source trail actually supports

A 2012 article on the website of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference gives a retrospective official Chinese version of the story. It says Deng made the remark during his 1992 southern tour and reproduces a longer formulation comparing China's rare-earth position with Middle Eastern oil and calling the resource strategically important.

Source: Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference, `聚焦中国稀土`, May 2, 2012
https://www.cppcc.gov.cn/zxww/2012/05/02/ARTI1335945839593161.shtml

This is useful evidence that the attribution became part of official Chinese institutional memory. It is not a contemporaneous 1992 transcript. The page was published roughly two decades after the supposed remark and does not identify an original speech record, diary, audio recording, or contemporaneous newspaper text.

Recent scholarship continues to repeat the 1992 attribution. A 2025 *China Quarterly* article says Deng made the statement in 1992 during a visit to Bayan Obo in Inner Mongolia.

Source: Jessica DiCarlo et al., `Fractured Extraction: Mining Firms, Provinces and Municipalities in the Decentralized Politics of China’s Rare Earth Production`, *The China Quarterly*, 2025
https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/fractured-extraction-mining-firms-provinces-and-municipalities-in-the-decentralized-politics-of-chinas-rare-earth-production/572B3AE7725861D148290BA7B9D8ABCB

That secondary account is plausible but does not solve the primary-source problem. Other scholarly retellings date the remark differently — including 1987 and even 1979 — or paraphrase it as a suggestion that China should become the “Middle East” of rare earths. The instability in date, setting, and wording is itself evidence that the polished English sentence should not be presented as a securely verified verbatim quotation without a contemporaneous source.

## The industrial history does not need the quotation

USGS historical evidence shows that China's rare-earth rise was already underway before or around the time the line is usually dated. A 1991 USGS open-file report states that U.S. and Chinese rare-earth production were approximately equal in 1986 and gives 1989 production of about 14,000 tonnes of REE oxide for the United States versus about 20,000 tonnes for China.

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, Open-File Report 91-427
https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1991/0427/report.pdf

A later USGS history describes the transition from Mountain Pass dominance through a 1984-1991 transitional period into a “Chinese era” beginning around 1991, and notes that Chinese production had been rising dramatically since 1985.

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, `Rare Earth Elements—Critical Resources for High Technology`, Fact Sheet 087-02
https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2002/fs087-02/

Contemporaneous technical literature also shows an established Chinese rare-earth industry by 1992. An invited lecture published in the March-April 1992 issue of *Materials Chemistry and Physics* reviewed China's rare-earth resources, production, applications, and market. That publication predates many later retellings of the Deng line and is useful evidence that the industrial system was not waiting for a slogan to begin.

Source: `Rare earth applications and their market in China`, *Materials Chemistry and Physics* 31 (1992), 85-88
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0254058492901574

## Strongest counterargument

The absence of a contemporaneous transcript does not prove Deng never said something substantially like the later formulation. The line appears consistently in later official Chinese accounts and in a large body of scholarship, and it fits the strategic importance Chinese institutions were already assigning to rare earths.

The correct boundary is therefore not “the quote is fake.” It is narrower: the familiar English wording, exact date, and setting are not secure enough to carry historical causation or to be presented as a verified verbatim quotation without qualification.

## Editorial implication

The current manuscript's opening is on the right side of this boundary: it calls the line a founding myth, says it is “usually attributed” to Deng in 1992, and explicitly notes that the familiar English wording is difficult to source cleanly.

A future prose revision should preserve that caution and make the causal point even harder to misread. The industrial story is stronger when the quotation is treated as retrospective shorthand for a strategy already visible in production, research, processing, and market data rather than as the moment the strategy began.

The line can remain because it is culturally and politically revealing. It should not become evidence of a single-command origin story.

## What would make this note wrong

A contemporaneous 1992 transcript, recording, official diary entry, newspaper report, or other primary document could establish the wording, setting, and date much more securely. If such a source is found, the manuscript should update the provenance and quote only the language supported by that record.
