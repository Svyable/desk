# Chapter 5 research note — the Lithium Triangle is diverging faster than the label suggests

Research checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 5, “The Lithium Triangle.”

## Claim under test

Chapter 5 is strongest when “the Lithium Triangle” describes shared brine geology rather than a unified market or political system.

Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia increasingly support three different claims about mineral strategy:

- Chile shows a state-led model moving through large public-private partnerships rather than simple nationalization;
- Argentina shows how quickly private and provincial project development can turn a perennial “future supplier” into material production growth;
- Bolivia shows the continuing gap between a very large identified resource and commercial-scale output.

That divergence matters because it makes the triangle a natural experiment in how geology, institutions, capital, technology, and politics convert resources into supply.

## Chile: the state-partnership model is no longer merely proposed

Chapter 5 currently describes Codelco as having “reached an agreement with SQM” to continue development in the Salar de Atacama. That is directionally correct but now understates how far the arrangement has advanced.

On December 27, 2025, Codelco and SQM formally created NovaAndino Litio, the joint venture that will develop lithium in the Salar de Atacama through 2060. Codelco describes the structure as having majority state participation and says the venture combines Codelco’s state role with SQM’s operating assets, permits, technical knowledge, subsidiaries, and workforce.

Primary source:
https://www.codelco.com/en/prensa/2025/codelco-y-sqm-forman-novaandino-litio-la-sociedad-conjunta-para-el

A week earlier, Chile’s Comptroller General had approved the Corfo-Codelco contracts that Codelco described as the final condition precedent to the partnership agreement.

Primary source:
https://www.codelco.com/en/contraloria-toma-razon-de-los-contratos-entre-corfo-y-filial-de-codelco

The useful editorial distinction is between state participation and state operation. Chile has increased state control over the resource while deliberately retaining private operating capability and outside capital.

That makes the episode more interesting than either “nationalization” or “privatization.” The state is trying to own more of the leverage without pretending it can supply every technical and commercial capability itself.

## Maricunga makes the same point even more clearly

Codelco’s Maricunga arrangement with Rio Tinto is another public-private structure, but at an earlier stage.

Rio Tinto agreed to acquire 49.99% of the venture and fund $350 million of studies and development work, followed by $500 million toward construction if the project reaches a final investment decision. A further $50 million is tied to an ambition to deliver first lithium by the end of 2030.

Primary source:
https://www.riotinto.com/news/releases/2025/rio-tinto-partners-with-codelco-to-develop-lithium-project-in-chiles-salar-de-maricunga

The financing milestones are useful counterevidence to language that makes announced resources sound like inevitable supply. Even a politically strategic deposit with a major state owner and a major global miner still has to clear studies, regulatory approvals, a final investment decision, construction, commissioning, and process qualification.

## Argentina: the strongest counterexample to a static “triangle”

The U.S. Geological Survey’s current Argentina country report shows how quickly Argentina has moved from promise toward production.

USGS estimates Argentina accounted for about 6% of world lithium production in 2024, making it the fifth-largest producer that year. Argentina’s lithium carbonate production rose 67% in 2024, to 69,515 tonnes, with the Cauchari-Olaroz project becoming the country’s largest lithium operation and reaching 85% of design capacity by the final quarter.

Authoritative source:
https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/argentina

This is the strongest evidence against treating South American brine supply as uniformly slow or politically constrained. Projects can scale rapidly when financing, provincial approvals, infrastructure, operators, and processing execution line up.

It also sharpens Chapter 5’s counterargument to scarcity panic. High prices and investment did summon meaningful new supply. Argentina is one reason the lithium market looks less geographically frozen than it did several years ago.

## Bolivia: resource size still does not equal supply

Bolivia remains the cleanest empirical version of one of the book’s recurring distinctions.

USGS reports that Bolivia had an estimated 23 million tonnes of identified lithium resources in 2024 but no identified lithium reserves under the USGS classification used in that country report. At the same time, the report describes plans for direct-lithium-extraction plants with much larger designed capacities than Bolivia’s recent output.

Authoritative source:
https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/bolivia

Bolivia’s own state lithium company, YLB, said in May 2026 that it expected to produce 3,600 tonnes of lithium carbonate during 2026.

Primary source:
https://www.ylb.gob.bo/index.php/nota_prensa/20656/

YLB separately said in May 2026 that Bolivia had 21 million tonnes of internationally certified lithium resources in the Salar de Uyuni and was working to certify additional resources at Coipasa.

Primary source:
https://www.ylb.gob.bo/index.php/nota_prensa/ylb-avanza-en-la-certificacion-internacional-de-recursos/

The exact resource figures differ because the institutions are using different resource definitions and reporting bases. That difference itself is a useful warning: a large geological number should not be compared casually with annual production or with economically recoverable reserves.

The durable point is the scale mismatch. Bolivia can possess one of the world’s largest identified lithium resources while producing only a small fraction of the output of established producers.

## The midstream still weakens a mine-only diversification story

The IEA’s 2026 outlook reinforces Chapter 5’s distinction between upstream diversification and usable battery supply.

The agency says lithium mining growth outside the dominant supplier is running ahead of existing and planned refining and cathode-material capacity outside the dominant refining country. Across energy minerals more broadly, refining concentration reached record levels in 2025, with China remaining the leading refiner for most of the key minerals covered.

Authoritative sources:
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/market-overview

This keeps the chapter from overlearning the lesson of the 2022-2025 lithium price cycle. More mines and brine projects can reduce one form of scarcity while conversion and downstream qualification remain concentrated.

The chokepoint can move rather than disappear.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest case against treating lithium as a durable strategic chokepoint is the market’s demonstrated ability to respond.

Argentina’s rapid production growth is concrete evidence. The IEA says expected supply-demand gaps for lithium over the next decade have narrowed as projects advance. Battery chemistry also remains adaptive, with technologies such as sodium-ion batteries capable of reducing lithium demand at the margin in some applications.

Authoritative source:
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

A credible chapter therefore should not imply that state strategy or geological concentration makes shortage permanent.

The better claim is narrower: lithium can move from shortage to glut faster than the mines, refineries, governments, and communities around it can adjust. That volatility changes which projects survive long enough to become resilient supply.

## Falsification boundary

Chapter 5’s political-economy argument should weaken if the three South American systems converge in practice rather than merely in rhetoric.

Evidence for convergence would include:

- Chile’s state-partnership model producing no material difference in project timing, value capture, environmental governance, or investment durability compared with more private-led systems;
- Argentina’s rapid project growth stalling long enough that its recent production gains prove temporary rather than structural;
- Bolivia translating its very large identified resource base into sustained commercial output at a scale comparable with major producers;
- non-Chinese refining and cathode-material capacity scaling quickly enough that upstream geographic diversification is matched downstream.

If those things happen, “three political economies on related geology” would become less analytically useful.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 5

The chapter already has the right sentence: “Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia share geology. They do not share an industrial model.”

The 2025-2026 evidence makes that line stronger.

Chile has moved from announcing a strategy to constituting a majority-state joint venture with SQM and pairing with Rio Tinto at Maricunga. Argentina has demonstrated real production acceleration. Bolivia still illustrates the distance between a giant resource number and a small operating industry.

The chapter should preserve those contrasts and resist collapsing them into a single story about resource nationalism or scarcity.

The stronger organizing question is not who owns the lithium in the ground. It is which institutional system can convert brine into qualified material repeatedly across a boom-and-bust cycle — while retaining enough local legitimacy to keep operating.
