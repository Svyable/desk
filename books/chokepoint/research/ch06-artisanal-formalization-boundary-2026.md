# Chapter 6 research — responsible sourcing without making miners disappear

Research checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 6, “Congo's Cobalt.”

## Claim under test

Chapter 6 argues that excluding artisanal and small-scale mining from formal cobalt supply chains can make corporate sourcing look cleaner without necessarily improving the lives of people who depend on mining income. The stronger version of the argument is not “artisanal mining is good” or “formalization solves abuse.” It is that due diligence has to distinguish risk reduction from risk displacement.

## Current authoritative evidence

The U.S. Department of Labor's 2024 findings on the Democratic Republic of the Congo still identify dangerous child labour in cobalt and copper mining. The report lists carrying heavy loads, digging, sifting, sorting, transporting, washing, and underground work among hazardous tasks performed by children. It also records major enforcement weaknesses: inadequate resources, no published labour-enforcement data, and no unannounced labour inspections during the reporting period.

Source: U.S. Department of Labor, Bureau of International Labor Affairs, “2024 Findings on the Worst Forms of Child Labor: Democratic Republic of the Congo.”
https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ilab/resources/reports/child-labor/congo-democratic-republic-drc

The Department of Labor's current List of Goods Produced by Child Labor or Forced Labor separately identifies cobalt ore from the DRC as associated with both child labour and forced labour. Its description notes reported indicators including inability to refuse hazardous work, abusive overtime, wage withholding, threats of dismissal, unsafe tunnels, and vulnerability created by a lack of alternative livelihoods.

Source: U.S. Department of Labor, “List of Goods Produced by Child Labor or Forced Labor — Cobalt Ore (DRC).”
https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ilab/reports/child-labor/list-of-goods

These are important boundaries for the manuscript. The ethical problem is not historical residue from the first wave of cobalt scrutiny in the mid-2010s; authoritative labour monitoring still treats it as current.

## Formalization is not the same thing as exclusion

The OECD's detailed cobalt/copper due-diligence study cautions against treating industrial and artisanal supply as perfectly separate systems. It documents interconnected flows and argues that due diligence should extend beyond child labour alone to corruption, security-force abuse, and other human-rights risks.

That finding matters because a procurement rule that says “buy only industrial cobalt” can reduce exposure to one class of traceability risk while missing abuses elsewhere in the chain. It can also obscure the economic question of what happens to miners excluded from formal buyers.

Source: OECD, *Interconnected Supply Chains: A Comprehensive Look at Due Diligence Challenges and Opportunities Sourcing Cobalt and Copper from the Democratic Republic of the Congo*, 2019.
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/interconnected-supply-chains_576804e7-en.html

The World Bank's work on artisanal and small-scale mining in the DRC likewise treats formalization as an economic-development problem, not merely a compliance exercise. Its analysis emphasizes land security, taxation, technical assistance, social organization, and market access. The Bank also describes artisanal mining as a major source of rural employment in isolated areas where alternative livelihoods can be scarce.

Sources:
- World Bank, *DRC Economic Update: Building the Long-Term Resilience of DRC*, 2015.
https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/drc/publication/boosting-resilience-to-economic-shocks-through-diversification-and-mobilization-of-extractive-industry-revenue
- World Bank, DRC artisanal and small-scale mining annex, 2023.
https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099101723140622516/pdf/P17516105e95da0de0a34c0dd670e6f75cf.pdf

The exact number of people dependent on artisanal cobalt should not be presented as a stable hard figure. Published estimates vary widely with cobalt prices, seasonality, definitions, and the difficulty of measuring informal activity. The defensible point is qualitative and well supported: ASM remains economically important, and informality makes both labour conditions and production volumes hard to observe.

## Fresh evidence that remediation, not simple avoidance, is still the policy direction

The ILO launched the GALAB cobalt project in November 2024 to strengthen child-labour monitoring, referral, remediation, public-sector capacity, and private-sector accountability in the DRC artisanal cobalt sector. Its stated approach builds on the earlier COTECCO project rather than assuming prior responsible-sourcing initiatives had solved the problem.

Source: International Labour Organization, “ILO Launches GALAB project in Democratic Republic of the Congo to address child labour in cobalt mining sector,” November 20, 2024.
https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-launches-galab-project-democratic-republic-congo-address-child-labour

On May 8, 2026, the ILO and Responsible Minerals Initiative announced a new partnership focused on prevention, due diligence, and remediation in mineral supply chains. Their rationale is useful for Chapter 6: they explicitly say child labour in artisanal and small-scale mining requires coordinated responses connecting downstream buyers to upstream communities, and they identify poverty, informality, limited education, and weak social protection as root causes.

Source: International Labour Organization, “ILO and Responsible Minerals Initiative partner to address child labour in mineral supply chains,” May 8, 2026.
https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-and-responsible-minerals-initiative-partner-address-child-labour

The ILO's 2026 midterm evaluation of the CLEAR Supply Chains programme reinforces the same causal boundary. Its intervention model combines labour rights, education, livelihoods, skills, social protection, responsible supply chains, and market access rather than treating mine-site auditing alone as sufficient.

Source: ILO, *Ending Child Labour in Supply Chains — Independent Midterm Evaluation*, 2026.
https://webapps.ilo.org/ievaldiscovery/files/file/2391422

## Strongest counterargument

There is a strong case for buyers preferring large industrial operations when traceability, occupational safety systems, audited management controls, and predictable production matter. Industrial mining now supplies the overwhelming bulk of DRC cobalt by volume, and a romanticized account of artisanal mining would be as misleading as a purely punitive one.

There is also no guarantee that formalization succeeds. Legal zones can be captured by intermediaries, cooperatives can become rent-seeking structures, traceability documents can be falsified, and formal buyers can withdraw when cobalt prices fall or reputational risk rises. If the economic alternative for a household remains worse than mining, paperwork alone does not remove the pressure that sends adults or children into pits.

The chapter should therefore avoid implying that inclusion is inherently ethical. The test is what happens to workers and children after the intervention, not whether the mineral receives a cleaner label.

## Strongest defensible formulation

Responsible sourcing has at least three distinct jobs: identify abusive material, reduce the abuse itself, and avoid pushing vulnerable workers into less visible markets. Those goals can conflict.

Excluding an unverified supplier may be necessary for a buyer. At system level, however, a durable improvement requires some combination of legal access to workable sites, safer mining practice, credible buyers, monitoring, remediation, education, social protection, and alternative household income. Otherwise the supply chain may become easier to audit while the underlying labour problem simply moves outside the audit boundary.

## What would make this framing misleading?

It would need revision if reliable evidence showed that exclusion from formal cobalt markets consistently caused hazardous artisanal mining to disappear rather than move into informal channels, or if formalization programmes demonstrably failed to improve safety, incomes, traceability, or child-labour remediation even when adequately funded and enforced.

It would also be misleading to use cobalt's labour abuses as a description of all Congolese cobalt. The chapter should continue distinguishing large mechanized operations from artisanal sites and should not use an artisanal mine image as shorthand for a supply chain whose largest volumes come from industrial mines.

## Editorial implication

The manuscript's existing line — that a supply chain can become “cleaner on paper by making the people at the bottom harder to see” — is supportable, but it should remain a warning about incentives rather than an accusation that every industrial-only sourcing policy causes harm.

The sharper point is more useful: **traceability measures what enters the formal chain; ethics also has to ask what happens to the people who fall out of it.**
