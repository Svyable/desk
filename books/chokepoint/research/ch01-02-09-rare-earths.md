## CHAPTER 1 — "The List of Seventeen" (cold open: China's rare-earth export controls as geopolitical weapon)

### Key facts and sources

- China's Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) and General Administration of Customs issued **Decree/Notice 2025 No. 61 and No. 62 on October 9, 2025**, dramatically expanding rare-earth export controls: No. 61 targets *foreign-made* products containing Chinese-origin rare earths or made with Chinese rare-earth technology (extraterritorial reach); No. 62 controls rare-earth-*related technology*, services and support, not just physical exports. Source: [MOFCOM Notice 2025 No. 61 — CSET/Georgetown](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/mofcom-notice-2025-61/); corroborated by [Squire Patton Boggs](https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/china-expands-export-control-on-rare-earth-minerals-and-more/), [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/10/chinas-expanded-export-controls-on-rare-earths), [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/10/prc-announces-new-export-controls-on-rare-earth-and-battery-materials-and-technology) [secondary-summary — WebFetch blocked on all four, relied on WebSearch snippets]. **Use:** the cold-open trigger event; verify decree numbers and exact text against MOFCOM's own site (commerce.gov.cn) before press.

- The October 2025 expansion added **five new rare-earth elements to licensing controls: holmium, erbium, thulium, europium, and ytterbium** — on top of the seven elements (scandium, yttrium, samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium) already controlled since April 2025. Source: [Squire Patton Boggs / Capital Thinking summary via WebSearch](https://www.capitalthinkingblog.com/2025/10/china-expands-export-control-on-rare-earth-minerals-and-more/); White & Case also describes a **"50% rule"** (analogous to the US FDPR) determining when a foreign product is captured. **Use:** gives you a concrete, escalating "list of seventeen" (of the 17 lanthanides + Sc/Y, twelve are now explicitly named in Chinese licensing rules across the April and October 2025 rounds) — strong thematic hook for the chapter title. **Limitation:** I could not directly confirm the precise "50%" de minimis threshold language from a primary MOFCOM text; White & Case's alert framed it as extraterritorial jurisdiction plus a value-content rule — verify exact percentage and mechanics before quoting.

- A second set of four MOFCOM/Customs announcements (**Nos. 55–58 of 2025**), effective **November 8, 2025**, extended controls to superhard materials, rare-earth processing equipment and raw materials, medium- and heavy-rare-earth products, and lithium-battery/artificial-graphite anode materials. Source: same WebSearch synthesis of the Squire Patton Boggs / CMS Law / China Briefing coverage — [CMS Law](https://cms.law/en/chn/legal-updates/china-tightens-export-controls-on-rare-earths-takeaways-for-global-businesses). **Use:** shows the controls cascading beyond rare earths into the whole battery/magnet ecosystem within a month.

- Market reaction was immediate and dramatic: **MP Materials stock hit an all-time high of $100.25 on October 14, 2025** (up 431% year-to-date); **Lynas Rare Earths' OTC shares traded at $14.00 on October 20, 2025** (up 249% YTD). Source: [WebSearch synthesis, Oct 2025 rare-earth stock coverage](https://www.marketindex.com.au/news/why-lynas-iluka-and-rare-earth-stocks-have-more-than-doubled-in-recent); NdPr prices projected to peak at **US$110–120/kg by early 2027**. **Use:** vivid, checkable market numbers for the cold open — verify exact closing prices against a financial data terminal (Bloomberg/Yahoo Finance historical) before publication, since these came from a secondary aggregator, not the exchange itself.

- **China holds roughly 69% of global rare-earth mine production and ~91% of global separation/refining capacity** (2024 figures), and **94% of global sintered rare-earth permanent-magnet production**. Source: [IEA data via WebSearch synthesis](https://www.iea.org/data-and-statistics/charts/china-s-share-in-rare-earth-magnet-production-2024); also cited by Mining Technology (~69% mine share). **Use:** the "why this is a chokepoint" number — the mining share is not even the real story; refining/magnet-making concentration is far more extreme, and that's what the export controls actually leverage.

- China's April 2025 controls (Ministry of Commerce, **April 4, 2025**) were explicitly framed as retaliation for new Trump tariffs, restricting exports of **scandium, yttrium, samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, and lutetium** and requiring license applications; applications "linked to foreign military programmes are automatically rejected." Source: [WebSearch synthesis of RSIS/CSIS coverage](https://rsis.edu.sg/rsis-publication/rsis/analysing-chinas-2025-rare-earth-export-controls/); European dysprosium prices reportedly reached **up to six times** the Chinese domestic price after the controls, and several automakers cut production within weeks. **Use:** shows April 2025 as the real opening shot before the October escalation — good for establishing the "this had already happened once this year" beat in the cold open.

- License-approval rates for European firms fell **below 25%** during the 2025–26 tightening, and the IEA estimated full implementation of the controls could put **$6.5 trillion of downstream production outside China at risk** across autos, high-tech, defense, and energy. Source: [WebSearch synthesis, "China's 2026 Export Controls Redraw the Global Supply Chain Map"](https://rareearthexchanges.com/news/chinas-2026-export-controls-redraw-the-global-supply-chain-map/) [secondary — trade-press aggregator, verify $6.5T figure against actual IEA report/commentary directly, e.g. iea.org/commentaries — WebFetch was blocked on iea.org in this session]. **Use:** big attention-grabbing number, but flag as needing primary-source verification — it's plausible IEA methodology but I could not confirm it in the IEA's own text.

- **China-Japan trade truce, October 30, 2025 (Busan, South Korea):** Trump and Xi agreed China would **suspend the October 9 rare-earth export controls for one year**, alongside a reduction of US tariffs on Chinese goods from 57% to 47%, and a cut in the fentanyl-related tariff from 20% to 10%. Confirmed by CNBC on the deal terms, and by CNBC on Nov. 10, 2025 that China had begun implementing the suspension. Sources: [CNBC, Oct 30, 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/30/trump-xi-south-korea-rare-earth-tariff-trade-war-nvidia.html); [CNBC, Nov 10, 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/10/china-suspends-some-critical-mineral-export-curbs-to-the-us-as-trade-truce-takes-hold.html); [White House fact sheet](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/11/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-strikes-deal-on-economic-and-trade-relations-with-china/). **Use:** the "weapon sheathed, not disarmed" ending beat for the cold open — the suspension is temporary (one year) and US-specific, not a global rollback.

- **2026 follow-up:** In **June 2026**, China added **10 US entities to its export-control list, including rare-earth miners MP Materials and USA Rare Earth**, in retaliation for the US adding Chinese tech firms to a military blacklist. Separately, in **January 2026** China tightened dual-use export controls affecting Japanese entities and military end-users, and in **May 2026**, two Japanese nationals were detained in Dalian on alleged export-control-violation smuggling charges — reportedly one of the first such detentions of foreign nationals in this context. Source: [Washington Post, June 22, 2026, via WebSearch](https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/06/22/china-takes-aim-us-rare-earth-companies-with-new-export-controls/); [Morgan Lewis, "Recent China Export Control Actions..." July 2026](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/07/recent-china-export-control-actions-signal-active-enforcement-for-rare-earths-and-strategic-minerals). **Use:** shows the "one-year truce" already fraying with new bilateral targeting even before the year is up — good material for a 2026 coda in Ch.1 or Ch.9. **Verify directly** with Washington Post original reporting before quoting the detention detail — that's a serious, checkable claim.

### Narrative material (vivid anecdotes / numbers for prose)

- The scale of price collapse used to illustrate how fast a chokepoint bites: after China's July 2023 gallium/germanium license requirement, **China's gallium exports fell from 6,876 kg in July 2023 to just 227 kg in October 2023**; **germanium exports fell from 7,965 kg to 590 kg** in the same window — essentially a 97% collapse in monthly export volumes within three months. Source: [WebSearch synthesis of USITC/Global Trade Alert data](https://www.usitc.gov/publications/332/executive_briefings/ebot_germanium_and_gallium.pdf). This is a strong "the tap was turned off" scene-setting statistic — cite the USITC executive briefing directly as primary source.
- **Global gallium prices jumped 27% in the single week after Beijing's July 3, 2023 announcement**, then rose 68–114% over the following months (assessed Rotterdam price moved from roughly $250–265/kg to $500–600/kg by mid-December 2023). Source: [Fastmarkets via WebSearch](https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/chinas-tighter-gallium-germanium-export-controls-more-of-the-same-or-a-shift-in-approach/).
- **Antimony's price rose from about $1,400/tonne in July 2024 to roughly $38,000/tonne by September 2024** — a ~2,600% increase — after China's August 15, 2024 announcement (Announcement 2024/33) of licensing controls effective September 15, 2024. China supplies **48% of global antimony production and 63% of US antimony imports**; the metal is essential for armor-piercing ammunition, night-vision goggles, infrared sensors, and precision optics. Source: [WebSearch synthesis of CSIS/Global Trade Alert coverage](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-antimony-export-restrictions-impact-us-national-security) [secondary — verify the $38,000/tonne figure against a metals-price database like Fastmarkets or Argus directly; that number is eye-popping and needs a hard primary check]. **Use:** the single most dramatic price statistic found in this research — but flag as needing verification given how extreme it is (some reports describe antimony rising "200%" with a "97% volume drop" instead — there may be different metrics being conflated across sources; reconcile before using).
- Concrete defense-hardware numbers: a single **F-35 fighter jet contains roughly 400–435 kg of rare-earth materials**; a **Virginia-class attack submarine uses ~4,600 kg (9,200 lb) of rare earths**; a **DDG-51 Arleigh Burke destroyer uses ~2,360–2,600 kg (5,200 lb)**. Source: CRS report "Rare Earth Elements in National Defense" (Sept. 2013) via [EveryCRSReport PDF](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20130917_R41744_f524dd012737e617d9a79c8bf3a62ad071a52904.pdf); F-35 figure via [WisdomTree blog](https://www.wisdomtree.com/us/insights/blog/the-400-kilogram-secret-inside-every-f-35) and [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-new-rare-earth-and-magnet-restrictions-threaten-us-defense-supply-chains). **Note:** the CRS report is from 2013 — figures are old but still the most-cited baseline; a 2025-era number for F-35 rare-earth content would strengthen this if found in a more recent DoD or GAO document.
- By mid-2025, **only about 25% of Chinese rare-earth export license applications were reportedly being approved**, causing US aerospace manufacturers using yttrium as a thermal coating to "ration material." Source: [WebSearch synthesis, CSIS "China's New Rare Earth and Magnet Restrictions"](https://www.csis.org/analysis/chinas-new-rare-earth-and-magnet-restrictions-threaten-us-defense-supply-chains) [secondary — WebFetch blocked on csis.org, verify directly].
- The **US Department of Defense's July 11, 2025 deal with MP Materials**: DoD invested **$400 million** in convertible preferred equity (becoming MP's largest shareholder, ~15% stake, conversion price $30.03/share), and separately created a **price floor of $110/kg for NdPr products**, running for roughly 10 years (Oct. 1, 2025–Dec. 31, 2035) — the government pays MP the shortfall if the market price falls below $110/kg. Source: [MP Materials investor press release](https://investors.mpmaterials.com/investor-news/news-details/2025/MP-Materials-Announces-Transformational-Public-Private-Partnership-with-the-Department-of-Defense-to-Accelerate-U-S--Rare-Earth-Magnet-Independence/default.aspx); [FAS explainer](https://fas.org/publication/unpacking-dod-and-mp-partnership/). **Use:** vivid illustration of how seriously Washington took the threat — the US government effectively became a commodities-price guarantor for one company, an extraordinary intervention worth dramatizing.

### Counterevidence / complications

- **The controls are not a blanket ban but a licensing regime**, and China has repeatedly used "carve-outs," slow-walked approvals, and selective enforcement rather than an outright embargo — giving Beijing calibrated escalation/de-escalation room (visible in the Oct. 2025 → Nov. 2025 one-year US suspension). This is the crux of why analysts increasingly describe it as a *precision* tool, not a blunt one.
- The **PIIE analysis ("China's export controls on critical minerals aren't starving the United States—at least so far," 2024)** argues the earlier gallium/germanium/graphite controls had **limited practical effect on the US specifically** because the US imports relatively little of these directly from China (much comes via intermediate countries or downstream products), and alternative suppliers/stockpiles cushioned the blow. Source: [PIIE](https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2024/chinas-export-controls-critical-minerals-arent-starving-united-states) [WebFetch blocked — re-fetch directly for exact figures before citing in prose].
- One-year truce (Oct. 30, 2025) shows the weapon is **negotiable and reversible** — undercutting any narrative that China's controls are an irreversible or purely ideological chokehold; they function as *leverage in an ongoing negotiation*, not a fixed embargo.

## CHAPTER 2 — "Everything Runs on Rock" (the critical-minerals framework)

### Key facts and sources

- **USGS's 2025 Final List of Critical Minerals contains 60 mineral commodities** (up from 50 on the 2022 list), including antimony, cobalt, copper *(added new in 2025)*, gallium, germanium, graphite, all the individual lanthanides plus scandium/yttrium, lithium, nickel, and more. Source: [USGS news release, "Department of the Interior releases draft 2025 List of Critical Minerals"](https://www.usgs.gov/news/science-snippet/department-interior-releases-draft-2025-list-critical-minerals); [Federal Register, Final 2025 List of Critical Minerals, published Nov. 7, 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/11/07/2025-19813/final-2025-list-of-critical-minerals) [WebFetch blocked on federalregister.gov — re-fetch directly, this is a key primary source for Ch.2]. **Note:** copper's *addition* to the 2025 US critical-minerals list (it wasn't previously classified as "critical" by USGS) is itself a notable, citable data point about how AI/data-center/grid demand has changed Washington's threat calculus.
- The **statutory definition of "critical mineral"** comes from the **Energy Act of 2020**: a non-fuel mineral or mineral material essential to the economic or national security of the US, with a supply chain vulnerable to disruption, and that serves an essential function in a product whose absence would have significant consequences for economic or national security. USGS/Interior use a quantitative methodology (economic-effects modeling of a trade-disruption scenario, plus a "single point of failure" test for sole domestic producers). Source: [Congress.gov CRS product IF13145, "U.S. Geological Survey's Critical Minerals List"](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF13145) [WebFetch blocked — re-fetch directly for exact statutory language]. **Use:** this is the closest thing to an "official" one-paragraph definition for the book's framework-setting section.
- **IEA's Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025** (published **May 21, 2025**) projects: in the Stated Policies Scenario, **lithium demand grows fivefold from today to 2040**; **graphite and nickel demand roughly double**; **cobalt and rare-earth-element demand grow 50–60%**; **copper demand grows about 30%** — all by 2040. In 2024 alone, lithium demand rose **nearly 30%** year-on-year, while nickel, cobalt, graphite, and rare earths grew **6–8%**. Source: [IEA, Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025) [WebFetch blocked on iea.org — re-verify directly, this is the single most important primary source for Ch.2 and should be fetched/read in full before drafting].
- **Copper supply risk:** the IEA projects a potential **30% supply shortfall by 2035** for copper due to declining ore grades, rising capital costs, limited new discoveries, and long mine-development lead times — despite copper being needed at growing volumes for grid buildout, EVs, and data centers. Source: [IEA / WebSearch synthesis](https://theoregongroup.com/commodities/copper/ai-data-centers-to-drive-2-of-global-copper-demand-by-2030/); nearly **60% of refined copper comes from just three countries**, illustrating concentration risk beyond China (this one is about smelting/refining geography broadly, e.g., China, Chile, DRC-linked supply chains — verify exact three countries against IEA source).
- **AI data centers and copper:** IEA analysis estimates data-center buildout could add roughly **2% to global copper demand, 3% to rare-earth-element demand, and up to 11% to gallium demand by 2030**. Absolute data-center copper demand is projected to reach **512 kilotonnes by 2030** (with **75 kilotonnes of silicon** also cited), and one estimate has data-center copper demand averaging **400,000 tonnes/year over the coming decade, peaking near 572,000 tonnes in 2028**. Source: [WebSearch synthesis of IEA-derived reporting, e.g. USFunds/Forbes coverage of a Dec. 2025 report](https://www.forbes.com/sites/greatspeculations/2025/12/08/data-centers-could-gobble-half-a-million-tons-of-copper-a-year-by-2030/) [secondary — verify against IEA's own data-center electricity/minerals analysis directly]. Global data-center electricity consumption is projected by IEA to **more than double from ~415 TWh (2024) to ~945 TWh by 2030**, growing ~15%/year — about 4x faster than electricity demand overall. **Use:** the clean, checkable throughline for why AI directly drives mineral demand: more compute → more power → more copper (wiring, transformers, cooling) and more gallium (semiconductors).
- **EU Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA):** adopted **March 16, 2023**, entered into force **May 23, 2024**. It designates **34 "critical raw materials"** and, within that, **17 "strategic raw materials"** (Annexes I and II) — the strategic list includes bismuth, boron, cobalt, copper, gallium, germanium, battery-grade lithium, magnesium metal, battery-grade manganese, battery-grade natural graphite, battery-grade nickel, platinum-group metals, "rare earth elements for magnets," silicon metal, titanium metal, and tungsten. Source: [Wikipedia summary via WebSearch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_Raw_Materials_Act); [criticalstrategicmetals.com guide](https://criticalstrategicmetals.com/lists/european-union-critical-raw-materials-act/strategic-raw-materials/). List reviewed every 4 years. **Use:** for the "three governments, three overlapping-but-different lists" framing device — USGS (60), IEA (its own critical-minerals-for-clean-energy set), EU CRMA (34/17) — showing the same handful of elements (rare earths, cobalt, lithium, nickel, gallium, graphite, copper) recur across every list regardless of methodology, which is exactly the book's "everything runs on rock" thesis.
- **Wind turbines and rare earths:** a large direct-drive offshore wind turbine can contain **upwards of 5 tonnes of permanent magnets**; each megawatt of direct-drive offshore capacity requires roughly **220–240 kg of neodymium-praseodymium oxide**. If ~380 GW of new offshore wind is installed by 2032 (GWEC estimate), that implies roughly **83,600 tonnes of NdPr** demand over the decade. Demand for Nd, Pr, and Dy in permanent magnets overall is projected to rise **4–7x by 2040 versus 2018 levels**, and Adamas Intelligence has forecast a potential **90,000-tonne/year NdPr shortfall by 2040**. Source: [Fastmarkets/Grist/CT Mirror coverage via WebSearch](https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/offshore-wind-industry-in-need-of-rare-earth-magnets/); [JRC EU report on rare earths in wind/EVs](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC122671/jrc122671_the_role_of_rare_earth_elements_in_wind_energy_and_electric_mobility_2.pdf).
- **EVs and battery minerals (IEA Global EV Outlook):** EV battery demand is projected to grow **4.5x by 2030 vs. 2023** under Stated Policies; lithium demand for EVs is projected to reach **~750 kilotonnes by 2040**, more than triple 2024 demand. Nickel and cobalt projections have actually been *revised downward* by the IEA in recent editions relative to earlier forecasts (see counterevidence below) — a genuine complication for a simple "demand only ever rises" narrative. Source: [IEA Global EV Outlook 2025/2026, via WebSearch synthesis](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-batteries) [WebFetch blocked — verify directly].

### Narrative material

- The "same short list, everywhere" framing device: **neodymium and praseodymium** show up in wind-turbine nacelles, EV drive motors, and precision-guided-missile actuators; **gallium** shows up in both solar cells and the compound semiconductors (GaN) used in 5G/military radar and AI data-center power electronics; **copper** is the single largest-volume constraint across literally every category (grid, EV, data center, defense wiring). This convergence — one short list of ~20–30 elements underpinning AI, EVs, wind, and weapons simultaneously — is the mechanical reason a single country's export-licensing pen can touch so many industries at once. Good scene: picture a Boeing engineer, a Tesla battery engineer, and an offshore-wind turbine technician all needing an export license from the same Beijing ministry office in the same week.
- Copper's 2025 promotion onto the USGS critical-minerals list — after AI data centers pushed it there — is a small but telling narrative beat: the "criticality" of a mineral is not fixed by geology, it's a *policy judgment* that moves as new demand (in this case, the AI boom) reshapes what counts as strategically vulnerable.

### Counterevidence / complications

- **Nickel and cobalt demand forecasts have been revised DOWN, not up**, by the IEA in recent Global EV Outlook editions — a genuine complication to any narrative of ever-rising demand for every mineral; battery chemistry shifts (LFP batteries needing no nickel/cobalt) are undercutting demand for those two specific minerals even as lithium, copper, and rare earths keep climbing. This is worth a caveat paragraph in Ch.2 to avoid overstating uniform scarcity across all "critical minerals."
- Recycling could materially blunt long-run demand growth: IEA's Announced Pledges Scenario projects recycling could cut 2050 lithium/nickel demand by ~25% and cobalt demand by ~40% relative to a no-recycling baseline — a genuine "this isn't destiny" counterpoint to worst-case scarcity narratives. [Same IEA EV Outlook source, WebFetch-blocked, verify directly.]
- Different lists (USGS 60, EU CRMA 34/17, IEA's own set) do **not perfectly overlap** — e.g., the US list includes things like potash, metallurgical coal, and uranium that aren't primarily "energy transition" minerals, showing "critical mineral" is a broader national-security category, not a synonym for "green-tech mineral." Worth flagging so the book doesn't conflate the two.

## CHAPTER 9 — "The Weaponization of Supply Chains" (2010 embargo + 2023–2026 licensing history)

### Key facts and sources

- **The trigger event:** on **September 7, 2010**, a Chinese fishing trawler collided with two Japan Coast Guard vessels near the Senkaku/Diaoyu Islands; the captain, Zhan Qixiong, was arrested by Japan. Source: [Wikipedia "2010 Senkaku boat collision incident," via WebSearch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Senkaku_boat_collision_incident); also documented in the [StratCom COE report "Economic Leverage: The 2010 Senkaku Crisis"](https://stratcomcoe.org/cuploads/pfiles/senkaku_crisis.pdf) and [AMTI/CSIS "Counter-Coercion Series: Senkaku Islands Trawler Collision"](https://amti.csis.org/counter-co-trawler-collision/).
- China's response: beyond diplomatic protests, Beijing **suspended talks on expanding bilateral air routes, cut back tourist-group travel to Japan, and — most consequentially — halted customs clearance of rare-earth shipments to Japan**, though Beijing never issued a formal, public written export ban (customs simply stopped clearing REE shipments, giving Beijing deniability). Japan at the time depended on China for **roughly 90% of its rare-earth imports**. Source: [WebSearch synthesis of AMTI/StratCom coverage](https://stratcomcoe.org/cuploads/pfiles/senkaku_crisis.pdf). Rare-earth **prices reportedly rose roughly 10x within a year** of the incident (other sources cite dysprosium/other individual oxides rising over 700%). **Use:** the canonical "weaponization" case study — but note the deniability angle (no formal ban) is itself a key narrative and analytical point.
- **Resolution:** the captain was released by Japanese prosecutors under diplomatic pressure; Japanese PM Naoto Kan reportedly informed President Obama of the release, helping de-escalate. Source: [WebSearch synthesis, Wikipedia/AMTI].
- **Japan's structural response** — the book's "how do you de-weaponize a chokepoint" case study: in **March 2011**, Sojitz Corporation and **JOGMEC (Japan Organization for Metals and Energy Security)** jointly provided **US$250 million** in loan and equity financing to Australia's **Lynas Corporation**, through a vehicle called **JARE (Japan Australia Rare Earth)**, co-founded in 2011 — in exchange, Sojitz secured rights to distribute Lynas's rare-earth output to Japanese customers. Source: [JOGMEC news release](https://www.jogmec.go.jp/english/news/release/news_10_00029.html); [Sojitz Corporation news releases](https://www.sojitz.com/en/news/article/20230307.html) (JARE/Lynas relationship was subsequently expanded with additional equity investments in 2022 and again reported as of March 2026). Separately, the Japanese government passed a **¥100 billion (~$1.2 billion) supplemental budget** for rare-earth source diversification, recycling technology, and stockpiling. Source: [WebSearch synthesis]. **Use:** this is a genuinely excellent, underused narrative — Japan's decade-plus, patient, government-backed diversification into Lynas as the direct institutional legacy of the 2010 shock, still being reinforced as late as March 2026.
- **Full China rare-earth export-control timeline, 2023–2026** (chronological spine for Ch.9's back half):
  - **July 3, 2023** — MOFCOM/Customs announce licensing controls on gallium- and germanium-related items, effective **August 1, 2023**. China then produced ~98% of world refined gallium/germanium supply. Source: [IEA policy database](https://www.iea.org/policies/17893-announcement-on-the-implementation-of-export-control-of-items-related-to-gallium-and-germanium); [USITC executive briefing](https://www.usitc.gov/publications/332/executive_briefings/ebot_germanium_and_gallium.pdf).
  - **October 20, 2023** — China announces graphite export controls (framed explicitly as a response to widened US semiconductor export controls); controls take effect **December 1, 2023** on high-purity/high-strength/high-density synthetic graphite and natural flake/spherical/expanded graphite products; licenses must be renewed every 6 months. MOFCOM publicly stated the move was "common international practice," not targeted at any country. Source: [scio.gov.cn official announcement](http://english.scio.gov.cn/m/pressroom/2023-10/20/content_116763338.htm); [scio.gov.cn follow-up, Dec 1, 2023](http://english.scio.gov.cn/pressroom/2023-12/01/content_116851500.htm).
  - **August 15, 2024** — MOFCOM Announcement 2024/33 imposes licensing controls on **antimony** (17 HS codes), effective **September 15, 2024**. China supplies ~48% of global antimony production. Source: [Global Trade Alert](https://globaltradealert.org/state-act/88204-china-government-announces-export-control-measures-for-antimony-and-related-items).
  - **December 2024** — China escalates from licensing to an outright **prohibition, in principle, on exports of gallium, germanium, antimony, and superhard materials specifically to the United States** (retaliation for expanded US chip-export restrictions). Source: [WebSearch synthesis, "China halts ban on gallium, germanium, antimony exports to US, but controls remain" — SME Mining Engineering](https://me.smenet.org/china-halts-ban-on-gallium-germanium-antimony-exports-to-us-but-controls-remain/).
  - **April 4, 2025** — MOFCOM imposes licensing controls on seven medium/heavy rare earths (Sc, Y, Sm, Gd, Tb, Dy, Lu) in retaliation for new Trump tariffs; foreign-military-linked applications automatically rejected.

- **The 2012–2015 WTO dispute — essential prior history for Ch.9:** In **March 2012**, the US, EU, and Japan jointly filed a WTO complaint (**DS431** and related cases) against China's rare-earth/tungsten/molybdenum export quotas and duties. On **March 26, 2014**, a WTO panel ruled against China; the Appellate Body upheld that ruling in **August 2014**. In **January 2015**, China's MOFCOM formally abolished its rare-earth export quota system. Source: [WTO DS431 case page](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/cases_e/ds431_e.htm); [USTR press release, March 2014](https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2014/March/US-wins-victory-in-rare-earths-dispute-with-China); [European Parliament think tank brief](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/thinktank/en/document/EXPO-INTA_SP(2014)522337). **Use:** critical structural point — China's *current* (2023–2026) toolkit is legally different from its pre-2015 quota system precisely *because* the WTO ruled the old quota/tariff approach illegal. The new approach (individualized export licensing justified on "national security" grounds, similar to how the US justifies its own export controls) is a more WTO-resistant legal architecture, learned directly from the 2014 loss. This is an important analytical thread for the chapter — China didn't just get more aggressive, it got legally smarter.
- **China's export-quota history that set up the 2010 crisis:** quotas were first imposed in 1999 (initially to *raise* prices for Chinese exporters, not restrict supply to foreign buyers); from 2006–2009 China cut quotas 5–10%/year; in **2010** it cut the total quota **40% versus 2009**, coinciding with the Senkaku incident. Source: [WebSearch synthesis, Global Asia / factsanddetails.com / Intereconomics].
- **Origins of Chinese rare-earth dominance — the price-war backstory:** China built its market position in the 1980s–1990s partly by **subsidizing production and undercutting global prices**, driving Western competitors out of business. The US's only major rare-earth mine, **Molycorp's Mountain Pass facility in California, closed operations by 1998–2002** after a toxic-waste spill (300,000 gallons of tailings) combined with unsustainable competition from cheap Chinese supply; it did not reopen commercially at scale until the 2010s (in direct response to the 2010 shock). Source: [WebSearch synthesis, capitalresearch.org / NPR / Time]. **Use:** essential prehistory for Ch.9 — China's leverage wasn't created by the 2010 embargo, it was *revealed* by it; the market concentration had been built for two decades prior through classic predatory pricing.

### Narrative material

- The deniability tactic in 2010 — Beijing never announced a formal ban, customs simply stopped processing paperwork — is a great scene of how modern economic coercion actually works: not a dramatic edict, but a bureaucratic slowdown that achieves the same result while preserving plausible deniability at the WTO and in diplomatic communiqués. This pattern echoes directly into the 2023–2025 "licensing, not banning" approach — Ch.9 can draw a direct throughline from 2010 customs foot-dragging to 2023-25 formal-but-slow-walked licensing.
- The Japan long-game: JOGMEC/Sojitz's US$250 million Lynas investment in 2011, expanded again in 2022 and 2026, is a 15-year, still-ongoing institutional response — a good closing beat showing that "de-weaponizing" a chokepoint isn't a single policy but a generational commitment.
- The legal-learning arc (WTO loss in 2014 → national-security-justified licensing regime by 2023) is a genuinely underused, analytically sharp narrative spine: China effectively re-engineered its coercive toolkit to be lawsuit-proof.

### Counterevidence / complications

- **The East Asia Forum piece ("Did China really ban rare earth metals exports to Japan?")** is directly relevant counter-narrative material questioning whether 2010 was a deliberate, centrally-ordered embargo at all, versus overzealous/uncoordinated customs enforcement amid diplomatic tension — worth reading in full during drafting (I could not WebFetch it in this session due to network restrictions; **flagged for direct follow-up**: https://eastasiaforum.org/2013/08/18/did-china-really-ban-rare-earth-metals-exports-to-japan/).
- **PIIE's 2024 analysis** argues the 2023-24 gallium/germanium/graphite controls had **limited practical effect on the US** specifically, because of low direct US import dependence on Chinese supply for those particular items and quick diversification — a genuine complicating data point against the "China's export controls are devastatingly effective" framing (re-verify directly: https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2024/chinas-export-controls-critical-minerals-arent-starving-united-states).
- The **October 30, 2025 Busan truce** (one-year suspension) itself is evidence the controls function as **negotiating leverage that gets traded away**, not a fixed strategic weapon Beijing is unwilling to holster — complicating any narrative that frames China's rare-earth policy as pure, unconditional economic warfare rather than one lever among several in an ongoing bilateral negotiation.
- Markets adapted faster than expected in some cases: a Fastmarkets retrospective found markets "largely adjusted" to the germanium/gallium/graphite controls **one year on** (i.e., by late 2024), suggesting the initial 2023 price shocks were not permanently sustained — worth checking directly for the "shock fades" narrative arc: https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/markets-largely-adjusted-to-chinas-germanium-gallium-graphite-export-controls-one-year-on/

## THE DENG XIAOPING QUOTE — verification/debunking

**Claim under test:** Deng Xiaoping said something like "The Middle East has oil, China has rare earths."

- **Best available sourcing:** Multiple secondary sources (China National Radio's own website, per researcher **Andrew S. Erickson**) place the remark in a **speech during Deng's Southern Tour, dated January 1992**, reportedly made in or near **Jiangxi/Baotou, Inner Mongolia**, where Deng is quoted elaborating on China's rare-earth reserves (~80% of then-identified global reserves). Source: [Andrew S. Erickson's analysis, "China's Rare Earth Dominance: How Usable a Weapon?"](https://www.andrewerickson.com/2019/06/chinas-rare-earth-dominance-how-usable-a-weapon/), reprinted at [The National Interest](https://nationalinterest.org/blog/buzz/chinas-rare-earth-dominance-how-usable-weapon-61307).
- **Dating is genuinely disputed even among specialists:** some accounts place the remark in **1987** rather than 1992; Erickson's sourcing (citing a Chinese state-media photo/reference) favors January 1992, but there is no single, universally agreed primary transcript pinned down across the sources found. **This is exactly the kind of "poorly sourced" situation the assignment asked to flag** — treat the quote as **widely repeated but weakly primary-sourced**; no contemporaneous, independently verifiable transcript or recording has surfaced in this research pass. Source discussing the historiography problem directly: [Domino Theory, "How a Deng Xiaoping Quote Became China Watchers' Favorite Anecdote"](https://dominotheory.com/how-a-deng-xiaoping-quote-became-china-watchers-favorite-anecdote/) [**WebFetch was blocked on this domain — this is the single most important source to physically read before writing about the quote; strongly recommend the author or a research assistant access it directly outside this session**], and a related MetaFilter discussion thread interrogating the same question: [ask.metafilter.com/132042](https://ask.metafilter.com/132042/Did-Deng-Xiaoping-say-There-is-oil-in-the-Middle-East-there-is-rare-earth-in-China).
- **Interpretive caveat — the "weapon" framing is contested even if the quote is authentic:** geographer **Julie Klinger** (University of Delaware), a leading academic historian of China's rare-earth industry, is cited (via WebSearch synthesis of secondary discussion) as arguing the common reading of Deng's line as a *deliberate geopolitical-weapon doctrine* is a misinterpretation — that Deng was more likely expressing recognition of a **potential strategic economic asset** (in the spirit of his broader 1992 Southern Tour push for market-oriented development) rather than announcing an OPEC-style coercion strategy. **Use:** this is valuable, citable pushback against the "ancient prophecy of resource war" framing that a lot of pop-geopolitics writing uses uncritically — worth a paragraph noting the quote is real-ish but its meaning has been retrofitted by decades of later analysts. **Recommend directly locating and reading Klinger's own scholarly work** (she has published academic articles/a book, "Rare Earth Frontiers," on this topic) rather than relying solely on secondary paraphrase.

**Bottom line for the book:** Treat the Deng quote as **probably real in substance, imprecisely dated (1987 vs. January 1992), never confirmed against a primary transcript in this research pass, and very likely over-interpreted** by decades of subsequent commentary as a statement of coercive intent it may not have originally carried. It is safe to use as an *illustration of how the idea took hold in Western China-watching discourse* — but risky to present as an unimpeachable, verbatim historical fact without the caveat. **Follow-up task before drafting:** get access to Julie Klinger's *Rare Earth Frontiers* (Cornell University Press) and/or the Domino Theory piece directly, since this session's network policy blocked both dominotheory.com and (indirectly) full verification of the primary Chinese-language source.
