# Chapter 9 research note — weaponization of mineral supply chains

Research checked September 3, 2026. This note records the factual spine, contested points, and strongest counterevidence for Chapter 9, “The Weaponization of Supply Chains.”

## 2010: what can safely be said

Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry provides the cleanest official retrospective on the 2010 rare-earth episode. Its WTO trade-policy review records that China cut its overall rare-earth export quota for 2010 to about 30,000 tonnes, roughly 40% below the prior year. It separately records that rare-earth exports bound for Japan were reportedly stalled from September 21, 2010 as Chinese customs procedures tightened. Contemporary reports described the disruption as retaliation for the Senkaku/Diaoyu fishing-boat collision, while China’s Ministry of Commerce denied imposing an embargo and said customs procedures had been strengthened to prevent unlawful exports. METI says the stagnation continued for nearly two months.

Source: METI, `Chapter 3 Reference: Export Restrictions` / WTO trade-policy review
https://www.meti.go.jp/english/report/data/2016WTO/pdf/02_05.pdf

A 2011 METI review makes the same boundary explicit: media called the episode an embargo, China denied an embargo, and the export slowdown to Japan was remedied after roughly two months.

Source: METI, `Part II Chapter 3 Quantitative Restrictions`
https://www.meti.go.jp/english/report/downloadfiles/2011WTO/2-3-2ExportRestriction.pdf

Boundary: the chapter should not say China formally announced a Japan-specific rare-earth embargo in 2010. The stronger and better-supported point is that a country-specific customs slowdown occurred during a diplomatic crisis, while the formal status and stated motive were disputed in real time.

## Japan’s adaptation after 2010

METI’s 2025 White Paper on International Economy and Trade says the 2010 Chinese rare-earth export restrictions became a catalyst for Japanese supply-chain resilience policy. Japan pursued alternative upstream projects, material-saving R&D, and WTO action. METI reports that China’s share of Japan’s total rare-earth imports fell from 85% in 2009 to 58% in 2020.

Source: METI, `White Paper on International Economy and Trade 2025 — Supply-chain resilience and critical minerals`
https://www.meti.go.jp/report/tsuhaku2025/2025honbun/i2140000.html

The IEA’s 2026 rare-earth report adds an important demand-side result: after the 2010 controls, Japanese policies coordinated with industry helped reduce total rare-earth demand by about 30% from 2010 levels through material efficiency, substitution, recycling, and related responses.

Source: International Energy Agency, `Rare Earth Elements — Executive summary`, 2026
https://www.iea.org/reports/rare-earth-elements/executive-summary

Counterargument: using leverage can work in the short run while degrading the leverage over the long run. The 2010 episode did not end China’s dominance, but it made diversification and substitution politically and economically easier to justify.

## WTO dispute: controls can collide with trade obligations

In 2012 the United States, European Union, and Japan challenged Chinese export restrictions on rare earths, tungsten, and molybdenum. The WTO panel and Appellate Body found key export duties and quotas inconsistent with China’s WTO obligations. China said it removed the challenged duties, quotas, and trading-right restrictions by 2015.

Source: WTO, `China — Measures Related to the Exportation of Rare Earths, Tungsten and Molybdenum (DS431)`
https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/cases_e/ds431_e.htm

Boundary: this WTO case concerned a broader export-control regime, not a legal adjudication of the disputed 2010 Japan-specific customs slowdown. Do not collapse those two events into one finding.

## 2023: licensing replaces the dramatic embargo image

On July 3, 2023, China’s Ministry of Commerce and General Administration of Customs announced export controls on gallium- and germanium-related items, effective August 1. Exporters were required to obtain licenses. MOFCOM said the controls were intended to safeguard national security and fulfill international obligations and stressed that export control did not mean a blanket prohibition.

Sources:
- MOFCOM / GACC Announcement No. 23 of 2023
https://dcj.mofcom.gov.cn/article/zcfb/zcblgg/202307/20230703419666.shtml
- MOFCOM press conference, July 6, 2023
https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/News/PressConference/art/2023/art_36fb2d80e4b4453891bb8fc83e2b3c4e.html

MOFCOM later said it had approved some gallium and germanium export applications. This is important because modern supply leverage often operates through licensing uncertainty and delay rather than a theatrical zero-export decree.

Source: MOFCOM press conference, November 30, 2023
https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/News/PressConference/art/2023/art_cd46d6e56bb6486a928ea2f16182ae90.html

## 2023 graphite: another licensing system, not a universal ban

In October 2023 China adjusted controls on graphite products, placing high-purity/high-strength/high-density artificial graphite and natural flake graphite and related products under export-control licensing from December 1. MOFCOM said the policy did not target a particular country or region and that compliant exports could be licensed.

Sources:
- MOFCOM / GACC Announcement No. 39 of 2023
https://interview.mofcom.gov.cn/mofcom_interview/front/swfgk/article?id=20231003447368
- MOFCOM spokesperson, October 20, 2023
https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/xwfb/art/2023/art_8114f91bafb3454b970857b825e8e44a.html

In January 2024 MOFCOM said it had approved qualified graphite exporters, including applications involving South Korean battery manufacturers.

Source: MOFCOM press conference, January 18, 2024
https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/News/PressConference/art/2024/art_7e1282d1fc37494aaa51bc84bb9b8014.html

## December 2024: country-specific tightening against the United States

On December 3, 2024, MOFCOM Announcement No. 46 explicitly tightened controls on dual-use exports to the United States. It prohibited dual-use items for U.S. military users or military uses; said gallium-, germanium-, antimony-, and superhard-material dual-use exports to the United States would in principle not be licensed; and imposed stricter end-user/end-use review on graphite.

Source: MOFCOM Announcement No. 46 of 2024
https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zfxxgk/gkml/art/2024/art_99f5d51bbe6a412ca309d5818c4dee52.html

This is much cleaner evidence of targeted economic-security leverage than retroactively labeling the disputed 2010 episode a formally declared embargo.

## April 2025: rare-earth controls become operationally visible downstream

On April 4, 2025, MOFCOM and Chinese customs placed samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, yttrium, and related metals, compounds, targets, and certain permanent-magnet materials under export controls requiring licenses.

Source: MOFCOM / GACC Announcement No. 18 of 2025
https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/Policies/AnnouncementsOrders/art/2025/art_0dd87cbee7b045bf93fabe6ab2faceee.html

The IEA reports that export volumes of affected rare earths and permanent magnets fell sharply in April and May 2025. Automakers in the United States, Europe, and elsewhere struggled to source magnets; some reduced utilisation or temporarily stopped production. Licences were later granted and trade recovered, but premiums for magnets produced outside China persisted.

Source: IEA, `Rare Earth Elements — Executive summary`, 2026
https://www.iea.org/reports/rare-earth-elements/executive-summary

The significance is not that every controlled kilogram stopped moving. The significance is that a license queue at a concentrated midstream stage could transmit quickly into factory schedules thousands of kilometres away.

## October–November 2025: leverage can be announced, broadened, and then suspended

The IEA records that China announced a much broader rare-earth control package in October 2025, including additional elements and proposed licensing requirements reaching internationally made products containing Chinese-sourced rare-earth material or made using Chinese technology. The expanded measures were suspended for one year in November 2025.

Source: IEA, `Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 — Executive summary`
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

Boundary: the chapter should distinguish rules that entered into force from announced measures whose implementation was later suspended. An announced choke can affect investment and bargaining even when it does not become a lasting physical cutoff.

## January 2026: explicit Japan-specific dual-use restrictions

On January 6, 2026, MOFCOM prohibited exports of all dual-use items to Japanese military users, for Japanese military uses, and to other end users or uses that could contribute to Japan’s military capability. MOFCOM explicitly linked the decision to a political dispute over remarks concerning Taiwan.

Sources:
- MOFCOM Announcement No. 1 of 2026
https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zfxxgk/gkml/art/2026/art_cd758ab3e7fb4f8e93d6863b5bfe391e.html
- MOFCOM spokesperson remarks, January 2026
https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/News/SpokesmansRemarks/art/2026/art_ad432d69b63545ef81b6b0c9d21c0fa1.html

This does not mean all rare-earth exports to Japan stopped. The legal scope is dual-use exports connected to military users, military uses, or uses judged to enhance military capability. The precision matters.

## How much leverage exists?

IEA’s 2026 critical-minerals outlook estimates China supplies more than 90% of refined output for several highly exposed materials, including gallium, graphite, and rare earths. Its rare-earth report estimates that full implementation of the broad 2025 rare-earth controls could place roughly USD 6.5 trillion in annual downstream production outside China at risk. That is a scenario estimate, not a realized loss.

Sources:
- IEA, `Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 — Outlook`
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook
- IEA, `Rare Earth Elements — Executive summary`, 2026
https://www.iea.org/reports/rare-earth-elements/executive-summary

The better mechanism is the ratio between the small value of a controlled input and the much larger value of the product that cannot ship without it. Chokepoint power comes from veto value, not commodity revenue alone.

## Strongest counterargument

Export controls are not magic. They can fail or weaken in at least five ways:

1. Licenses are granted, so the measure becomes friction rather than a cutoff.
2. Firms redesign products, reduce material intensity, recycle, or substitute away from the controlled input.
3. Governments finance alternative mines, separators, refiners, magnets, stockpiles, or allied supply.
4. High prices attract investment and make previously uneconomic alternatives viable.
5. Controls can accelerate political coalitions whose explicit purpose is to reduce the controlling country’s future leverage.

Japan after 2010 is the book’s strongest disconfirming case. China remained dominant, yet Japan materially reduced import dependence and demand intensity. The durable thesis is therefore not `export controls always work`. It is narrower: concentrated supply chains give governments a coercive option whose short-run effect can be much larger than the market value of the mineral, while every use of that option changes the incentives of buyers to escape it.

## Editorial implication

Chapter 9 should make weaponization look less like an oil embargo and more like modern administrative control: licenses, end-use tests, entity lists, customs friction, technology rules, and delayed approvals. The lever can be powerful precisely because it often operates below the drama threshold of a complete ban.

That framing also creates the handoff to Chapter 10. A buyer can respond to a license shock in days by calling every supplier it has. It cannot create a mine, separator, or qualified magnet factory in days. Policy can move at the speed of a notice; mineral capacity moves at the speed of capital projects.
