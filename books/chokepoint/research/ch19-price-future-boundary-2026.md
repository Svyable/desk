# Chapter 19 research note — prices are signals, not prophecies

Research checked September 4, 2026 for Chapter 19, “The Price of the Future.”

## Why this note exists

Chapter 19 should not become the place where the book finally makes one heroic commodity-price forecast. The earlier chapters have already shown why that would be intellectually inconsistent. Prices change investment, investment changes supply, chemistry changes demand, policy can create or remove trade barriers, recycling grows with the stock of material already in use, and a single producer can change the balance with a quota.

The stronger job is to identify the evidence that would tell a reader which future is starting to arrive.

That means separating three questions that are often collapsed into one:

1. Is the world producing enough material in aggregate?
2. Is that material available through a resilient enough set of suppliers and processing routes?
3. Are prices and financing conditions strong enough to keep the next wave of supply moving before the current one is exhausted?

A market can look comfortable on the first question and dangerous on the second or third.

## The 2025-to-2026 outlook revision is the chapter’s best warning against false precision

The International Energy Agency’s 2025 critical-minerals outlook projected a roughly 30% copper supply shortfall in 2035 under its base project pipeline and Stated Policies Scenario. It also projected a large lithium gap, while nickel and cobalt looked much closer to balance if early-stage projects arrived.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025*, “Overview of outlook for key minerals,” May 21, 2025.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/overview-of-outlook-for-key-minerals

One year later, the IEA’s 2026 outlook had already moved. The projected 2035 copper gap narrowed from about 30% to 25% as projects advanced, and the outlook for lithium also improved. Cobalt moved in the opposite direction: the projected gap widened to more than 25%, reflecting the Democratic Republic of the Congo’s export-quota policy and revised production expectations.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, “Outlook,” July 16, 2026.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook

This is not evidence that either outlook was careless. It is evidence that an outlook is conditional. Projects advance. Projects slip. Governments intervene. Demand technologies change. A forecast that survives those changes without moving would be more suspicious than one that updates.

Editorial implication: use scenario ranges and observable conditions, not a single future copper or lithium price presented as destiny.

## Price itself is a fast variable — and therefore an incomplete guide

The IEA reports that critical-mineral prices rebounded sharply in 2025 and early 2026 after the battery-metal downturn of 2023 and 2024. Between January 2025 and April 2026, base-metal prices including copper rose by about one-third, lithium prices more than doubled, and cobalt prices rose around 130%, with the cobalt move driven largely by DRC export restrictions. Strategic minor minerals rose even more sharply in several cases.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

The preceding 2025 outlook had described the opposite pressure: rapid supply growth in China, Indonesia, and the DRC pushed battery-metal prices down even while demand continued rising.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary

That sequence is useful because it prevents a familiar mistake. A low mineral price does not prove that long-run supply is secure. It may be the result of a temporary wave of new capacity. A high price does not prove that physical scarcity will persist. It may be exactly the signal that brings more supply, substitution, thrift, recycling, inventories, or policy intervention.

The price is news about the market now. It is also an instruction to the market about what to do next.

## The most important leading indicator may be investment while prices are boring

In 2025, total critical-mineral investment fell 9%, the first substantial decline since 2020. Battery-material companies cut capital spending by more than 20%, lithium-focused companies by around 40%, and exploration spending declined by about 10%. Copper-focused companies moved the other way, increasing investment by 8%.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, “Market overview.”
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/market-overview

That is the capital-cycle problem from Chapters 10 and 11 appearing in real time. A period of weak or volatile prices can improve affordability for consumers while quietly weakening the project pipeline needed years later.

For Chapter 19, investment and exploration therefore belong beside spot prices. A useful stress signal is not merely “the mineral is expensive.” It is “demand is still growing, inventories and spare capacity are thinning, and companies are still not spending enough to replace future supply.”

Conversely, a genuine improvement would show up before the market declares victory: sustained exploration, final investment decisions, construction progress, processing capacity, qualified output, and projects surviving through a weaker part of the commodity cycle.

## Announced tonnes are not the same thing as resilient tonnes

The 2026 IEA outlook shows that project pipelines can improve aggregate balances while leaving the middle of the supply chain thin. Outside the dominant refining countries, announced rare-earth mining projects could deliver nearly 50,000 tonnes of capacity by 2035, but planned refining and separation capacity is under 40,000 tonnes, and downstream metals, alloys, and magnets total only about 18,000 tonnes on a rare-earth-content basis.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, “Outlook.”
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook

Similar mismatches appear in battery materials. Mining can diversify faster than refining, cathode production, anode material, qualification, or manufacturing know-how.

This is why Chapter 19 should resist using “projected mine supply versus demand” as the whole scorecard. The relevant unit is qualified material delivered through a chain that can survive a disruption.

A new mine that still sends its concentrate through the old chokepoint may improve the geological map without changing much leverage.

## A market can be balanced and still be brittle

The IEA’s 2025 outlook used an N-1 stress test: remove the largest supplier and its own demand, then ask whether the remaining system can serve everyone else. Under that test, minerals that appeared broadly adequate in aggregate looked much more exposed. For graphite and rare earths, remaining 2035 supply covered only about 35–40% of remaining demand; for nickel, less than 55%; for lithium and cobalt, about 65%.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025*, “Overview of outlook for key minerals.”
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/overview-of-outlook-for-key-minerals

The exact percentages will change with each project cycle, so they should not be turned into timeless book facts. The method is more durable than the number.

A well-managed future is not merely one in which global supply equals global demand on a spreadsheet. It is one in which the loss of a large producer, refinery, shipping route, reagent supplier, or technology license does not immediately make the rest of the system nonfunctional.

That distinction also explains why a security premium can be rational. Spare capacity, stockpiles, second suppliers, and more expensive non-dominant processing can look inefficient in quiet years precisely because resilience is partly the purchase of unused options.

## Government support should be measured by disbursement and operating capacity, not announcements

Public finance commitments for critical minerals in advanced economies reached about $65 billion in 2025, more than four times their 2023 level, according to the IEA. But the agency explicitly warns that there remains a substantial gap between commitments and actual disbursements.

Source: IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary.
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

Chapter 14 made the same point at the project level. Chapter 19 can turn it into an indicator: if grants, loan guarantees, contracts for difference, strategic purchasing, and offtake support are working, they should eventually produce financed construction, commissioned facilities, qualified product, and customers buying from them.

The press release is a leading indicator only if the money leaves the treasury and the plant survives first contact with the market.

## Recycling and substitution belong inside the price outlook, not after it

The IEA’s 2026 outlook projects demand for critical minerals to keep rising strongly across its scenarios, but the composition is already moving. Cobalt-demand growth has moderated because LFP batteries have taken share from nickel-cobalt chemistries. Recycling is expected to increase secondary supply’s contribution over time, reducing the amount of primary material required for the same total demand.

Sources:
- IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, “Outlook.”
  https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook
- IEA, *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, executive summary.
  https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

The book should therefore avoid a demand curve that behaves as though product designers and recyclers are spectators. Chapter 16 already showed that chemistry can rewrite mineral intensity. Chapter 12 showed that secondary supply grows as enough material reaches end of life.

A high-price scenario must include the possibility that high prices accelerate those responses. Otherwise the scenario contains the shock but deletes the reaction.

## Three futures the chapter can hold at once

A useful structure is not bull case / base case / bear case for commodity prices. It is three system states.

**Managed abundance.** Demand grows, but projects advance fast enough; refining and downstream capacity diversify; recycling and substitution reduce primary requirements; financing survives price cycles; strategic stockpiles and alternative suppliers make disruptions tolerable. Prices can still spike, but shortages do not become chronic leverage.

**Cheap now, short later.** A wave of supply suppresses prices. Marginal projects are cancelled, exploration budgets shrink, and diversification projects with higher costs lose financing. Demand continues growing. Years later the apparent surplus has removed part of the pipeline needed to replace it. This is the capital cycle’s most dangerous version because the warning arrives while the market feels comfortable.

**Adequate tonnes, fragile chain.** Global mine supply looks sufficient, but refining, processing, reagents, technology, or one dominant exporter remains concentrated. Average prices can look normal until an export control or physical disruption creates regional scarcity and large price differences. In this future the problem is not geological shortage so much as access.

None of the three requires a precise 2035 copper-price target to be useful.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest objection is that a chapter built from indicators can become an elaborate way of refusing to make a prediction. Readers reasonably want to know whether the book expects a crisis.

The answer should be more specific than “anything can happen.” The evidence supports several directional judgments: demand for copper and other key minerals is likely to keep growing under a wide range of energy scenarios; copper’s project pipeline remains comparatively difficult; refining concentration remains high; and the recent decline in battery-material investment creates a real future-supply risk if it persists.

But the book has also documented mechanisms that work against crisis: high prices have already summoned supply in lithium, nickel, and cobalt; LFP changed cobalt demand; recycling is scaling; rare-earth refining concentration has begun to ease at the margin; and policy support can move projects that ordinary market economics would not.

The honest claim is therefore conditional rather than evasive: **a prolonged crisis becomes more likely when demand keeps growing while investment, project completion, processing diversification, recycling, and substitution fail to respond. It becomes less likely when those responses arrive before inventories and spare capacity disappear.**

That is testable. It can be watched.

## What would make the chapter misleading?

- Treating IEA scenarios as commodity-price forecasts or guaranteed outcomes.
- Quoting one projected 2035 supply gap without its scenario, project-pipeline assumptions, and data vintage.
- Treating announced mining capacity as equivalent to commissioned, qualified, diversified supply.
- Reading low spot prices as proof that long-run scarcity risk has vanished.
- Reading high spot prices as proof that scarcity is permanent.
- Ignoring investment and exploration cuts because current production is adequate.
- Counting government commitments as delivered capacity before funds are disbursed and projects operate.
- Treating aggregate global balance as resilience to the loss of a dominant supplier.
- Holding technology, recycling, product design, and policy fixed inside a scenario whose price shock would cause all four to react.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 19

Chapter 18 asks what remains after a mineral rent is realized. Chapter 19 can begin with a different balance-sheet problem: a mine must commit billions today against a price that nobody knows years from now.

From there, resist the temptation to announce the future. Use the contrast between the 2025 and 2026 outlooks to show how quickly a seemingly distant balance can change when projects or policies move. Then give the reader a small set of things worth watching: investment, exploration, projects that actually reach construction and production, refining diversification, secondary supply, substitution, spare capacity, concentration, and the difference between committed public money and delivered assets.

The chapter should leave the reader with a way to update the argument after publication.

That is more durable than a price target.

It also creates the right handoff to Chapter 20. If the indicators improve together, the mineral economy on the other side of the rush is not one without mining or volatility. It is one with more routes through the system, more material returning through recycling, more product designs that can change inputs, and fewer single points whose failure can stop everything downstream.
