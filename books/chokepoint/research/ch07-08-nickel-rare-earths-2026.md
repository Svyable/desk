# Chapters 7–8 research note — Indonesia nickel and rare-earth processing

Research checked September 3, 2026. This note records the factual spine, source boundaries, and strongest counterevidence for the new Chapter 7 and Chapter 8 drafts.

## Chapter 7 — Nickel and the Indonesian Gambit

### Export ban and WTO boundary

The WTO's DS592 case summary is the cleanest authoritative source for the legal mechanism. Indonesia's policy combined a prohibition on nickel-ore exports with a domestic processing requirement. The panel found both inconsistent with GATT Article XI:1 and rejected Indonesia's argument that the measures were temporary restrictions needed to prevent an imminent critical shortage. Indonesia appealed in December 2022.

Source: World Trade Organization, `Indonesia — Measures Relating to Raw Materials (DS592)`
https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/cases_e/ds592_e.htm

Boundary: the chapter does not claim the WTO ruling erased the policy or reversed industrial investment already made. It distinguishes legal vulnerability from the persistence of physical capital.

### Production concentration and ownership

IEA's 2024 market review reported that Indonesia's share of mined nickel rose from 34% in 2020 to 52% in 2023, while its share of refined nickel rose from 23% to 37%. The same IEA analysis shows why geography and ownership should not be conflated: Chinese-headquartered companies accounted for around 40% of nickel mine production in the ownership analysis, while Indonesian-headquartered companies accounted for less than 10%.

Source: International Energy Agency, `Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2024 — Market review`
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2024/market-review

The 2026 IEA outlook says Indonesia remained the dominant refined nickel supplier and that almost all recent nickel supply growth came from the leading supplier.

Source: International Energy Agency, `Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 — Executive summary`
https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary

USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 estimates world nickel mine production at about 3.9 million tonnes in 2025 and says Indonesian production rose an estimated 13% as new operations ramped.

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, `Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 — Nickel`
https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-nickel.pdf

### Downstream investment — use carefully

Indonesia's Ministry of Investment and Downstream Industry/BKPM reported IDR584.1 trillion in downstream investment realization in 2025, with mineral downstreaming accounting for IDR373.1 trillion. This is a government-reported investment metric, not proof of broad welfare gains or causality from the nickel export ban alone.

Source: BKPM, `Investment Realization in 2025 Exceeds Target, Downstream Investment Jumps 43.3 Percent`, January 15, 2026
https://bkpm.go.id/en/info/press-release/investment-realization-in-2025-exceeds-target-downstream-investment-jumps-43-3-percent

### Captive coal counterweight

Global Energy Monitor and CREA estimate that Indonesia had roughly 19–20 GW of operating captive coal capacity by the end of 2025, with nickel and other metals driving much of the recent growth and concentration in Central Sulawesi and North Maluku. This is independent research rather than Indonesian government data; the chapter treats it as an estimate, not an official national total.

Sources:
- Global Energy Monitor, `Boom and Bust Coal 2026`
- GEM/CREA, `Out of Sight, Out of Control`, January 2026
https://globalenergymonitor.org/research/out-sight-out-control

### Strongest counterargument

The strongest case for Indonesia is not that export restrictions are costless. It is that the policy visibly changed industrial geography: refining capacity, industrial parks, and investment moved toward the ore. The strongest case against the triumphalist version is that ownership remains international, much processing is coal-powered, trade-law exposure persists, and rapid supply expansion can itself depress prices and weaken higher-cost producers elsewhere. Battery chemistry is another disconfirming force: growing LFP use can reduce nickel intensity in parts of the EV market.

The chapter therefore avoids both `the ban failed because WTO found it illegal` and `the ban proves resource nationalism always works`.

## Chapter 8 — Rare Earths and the Refining Monopoly

### Mining versus refining versus magnets

IEA's 2026 rare-earth report supplies the chapter's central quantitative distinction for magnet rare earths. In 2024 China accounted for about 60% of mined production, 91% of refined output, and 94% of sintered permanent-magnet production. These figures apply to magnet rare earths and should not be generalized mechanically to every rare-earth element or product.

Source: International Energy Agency, `Rare Earth Elements — Executive summary`, 2026
https://www.iea.org/reports/rare-earth-elements/executive-summary

The same report describes separation as the technical core of processing and notes that diversified capacity outside China remains much thinner in refining and magnet manufacturing than in mining.

### Mountain Pass chronology

MP Materials' company history records Mountain Pass production idling in 2015, MP's founding and restart in 2017, concentrate sales beginning in 2018, and midstream separation/refining restarting in 2023. Company disclosures report record 2025 NdPr oxide production of 2,599 tonnes.

Sources:
- MP Materials, `History`
https://mpmaterials.com/history
- MP Materials, `Fourth Quarter and Full Year 2025 Results`
https://investors.mpmaterials.com/investor-news/news-details/2026/MP-Materials-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Results/default.aspx

Boundary: company history is primary for MP's own milestones but promotional. Market-share or systemic claims rely on IEA rather than the company.

### U.S. government mine-to-magnet policy

The U.S. Department of Defense explicitly describes sourcing, separation, processing, metallization, alloying, and magnet manufacturing as separate critical nodes. In August 2025 the Office of Strategic Capital announced a $150 million loan to add heavy-rare-earth separation capability at Mountain Pass.

Sources:
- U.S. Department of Defense, `DOD Looks to Establish 'Mine-to-Magnet' Supply Chain for Rare Earth Materials`, March 11, 2024
https://www.defense.gov/News/News-Stories/Article/Article/3700059/dod-looks-to-establish-mine-to-magnet-supply-chain-for-rare-earth-materials/
- U.S. Department of Defense, `Office of Strategic Capital Announces First Loan Through DoD Agreement With MP Materials`, August 10, 2025
https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/4270722/office-of-strategic-capital-announces-first-loan-through-dod-agreement-with-mp/

### Lynas as counterevidence to inevitability

Lynas says it operates the world's largest single rare-earth processing plant in Malaysia and is the only significant producer of separated rare-earth material outside China. Its Kalgoorlie facility in Western Australia performs cracking and leaching before mixed rare-earth carbonate is sent for further separation in Malaysia. Treat the `only significant producer` wording as a company characterization; IEA independently identifies Malaysia as the distant second in refining.

Sources:
- Lynas Rare Earths, company overview
https://lynasrareearths.com/
- Lynas Rare Earths, `Kalgoorlie, Western Australia`
https://lynasrareearths.com/kalgoorlie-western-australia/

### Environmental and cost boundary

IEA notes that rare-earth mining and processing can generate acidic leachate, radioactive tailings or residues where thorium/uranium co-occur, toxic sludge, and other environmental burdens. This supports the chapter's argument that diversified processing can cost more partly because stricter waste and environmental controls are real costs rather than mere inefficiency.

Source: IEA, `Rare Earth Elements — Executive summary`, 2026.

Lynas's 2025 sustainability reporting describes water treatment, emissions monitoring, zero-discharge Western Australian sites, and radiological monitoring in Malaysia. These are company-reported compliance claims and are presented as such rather than independently verified environmental performance.

### Strongest counterargument

The strongest disconfirming evidence to a static `Chinese monopoly` story is that diversification is already happening. IEA's 2026 outlook says rare-earth refining was a notable exception to the broader trend toward higher concentration between 2023 and 2025 because new U.S. capacity and increased Malaysian output modestly reduced concentration. IEA also notes recycling and material-efficiency/substitution as additional routes to reduce dependence.

The chapter therefore uses `processing gravity` and stage-specific concentration rather than claiming China literally owns every mine, separator, or magnet factory. The durable claim is that the current concentration at refining and magnet stages is high enough to create strategic vulnerability while alternatives remain costly and slow to scale.
