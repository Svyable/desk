# Chapter 10 — Mine lead times and the clocks inside them (2026)

## Purpose

Chapter 10 already makes the right conceptual move: the familiar mine-development statistic is a discovery-to-production clock, not a synonym for permitting delay. Fresh 2026 evidence makes that argument stronger, but also changes the number enough that a future manuscript pass should update the chapter rather than keep leaning on the older sixteen-year figure.

The useful revision is not “mines now take thirty years.” That would recreate the same error with a more dramatic number. The new evidence instead lets the chapter distinguish three populations: mines that actually reached production, the broader operating-plus-advanced-project sample, and nonoperating projects with feasibility work and estimated startup dates.

## The updated S&P Global evidence

S&P Global Market Intelligence’s July 8, 2026 update studies 232 assets discovered between 1990 and 2025. Of those, 203 are operating mines and 29 are nonoperating projects that have undergone feasibility studies and have estimated startup dates where owners did not provide guidance.

For the 203 operating mines, the average discovery-to-production lead time is about **14 years**.

For the combined operating and nonoperating sample, the average is about **16 years**.

For the 29 nonoperating advanced projects, the implied discovery-to-production lead time has stretched to **nearly 30 years**.

Those numbers should not be blended. The near-30-year figure is not an average for all mines and is not a measure of permit-processing time. It describes a selected set of advanced projects that have not yet reached production.

S&P’s 2025 edition offers another useful cut: mines that actually began operating in 2020–2024 averaged about **17.8 years** from discovery to production. That was nearly three times the average for mines that entered production in the 1990s. The study attributes the increase to longer exploration, permitting, financing and post-feasibility waits rather than one universal bottleneck.

## Copper is slower than the generic headline

S&P Global’s January 2026 *Copper in the Age of AI* report gives Chapter 10 a commodity-specific decomposition rather than forcing copper into an all-minerals average.

Its sample of 214 copper mines and projects produces an average of roughly **17 years** from discovery to first production. More than two-thirds of that time — about **12.2 years** — sits in discovery, exploration, studies, environmental assessment, litigation and permitting-related work before the construction decision. The report then assigns roughly **2.6 years** to the construction-decision stage and **2.3 years** from construction to production.

The decomposition is editorially valuable because it supports the chapter’s “portfolio of clocks” idea. But the first 12.2-year bucket is broad. It must not be rewritten as “permitting takes twelve years.” It contains geological exploration, studies and legal/administrative processes together.

S&P’s September 2026 copper-discovery update also reports a latest-study copper discovery-to-production average of **17.5 years**, while noting that average drilling depth has risen by nearly 50% since 2010 to around 600 metres and that ore grades have generally declined. This strengthens the chapter’s argument that the clock is partly geological and technical, not merely administrative.

## The strongest new counterevidence

The chapter currently warns against treating sixteen years as a law of nature. Keep that warning and make it harder to dismiss.

The 2026 S&P data show that the historical operating-mine average in its expanded sample is 14 years, below the familiar 16-year headline. Mines that already succeeded are not all becoming thirty-year projects.

More importantly, the distribution is shaped by project maturity and survival. A discovery can spend years becoming better understood before a shortage appears. Brownfield expansions, restarts, by-product production and processing expansions can respond faster than a new greenfield discovery. A high price therefore does not always begin at year zero.

The near-30-year nonoperating figure also contains a selection problem worth stating plainly. These are projects that have advanced far enough to undergo feasibility work yet remain nonoperating; they are exactly where accumulated delay is likely to be visible. It is evidence of a clogged pipeline, not proof that the next mine discovered will require thirty years.

## Permitting: real bottleneck, bad monocausal story

S&P reports that permitting issues or permit revocations are the primary cause of startup delays for mines scheduled to begin production in 2026 and beyond in its studied pipeline. Some projects have had expected startup dates pushed by as much as five years.

That is stronger evidence for permitting as a present bottleneck than the older generic lead-time statistic. The chapter can now say both things at once:

- permitting is materially delaying current projects;
- the total discovery-to-production clock cannot be assigned to permitting.

This is a better argument than either political caricature. “Permitting does not matter” is contradicted by the current project pipeline. “Permitting is why a mine takes sixteen years” is contradicted by the decomposition of the clock.

## Resolution as an illustration, with a boundary

S&P’s July 2026 update uses the Resolution copper project in Arizona as an extreme case. It describes a discovery-to-production path that had already implied roughly 35 years under a 2030 startup assumption and notes that Rio Tinto’s copper chief said startup could slip into the mid-2030s.

Resolution is useful because it makes delay concrete: geology, sacred-land conflict, federal land-transfer litigation, environmental review and project economics all sit inside one project history.

It is not useful as “the average American mine.” It is an unusually contested megaproject. If used in prose, it should illustrate how clocks compound, not stand in for the whole industry.

## IEA 2026: diversification has clocks after the mine too

The IEA’s 2026 *Global Critical Minerals Outlook* reinforces the chapter’s later point that mine lead time understates the full industrial response. Diversification requires processing technology, specialised equipment and skilled expertise. The IEA notes that equipment providers outside dominant suppliers can be few, lead times can be lengthy, and process know-how itself takes time to accumulate.

That supports the chapter’s line that the customer needs qualified material rather than a ribbon-cutting ceremony at a pit. A mine reaching first production retires one set of uncertainties. Processing yield, equipment availability, product consistency and customer qualification can keep another clock running.

## Editorial implication

A future prose pass should replace the chapter’s reliance on one “sixteen years” statistic with a short ladder of evidence:

**14 years** — average for operating mines in S&P’s expanded 2026 sample.

**16 years** — operating plus selected advanced nonoperating projects.

**17–17.5 years** — recent copper-specific estimates, depending on sample.

**nearly 30 years** — selected nonoperating feasibility-stage projects with estimated startups, not the industry average.

The point is not that one of these numbers is the true mine lead time. The variation is the point. “How long does a mine take?” is incomplete until we know the commodity, project stage, jurisdiction, geology, infrastructure, financing and which clock has become binding.

The chapter’s best existing formulation is therefore still the right one: a mine is a portfolio of clocks. The 2026 evidence gives that sentence empirical teeth.

## What would make this argument weaker?

- A sustained wave of greenfield projects moves from discovery to qualified production much faster than the recent historical samples.
- Permitting reforms shorten administrative stages without delay simply moving into litigation, financing, infrastructure or community consent.
- New exploration technology materially compresses the discovery and resource-definition phase rather than only improving targeting.
- Processing and qualification capacity scales quickly enough that mine startup becomes a reliable proxy for usable diversified supply.

Any of those developments would reduce the time premium that gives mineral chokepoints strategic leverage. They should be treated as evidence against the strongest version of the chapter’s thesis, not as exceptions to explain away.
