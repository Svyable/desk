## Thesis

Ronald Reagan's White House sat at an unusual intersection of public Christianity, deliberately private prayer networks, Cold War secrecy, Nancy Reagan's documented use of an astrologer, and a president who repeatedly reached for extraterrestrial imagery when talking about humanity's common fate. Those facts are real. The stronger stories built from them often are not.

This book investigates how religious conviction, spiritual counsel, occult practice, anomalous-experience claims, intelligence secrecy, and presidential decision-making actually touched one another during the Reagan years. Douglas Coe and the Fellowship matter because Reagan himself publicly praised a private fellowship operating across government. Joan Quigley matters because her role advising Nancy Reagan is documented, while the extent of her influence remains contested. UFO and extraterrestrial stories matter because Reagan used alien-threat language publicly and because later accounts attribute unusual sightings and private interests to him, but those later claims must be reconstructed source by source.

The governing question is not whether the Reagan presidency was secretly run by mystics. It is how a modern presidency decides what counts as counsel, belief, intelligence, symbolism, coincidence, and evidence when all of them can arrive at the same door.

## Evidence rule

The book separates four categories throughout: documented contemporaneous record; credible but incomplete recollection or archival inference; disputed later allegation; and authorial synthesis. Religious belief is not treated as evidence of a paranormal event. Astrology's use in scheduling is not silently inflated into proof that astrology determined policy. Reagan's public extraterrestrial metaphors are not treated as evidence that he possessed secret UFO knowledge. Privacy and classification are not counted as confirmation of whatever a missing record would be most exciting to contain.
