# 2. Intelligence Becomes a Consumable

Militaries understand consumables.

Fuel is consumed. Ammunition is consumed. Spare parts are consumed. Bandwidth is consumed. Human attention is consumed too, although organizations rarely put it on the logistics board with the same honesty.

For most of history, high-quality cognition has behaved like a scarce craft rather than a consumable commodity. You could train more analysts, educate more officers, hire specialists, establish larger staffs, or build better institutions, but another thousand competent hours of reasoning could not simply be summoned on demand.

Machine inference changes that relationship.

It does not make judgment free. Compute costs money. Models require infrastructure. Data must be collected, cleaned, secured, moved, and interpreted. Systems need evaluation. Human supervision remains expensive. Electricity, chips, networking, cooling, software, and maintenance all impose physical constraints.

Yet the marginal cost of another analytical pass can become remarkably small compared with the cost of another human shift.

That changes behavior.

When something becomes cheap, organizations stop rationing it in the old way.

Consider how software changed arithmetic. The spreadsheet did not merely allow accountants to calculate the same numbers faster. It encouraged them to construct models that would have been absurdly laborious by hand. Cheap recomputation changed which questions were worth asking.

Cheap inference can do something similar to military analysis.

Instead of asking for one assessment, an institution can ask for variants. Instead of choosing one model of an ambiguous situation, it can preserve ten. Instead of relying on a periodic review, it can continuously compare new evidence with old assumptions. Instead of assigning one person to search for contradictions, it can make contradiction-search a default background process.

The resource being consumed is not intelligence in some metaphysical sense.

It is computationally instantiated analytical effort.

That distinction matters because effort can be wasted.

A military could spend enormous compute generating polished restatements of the same flawed premise. Ten thousand agents sharing the same blind spot do not create ten thousand independent minds. They create an expensive echo.

So the useful quantity is not inference volume.

It is **independent, evidence-sensitive inference**.

That makes diversity of method strategically relevant. Different models, data sources, prompts, analytical decompositions, institutional owners, and human perspectives can produce genuine disagreement. A system that preserves those disagreements may be more robust than one that forces everything into a single consensus score.

The analogy to industrial mass becomes tempting here, but it should be used carefully.

Factories made physical replication cheap. Inference systems make certain forms of cognitive replication cheap. Yet a replicated shell remains a shell. A replicated reasoning process may share hidden assumptions with every copy.

This is why the unit of account should eventually move from tokens or model calls toward something closer to **useful uncertainty reduction**.

How much did the institution learn per unit of compute?

Which observation changed the assessment?

Which model discovered a contradiction?

Which search eliminated a plausible alternative?

Which simulation exposed an assumption that had been invisible in prose?

Which expensive inference merely repeated what was already known?

An organization capable of answering those questions can allocate machine cognition deliberately. One that cannot may simply produce more text.

There is a logistical consequence too.

If inference becomes operationally important, then compute infrastructure becomes part of military readiness. Models that depend on remote connectivity may be unavailable precisely when communications are contested. Large centralized systems may be powerful but brittle. Smaller local models may be less capable in general yet more valuable when they can continue operating with local data and limited power.

The resulting architecture begins to resemble a supply chain for cognition.

At one end are large, expensive systems used for difficult synthesis, planning support, simulation, and broad analysis. At another are small models embedded close to sensors, maintenance systems, logistics nodes, vehicles, or local headquarters, performing bounded classification, search, translation, anomaly detection, and evidence collection. Between them are networks, caches, databases, permissions, provenance systems, and human decision makers.

The strategic question becomes not "Do we have AI?"

It becomes: **How much trustworthy inference can we sustain under the conditions in which we actually expect to operate?**

That includes degraded conditions.

A force that looks brilliant while connected to abundant cloud compute may become cognitively primitive when links fail. A force designed around graceful degradation may retain a useful hierarchy of capabilities: local systems continue bounded tasks, regional systems synthesize what they can, and human procedures remain viable when the most capable models disappear.

This makes inference capacity resemble other forms of military endurance.

The relevant measure is not peak performance in a demonstration. It is sustained performance under friction.

There is also a political economy lurking underneath.

The civilian world may build most of the underlying models, chips, software frameworks, data infrastructure, and developer tools. Military inference will therefore depend partly on a vast commercial ecosystem whose incentives, update cycles, supply chains, and security assumptions were not designed around wartime resilience.

The distinction between civilian and military cognitive infrastructure may blur.

That does not mean every commercial model becomes a weapon. It means the industrial base for reasoning may become as strategically significant as other dual-use technologies: semiconductors, telecommunications, cloud infrastructure, navigation, and satellite services.

Once cognition becomes purchasable at scale, disparities can compound.

A richer institution can afford more experiments, more evaluations, more simulation, more redundant models, more adversarial testing, and more continuous analysis. But raw spending is not enough. Bureaucracies can waste unlimited intelligence just as they can waste unlimited information.

The organizational ability to convert cheap reasoning into better judgment becomes the scarce complement.

This may favor institutions that are unusually good at asking questions.

That sounds almost trivial. It is not.

A system with abundant inference but poor question formation will optimize whatever problem it is handed. If the problem is framed incorrectly, more intelligence may deepen the error. The quality of objectives, constraints, evidence standards, and escalation rules therefore rises in importance as execution gets cheaper.

Human beings move up the stack.

Instead of spending scarce hours manually searching every record, they increasingly decide which uncertainties matter, what counts as sufficient evidence, where independent verification is mandatory, which decisions can be delegated, and which decisions must remain accountable to human authority.

This is the same economic pattern visible elsewhere when automation makes one layer cheap. Scarcity migrates.

When calculation becomes cheap, model design matters more.

When generation becomes cheap, verification matters more.

When inference becomes cheap, epistemic governance matters more.

The military that understands this will not celebrate the number of model calls it can make.

It will ask what those calls buy.

Not tokens.

Not summaries.

Not the appearance of omniscience.

Better contact with reality.
