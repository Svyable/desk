# 23. Deterrence by Understanding

Deterrence is usually described through capability and belief.

One side possesses the ability to impose unacceptable costs. The other side believes the capability is real and the willingness to use it under certain conditions is credible. Strategy tries to shape that belief strongly enough that action becomes unattractive.

Military inference adds another dimension.

What if part of deterrence comes from demonstrating that surprise, deception, and ambiguity are less likely to produce the advantage the adversary expects?

Call this **deterrence by understanding**.

The phrase should not be mistaken for omniscience. No state sees everything. No intelligence system abolishes uncertainty. The argument is narrower: a force that is difficult to surprise, difficult to manipulate, capable of recovering from deception, and able to preserve coherent judgment under degraded conditions can reduce the expected payoff of strategies that depend on confusion.

Resilience changes incentives.

If a decoy can cheaply divert a force, decoys become attractive.

If a cyber or information operation can poison one shared picture and paralyze decision-making, epistemic attack becomes attractive.

If disrupting centralized communications causes local systems to lose authority and coherence, network disruption acquires outsized strategic value.

If those tactics produce only temporary confusion because the force has diverse evidence, bounded autonomy, recoverable provenance, and disciplined uncertainty, the expected gain falls.

This is deterrence through **epistemic resilience**.

It resembles other forms of resilience-based deterrence. A society that can absorb disruption reduces the leverage of coercion. A military that can repair, reroute, and continue reduces the value of attacking one chokepoint. An alliance that can maintain political cohesion under pressure reduces the payoff of division.

An inference force can similarly make confusion less profitable.

This is strategically important because military AI is often imagined mainly as a tool for offense: faster targeting, autonomous platforms, more efficient planning, larger decision advantage. The defensive value of better inference may be at least as significant.

A force can use machine reasoning to discover inconsistency before acting on it.

To compare independent sources.

To preserve alternative hypotheses.

To detect when confidence depends on one fragile assumption.

To maintain readiness models under disruption.

To rehearse failure.

To recover from corrupted state.

To explain what changed after a model update.

These capabilities make the institution harder to manipulate.

That hardness can be communicated.

Not every detail should be public. But doctrine, exercises, procurement priorities, governance standards, and visible recovery practices can signal that the force does not rely on one brittle chain of machine interpretation.

An adversary observing such a force may revise expectations.

A deception campaign must become more expensive.

A surprise must overcome more independent evidence.

A communications disruption must contend with local continuity.

A false signal must survive more verification.

The expected cost of producing confusion rises.

This is deterrence through the economics of counter-inference.

There is a second component: **deterrence by legibility**.

States can reduce some forms of instability by making selected aspects of their machine authority understandable.

Which decisions remain under human command?

Which systems are bounded to defensive or administrative roles?

Which kinds of anomalous evidence trigger additional verification?

Which mechanisms exist for crisis communication?

A state does not need to reveal vulnerabilities to make its restraint architecture credible.

The challenge is credibility.

Public statements about human control are easy to make. Strategic confidence depends on whether institutions, procedures, training, and technical architecture support the statements.

This is similar to other arms-control and confidence-building problems. Declarations matter more when there are observable practices behind them.

Machine governance therefore becomes part of strategic signaling.

A force that demonstrates meaningful human authorization, auditability, and slowing mechanisms may reduce an adversary's fear that an ambiguous event will trigger uncontrollable machine escalation.

The signal is not pacifism.

It is controllability.

Controllability can strengthen deterrence because a threat is more credible when decision-makers believe the system will behave as intended rather than accidentally.

A brittle automated threat may frighten both sides without reassuring either.

A controlled capability can be dangerous and stabilizing simultaneously because the boundaries are more legible.

This points toward a broader concept: **epistemic assurance**.

Deterrence depends partly on each side having confidence in its own systems. If leaders do not trust their warning architecture, they may compensate through hair-trigger procedures or worst-case assumptions. If they trust it blindly, they may overreact to confident error.

The desirable condition is calibrated trust.

Leaders understand the system's strengths, failure modes, and uncertainty. They know which outputs deserve rapid action and which require more evidence. They know how the architecture behaves under disconnection and novelty.

That confidence can increase strategic patience.

A leader who trusts the institution's ability to keep watching may feel less pressure to act on the first ambiguous signal.

This is an underappreciated form of deterrence stability.

The capability to continue observing can buy time.

Persistent inference creates this possibility because the system does not need to settle every question immediately. It can maintain unresolved hypotheses, watch for discriminating evidence, and update over time.

The strategic posture becomes less binary.

Not believe or disbelieve.

Not act or ignore.

Instead: maintain concern, increase observation, preserve options, and wait for evidence while preparing for several futures.

This is **deterrence by patience**.

Again, patience is not always available. Some threats compress time genuinely. But many strategic environments contain uncertainty that unfolds over days, weeks, months, and years. Persistent machine reasoning can reduce the cost of maintaining attention across those horizons.

That matters because human institutions forget.

A weak signal appears. Another crisis takes attention. Personnel rotate. The original concern disappears until it returns as surprise.

Persistent inference can keep dormant strategic questions alive cheaply.

The force remembers what future evidence would matter.

This is particularly valuable in deterrence because credibility depends on sustained understanding of the adversary rather than one snapshot.

What do they value?

What do they fear?

Which signals have historically mattered?

Which changes in doctrine or posture are meaningful?

Which apparent changes are mostly rhetoric?

Machine systems can maintain that history, but they should not reduce it to a static behavioral profile. States adapt. Leaders change. Cultures contain factions. Bureaucracies contradict themselves.

The adversary is not a stationary model.

This is one reason deterrence by understanding is difficult. It requires enough persistence to remember and enough humility to update.

A system that remembers everything and revises nothing becomes dogma.

A system that updates constantly without history becomes gullible.

Strategic understanding lives between them.

There is also a danger in the phrase itself. It can imply that if we simply understand the opponent well enough, we can manipulate behavior reliably.

That is an illusion.

Deterrence always contains autonomy on the other side. The adversary makes choices. Domestic politics intrudes. Misperception remains. Leaders may accept costs we consider irrational. Values differ. Organizations act inconsistently.

Understanding improves estimates.

It does not create control.

This humility should be built into machine deterrence models.

A system that produces one predicted adversary response can create false confidence. A better system preserves a distribution of plausible responses and shows which assumptions separate them.

What if leadership prioritizes prestige over material cost?

What if domestic politics alters the payoff?

What if the visible action is driven by a bureaucratic faction rather than unified strategy?

What if the adversary misunderstands us?

The machine staff can help explore these branches.

The political leader still chooses what risks to accept.

This is why deterrence by understanding should be paired with **deterrence by restraint from overconfidence**.

The force demonstrates capability while retaining the capacity to recognize ambiguity.

It communicates resolve without making every signal irreversible.

It builds systems that can move quickly while preserving the right to verify.

It makes itself hard to deceive without pretending deception is impossible.

There is a further distinction between **deterring action** and **deterring exploitation of confusion**.

A state may not be able to convince an adversary never to challenge it. It can still convince the adversary that certain methods are unlikely to yield disproportionate advantage.

If the force has practiced operating through data corruption, degraded communications, model disagreement, and false warning, then an opponent must assume that confusion alone will not necessarily paralyze decision-making.

This is analogous to hardening critical infrastructure. The attacker may still cause damage. The expected political leverage of disruption falls when recovery is credible.

Epistemic recovery can therefore be demonstrated in exercises.

A force can deliberately train with a compromised dataset discovered midway through the scenario. It can operate while two major inference systems disagree. It can rehearse loss of a model provider, stale shared state, or a sudden requirement to rebuild an assessment from primary evidence.

The public need not see every detail. The existence of a disciplined recovery culture can be visible.

That visibility matters because deterrence depends on the adversary's estimate of what will happen after the first move.

If the first move is expected to create enduring confusion, it may be attractive.

If the first move is expected to trigger a practiced recovery process, the calculus changes.

This is **deterrence by recoverability**.

The concept also applies to alliances. An alliance that shares one brittle inference stack can be easier to divide than an alliance that can compare independent national assessments, reconcile differences, and continue operating when shared systems fail.

Coalition resilience is epistemic as well as political.

The adversary may attempt to exploit differences in information access, confidence thresholds, or machine recommendations. One ally sees high confidence. Another sees ambiguity. A third cannot reveal its best source. The disagreement itself can become a wedge.

A mature alliance should expect this and have procedures for **disagreement without disintegration**.

Machine systems can help by exposing which part of the disagreement comes from evidence, which from policy, and which from different tolerance for risk. That does not guarantee consensus. It makes the conflict legible.

Legible disagreement can strengthen deterrence because an adversary sees an alliance capable of arguing internally without becoming incoherent.

The same principle applies to national command. A resilient state does not need every model to agree. It needs the ability to convert disagreement into a bounded decision process.

This is an important signal because adversaries often look for decision paralysis as much as material weakness.

Deterrence by understanding therefore includes **deterrence by governability**.

The state can control its own machine systems under stress.

It can slow them.

It can replace them.

It can explain their authority.

It can recover from error.

A capability that cannot be governed may look impressive in peacetime and frightening in crisis, but it is not necessarily credible as an instrument of strategy. Leaders may hesitate to rely on it because they do not trust the consequences.

Governability converts technical capability into usable state power.

This is a useful corrective to the assumption that autonomy automatically strengthens deterrence. A highly autonomous system whose behavior is opaque or difficult to interrupt can weaken credibility by increasing fear of accident. The adversary may not know whether an observed action reflects deliberate policy, local machine behavior, or malfunction.

Ambiguous control creates ambiguous signaling.

In strategic relationships, ambiguity about intention can be dangerous even when ambiguity about capability is useful.

That suggests a design principle: **machine behavior should be attributable to an authority regime**.

The other side may not know every technical detail, but the state itself should know whether a system acted under routine delegated authority, emergency policy, human authorization, or local fallback rules. This supports internal control and makes external signaling more deliberate.

There is also a role for **epistemic arms control**, though the phrase should be approached cautiously. The challenge is not to negotiate which side may think more. It is to identify specific machine-enabled behaviors whose opacity creates shared instability.

Possible confidence-building areas might include communication channels around unusual automated events, shared terminology for certain categories of human authorization, or norms around testing systems that could produce ambiguous strategic signals. The details would be politically difficult and technology would change rapidly.

The broader principle is enough for this book: when both sides fear invisible automation, some mutual legibility can create value.

This is similar to earlier eras in which states developed practices for reducing the risk that exercises, alerts, or technical anomalies would be mistaken for preparations for war.

The inference age adds new objects of uncertainty.

Was this action machine-generated?

Was it automatically triggered?

Can it be recalled?

Does the state know it occurred?

Which human authority is responsible?

These questions can become strategically relevant even if the machine behavior itself is limited.

Understanding the opponent's governance may therefore become part of deterrence analysis.

Not merely what systems they possess, but how those systems enter authority.

The difficulty is that governance can be misrepresented. A public policy may say one thing while crisis practice differs. A system may be designed with human authorization but create such time pressure that authorization becomes automatic. The same problem appears domestically.

Credibility requires observable institutional behavior over time.

Exercises, procurement, doctrine, training, and incident response create a pattern.

This is why deterrence by understanding cannot be manufactured by one public statement. It is accumulated.

The same is true of understanding the adversary.

A machine staff can preserve decades of interaction history, but deterrence analysis should distinguish **memory from prediction**. The fact that an adversary behaved one way previously is evidence, not destiny.

Persistent models should therefore track confidence decay as political context changes. Leadership turnover, economic shocks, alliance changes, domestic unrest, technological transitions, and war itself can change what old behavior means.

The system should be able to say: this pattern was once predictive; it is now weak evidence.

That is a form of strategic forgetting.

It prevents the model of the adversary from becoming a prison for both sides.

There is a deeper philosophical point. Deterrence has always required a peculiar combination of confidence and humility.

A state must communicate credible resolve.

It must also understand that the adversary remains free to choose differently than expected.

Machine inference can increase confidence faster than it increases humility because the output looks analytical. The danger is a generation of leaders surrounded by probability distributions that make uncertainty feel domesticated.

The force should instead use machine reasoning to make uncertainty more articulate.

Not "the adversary will do X."

These are the live branches.

These are the assumptions.

These are the signals that would change our estimate.

These are the outcomes we cannot rule out.

This is how inference supports deterrence without pretending to control another state.

The strongest deterrent in the inference age may therefore not be a machine that always knows what to do.

It may be an institution that an adversary cannot easily make stupid, cannot easily make panic, and cannot easily convince that one temporary picture of the world is the only picture available.

That is deterrence by understanding.

Not knowing everything.

Making confusion a poor investment.
