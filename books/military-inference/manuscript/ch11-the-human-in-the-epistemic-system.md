# The Human in the Epistemic System

The phrase “human in the loop” has survived because it sounds reassuring.

There is a machine. There is a process. Somewhere inside the loop, a person remains.

The diagram implies control.

The person may have almost none.

A human can be technically present while the system determines what the human sees, how quickly the decision must be made, which alternatives are considered, what evidence is summarized, and what counts as normal. The person can retain formal authority while losing practical independence.

This is why the future of human-machine military decision-making cannot be settled by drawing a human-shaped icon between two arrows.

The relevant question is what kind of epistemic role the person retains.

Can the human see enough of the evidence to understand what is being claimed?

Can the human disagree without having to reconstruct the entire system under time pressure?

Can the human ask for another source?

Can the human recognize when the problem has moved outside the model’s experience?

Can the human slow the process when the evidence becomes strange?

Does the human remain responsible for a decision whose premises were selected by machines?

These questions become more important as the machine gets better.

Early automation often preserves human engagement because its limitations are obvious. Users expect mistakes. They learn workarounds. They keep one hand on the process because the system has not earned independence.

Mature automation creates the opposite problem. It succeeds so frequently that human attention becomes expensive to maintain. The person stops evaluating each recommendation independently because doing so would duplicate a system that is usually right. Eventually the human role migrates from decision-making toward monitoring.

Monitoring sounds easier.

It is often harder.

The monitor must remain alert for rare failures during long periods of routine success. The system handles normality. The human inherits surprise.

Military human-machine integration research has repeatedly identified trust as a central problem for this reason. RAND’s work on Army integration emphasizes that military organizations require unusually high trust because consequences are severe, operational security matters, and mission command itself rests on relationships of responsibility and confidence. The report also notes that integration is sociotechnical. People, organizations, training, acquisition, and machines have to change together.

That word—sociotechnical—is useful because it resists the fantasy that better models automatically produce better teams.

A brilliant machine placed in a badly designed organization can make the organization worse.

It can accelerate micromanagement. It can obscure responsibility. It can concentrate power in the people who control data and systems rather than the people formally designated to command. It can reduce expertise by removing practice. It can create dependency on infrastructure that fails under pressure. It can change incentives in ways no benchmark measures.

The human role has to be designed as deliberately as the model role.

This begins with authority.

Military command carries legal, moral, and political responsibility that software does not possess. A machine can rank options. It can identify patterns. It can simulate. It can monitor. It can act within delegated boundaries. It cannot bear institutional responsibility in the human sense. The question “Who decided?” cannot be answered adequately with a model identifier.

This does not mean every machine-mediated action requires direct human approval.

That would be impossible at scale and unnecessary for many low-consequence processes. Militaries already delegate enormous amounts of action through doctrine, standing orders, automation, control systems, and subordinate authority. The relevant design task is deciding where responsibility requires human judgment and where prior human intent can safely govern automated execution.

The answer will vary with consequence and reversibility.

A maintenance agent scheduling an inspection occupies a different category from a system shaping a strategic assessment. A communications tool rerouting data is different from an analytical system inferring adversary intent. The phrase “AI use” obscures these distinctions.

The human should not be equally present everywhere.

The human should be meaningfully present where human authority adds something the machine cannot supply.

That may be moral judgment.

It may be political context.

It may be responsibility for accepting risk.

It may be tacit knowledge unavailable in the data.

It may simply be the ability to recognize that a system is solving the wrong problem.

The last capability deserves attention because machine reasoning tends to be evaluated on tasks as defined. A model can become excellent at answering a question without noticing that the question stopped mattering.

Humans are not automatically good at this either. Organizations persist with obsolete problems all the time. But people embedded in lived circumstances have access to forms of context that technical systems may not represent: relationships, atmosphere, institutional memory, moral salience, and the strange detail that does not fit the schema.

The human advantage is therefore not a permanent cognitive trait.

It is structural position.

A local commander stands in consequences the model only represents. A political leader owns risks that cannot be optimized into one metric. A maintainer can feel that the machine sounds wrong before a sensor reports failure. An intelligence analyst may know that a source’s wording has changed in a way the database does not capture.

These examples should not be romanticized. Humans miss obvious patterns and cling to bad beliefs. The point is diversity of access to reality.

The epistemic system is stronger when different participants know different things.

This reframes human-machine teaming away from competition.

The machine does not need to be less intelligent for the human to remain necessary.

The human does not need to be uniquely creative, empathetic, or wise in every domain. The useful question is which participant has the best access, authority, and failure mode for the task at hand.

Machine reasoning is particularly strong where scale, repetition, search, comparison, and persistence matter. Humans remain especially important where legitimacy, consequence, tacit context, responsibility, and genuinely novel framing matter.

Those boundaries will move.

The architecture should be able to move with them.

A rigid rule that “humans always do X” may become obsolete as systems improve. A rigid rule that “machines do Y because they are faster” may fail when the environment changes. Delegation should depend on measured reliability, context, consequence, and the ability to recover from error.

This is a more demanding governance model than simply certifying a system once.

It requires continuous evidence.

Can the machine’s authority expand as performance is demonstrated?

Can it contract when behavior degrades?

Can operators see that the authority boundary has changed?

Can a commander override the default without rebuilding the system?

This is dynamic delegation.

Mission command offers an institutional analogy. Commanders provide intent and delegate authority while retaining responsibility. Subordinates act within that intent and exercise judgment when circumstances change. Good mission command is not the absence of control. It is control designed to survive uncertainty and communication limits.

Machine agents can fit into a similar architecture if they are treated as delegated processes rather than autonomous sources of legitimacy.

The system receives bounded authority.

It knows its objective.

It knows constraints.

It knows which conditions require escalation.

It acts where the action is reversible or well understood.

It preserves evidence of what it did.

When the environment becomes strange, it asks for help.

That last behavior may be more important than raw capability.

A military system that knows when to escalate is easier to trust because the human does not have to monitor every normal case. The machine protects human attention by making its uncertainty visible at the right moments.

This is the basis of command by exception.

The danger is that exception handling can become a dumping ground for the hardest problems. Humans receive only ambiguous, high-consequence, time-sensitive cases. They are expected to perform perfectly with the least practice and the most stress.

That is not a sustainable human role.

Training has to recreate the missing normality.

People need opportunities to perform tasks without automation so they retain mental models of the underlying process. They need exercises in which automation fails gradually rather than theatrically. They need to see subtle miscalibration, stale data, correlated evidence, and recommendations that are plausible but wrong.

The goal is not nostalgic manual competence for its own sake.

It is enough independent understanding to intervene.

This may require deliberately limiting automation in training environments even when automation would improve short-term performance. A pilot practices abnormal procedures because the emergency is rare. A military staff may need similar practice reconstructing assessments without machine synthesis, operating with degraded data, or resolving disagreement between systems.

Competence is a reserve.

If it is never exercised, it decays.

The human also needs institutional permission to use it.

A person who disagrees with a machine recommendation incurs social cost when the system is highly trusted. The cost increases if delay is visible. “Why did you hold the decision?” is easier to ask than “Why did you accept a recommendation you did not understand?” The first has a timestamp. The second may become visible only after failure.

Organizations therefore drift toward measurable speed.

The human supervisor can become a scapegoat for latency.

This is why the right to slow down has to be formal rather than heroic. An operator should not need extraordinary courage to request another source when evidence quality collapses. The system should define conditions under which review is expected.

Formalization does not eliminate judgment. It protects it.

The same principle applies to dissent.

A human-machine team benefits when people can express uncertainty or disagreement in forms the system records rather than smoothing them away. A junior analyst’s objection should not disappear because the model and majority agree. The objection can be stored as a minority hypothesis and monitored. It may never matter. If the world begins to support it, the system can raise it again.

Machine memory can protect human dissent from organizational forgetting.

That is a surprisingly democratic use of military AI.

It can also create surveillance pressure. If every disagreement, override, and judgment is permanently logged, people may become less willing to speak freely. The organization needs rules governing how analytical history is used. Learning requires records. Intellectual risk-taking requires some protection from punitive hindsight.

This is a governance problem, not merely a data-retention problem.

The human in the epistemic system therefore needs rights as well as responsibilities.

A right to understand the basis of consequential recommendations.

A right to see uncertainty when it matters.

A right to disagree.

A right to request verification.

A right to operate when the system is unavailable.

A right to know which authority has been delegated to the machine.

And, in some contexts, a right not to be flooded with machine output that shifts accountability without providing decision value.

These are not universal legal rights in current doctrine. They are design principles for meaningful human control.

The alternative is human presence without human agency.

That failure may be especially tempting because it satisfies institutional checklists. The human signs. The human approves. The human is on the diagram. Responsibility remains formally assigned.

Yet the actual decision can have been made earlier, when software filtered the evidence, framed the options, chose the comparison set, and compressed uncertainty into one recommendation.

By the time the human arrives, disagreement means reopening an entire upstream process under time pressure.

That is not a loop.

It is a ratchet.

The machine pushes the process toward action and the human can stop it only by exerting exceptional force.

A better design makes intervention proportional. The user can ask why. The user can inspect a critical dependency. The user can request an alternative model. The user can downgrade confidence. The user can pause one branch without freezing the system. The interface makes dissent an ordinary operation.

This is how human authority becomes usable.

The same principle applies at the strategic level. Political leaders may increasingly receive AI-assisted intelligence and scenario analysis. The tools can expand the set of possibilities considered and help reveal hidden dependencies. They can also create pressure toward technicalizing political decisions.

A model can estimate costs.

It cannot decide what a society should value.

A simulation can explore escalation pathways.

It cannot confer legitimacy on risk imposed on others.

A machine can help a leader understand consequences without owning the choice among them.

This boundary is not a statement about consciousness. It is about political authority.

War remains a human institution because humans and societies bear its consequences.

That fact should remain visible inside increasingly automated systems.

The best military AI may therefore do something that sounds less ambitious than replacing judgment. It may increase the quality of judgment by protecting humans from cognitive work machines can perform while preserving the evidence, context, time, and authority humans need for the decisions machines should not quietly inherit.

The person in the loop is not the objective.

The objective is a person who can still change the loop.
