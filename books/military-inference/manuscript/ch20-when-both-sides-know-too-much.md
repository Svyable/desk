# 20. When Both Sides Know Too Much

Military history often treats information as an advantage because information was scarce.

Know more than the opponent and you can avoid surprise, exploit weakness, coordinate better, and force the other side to guess.

What happens when both sides know much more?

Not everything. Not enough to abolish uncertainty. But enough that concealment becomes harder, movement is easier to observe, logistics patterns are more legible, open-source information is richer, commercial sensors proliferate, machine reasoning can search enormous evidence fields, and persistent agents can maintain long-lived models of the opponent.

The intuitive answer is that war becomes more transparent.

Transparency sounds stabilizing.

It can be.

If both sides can observe major preparations, surprise becomes harder. If deception is difficult, miscalculation may decline. If leaders possess more accurate information about the other's capabilities and intentions, deterrence may become more credible.

But knowledge has second-order effects.

When both sides know more, they also know more about what the other side can know.

That creates a recursive environment.

I know you can see this.

You know I know you can see it.

Therefore my visible behavior may be intended to shape your inference.

You know that too.

Transparency becomes strategic theater.

The observed world contains signals deliberately produced for an observer known to be observing.

This is not new. Military signaling is ancient. Machine inference increases the density of the signaling environment because many more behaviors become measurable.

Supply movements.

Software changes.

Procurement patterns.

Communications rhythms.

Public statements.

Commercial imagery.

Industrial output.

Network activity.

Training schedules.

Financial indicators.

None is definitive alone. Together they become material for persistent inference.

This can create a strange form of stability based on **mutual legibility**.

Each side knows that major changes are difficult to hide completely. That may reduce incentives for certain surprises.

It can also create incentives for subtler forms of deception precisely because obvious concealment becomes less credible.

The world moves from hiding activity toward manipulating interpretation of visible activity.

The deception economy matures.

There is another paradox.

More information can increase uncertainty when the number of interpretations grows faster than the ability to verify them.

A sparse picture may support three plausible explanations.

A sensor-rich picture may support thirty because every anomaly acquires significance.

Machine reasoning can manage those hypotheses, but it can also surface patterns that humans would never have considered. Some are real. Some are coincidental. Some are adversarially constructed.

The institution begins seeing possibility everywhere.

That can produce **strategic apophenia**: the tendency of a richly instrumented system to discover meaningful structure in noise because it possesses enough data and enough inference to generate explanations endlessly.

The defense is not less information.

It is stronger evidence discipline.

Predictions must be tracked against outcomes.

Hypotheses should specify what observations would distinguish them.

Confidence should respond to independent evidence rather than sheer data volume.

Historical analogies should remain analogies, not templates.

The system must know how often it previously discovered patterns that disappeared under scrutiny.

This turns calibration into strategic memory.

An inference force should remember not only what it got right, but how it tends to be wrong.

Does it overinterpret unusual logistics activity?

Does it underweight public political signals?

Does it mistake correlation among commercial datasets for independent confirmation?

Does it assume rational consistency where bureaucratic confusion is a better explanation?

These recurring errors can become part of the system's self-model.

This is military epistemology becoming empirical.

There is also a psychological effect when leaders know that the opponent knows a great deal.

Secrecy becomes harder to preserve internally and externally. Plans may have shorter useful lives. Political leaders may fear that any visible preparation will be interpreted as intent. Military exercises can become more consequential signals because machine systems may model them continuously.

The boundary between routine activity and strategic communication blurs.

This can create **signaling congestion**.

Too many actions are interpretable as messages.

Leaders begin asking not only what a move accomplishes physically but what machine models on the other side are likely to infer from it.

That is counter-inference at the strategic level.

The danger is recursive paralysis.

If every action is chosen partly for how it will be interpreted, decision-making can become trapped in speculation about speculation.

What will they think we think?

What if they know we intend them to think that?

Machine staffs can make this recursion worse because they can generate enormous trees of possible opponent interpretations.

At some depth, more modeling stops improving judgment.

The system needs a **recursion budget**.

How many layers of modeled intention are actually supported by evidence?

Beyond that point, the institution should admit it is generating fiction.

This is another place where the right to remain uncertain matters.

There is a more hopeful consequence of mutual legibility: verification can become easier in some domains.

If major actions produce observable signatures across multiple independent systems, then claims can be checked more quickly. International monitoring, commercial imagery, open-source analysis, and persistent machine review can make certain forms of denial harder to sustain.

That may improve accountability.

It may also alter crisis diplomacy.

Leaders can enter negotiations with richer shared factual baselines while disagreeing profoundly about interpretation and legitimacy.

The factual dispute narrows.

The political dispute remains.

This is important because better inference does not solve conflict of interest.

Two sides can understand the same reality perfectly and still want incompatible outcomes.

The inference age therefore should not be mistaken for the end of strategy.

Intelligence is not agreement.

Transparency is not peace.

Knowledge can improve bargaining by clarifying constraints. It can also intensify conflict by making vulnerabilities obvious.

A side that discovers a temporary weakness may feel pressure to exploit it before it disappears. A side that knows its weakness is visible may feel pressure to act defensively before the opponent acts. Better information can shorten windows of perceived opportunity.

This is the **knowledge-instability problem**.

Some information stabilizes by reducing uncertainty.

Other information destabilizes by revealing a fleeting advantage.

Machine inference can accelerate discovery of both.

Strategic stability therefore depends not only on how much each side knows, but on how institutions respond to knowledge about temporary asymmetry.

Do they interpret it as an opportunity?

A warning?

A reason for diplomacy?

A reason to hide change?

The model cannot decide that normatively. Doctrine and political leadership do.

This is where the obsession with decision superiority becomes incomplete.

Suppose both sides improve their inference dramatically. Each sees more. Each predicts better. Each detects weakness sooner. Each models the other's reactions more accurately.

There is no guarantee the result is a more peaceful equilibrium.

It may be a more competitive equilibrium with less room for surprise and less room for misunderstanding—but also less room for bluff, recovery, and quiet adjustment.

Some ambiguity has historically created danger.

Some ambiguity has also created diplomatic space.

A state may tolerate a face-saving explanation it privately doubts. Leaders may leave intentions partially unstated because explicit knowledge would force public reaction. Strategic relationships sometimes depend on everyone understanding something without proving it too loudly.

Machine systems designed to resolve every ambiguity may inadvertently make politics more brittle.

This is the case for **selective epistemic restraint**.

Not deliberate ignorance about threats.

An institutional recognition that not every uncertainty must be compressed to zero and not every private inference must become public fact.

Diplomacy has always operated partly through controlled ambiguity. Inference-rich states will need to decide which ambiguity is dangerous and which is stabilizing.

That is a profoundly human judgment because it involves legitimacy, face, domestic politics, and values.

The future strategic environment may therefore be characterized by a strange combination:

more observation;

more inference;

more mutual modeling;

less ability to hide large changes;

and continued uncertainty about intention.

Machines can watch movement.

They cannot directly observe meaning.

Meaning is inferred through history, incentives, culture, institutions, and signals that may themselves be strategic.

That is why both sides can know too much and still not know enough.

The inference age does not abolish fog.

It moves the fog upward.

The question stops being only, "What happened?"

It becomes, "What does what happened mean when everyone knows everyone is watching?"
