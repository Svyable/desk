# The Asymmetry of Regret

Regret is a comparison with a world that did not happen.

You took the job. You imagine the one you declined. You sold the stock. It rose. You kept the stock. It fell. You moved. You picture the life in the old city. You stayed. You picture the move.

The counterfactual has an unfair advantage.

It contains none of the inconveniences that would have accompanied reality.

The job you declined has interesting work and kind colleagues because you never had to attend its meetings. The city you left has perfect weather in memory. The investment you sold never experiences the future drawdown that might have followed the rally. The person you did not marry remains composed of selected possibility.

Regret is therefore not just a feeling about outcomes. It is a model-selection problem performed by a biased simulator.

This matters for reversibility because people often pay enormous costs to avoid anticipated regret.

They keep options open “just in case.” They buy insurance. They diversify. They delay decisions. They refuse to sell losing investments because selling would make the loss real. They avoid trying because failure would be painful. They choose familiar products, employers, and neighborhoods because the unknown contains more imaginable regret than the known.

Reversibility can reduce regret by keeping the counterfactual available.

A refundable ticket lets you postpone the final decision. A trial period lets you test fit. A liquid investment can be sold. A reversible career experiment keeps the old job for a while. A return policy allows ownership without complete commitment.

But optionality can also increase regret because the road not taken remains visible.

The person who could still leave keeps evaluating whether to leave. The employee with many attractive offers may be less satisfied with one job. The investor with a constantly repriced portfolio gets a daily list of alternatives that performed better. Dating apps produce a scrolling counterfactual population.

A closed door can be psychologically merciful.

This is one reason people sometimes feel relief after an irreversible decision even when the decision is difficult. The search ends. The mind stops pricing alternatives.

Decision theorists and psychologists have studied these asymmetries in many forms. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky’s prospect theory showed that people evaluate gains and losses relative to reference points and often weigh losses more heavily than comparable gains. Later work on regret, omission bias, status quo bias, and the endowment effect explored how counterfactuals, defaults, ownership, and responsibility shape choice.

The exact size and universality of individual effects vary by context. The larger point is robust enough for ordinary observation: humans do not experience decisions as neutral expected-value calculations.

How a choice is framed as a gain, loss, action, omission, continuation, or reversal changes its emotional price.

This creates a systematic bias against return paths once a decision has become the status quo.

Consider an investment that falls twenty percent. Before buying, you might have rejected the same asset at today’s price because the outlook had worsened. After buying, selling feels like accepting a loss. The reference point is the purchase price, even though the market does not care what you paid.

The asset and the decision become entangled.

A cleaner question is: if I held cash today, would I buy this asset at this price?

That reframing turns continuation into a fresh choice.

It is not perfect because taxes, transaction costs, diversification, and information may differ. But it attacks the status-quo privilege directly.

Organizations can use the same test. If we did not already own this business, would we acquire it today? If this meeting did not exist, would we create it? If this policy were proposed now, would we adopt it? If this vendor were new, would we sign the contract?

The answers reveal zombie commitments.

The method is powerful because continuation often escapes scrutiny. Starting requires a decision. Stopping requires a decision. Continuing happens by default.

Defaults have enormous force because they avoid visible responsibility.

If a project continues and fails, responsibility is diffuse. If you personally cancel it and the market later turns favorable, the counterfactual points at you.

This creates omission bias in management. Harm caused by inaction can feel less blameworthy than harm caused by action, even when outcomes are equivalent or worse.

Leaders learn this quickly. It is safer to inherit a bad process than to remove a process that later proves useful. It is safer to maintain a mediocre product than cancel one customer loves. It is safer to hold a committee than disband it.

Bureaucracy is partly regret avoidance institutionalized.

Every procedure has a remembered incident that justifies it. Remove the procedure and a similar incident may occur. The person who removed it will be named. Keep the procedure and its daily cost is spread invisibly across everyone.

This is why organizational simplification is politically difficult even when everyone complains about complexity.

The benefit of removal is diffuse. The regret from one future failure is concentrated.

The same asymmetry shapes public safety. After a rare disaster, institutions add rules. Some rules are sensible. Over time, layers accumulate. Removing any one of them creates a visible narrative: “safety rule eliminated before accident.” Keeping all of them creates no headline, only friction.

Risk management becomes one-way.

A mature system needs a procedure for subtracting precautions as well as adding them.

This sounds dangerous because accidents are irreversible. The solution is not casual deregulation. It is evidence. Measure whether the control reduces the targeted risk, whether the underlying hazard changed, what costs the control imposes, and whether another measure now provides the same protection.

Reversal should face the same burden of proof as adoption, adjusted for stakes.

Regret also distorts entrepreneurship in the opposite direction. People fear future regret from not trying and use that fear to justify reckless action.

“You’ll regret it if you don’t” is persuasive because omission regret is imagined in a flattering way. The future self who never started the company pictures the successful company that might have existed, not the debt, lost sleep, or years spent on a market that never arrived.

Counterfactual entrepreneurship has survivor bias built in.

The correct question is not which future regret feels larger. It is which experiment can convert uncertainty into evidence at acceptable cost.

Reversibility turns regret into a design variable.

Instead of choosing between “quit my job and start the company” and “stay forever,” create intermediate states. Sell before quitting. Save runway. Work nights for a defined period if health and obligations allow. Test the customer problem. Find a cofounder. Build a prototype.

Now future regret is bounded by real information.

This is the book’s recurring move: replace emotional binary decisions with sequences that preserve learning.

The technique also works for personal relationships with place and lifestyle. Instead of buying the rural property immediately, rent nearby. Instead of retiring permanently after a difficult year, take a leave if possible. Instead of assuming a hobby should become a career, test whether paid repetition changes enjoyment.

But life will not always allow experiments.

Some decisions are discrete. Have a child or do not. Accept a narrow time-sensitive opportunity or lose it. Undergo a procedure or decline. Report wrongdoing or remain silent. Leave a dangerous situation now.

No sequence can eliminate moral weight.

In those cases, probability and regret need a stronger companion: asymmetry of harm.

What happens if we are wrong in each direction?

This is a better question than “which outcome is more likely?” when consequences differ sharply.

A doctor deciding whether to order an invasive test considers the harm of missing disease and the harm of unnecessary intervention. A policymaker considers false positives and false negatives. A security system balances blocking legitimate users against allowing attackers. A court balances different errors under legal standards.

The two mistakes are not equal merely because both are errors.

Reversibility helps quantify the difference conceptually. Which error can be corrected later? How quickly? At what cost? Who bears the interim harm?

A false fraud alert reversed in ten minutes differs from a missed fraud that drains an account. A false accusation that remains public for months differs from one contained pending review. A missed cancer diagnosis differs from an unnecessary follow-up scan, though follow-up itself can have risks.

Error costs have time profiles.

This suggests that decision thresholds should depend on recovery curves, not just static losses.

How fast does damage compound while we wait? How much can intervention repair? Is there a point after which recovery becomes much harder? Are there irreversible side effects from acting too early?

Medicine lives inside these questions because treatment and delay both have consequences. So does maintenance. Replace an aircraft part too early and cost rises. Replace it too late and safety risk rises. Predictive maintenance tries to estimate the window.

The return path has an optimal timing problem.

Too early and you pay unnecessary switching cost. Too late and reversibility has decayed.

This is why decisions often need triggers rather than dates.

A trigger says: if this condition appears, act. Cash falls below this level. Defects exceed this rate. A treaty violation is verified. A clinical marker crosses this threshold. Customer churn persists for this many cohorts.

Triggers reduce the emotional renegotiation at the moment of action.

They also create gaming risk. People optimize around thresholds. Metrics can be manipulated. Conditions can be chosen to justify a desired action. Good triggers therefore combine quantitative thresholds with judgment and review.

Again, procedure supports but does not replace character.

Regret is deeply social. We regret not only outcomes but how others interpret our decisions. A manager may keep a project alive because canceling makes her look inconsistent. A politician may continue a policy because reversal gives opponents a talking point. A person may stay in a career because relatives admired the original choice.

The audience becomes part of the option cost.

This is why cultures that punish changing one’s mind become less adaptive.

They teach people to hide updates.

A scientist who publicly revises a belief should gain credibility when the revision follows evidence. A leader who changes a reversible policy after a pilot should not automatically be accused of weakness. A company that kills a product early may be displaying capital discipline rather than failure.

Of course opportunistic reversals exist. Politicians flip positions for convenience. Executives rewrite strategy every quarter. Investors chase trends. People abandon commitments at the first discomfort.

The solution is to demand a reason, not consistency for its own sake.

What changed?

That question is powerful because it distinguishes updating from drift.

If nothing changed except discomfort, maybe commitment should hold. If new evidence arrived, a revision may be rational. If the original evidence was misunderstood, say so. If values changed, say that instead of pretending the facts changed.

Honest reversal preserves trust by preserving causality.

This matters for self-respect too. People often invent reasons after decisions because they want to see themselves as consistent. But consistency is not the same as integrity.

Integrity can include admitting that the model failed.

The regret we fear most is sometimes not “I chose wrong.” It is “I was the kind of person who could choose wrong.”

That is impossible to avoid.

A life with meaningful uncertainty will contain wrong choices.

The mature objective is not a regret-free life. It is a life in which ordinary errors remain informative rather than fatal, and in which the irreversible commitments are chosen with enough care that we can live with their consequences.

This reframes regret as a signal rather than a verdict.

Regret can reveal what we value. It can expose a neglected option. It can teach us which decisions deserve slower commitment next time. It can also be noise generated by counterfactual fantasies.

We do not need to obey it.

We need to learn what kind of error produced it.

The person with a return path is not the person who never regrets.

It is the person whose regrets do not automatically become prisons.
