# The Sunk-Cost Machine

The first million dollars is an investment.

The tenth million may be an argument with the first.

Projects change meaning as money, time, reputation, and identity accumulate around them. At the beginning, the question is whether the opportunity is worth pursuing. Later, the question quietly becomes whether stopping would prove that the earlier effort was wasted.

That is the sunk-cost trap.

Economics gives the clean rule: costs already incurred and unrecoverable should not determine whether you spend the next dollar. Future choices should depend on future costs and benefits. The past cannot be purchased back.

Human beings understand this rule perfectly well when the sunk cost belongs to someone else.

A friend has paid for a bad movie and wants to stay because leaving would waste the ticket. You point out that the money is gone either way. A colleague keeps a failing software project alive because two years have been invested. You ask whether you would start the same project today given what is now known. A government continues funding a program because cancellation would “waste” what has already been spent. You notice that continuing can waste more.

Then the project is ours.

The arithmetic becomes philosophical.

We say the next investment will unlock the value of the previous one. Sometimes that is true. Many projects look bad immediately before they work. Research contains long periods of failure. Infrastructure requires years of spending before benefits arrive. A factory half built is usually worth more completed than abandoned. Persistence would be impossible if every disappointing quarter triggered exit.

The challenge is distinguishing endurance from escalation.

Barry Staw’s classic work on escalation of commitment examined how decision makers can allocate more resources to a failing course of action, especially when they feel responsible for the original choice. Later research expanded the picture: self-justification, loss framing, project completion effects, social pressure, organizational politics, and the desire to protect reputation can all make withdrawal harder.

Responsibility changes evidence.

The manager who inherited a failing project may shut it down. The manager who proposed it sees one more reason to continue.

This asymmetry is why organizations often rotate leadership during turnarounds. A new chief executive can write down assets, close divisions, or abandon strategies that the previous leadership found psychologically difficult to reverse. Investors sometimes celebrate the “kitchen sink” quarter in which new management recognizes accumulated losses. The facts did not arrive with the new executive. The social cost of admitting them changed.

A return path can therefore require a new decider.

This is not always fair. New leaders can blame predecessors too easily, discard valuable long-term work, or manufacture a low baseline that makes later performance look better. But the phenomenon reveals something about commitment: facts are filtered through identity.

Public commitment intensifies the filter.

When a leader has declared a project central to the company’s future, reversal threatens more than money. It threatens the leader’s image as a person who sees farther than others. The project becomes a referendum on judgment.

Visionary cultures are especially vulnerable.

The founder who ignored skeptics once and became successful learns a dangerous lesson: skeptics are evidence that the idea is bold. Past survival reinforces a narrative in which persistence itself causes reality to yield. Sometimes it does. The founder recruits talent, raises capital, and creates a market because conviction persuades others to coordinate around a possibility.

But stubbornness has no built-in mechanism for distinguishing visionary persistence from denial.

Outcomes do that, eventually. The cost is what accumulates before eventually.

Business biographies often flatten this ambiguity because stories are told after the ending. The stubborn founder who succeeds becomes courageous. The stubborn founder who fails becomes delusional. The behavior may have looked similar in year three.

That is why organizations need decision processes that do not depend entirely on character judgments.

The question “Is this leader a visionary?” is not operational. The question “What evidence would make us reduce or stop this commitment?” is.

A pre-mortem is one device. Before launching a project, imagine that it failed and ask why. This surfaces risks before social momentum makes them impolite. Another device is a kill criterion defined in advance. Another is staged funding. Another is an independent review at specified milestones.

The shared purpose is to create a future door before the hallway fills with furniture.

Sunk costs become most dangerous when they combine with proximity to completion. A project that is ninety percent finished feels harder to abandon than one that is ten percent finished, even when the final ten percent contains most of the unresolved difficulty. The phrase “we are almost there” can continue for years because completion is not measured in effort already spent but in unknown problems remaining.

Software is notorious for this. A rewrite is declared nearly complete while edge cases, data migration, operational tooling, customer compatibility, security, documentation, and performance remain. Each missing piece appears small relative to the code already written. Together they are the product.

Large physical projects can behave similarly. A tunnel encounters geology nobody predicted. A nuclear project faces regulatory changes, supply-chain problems, or first-of-a-kind construction issues. A defense program discovers integration failures after subsystems worked separately. The percentage of budget spent does not tell you the percentage of uncertainty resolved.

Progress and expenditure are not the same variable.

This is why the phrase sunk-cost fallacy can itself be abused. Critics sometimes invoke it against any long project that has become expensive. But past spending can contain information. It may have produced assets, learning, permits, customer relationships, trained teams, or intellectual property that change future economics. The right decision is not “ignore the past” in a literal sense. It is “do not spend more merely to justify the past.”

The assets created by past spending are part of the present state. The emotional need to vindicate past spending should not be.

That distinction sounds clean on paper and becomes difficult inside a boardroom.

Suppose a company has spent five hundred million dollars on a new platform. The platform is late. Customers are unhappy with the old system. Competitors have improved. Completing the platform will require another two hundred million. Canceling would trigger a write-down, layoffs, and public embarrassment.

The relevant questions are forward-looking but historically informed. What is the platform now worth if completed? How likely is completion within the new budget? What alternatives exist? Which assets from the project can be reused? What customer costs arise from further delay? Does the current team’s forecast incorporate the errors that made the prior forecast wrong?

The irrelevant question is whether canceling would make the first five hundred million feel wasted.

It is already gone.

The psychological difficulty is that accounting makes the loss visible only when recognized. As long as an asset remains on the balance sheet at an optimistic value, the organization can preserve the appearance that the money still exists in another form. A write-down turns uncertainty into an acknowledged loss.

Recognition feels like causation.

The executive who signs the impairment may be blamed for “losing” value that disappeared earlier. This creates incentives to delay reality.

The same pattern appears in personal life. A person stays in a relationship because of the years invested. A student completes a degree she no longer wants because she is already halfway through. A homeowner keeps repairing an unsuitable house because of the renovations already made. A collector continues buying because stopping would force him to see previous purchases differently.

Time is a particularly powerful sunk cost because it is inseparable from identity.

Money can be earned again. Years cannot.

This makes the advice to “ignore sunk costs” emotionally incomplete. The years mattered. They contained relationships, effort, hopes, and versions of the self. Leaving does not make them meaningless, but it can feel like a verdict on them.

A healthier return path reframes the past as tuition rather than a debt owed by the future.

You do not need the next ten years to justify the last ten.

This is easy to turn into motivational cliché, so it deserves precision. The value of prior experience can be real. A lawyer who leaves law still carries analytical skills. A failed founder may carry knowledge and relationships into another company. A marriage that ends may still have contained love, children, or growth. A research program can fail scientifically and still produce instruments or methods.

The fact that something ends does not settle whether the time before the ending was wasted.

That separation is psychologically important because it lowers the identity cost of reversal.

Organizations can design it deliberately. Celebrate well-run project shutdowns. Promote people from failed but informative efforts. Publish postmortems that preserve learning. Reassign teams instead of treating cancellation as exile. Make it possible to say “this was a reasonable experiment; the evidence changed.”

If every canceled project stains careers, the portfolio will fill with undead projects.

They are easy to recognize. Nobody believes strongly enough to expand them, but nobody wants responsibility for stopping them. They consume maintenance budgets and meetings. Their metrics are periodically redefined. Their original strategic rationale has faded, but some important customer, executive, or internal team still depends on them.

Zombie projects survive because closure is an event while continuation is a default.

This asymmetry suggests a simple institutional fix: make continuation a decision.

Every substantial project should periodically earn renewal. Not through performative reviews where everyone knows the answer, but by comparing expected future value against alternatives. The review should include people who do not depend on the project for status or employment, while still respecting the knowledge of those who understand it deeply.

Sunset clauses do this for policy. Portfolio reviews can do it for corporate projects. Grant renewals can do it for research. Military procurement programs use milestone reviews, though politics can overwhelm them. Venture investors make follow-on financing a fresh choice.

The key word is fresh.

The next dollar should face the current opportunity set.

This is harder in institutions because budgets are not neutral pools. A project has employees, offices, contracts, champions, and beneficiaries. An alternative is often a spreadsheet row without a constituency. Existing programs therefore have an organizational advantage beyond economic merit.

Political scientists sometimes describe this through path dependence and increasing returns. Early choices create institutions and constituencies that make later reversal costly. The sunk-cost effect operates not just within minds but across organizations.

A subsidy creates firms optimized around the subsidy. A military base creates a local economy. A tax preference creates advisers and business structures. A software platform creates customer integrations. A road creates neighborhoods. The decision builds its own defenders.

The return path closes socially as well as financially.

This is one reason early temporary measures become permanent. During a crisis, a program is adopted quickly. It creates benefits. Beneficiaries organize. The crisis fades, but removal now imposes concentrated losses on identifiable people while savings are diffuse. Politicians face stronger pressure from those losing the program than from taxpayers receiving a tiny share of the savings.

Loss aversion has an institutional form.

Reversal turns a benefit people possess into a loss, and losses mobilize.

The same asymmetry helps explain why organizations can add processes more easily than remove them. A new report promises information. Removing it later means telling a specific stakeholder that the information will disappear. A new approval promises safety. Removing it means accepting visible risk. Nobody owns the cumulative cost of all reports and approvals.

Bureaucracy becomes a sunk-cost machine because every layer has a local reason to remain.

The solution is not permanent disruption. Constant reorganization destroys memory and trust. People need stable rules to coordinate. The solution is to design closure at creation.

Every new recurring process should have an owner, a purpose, a review date, and a condition under which it ends. Every large project should distinguish irreversible commitments from staged ones. Every public pledge should be careful not to make tactical adjustment sound like moral betrayal.

Leaders often create sunk costs with language.

Call a market entry “the defining expansion of our generation,” and closing one office later becomes a crisis of identity. Call it “a three-year attempt to determine whether our model transfers,” and the same evidence can be processed differently.

Narratives allocate reversibility.

This is not a case for weak language. Sometimes strong commitments are necessary to coordinate. A factory needs suppliers to believe it will exist. Employees move families. Governments sign treaties. Partners invest. Ambiguity can make a project fail because nobody commits enough for it to work.

The art lies in being firm about purpose and honest about uncertainty.

“We are committed to serving this market, and this specific channel is an experiment” is different from “this channel must work because our strategy depends on it.”

One statement creates room to adapt methods. The other ties method to identity.

The sunk-cost trap is ultimately a problem of narrative accounting. We want the past to balance. We dislike entries that say loss, mistake, wrong turn, time spent.

So we ask the future to repair the ledger.

The future has no such obligation.

The most mature return path may be the ability to close a project without requiring a story in which the project was secretly a success all along.

It failed. We learned. We stop.

That sentence is brutally expensive in some cultures.

It is also how capital, attention, and life become available again.
