# The City That Could Not Move

Cities remember decisions in concrete.

A software mistake can sometimes be reversed in minutes. A road alignment can survive a century.

The difference is not that engineers are less flexible than programmers. It is that physical infrastructure accumulates dependencies at human scale. Houses face the road. Shops depend on traffic. Sewers run beneath it. Property lines adjust. Transit routes connect. Land values change. Children grow up assuming the map is natural.

By the time anyone asks whether the road was a good idea, the road is no longer one project. It is a fact around which thousands of other projects have organized.

This is path dependence with curbs.

Urban planning is full of these ratchets. Build a highway to the edge of a city and development follows the access. Development creates traffic. Traffic creates pressure for more lanes. More lanes make farther development practical. The original decision becomes part of a feedback loop that manufactures demand for its continuation.

The loop can run in other directions. Build frequent rail service and dense housing becomes more attractive near stations. Dense housing increases ridership. Ridership can justify more service. A waterfront park attracts residents and businesses that later defend the park. A university campus creates laboratories, apartments, cafés, and firms that make moving the university almost unimaginable.

Infrastructure does not merely serve behavior. It helps produce it.

This is why “we can change it later” is a dangerous phrase in physical planning. Later inherits the built response to now.

The same problem appears in industrial geography. A factory creates a supplier cluster. The cluster creates specialized labor. Specialized labor attracts related firms. The region becomes good at a particular industry. That specialization can be a powerful advantage until technology changes or demand collapses. Then the very density that created competitiveness makes transition socially painful.

Capabilities and lock-in are twins.

This complicates the idea that reversibility is always desirable. A city that never commits cannot become much of a city. Water systems, railways, power networks, parks, schools, housing, ports, and streets require long-lived investment. Their value comes partly from durability. People build lives around the expectation that the bridge will still be there.

Infrastructure is a promise made in steel and land.

The design challenge is not to avoid permanence. It is to make permanence selective.

Some parts of a system should be stable while others remain adaptable. A street grid can last for centuries while the uses inside each block change. A building can have a long-lived structural frame and short-lived interior partitions. A rail corridor can be preserved even as vehicles and service patterns evolve. Utility conduits can be accessible so components can be replaced without excavating the entire street.

Good infrastructure often stores option value in geometry.

Width, clearance, load capacity, modular connections, rights-of-way, reserved space, interoperable interfaces: these features can look like overbuilding when measured against current demand. They become valuable when future uses arrive.

The cost of preserving the option must still be justified. A city cannot reserve every parcel for hypothetical transport. A bridge sized for implausible loads wastes money and materials. Flexibility can become a pretext for gold-plating.

But under uncertainty, some spare capacity is strategic rather than wasteful.

This is easier to see after lock-in occurs.

Consider buried utilities. A street may be resurfaced, then opened months later for water work, then opened again for fiber or sewer repair. Each system was planned on its own timeline. The physical environment becomes a record of fragmented governance. Coordinated utility corridors or accessible conduits cost more upfront but can make future change cheaper.

The city’s return path was designed—or neglected—at installation.

Housing policy offers another example. Land-use rules can be changed on paper faster than neighborhoods can change physically. A zoning code that permits only one kind of low-density development over large areas shapes parcel sizes, street patterns, school catchments, tax bases, travel behavior, and political expectations. Decades later, legal reform may allow denser housing, but the physical and social system still reflects the earlier rules.

Reversal in law is not reversal in reality.

This is a useful warning for reformers. Repealing a constraint may be necessary without being sufficient. Once institutions have accumulated around the constraint, adaptation can take a generation.

The reverse is also true. A city can make a new rule immediately and discover that people, financing, and construction industries cannot reorganize on command.

Policy works through capital stocks.

Economists use that phrase for durable assets that persist through time. Buildings, machines, roads, power plants, vehicles, and networks turn past investment into present structure. When a society wants rapid change—decarbonization, housing reform, transportation shifts, industrial policy—it confronts these stocks.

The world is not rebuilt annually from a spreadsheet.

This is one reason forecasts that assume frictionless substitution can be misleading. A cleaner technology may be economically superior for new installations and still take decades to dominate because old assets remain functional. A transport mode may be attractive and still require rights-of-way that no longer exist. A city may want more housing but lack enough skilled trades, permitting capacity, financing, or utility upgrades.

Irreversibility lives in replacement cycles.

A durable asset is valuable partly because it does not need constant replacement. That durability also slows transitions.

Strategy therefore depends on moments when systems are naturally open to change. A building renovation, equipment replacement, lease expiration, road reconstruction, regulatory reset, corporate refinancing, leadership transition, or generational turnover can create a temporary window in which switching costs fall.

The return path has seasons.

Miss the window and the next chance may be expensive.

Cities see this when major corridors are rebuilt. If a street will be torn up anyway, adding conduit, changing drainage, redesigning intersections, or preparing for future transit may cost far less than doing the same work separately later. The incremental decision should be evaluated against the fact that the irreversible disruption is already happening.

Companies face similar replacement windows. A legacy enterprise system may be painful to replace at any time, but a merger, regulatory change, or end-of-support date can create a moment when the organization is already absorbing transition cost. Families make major moves when jobs change or children shift schools. Countries alter energy systems when plants retire.

Timing can be more important than abstract optimality.

A theoretically superior future design may lose if it arrives after the window closes.

This helps explain technological standards. A better keyboard layout, video format, payment protocol, or industrial connector can struggle against an incumbent because users own complementary assets, skills, and habits. The new option is judged not against a blank slate but against the cost of switching an ecosystem.

Infrastructure turns network effects into geography.

Once many people coordinate around the same system, private reversal becomes socially costly. A household cannot unilaterally replace the road network. A commuter cannot create a rail line. A manufacturer cannot single-handedly replace a port. Collective return paths require governance.

This is where democratic planning becomes difficult. The people who bear the cost of transition are not always the people who benefit, and the timing differs. Closing a street to cars may improve safety and public space while harming businesses that depended on current access. Building a transit line may raise regional productivity while displacing households near construction. Removing a highway can reconnect neighborhoods while creating congestion elsewhere during adaptation.

Every reversal redistributes.

This is why large infrastructure reversals generate intense politics. The old system has beneficiaries who are visible. The future system’s beneficiaries may not exist yet.

A child who will ride a train in 2040 cannot attend today’s planning meeting.

The political system therefore discounts future option value for the same reason businesses do: current costs have names and addresses; future flexibility is a probability.

One answer is experimentation with temporary infrastructure. Cities increasingly use paint, planters, movable barriers, temporary plazas, demonstration bike lanes, and pilot bus treatments before permanent construction. These “quick-build” approaches are controversial because a temporary change can still affect people and may be used strategically to create a new status quo. But they can also generate evidence before concrete makes the decision expensive.

The strongest pilots are honest about both benefits and disruption.

They specify the period, collect data, invite feedback, and define what would justify permanence or reversal. Weak pilots are propaganda: temporary in name, politically irreversible from the start.

The difference is whether the return path is credible.

Public trust deteriorates when “pilot” means “decision already made.” Future experiments then face skepticism even when genuine.

Credibility is cumulative infrastructure too.

The same principle matters in megaprojects. Large dams, airports, rail systems, defense installations, and industrial complexes create enormous sunk costs before benefits can be fully observed. Cost overruns and delays become politically difficult to evaluate because cancellation after substantial construction may waste more than completion, even if the original project should never have begun.

This is where staged commitment is most valuable and hardest to practice.

A project can be broken into gates before major irreversibility: route selection, land acquisition, design maturity, permitting, early works, full construction. Each stage should reduce a specific uncertainty. If it does not, the project is consuming option value without purchasing information.

There is a brutal question project sponsors should ask: what can we still learn before we make stopping irrational?

Because once stopping becomes irrational, governance changes.

The project no longer needs to prove that it is good. It needs only to prove that completion is less bad than abandonment.

This is the infrastructure version of the sunk-cost machine.

Private firms can sometimes walk away. Governments face additional political pressure because abandoned public works become monuments to failure. A half-built bridge is visible in a way a canceled software project is not. Leaders therefore have incentives to push through, even when the remaining economics deteriorate.

Visibility makes physical irreversibility socially contagious.

There is also a distributive asymmetry. Wealthier communities often have more capacity to fight irreversible projects before construction begins. They hire lawyers, commission studies, organize, and influence routing. Poorer communities may receive the infrastructure and discover later that reversal is prohibitively expensive.

The history of urban highways, industrial facilities, and environmental burdens includes many examples where the power to preserve local option value was unequally distributed.

Reversibility is a justice issue because some groups are more likely to have permanent decisions imposed on them.

This changes how cost-benefit analysis should treat uncertainty. If a project’s harms are concentrated, long-lived, and hard to reverse, a simple net-benefit calculation may miss the moral significance of who loses the return path.

Compensation can help but may not restore place, community, or health. Money is not a universal undo.

The same warning applies to climate adaptation. A seawall protects one area and may alter erosion or development incentives elsewhere. Rebuilding after disaster can recreate vulnerability or create a chance to move. Insurance policies can encourage rebuilding in exposed places or make retreat financially possible. Infrastructure choices become commitments about where future populations are expected to live.

The map is a portfolio of irreversible bets.

Cities therefore need institutional memory about why those bets were made. Otherwise future planners encounter infrastructure as nature. The road is there, therefore it must be needed. The zoning map is old, therefore it must reflect some timeless logic. The industrial district exists, therefore relocation must be impossible.

Historical knowledge reopens the imagination.

If a system was designed, it can sometimes be redesigned.

Not cheaply. Not completely. But understanding contingency prevents inherited choices from becoming moral laws.

This is one reason old maps are politically powerful. They reveal lost rail lines, buried streams, demolished neighborhoods, earlier shorelines, former street grids. The city that feels inevitable becomes visibly constructed.

Reversibility often begins by remembering that the present once had alternatives.

The goal is not nostalgia. Past cities were not automatically more humane or efficient. The goal is to restore the idea of choice.

A resilient city contains long-lived commitments and short-lived layers. It knows which is which. It preserves corridors, access, public rights, and structural capacity where future change is plausible. It uses temporary methods when uncertainty is high. It recognizes replacement windows. It avoids creating dependencies before evidence arrives. It remembers that every durable asset becomes somebody’s inherited constraint.

You cannot build a city with an eraser.

But you can build one that leaves room for the next generation to draw.
