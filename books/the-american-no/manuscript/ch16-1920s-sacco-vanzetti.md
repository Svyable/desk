# Chapter 16 — 1920s: Sacco and Vanzetti

By August 1927, the question of whether Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti had received justice had traveled far beyond the Massachusetts courtroom where they had been convicted of murder.

People demonstrated in Boston, New York, London, Paris, Buenos Aires, and elsewhere. Writers, labor activists, radicals, liberals, immigrant communities, and opponents of capital punishment turned the case into an international cause. Bomb threats and violence by some anarchists darkened the atmosphere. Massachusetts authorities insisted the convictions would stand.

On August 23, Sacco and Vanzetti were executed.

The protest did not save them.

That is why it matters.

The 1920s are often remembered through prosperity, automobiles, jazz, consumer culture, Prohibition, and the apparent modernity of cities. They were also years of severe political repression after World War I, immigration restriction, the Red Scare, the Palmer raids, deportations, revived Ku Klux Klan power, racial violence, and suspicion toward radical immigrants.

Sacco and Vanzetti stood where those anxieties intersected.

They were Italian immigrants. They were anarchists. They were working men. They were accused of participating in a robbery in South Braintree, Massachusetts, in which a paymaster and guard were killed. They were convicted in 1921.

The case has generated argument for more than a century about evidence, ballistics, witnesses, judicial bias, anarchist networks, later confessions, and the defendants’ actual guilt or innocence. This chapter does not need to solve what generations of historians, lawyers, and partisans have disputed.

Its subject is the protest created by the dispute.

The essential fact is that millions of people came to believe the trial represented more than two defendants.

A case becomes a cause when people see a system in it.

For Sacco and Vanzetti’s supporters, the system included nativism, class prejudice, anti-anarchist repression, prosecutorial power, and a judiciary they believed was hostile to the defendants’ politics and background. The prosecution argued that two men had been convicted of murder on evidence and that political campaigns should not override the legal process.

The conflict therefore became a referendum on institutional trust.

Do courts deserve legitimacy because they are courts, or does legitimacy depend on the fairness of what happens inside them?

The answer seems easy in theory.

In practice, movements and institutions start from different burdens of proof.

The court says: a verdict has been reached through recognized procedure; challengers must show why it should be disturbed.

The movement says: the procedure itself is part of what we are challenging; do not use its existence as evidence of its fairness.

This circularity can make legal protest extraordinarily difficult.

The institution possesses records, deadlines, standards of review, presumptions, and finality. The movement possesses public pressure, alternative investigation, moral argument, and the possibility of making enforcement politically costly.

Sacco and Vanzetti became one of the century’s great examples of the movement trying to reopen a case the institution was determined to close.

The protests also revealed how identity affects perceived credibility.

An Italian anarchist defendant entered an American courtroom carrying political meaning before any witness spoke. The Red Scare had taught much of the public to associate radicals, immigrants, bombings, and disorder. The defendants’ own political beliefs were real. Anarchists had carried out acts of violence in the United States. The government was not inventing radical militancy from nothing.

The danger was using true facts about a political movement to decide an individual criminal case.

This is the Haymarket problem again, modernized.

A person can hold ideas many Americans detest and still be entitled to a trial in which those ideas do not substitute for proof of a specific crime.

That principle is easy to defend when the accused is politically harmless.

Civil liberty becomes meaningful when the accused is not.

Sacco and Vanzetti’s anarchism therefore made the case harder and more important.

Supporters had to argue for fairness without pretending the defendants were conventional patriots misunderstood by an intolerant society. The men did not need to become ideologically respectable to deserve due process.

This is a central protest principle.

Rights conditioned on respectability are permissions.

A government committed to civil liberty has to apply rules to people who would happily abolish parts of the government.

The case also became an immigrant protest because the defendants’ experience resonated beyond anarchist circles. Italian immigrants lived inside a racial and ethnic hierarchy that treated southern and eastern Europeans as suspect, inferior, or not fully assimilable. Immigration laws in the 1920s explicitly redesigned national admission around restrictive quotas that favored some European origins over others and excluded many Asians through existing and expanded policies.

The trial therefore took place inside a national argument about who counted as American.

Supporters did not need to prove that every juror or official acted from nativist prejudice for that context to matter politically.

Movements interpret cases through structures as well as intentions.

A judge can believe himself impartial inside a culture saturated with stereotypes. A police department can follow routine procedures that produce unequal patterns. An employer can apply a policy neutrally to workers situated unequally. Institutional bias does not require a villain whispering the plan.

That insight would become increasingly important to twentieth-century protest.

The Sacco-Vanzetti movement was early in making a criminal case stand for structural distrust.

It also globalized American protest.

The case attracted demonstrations and commentary across Europe and Latin America. The United States was no longer a young republic whose domestic disputes remained local curiosities. It had become an industrial and military power whose political trials could symbolize American democracy abroad.

International attention changed the pressure structure.

Officials in Massachusetts were not only answering local critics. They were defending the legitimacy of an American legal process before a global audience.

That is a form of leverage movements would use repeatedly.

Civil-rights activists later understood that segregation damaged the United States’ image during the Cold War. Antiwar movements built transnational networks. Anti-apartheid activists pressured American institutions through a global moral campaign. Environmental and labor movements coordinated across borders against multinational firms.

The Sacco-Vanzetti protests showed that a local case could become a foreign-policy embarrassment without any government declaring it one.

Public opinion crossed borders faster than institutional jurisdiction.

The movement’s scale also depended on culture.

Writers, artists, intellectuals, newspapers, and pamphleteers turned the case into narrative. The defendants became characters in plays, poems, essays, paintings, songs, and later films. Cultural production extended the protest after the executions.

This is a different kind of afterlife from a statute.

Law changes rules.

Art changes memory.

The second can last longer.

A movement that loses its policy objective may still win a durable place in culture, and culture can reopen questions institutions considered settled.

Sacco and Vanzetti’s names became shorthand for disputed justice.

That shorthand has risks. Symbols simplify people. The actual victims of the South Braintree murders can disappear when attention centers exclusively on the defendants. A movement convinced of wrongful conviction may treat the original crime as background scenery.

That is ethically dangerous.

A serious account keeps the murdered paymaster and guard inside the story even while scrutinizing the state’s case against Sacco and Vanzetti. The demand for fair process does not require indifference to victims.

This tension recurs in campaigns around criminal justice.

Movements may focus on wrongful conviction, police violence, sentencing, prison conditions, or capital punishment. Opponents can accuse them of forgetting crime victims. Sometimes that accusation is cynical. Sometimes movements really do flatten victims into abstractions.

Justice cannot require choosing which humans are allowed to remain visible.

The Sacco-Vanzetti movement was also a capital-punishment protest.

Execution creates a unique political deadline.

A prison sentence can theoretically be corrected later. An execution cannot.

This changes protest tempo. Appeals, petitions, demonstrations, investigations, and political pressure accelerate as the date approaches. Authorities interpret the same urgency as an attempt to overwhelm legal finality through mob pressure.

The clock becomes part of the conflict.

Movements facing irreversible state action often become most intense at the point institutions become least willing to reconsider.

That is a recipe for collision.

Massachusetts Governor Alvan Fuller appointed an advisory committee to review the case before the executions. The committee supported the verdicts. To officials, this was evidence that concerns had been considered. To supporters, it was another institution validating the original institution.

Review can restore legitimacy only if critics trust the reviewer.

This sounds procedural and is deeply political.

Who investigates the police?

Who reviews the intelligence agency?

Who evaluates the regulator?

Who appoints the commission?

Who audits the election?

Independent review is not an adjective. It is a relationship that must be designed convincingly enough that losing parties accept it.

The Sacco-Vanzetti case showed how difficult that becomes after trust collapses.

By the time the executions approached, supporters were not asking for reassurance from the same establishment they blamed.

They wanted a different outcome.

The failure to achieve it radicalized the memory of the case.

The executions became evidence for a story in which American institutions could kill unpopular immigrants while preserving procedural respectability. Defenders of the verdict saw the protests as proof that radicals would attack courts whenever law touched their comrades.

Each side’s interpretation survived the electric chair.

This is another reason the protest mattered more than the final legal decision.

Law produced closure in the narrowest sense: the sentences were carried out.

Politics remained open.

Fifty years later, Massachusetts Governor Michael Dukakis issued a proclamation declaring that the atmosphere of the trials and appeals had been permeated by prejudice and that any disgrace should be removed from Sacco and Vanzetti’s names, while not formally declaring them innocent.

The phrasing itself showed how contested memory remained.

A later government could acknowledge injustice in process without claiming certainty about guilt.

That is sometimes the most historically responsible position available.

Movements often demand clarity because clarity mobilizes.

History sometimes has to preserve ambiguity because evidence requires it.

This book should prefer the second when the two conflict.

The protests also illustrate the political cost of foreignness as a category.

An immigrant can live and work in the United States for years and still be described as an outsider when conflict arises. Political radicalism intensifies the designation. The accusation becomes not simply that the person committed an offense but that the person imported a dangerous idea into America.

The country then protects itself by imagining the problem came from somewhere else.

The garment strikes of the previous chapters complicate that story. Immigrants did bring political traditions. They also encountered American factories that generated new grievances. Sacco and Vanzetti were anarchists before their arrest, but American institutions chose how to investigate, try, and punish them.

Foreign ideology and domestic power met in the courtroom.

Neither erases the other.

The protest movement itself was similarly hybrid. Immigrant radical networks mobilized alongside American intellectuals and liberals who did not share anarchist politics. This made the coalition broad and unstable.

People could agree that the trial was unfair for different reasons.

Anarchists saw class repression.

Civil libertarians saw due-process failure.

Immigrant communities saw nativism.

Death-penalty opponents saw irreversible state violence.

Writers saw a symbol of institutional arrogance.

A coalition does not need one diagnosis if it can agree on one intervention.

Here the intervention was clemency or a new trial.

The state refused.

That refusal made the coalition’s differences less important until after the executions.

This is how deadlines create unity.

The 1927 demonstrations were enormous in moral intensity even where their numbers varied. They did not stop the execution. They did not produce immediate criminal-procedure reform. They did something subtler and historically durable.

They taught Americans that a courtroom can be protested like a factory, bus system, draft office, or federal agency.

The legal system is not outside politics simply because judges wear robes.

That insight can be abused. Crowds should not decide guilt. Judges must be protected from threats. Evidence cannot be replaced by popularity.

But public scrutiny of legal institutions is not mob justice by definition.

Courts derive legitimacy from procedures that the public is entitled to examine, criticize, and reform.

The line between pressure and intimidation matters.

So does the line between independence and impunity.

Sacco and Vanzetti live on that line.

The case is therefore a fitting protest for the 1920s: a decade obsessed with modern American identity confronted two immigrant radicals whom millions believed the system had condemned partly because they did not fit it.

The electric chair ended their lives.

It did not end the trial.
