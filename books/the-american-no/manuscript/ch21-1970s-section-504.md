# 1970s: Section 504 — We Will Not Leave

By April 1977, people with disabilities had spent years waiting for the federal government to finish a sentence Congress had already written.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 was only a few lines long. It said, in substance, that an otherwise qualified person with a disability could not be excluded from, denied the benefits of, or subjected to discrimination under a program receiving federal financial assistance solely because of disability.

The language was compact.

Its consequences were not.

The statute placed disability discrimination inside a legal architecture Americans already understood from civil rights law. Federal money would come with conditions. Public institutions and federally funded programs could not treat exclusion as merely a matter of custom, charity, architecture, or administrative convenience.

But a statutory promise is not the same thing as an operating rule.

Section 504 needed regulations.

Without them, agencies, schools, hospitals, transit systems, universities, and other institutions could continue arguing over what the law required, how broadly it applied, and what changes counted as reasonable. The Department of Health, Education, and Welfare drafted regulations under the Ford administration, but they were not signed before Jimmy Carter took office. Carter's new HEW secretary, Joseph Califano, continued reviewing them.

Disabled activists had heard enough review.

On April 5, 1977, protests began at federal offices around the country. Most ended relatively quickly.

San Francisco did not.

There, demonstrators occupied the regional offices of HEW and stayed.

The occupation would last nearly a month.

That fact alone can flatten the story into a record: the longest occupation of a federal building in United States history up to that point, or one of the longest, depending on how the category is defined.

The more important fact is what the occupiers made visible.

They showed that disability rights was not a request for benevolence.

It was a demand for civil rights enforcement.

The coalition inside the building included people with many kinds of disabilities and many kinds of political experience. Some used wheelchairs. Some were blind. Some were deaf. Some had developmental or psychiatric disabilities. Some had been shaped by the independent-living movement emerging in the Bay Area, particularly around Berkeley. Some were experienced organizers. Others were not.

They did not share one body.

They shared an exclusion problem.

That distinction mattered because American policy had often treated disability as an individual medical condition first and a political condition second.

If a person could not enter a school because of stairs, the problem was described as the person's inability to climb.

If a deaf person could not participate because no interpretation was provided, the problem was described as deafness.

If a blind applicant could not use a printed form, the problem was blindness.

The disability-rights movement changed the frame.

The stairs were also a design choice.

The missing interpreter was also an institutional choice.

The inaccessible form was also an administrative choice.

A body might be different without the world around it being neutral.

Section 504 gave that idea legal force.

The San Francisco occupation gave it a public drama.

The practical difficulty of occupying a federal office made the argument unavoidable. Demonstrators needed attendants, medication, food, communication support, accessible sanitation, and medical care. The occupation could not be sustained by slogans alone.

A broader network helped keep it alive.

Community organizations, labor allies, churches, and political groups provided support. The Black Panther Party supplied meals during the occupation, a detail that reveals how disability activism intersected with the wider coalition politics of the Bay Area. Supporters helped with logistics and public pressure. Members of Congress and local politicians became involved. News coverage carried the confrontation beyond the building.

The occupiers were not simply waiting for Washington.

They were changing Washington's cost of delay.

This is the central mechanism of protest.

Officials can postpone an administrative decision when postponement is quiet. Delay can be described as review, consultation, drafting, caution, or technical work.

Occupation converts delay into a visible choice.

Every additional day creates another story.

Every meal delivered to protesters says they are still there.

Every press conference forces the government to explain why it has not acted.

Every photograph turns a regulation into a moral question.

The protesters understood the asymmetry.

They did not control HEW.

They could control whether HEW's inaction remained obscure.

Delegates from the occupation eventually traveled to Washington, D.C., to press the demand directly. The political pressure intensified. On April 28, 1977, Califano signed the Section 504 regulations without the weakening changes activists feared.

The San Francisco occupation ended after twenty-five days.

The signature mattered.

So did the method that produced it.

The regulations helped establish practical requirements across federally funded institutions. Schools, universities, hospitals, social-service agencies, and other recipients of federal assistance faced obligations not merely to refrain from explicit exclusion but to examine how programs actually operated.

Physical access mattered.

Communication access mattered.

Program eligibility mattered.

Rules that looked neutral on paper could still exclude disabled people in practice.

The concept of reasonable accommodation, developed through disability law and policy, pushed institutions toward a different question. Instead of asking only whether a person could fit an existing system, institutions increasingly had to ask whether the system could be modified without fundamentally altering the program or imposing undue burdens.

That is a profound redistribution of responsibility.

Before disability-rights law, many barriers were socially invisible because the person confronting them was expected to adapt, depend on family, accept segregation, or stay away.

Afterward, at least in law, the institution also had something to explain.

Why are there only stairs?

Why is the test administered in only one format?

Why is the service delivered only through speech?

Why does a rule exclude someone whose disability could be accommodated?

Why is a public program designed as though certain citizens do not exist?

The questions do not answer themselves.

They do change who bears the burden of justification.

Section 504 was not the end of the disability-rights struggle.

Far from it.

Enforcement was uneven. Institutions resisted costs. Courts interpreted language. Activists continued organizing around transit, education, housing, employment, institutionalization, independent living, and access to public space. Disability communities themselves debated priorities and representation. People with disabilities of color, poor disabled people, disabled women, and people with psychiatric or intellectual disabilities often confronted overlapping exclusions that a single legal rule could not erase.

The built environment also changed slowly.

A regulation cannot put a curb cut on every corner overnight.

It can make the absence of the curb cut politically and legally legible.

That distinction is easy to underestimate.

The most durable civil-rights victories often begin by changing the category of the problem.

Before: unfortunate circumstance.

After: discrimination.

Before: special favor.

After: access.

Before: private hardship.

After: public obligation.

The 504 movement helped move disability across those boundaries.

The later Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 would extend the framework much further. The ADA addressed employment, state and local government, public accommodations, transportation, telecommunications, and other domains beyond the narrower federal-funding hook of Section 504.

That later law is more famous.

It also stands on ground that had already been fought over.

Section 504 supplied statutory language, administrative experience, legal concepts, activist networks, and proof that disability discrimination could be treated as a civil-rights problem.

The 1977 occupation supplied something else.

It supplied memory.

Movements need proof that resistance can work.

A group enters a federal office because officials will not sign regulations.

They remain after the cameras should have left.

They organize food and care inside a building not designed for them.

They build alliances across movements.

They send representatives across the country.

The secretary signs.

The building empties.

The law remains.

That sequence becomes a usable story for the next fight.

It also complicates the stereotype of disability as dependence.

The protesters certainly depended on one another.

That was not weakness.

Every political movement depends on logistics, money, communication, food, transportation, legal help, and care. Disability activism simply made those dependencies harder to pretend away.

The occupation demonstrated a broader truth about politics: independence is rarely the absence of assistance.

It is the ability to direct one's own life within systems of mutual dependence.

That insight shaped the independent-living movement, which rejected the assumption that disabled people should be controlled by institutions, professionals, or family members simply because they required support.

Support and self-determination were not opposites.

The same principle applies to citizenship.

A person can need a ramp and still be politically autonomous.

A person can need an interpreter and still speak for herself.

A person can need an attendant and still organize a federal occupation.

The 1970s contained many louder American refusals: antiwar protest, tax revolt, prison uprisings, environmental fights, labor conflict, feminist organizing, gay liberation, battles over busing and school desegregation, the aftermath of Watergate.

Section 504 is easier to miss because the immediate demand was administrative.

Sign the regulations.

But that narrow demand carried a larger refusal.

Disabled Americans were refusing to accept the role assigned to them by buildings, bureaucracies, schools, hospitals, transit systems, and public language.

They were refusing the idea that exclusion was natural simply because it had been designed into ordinary life.

They were refusing to wait politely for rights Congress had already enacted.

The American no is not always a crowd in a street.

Sometimes it is a group of people entering an office and deciding not to leave.

In April 1977, that refusal made a few pages of federal regulation into part of the country's civil-rights architecture.
