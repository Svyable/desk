# Chapter 5 — 1810s: We Won’t Go

In January 1817, roughly three thousand Black men crowded into the church founded by Richard Allen in Philadelphia to answer a proposal being discussed by powerful white Americans: free Black people should leave the United States.

The meeting’s answer was no.

That refusal is easy to miss in the standard history of American protest because it did not end with a riot, a famous march, or a law signed in Washington. It was a mass meeting. Its target was an idea before the idea hardened into policy. Its participants were people whose political status was constrained and whose claim to belong in the country was being debated by people with more formal power.

The meeting matters because it named a form of protest that would recur for two centuries: refusing somebody else’s solution to your existence.

The American Colonization Society had been formed in late 1816. Its supporters came from different motives that should not be collapsed into one. Some white antislavery figures believed racial prejudice in the United States was so entrenched that free Black people could never live with full equality and therefore imagined colonization in Africa as a benevolent project. Some slaveholders and racial conservatives saw free Black communities as dangerous examples to enslaved people and wanted them removed. Others combined paternalism, racism, missionary ambition, strategic antislavery thinking, and national anxiety in proportions that varied by person.

Whatever the motive, the proposal shared a premise: the future of free Black Americans could be planned without treating their own desire to remain as decisive.

Philadelphia’s Black community rejected that premise.

James Forten, a prosperous sailmaker, Revolutionary War veteran, abolitionist, and civic leader, chaired the meeting. Richard Allen, founder of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, stood at the center of a community that had already built institutions outside white control. The gathering at Bethel was not an isolated burst of opinion. It emerged from churches, mutual-aid societies, abolition networks, families, businesses, and decades of Black institution-building in Philadelphia.

The crowd was itself the argument.

Colonization advocates could describe free Black people as a social problem scattered across the republic. Three thousand men assembled in one place looked like a political constituency.

This is the decade’s most important protest because it demonstrates a form of power often obscured by election-centered history: a community can establish political standing by acting as though it has standing before institutions are ready to concede it.

The men in the church did not need Congress to certify that they were Americans before they claimed America as home.

That claim carried particular force in Philadelphia. The city was central to the founding mythology of the United States. The Declaration of Independence had been adopted there. The Constitution had been written there. Free Black Philadelphians lived within walking distance of buildings that represented universal political language drafted by men who had tolerated slavery and racial exclusion.

Their protest did not ask to be transported somewhere else so the contradiction would become less visible.

It insisted on the contradiction.

The slogan remembered from the meeting—“We won’t go”—compresses a more complicated politics, but the compression is useful. A colonization plan depended on treating removal as an answer. Refusal returned the problem to the country that had created it.

Racial oppression would not be solved by relocating the people subjected to it.

That sentence sounds obvious now because generations of Black activism made it obvious.

In 1817, the United States had not accepted it.

The country was expanding slavery. Free Black communities faced discriminatory laws, violence, exclusion from many occupations, voting restrictions that varied by place and were often worsening, segregated institutions, kidnapping risk, and constant pressure to prove that freedom did not make them socially dangerous. White Americans who considered themselves opponents of slavery could still doubt the possibility or desirability of a multiracial democracy.

Colonization gave that doubt a reformist language.

That made it more difficult to oppose than open white supremacy alone.

Movements often face proposals that are advertised as solutions to their grievances while preserving the structure that produced the grievance. A company offers a committee instead of a union. A city offers a commission instead of changing police practice. A school offers a separate program instead of access. A government offers relocation instead of land rights. A regulator offers consultation instead of enforcement.

The difficult political work is recognizing when an offered remedy answers the institution’s discomfort rather than the affected community’s demand.

Philadelphia’s 1817 meeting did that.

The protest also demonstrates why community institutions matter before a crisis. Bethel was not built for the colonization meeting. Black churches and mutual-aid organizations existed because Black Philadelphians had needs, beliefs, social lives, and political ambitions that white institutions did not adequately serve. When a political threat arrived, those institutions became organizing infrastructure.

A movement that appears suddenly in public usually has a long private prehistory.

People need places to meet. They need trusted messengers. They need norms for decision-making. They need leaders whose authority has been tested by ordinary community life rather than invented for a news cycle. They need money, printers, couriers, food, housing, legal knowledge, and networks that can survive disagreement.

Protest is often the visible use of infrastructure built for something else.

Church basements become meeting halls. union offices become strike headquarters. beauty shops and barbershops become communication hubs. college campuses become staging grounds. neighborhood groups become mutual-aid systems. friendship networks become transportation networks. Digital movements are not exempt; even online mobilization eventually depends on relationships, skills, credibility, and material support.

The 1817 meeting is a particularly clean example because the institution and the political claim were connected. Independent Black churches represented the capacity of Black Americans to build durable civic life. The colonization proposal implied that such communities were somehow out of place in the nation. Holding the protest in a Black-built institution answered with architecture.

We are already here.

The meeting also complicates the way “voice” is discussed in democratic politics.

To say marginalized people need “a voice” can sound generous while implying they were silent until somebody handed them a microphone. Black Americans in the early republic were not voiceless. They were frequently unheard, excluded, censored, or denied institutional response.

Those are different problems.

James Forten wrote. Black churches published. Conventions met. Petitions circulated. Mutual-aid societies organized. People debated strategies internally. The political system’s failure was not an absence of Black thought. It was a refusal to grant Black thought equal authority over Black lives.

This distinction will matter throughout the book. Protest does not create grievances out of silence. It changes the cost of ignoring voices that already exist.

The colonization debate also shows why the target of protest can be partly cultural rather than governmental. The American Colonization Society was not itself the federal government, though it attracted influential political supporters and later received federal and state backing in various forms. The 1817 meeting targeted an emerging national project and the assumptions that made it respectable.

Movements sometimes have to defeat legitimacy before they can defeat policy.

If an idea becomes common sense, laws can follow with little resistance. If a community makes the idea publicly controversial early, institutional actors may have to defend assumptions they preferred to leave unstated.

The Philadelphia protest forced a question: by what authority were white reformers deciding that free Black Americans belonged somewhere else?

That question exposed divisions among antislavery people. Opposition to slavery did not automatically produce belief in racial equality. Some reformers wanted slavery to end without imagining Black Americans as equal neighbors and citizens. Colonization could serve as a bridge between those positions.

Black opposition weakened that bridge.

The refusal was not merely negative. It contained a positive claim about nationhood.

Black Americans were Americans.

They had been born in the country. Their labor had built it. Some, like Forten, had served the revolutionary cause. Their families and communities were rooted here. The proper answer to racial oppression was not exile but rights.

That claim would become foundational to later abolitionism, Reconstruction politics, civil rights, and every subsequent argument over belonging.

It also reshaped white abolitionism. William Lloyd Garrison, who initially had some contact with colonization thinking, later became a fierce opponent. Forten supported Garrison’s work and helped finance The Liberator. Black activists were not auxiliary beneficiaries of a white-led antislavery movement. They helped determine what antislavery politics would mean.

That correction is essential.

The familiar story of reform often moves from enlightened advocate to grateful constituency. The actual traffic of ideas is messier. People subjected to a system frequently have the clearest view of which proposed reforms leave the system’s assumptions intact. Allies can learn from them or keep rediscovering paternalism under new names.

The 1817 meeting was political education directed outward.

It was also political education directed inward.

A mass gathering lets a community hear itself become a constituency. A person may arrive with a private objection and leave knowing the objection is shared by hundreds or thousands. That knowledge changes risk. It changes what leaders can claim. It changes what opponents must account for.

Numbers do not merely impress outsiders. They update participants’ understanding of one another.

That is one reason authorities and dominant groups often discourage assembly before any specific unlawful act occurs. Assembly produces information. It reveals common grievance. It allows comparison of stories. It creates witnesses. It makes isolation harder to maintain.

For people whose political marginalization depends partly on being treated as separate individuals, gathering is already a form of power.

The 1817 meeting also highlights a basic distinction between migration and removal.

People migrate for opportunity, safety, family, faith, work, adventure, or survival. Black Americans would participate in migrations throughout U.S. history, including emigration projects chosen by some on their own terms. The issue was not whether a person could choose to leave the United States. The issue was whether white political society would define departure as the preferred solution for a group whose equality it did not want to secure.

Choice changes the moral structure of movement.

This would reappear in Indigenous removal, urban renewal, labor migration, refugee policy, housing displacement, school desegregation, and immigration enforcement. Government frequently describes movement in neutral verbs—relocate, resettle, transfer, return—when the political question is who chose the destination.

“We won’t go” is therefore larger than colonization.

It is a sentence about consent.

The meeting’s importance becomes clearer when set beside the decade’s other plausible candidates. The War of 1812 produced dissent, including the Hartford Convention and protests over national policy. Industrial workers were beginning to develop forms of collective action. Indigenous nations resisted American expansion through diplomacy, confederation, and war. Enslaved people resisted constantly, and the 1811 German Coast uprising in Louisiana was one of the largest slave revolts in U.S. history.

Choosing Philadelphia in 1817 does not rank suffering. It identifies a political innovation with a long afterlife: an excluded community organized at scale to reject a nationally respectable reform proposed on its behalf and to assert belonging as the nonnegotiable premise of future politics.

That premise would become indispensable.

It is also unusually modern.

Many contemporary protests are not demands for a new program. They are refusals of the frame in which a program has been offered. Disabled activists reject “care” when care means institutional segregation. Indigenous activists reject “development” when development means extraction without consent. workers reject “flexibility” when flexibility means insecurity assigned downward. Black communities reject “order” when order means unequal enforcement. Immigrants reject “reform” when reform treats their families primarily as enforcement problems.

The argument often begins one level beneath policy: who gets to define the problem?

Philadelphia’s Black community seized that right for itself.

The episode also reminds us that the most important protest of a decade need not be spectacular. Spectacle helps events survive in national memory. Fire photographs well. A cavalry charge creates narrative. A burning building can become a symbol in one image. A crowded church meeting is harder to package.

But politics is not proportional to drama.

A meeting can close off an ideological path.

The 1817 gathering did not end colonization. The American Colonization Society continued for decades and helped establish Liberia. Some Black Americans supported emigration at different moments, especially when racial violence made the promise of equal citizenship seem brutally distant. Black political opinion was not unanimous across time, and it would be false to treat one Philadelphia meeting as a permanent plebiscite for every Black American.

What the meeting did establish was that colonization could not honestly be described as the obvious wish of the people whom it purported to benefit.

That changed the burden of proof.

A paternalistic program is easier to sell when the supposed beneficiary is abstract. Once thousands of people appear and reject it, supporters must decide whether they are willing to proceed against the expressed will of those people.

That decision reveals the paternalism.

This is a form of protest success that rarely gets a statue. The policy may continue. The organization may remain powerful. But its moral cover becomes thinner.

Movements often win first by making hypocrisy expensive.

The meeting is even more interesting when the people at the front of the room are restored to the story as participants in uncertainty rather than monuments who already know the correct line. Forten had previously been sympathetic to Paul Cuffee’s voluntary emigration work. Allen and other prominent Black leaders had reasons to take African settlement seriously, including the brutal limits placed on Black life in the United States. At Bethel, leaders did not simply summon a crowd to ratify a conclusion they had already imposed.

The crowd answered them.

Accounts of the meeting describe the question being put to the assembled men and an overwhelming rejection of colonization. Forten’s later recollection stressed the absence of affirmative voices and the force of the opposition. That moment matters because it turns the meeting from elite advocacy into internal democracy. A movement constituency corrected some of its own most respected leaders.

This is easy to miss in histories that need famous people to carry the plot. Leadership is often portrayed as the source of political insight and crowds as the mechanism for amplifying it. Bethel shows the reverse can happen. Leaders convene. The constituency decides. Good leaders update.

That is not romantic populism. Crowds can be wrong, cruel, impulsive, or exclusionary. The important point is institutional: a community needs mechanisms through which leaders can discover that their assumptions are not shared.

The American Colonization Society had its own version of the opposite problem. It contained powerful men discussing the future of free Black Americans while too often treating Black consent as secondary to white theories of race, slavery, and national order. The Bethel meeting supplied information the project could not honestly claim to lack afterward.

This is why the protest belongs in a book about power rather than merely identity. The no imposed a constraint on representation. Anyone proposing colonization as a benevolent solution now faced a visible fact: a huge Black gathering in one of the country’s most important cities had publicly rejected the premise.

The refusal did not stop the society from operating. It stopped the society from speaking uncontested in the name of its supposed beneficiaries.

The distinction is profound. Political power often begins not with the ability to enact one’s preferred policy but with the ability to prevent someone else from claiming your consent.

Labor unions do this when they insist management cannot speak for workers. Indigenous governments do it when they reject consultation as a substitute for consent. Disability movements do it when they challenge institutions that define quality of life without disabled people in the room. Patients do it when they demand standing in research and treatment policy. A constituency becomes politically real when it acquires the capacity to say: you do not get to represent us without us.

Bethel is an early, unusually vivid American example.

The meeting also helps separate voluntary emigration from colonization as a political program. Paul Cuffee had financed a voyage carrying Black emigrants to Sierra Leone in 1815. Some Black Americans would continue to explore African emigration for reasons of autonomy, safety, religion, commerce, or despair at American racism. Those decisions do not weaken the force of the 1817 refusal. They clarify it.

The argument was not that no Black person could rationally choose Africa.

The argument was that Black departure could not be presumed as the national solution to white prejudice.

A free choice to leave is compatible with a claim to belong. A policy that treats leaving as the answer to someone else’s racism is not.

That distinction gives the chapter a longer afterlife than the eventual fate of the Colonization Society. American institutions repeatedly encounter populations whose presence is described as the problem: free Black communities, Native nations on desired land, immigrant laborers, poor residents in redevelopment zones, unhoused people in public space. Policy can then become an exercise in moving the population rather than changing the structure around it.

The political question is always the one Bethel made audible.

Who gets to define removal as a remedy?

The 1817 meeting also extends the book’s emerging theory of “the people.” Boston patriots called themselves the Body of the People while living in a society that excluded much of the population from formal politics. Shays’ Regulators claimed the people against state institutions. Whiskey rebels and federal officials each claimed to defend republican liberty. Gabriel’s conspiracy revealed a population governed as property rather than citizen.

Philadelphia’s Black protesters did something distinct.

They claimed the country without waiting for the country to claim them.

This is one of the most consequential moves available to marginalized groups. It reverses the direction of recognition.

Ordinary politics says membership is conferred by law.

Protest often says membership is asserted through participation.

When people march, vote where they are barred, sit where they are excluded, strike where they are treated as replaceable, speak where they are expected to defer, or simply refuse to leave, they behave as political members first and force institutions to answer afterward.

The answer can be repression. It can be accommodation. It can be delay. But the action changes the relationship.

The Black men gathered at Bethel in 1817 had limited formal power in a nation increasingly comfortable imagining citizenship in racial terms.

They nevertheless did something no colonization plan could do for them.

They made their own preference public.

The preference was not a request to be tolerated as temporary residents.

It was a claim to home.

The United States would spend the next century and a half trying to decide whether it meant the words it had written about equality and citizenship.

Long before that argument reached the Supreme Court or Congress in its modern form, three thousand men in a Philadelphia church had already supplied the first answer that mattered.

We are here.

We are staying.

You do not get to solve America by subtracting us from it.
