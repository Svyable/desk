# Back Matter — The Protests That Almost Won

A book that chooses one protest per decade creates twenty-six winners and a small army of people who should be annoyed.

Good.

The point of the exercise was never to close the argument. It was to make the argument specific enough to inspect.

A ranking forces tradeoffs that a survey can avoid. If the 1960s belong to Birmingham, then what happens to the March on Washington, the Freedom Rides, Mississippi Freedom Summer, Selma, the antiwar movement, Stonewall, the United Farm Workers, the East L.A. walkouts, the Poor People’s Campaign, and dozens of local movements that changed American life? If Ferguson wins the 2010s, what does that imply about Occupy, Standing Rock, the Women’s March, March for Our Lives, #MeToo, teacher strikes, immigrant-rights organizing, climate strikes, and the protests over family separation?

It implies less than the word “wins” may suggest.

The winner is the event that best satisfies this book’s test: which protest most changed the language, tactics, institutions, coalitions, or limits of American dissent in ways that survived beyond the immediate confrontation?

A different test would produce a different book.

Choose immediate legal victory and Montgomery becomes even harder to dislodge while Seattle weakens. Choose crowd size and the 2006 immigrant-rights mobilization grows stronger while the 1941 March on Washington Movement becomes harder to explain. Choose moral courage under repression and the abolitionist, civil-rights, labor, Indigenous, queer, disability, antiwar, and immigrant histories contain more candidates than one shelf can hold. Choose cultural memory and the famous photograph begins beating the less photographed institution. Choose violence and the book becomes something darker. Choose policy output and movements whose real achievement was changing the public question look smaller than they were.

I chose institutional afterlife because afterlife is what protest gives the next generation.

The Boston Tea Party left more than wet tea. It left a reusable argument about property destruction, representation, commercial leverage, and punishment that expands the constituency of resistance. Shays’ Rebellion left more than a defeated uprising. It became part of the argument over whether the Confederation was strong enough to govern. The Whiskey Rebellion left more than an excise dispute. It tested whether the new federal state could enforce law against organized resistance without simply becoming the empire the Revolution had rejected.

Those early choices contain the book’s central tension. American political culture celebrates rebellion in retrospect and fears rebellion in the present.

The line between patriot and insurgent is often drawn after the winner is known.

The 1800s choice, Gabriel’s planned rebellion, makes that tension impossible to romanticize. Enslaved people were denied the ordinary political channels later Americans would treat as the default architecture of dissent. Planning an armed revolt against slavery cannot be understood as merely another policy protest. It emerged inside a system that defined the protesters themselves as property. The conspiracy was suppressed. Its historical significance lies partly in the fear it produced and the repression that followed.

The 1810s chapter takes the opposite form. Free Black Philadelphians met publicly to reject colonization. There is no harbor spectacle. No arsenal. No factory occupation. The political act is collective self-definition. White reformers could discuss what should happen to Black Americans; Black Americans could answer that they were Americans and did not consent to being exported from the national future.

That meeting beats more visually dramatic alternatives because the argument it makes—about belonging, voice, and who gets to define a population’s interests—recurs through the rest of the book.

The Pawtucket strike of the 1820s wins because industrialization was creating a new kind of conflict. When work moved into factories governed by clocks, machinery, wages, and centralized ownership, refusal could target production itself. That method would become one of the most important political technologies in American history.

The 1830s abolitionist mail campaign wins for another reason. It turned distribution into protest and censorship into evidence. Abolitionists tried to use the national postal network to reach Southern audiences. Opponents interfered with the material. The controversy forced a national argument about whether the infrastructure binding the republic together would carry speech that one region considered socially explosive.

Seneca Falls wins the 1840s even though women’s-rights organizing did not begin there and suffrage did not arrive quickly. The Declaration of Sentiments created a document portable enough to outlive the meeting. Movements need language as much as crowds. A declaration can become infrastructure.

Anthony Burns wins the 1850s because federal enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Law made the national compromise physically visible in Boston. A freedom seeker was arrested, a rescue attempt failed, troops guarded the process, and enormous crowds watched the federal government send a man back into slavery. The state could no longer present the fugitive-slave system as an abstraction operating somewhere else.

Then come the Draft Riots.

They remain one of the book’s most uncomfortable selections because they violate the comforting narrative in which protest is democracy becoming more democratic. Resistance to conscription became mob violence directed heavily at Black New Yorkers. If the book removed the event because its participants used protest for a vicious end, it would teach the wrong lesson. Collective refusal is morally contingent. A crowd can expose unjust power. A crowd can also manufacture its own victims.

The labor sequence that follows—the Great Railroad Strike, Haymarket, Pullman, the Uprising of 20,000, Bread and Roses—could easily have become repetitive. It does not, because each decade changes the mechanism.

Railroad workers reveal the power and vulnerability of a national network. Haymarket shows how a movement’s public meaning can be transformed by violence whose authorship and political implications become contested. Pullman demonstrates the collision among corporate paternalism, labor organization, federal injunctions, mail, rail transport, and federal force. The garment strike shows young immigrant women occupying the center of industrial protest rather than its margins. Lawrence demonstrates how a multilingual workforce could organize under conditions designed to fragment it.

Sacco and Vanzetti win the 1920s not because the book can relitigate a murder case from a century away to everyone’s satisfaction. They win because the case became an international container for anxieties about immigration, anarchism, class, criminal justice, political repression, and the legitimacy of the state. Protest can attach itself to a trial and turn a courtroom into a referendum on a political order.

Flint wins the 1930s because the sit-down strike altered the geography of labor power. Workers did not merely withhold themselves from production. They occupied the productive asset. The factory became both workplace and leverage. That distinction helped produce a new industrial relationship rather than only a dramatic confrontation.

A. Philip Randolph’s threatened 1941 March on Washington wins because it breaks the assumption that a protest must happen to matter. The threatened mass march created enough political cost that Roosevelt issued Executive Order 8802 banning discrimination in defense industries and creating the Fair Employment Practice Committee. A canceled protest can be a successful protest when cancellation is the price of a concession.

Montgomery wins the 1950s because it fused disciplined mass refusal with logistics. A boycott becomes sustainable only when people can still get to work. Car pools, churches, local leadership, legal strategy, fundraising, communications, and endurance turned absence from buses into an institution that could survive retaliation.

Birmingham wins the 1960s because confrontation was not an accident of the campaign. It was part of the strategy. Organizers understood that local segregation had survived moral criticism and legal pressure. They created a crisis that could not remain locally contained. Police dogs, fire hoses, arrests, children in the streets, economic pressure, national media, negotiations, and federal politics became one system. The campaign’s power came partly from making the defense of segregation visible in forms the country could no longer comfortably absorb.

The Section 504 sit-in wins the 1970s because it turns enforcement delay into occupation and disability into civil-rights law. Congress had written a promise. The administration had not finalized the rules that would make the promise operational. Disabled activists entered federal offices and stayed. The San Francisco occupation forced the government to explain every additional day of waiting. The later Americans with Disabilities Act is better known, but 504 helped build the legal and movement architecture that made the later law possible.

ACT UP wins the 1980s because it changed not only policy pressure but the status of the patient. People living with AIDS and their allies learned clinical-trial design, regulatory procedure, drug access, epidemiology, and research policy because expert systems were making life-and-death choices. They did not reject expertise. They rejected the idea that expertise erased accountability. That model traveled into later patient movements.

Seattle wins the 1990s because protesters punctured the era’s language of inevitability. Globalization was often narrated as though technology and economics had simply made a set of political choices unavoidable. The WTO protests did not stop world trade, and street actions did not single-handedly cause the failure of the ministerial meeting. What Seattle changed was the public burden of explanation. Trade policy had to answer questions about labor, environment, sovereignty, developing-country power, corporate influence, and democratic legitimacy.

The 2006 immigrant-rights marches win the 2000s because people regularly discussed as outsiders made themselves visible as a constituency already inside American life. The May Day boycott sharpened the point through absence: work, consumption, school, family, church, neighborhood, and immigration status were not separate systems. The legislative outcome was incomplete, but the organizing generation that emerged would shape DREAMer activism, DACA politics, state and local campaigns, and the national immigration fight that followed.

Ferguson wins the 2010s because a local police shooting became an institutional x-ray. The federal government did not find grounds to prosecute Officer Darren Wilson under federal civil-rights law, and its shooting investigation did not substantiate the most famous version of “hands up” surrender. A separate Justice Department investigation found unconstitutional and racially discriminatory practices in Ferguson’s police and municipal court systems, including the use of law enforcement as a revenue mechanism. Both findings matter. The movement’s significance does not require flattening the evidence. Ferguson changed how Americans connected police force, municipal finance, militarization, race, smartphones, prosecutorial legitimacy, and who gets to author the public record.

Then the ranking reaches the unfinished decade.

George Floyd and the 2020 protest wave are the provisional winner through 2026. “Provisional” is not a hedge hidden in a footnote. It is part of the method. The 2020s are not complete. January 6 is historically enormous but belongs to a different kind of collective refusal: an attempt to interrupt the constitutional transfer of presidential power after an election. The nationwide protests after the Supreme Court’s decision in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization*, campus protests over the Gaza war, labor resurgence, and immigration-related confrontations may look different from the vantage of 2030. A living series should be willing to change its mind.

The runner-ups therefore remain important.

The March on Washington matters even when Birmingham wins. The Freedom Rides matter. Selma matters. Stonewall matters. Occupy matters. Standing Rock matters. #MeToo matters. The Women’s March matters. Antiwar protest matters in several decades. Indigenous resistance cannot be reduced to one chapter because the conflict over land, sovereignty, and the American state predates the republic and survives every decade in this book. Nor can the Black freedom struggle be treated as though it began with Montgomery or ended with civil-rights legislation.

One-winner structure is a lens, not a claim that everyone else came second in history.

The lens does something a complete catalog cannot.

It forces causation into view.

Why this event and not its neighbor?

What actually traveled forward?

Was it the slogan, the organization, the legal rule, the coalition, the technology, the spectacle, the repression, the funding network, the litigation strategy, the occupation, the boycott, the strike, the public document, or the proof that an institution could be made to retreat?

That is the useful argument hiding inside an impossible ranking.

The American protest tradition is not a museum of heroic crowds.

It is a library of political instruments.

Some should be admired.

Some should be studied with horror.

All should be studied closely enough that we stop confusing the existence of a crowd with the existence of a cause.

The country keeps saying no.

The history is in what the no makes possible next.
