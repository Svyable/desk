# 2020s: George Floyd — I Can't Breathe

On May 25, 2020, George Floyd died under the knee of Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin while other officers stood nearby.

The killing was recorded.

That mattered.

Americans had seen police violence before. Black Americans had organized against it for generations. The names before Floyd were already numerous: Amadou Diallo, Oscar Grant, Eric Garner, Michael Brown, Tamir Rice, Freddie Gray, Philando Castile, Breonna Taylor, and many more.

The country did not enter 2020 innocent of the issue.

Then millions of people watched a man die in public custody over the course of minutes.

The video made distance difficult.

The first refusal was immediate: this cannot be treated as routine.

Protest began in Minneapolis and spread across the country. Demonstrations appeared in large cities, suburbs, small towns, and communities with very different racial and political histories. Black Lives Matter, founded years earlier, became the language through which many participants understood the moment, though the protests were never controlled by one organization or one set of demands.

The scale was extraordinary.

So was the argument inside it.

Some protesters demanded prosecution of the officers involved. Some demanded changes in use-of-force policy, training, discipline, qualified immunity, police-union contracts, or civilian oversight. Others argued that reform had failed and called for shrinking, defunding, or abolishing police institutions. Still others joined because the killing represented a broader refusal of racial hierarchy without endorsing every policy demand attached to the movement.

A mass protest is not a unanimous legislature.

Its unity is often narrower than its slogans suggest.

The shared no in 2020 was clearer than the shared yes.

No, the death could not disappear into administrative language.

No, the video could not be explained away as a private misunderstanding between officer and suspect.

No, police legitimacy could not rest entirely on police accounts of police conduct.

No, racial disparity could be treated as irrelevant merely because a policy was written in neutral terms.

That refusal changed institutions even where the movement's largest demands did not become law.

Chauvin was convicted in state court of murder and manslaughter. Other officers faced state and federal cases. The Justice Department later opened a pattern-or-practice investigation into the Minneapolis Police Department and concluded that the city and department engaged in systemic constitutional and federal-law violations.

The federal investigation was not a referendum on every slogan shouted in 2020.

It was something more institutionally important: an official finding that the problem could not be reduced to one officer.

That distinction is central to this chapter.

The American No is often directed at an individual act. Its deepest consequences arrive when the refusal forces attention onto the system that made the act possible, tolerated it, obscured it, or repeated it.

Floyd's death became a global symbol because the evidence was both specific and systemic.

A specific man died.

A specific officer pressed his knee onto Floyd's neck and back.

Specific bystanders pleaded with police to stop.

Specific officers were later held accountable in court.

But the movement that followed asked a larger question: what if the incident was not an exception to an otherwise sound institution?

That question moved quickly beyond Minneapolis.

Cities reconsidered chokeholds, no-knock warrants, body-camera rules, use-of-force standards, school-police contracts, budget allocations, civilian review, and public access to disciplinary records. Corporations issued statements. Sports leagues painted slogans on courts and fields. Universities renamed programs and reconsidered campus policing. Newsrooms changed style rules and coverage priorities. Philanthropic money moved. Museums, publishers, and cultural institutions revisited whose experience counted as central rather than supplemental.

Some changes lasted.

Some did not.

Some produced backlash almost immediately.

Violent crime rose in many American cities during the pandemic era, though the causes were contested and varied. Political rhetoric shifted toward public disorder and staffing shortages. Police departments struggled with recruitment and retention in some places. Voters rejected or narrowed reform measures in others. The slogan "defund the police" proved mobilizing for some activists and politically damaging for some candidates.

The backlash does not cancel the movement's importance.

It helps measure it.

A refusal that changes nothing rarely reorganizes national politics around the question of whether it went too far.

George Floyd's death did not begin the American argument over policing, race, or state violence.

It changed the argument's scale and vocabulary.

The strongest runner-up for the 2020s is the January 6, 2021 attack on the United States Capitol, another spectacular no directed at the legitimacy of an American institution. But the forms of refusal differ.

January 6 sought to interrupt the constitutional transfer of presidential power after an election. It is historically enormous.

The George Floyd protests, however, better fit the long arc of this book: collective refusal by people demanding that an institution claiming legitimate authority submit itself to public judgment.

That does not mean every act committed during the protests was legitimate. Property destruction, arson, assault, opportunistic looting, and indiscriminate violence harmed people and communities, including communities the movement claimed to defend. Police responses also varied from restraint to documented abuses against protesters and journalists.

The existence of disorder cannot substitute for analysis.

Nor can the existence of official misconduct.

A movement this large contains contradiction.

The question is what changed because Americans refused to return to normal.

One answer is evidentiary.

The smartphone became a form of civic oversight.

Recording police was not new, but by 2020 nearly every bystander could become a witness with a distribution network. The traditional sequence—incident, official report, local news story, perhaps investigation—could be disrupted by a video uploaded before institutions had settled on their explanation.

That shifted power without equalizing it.

A video can reveal and still be ignored.

A video can circulate and still be misread.

A video can become evidence and still fail to produce accountability.

But it changes the cost of secrecy.

Another answer is institutional.

The protests made police governance a mainstream subject of municipal politics in places where it had been treated as specialized administration. Budgets, union contracts, disciplinary systems, qualified immunity, traffic enforcement, mental-health calls, and armed response became ordinary political arguments rather than technical matters left to departments and city attorneys.

A third answer is cultural.

The phrase "Black Lives Matter" moved from a contested movement slogan into a reference point that institutions across American life had to answer, whether by adoption, rejection, reinterpretation, or backlash.

That did not create consensus.

It destroyed the option of pretending there was no argument.

The chapter should not end by claiming the uprising solved what it named.

It did not.

Police killings continued. Racial disparities continued. Reform laws produced mixed results. Political coalitions fractured. Some departments changed policies while resisting deeper oversight. Some activists concluded that the moment had been absorbed by symbolic gestures. Some voters concluded that reformers had ignored public safety.

The consequence is not a completed transformation.

It is a changed burden of proof.

After 2020, an institution defending the status quo had to answer evidence that millions of Americans had already seen and arguments they had already heard.

The country had been told before.

In 2020 it was shown.

And the answer from the streets was no.

**Verdict:** George Floyd and the 2020 protest movement win the decade because they converted a long-running struggle over police violence and racial inequality into a national institutional crisis, forcing courts, cities, police departments, corporations, media, and voters to argue publicly over what legitimate policing requires and who has the authority to define it.
