# Chapter 26 — 2020s: George Floyd — I Can't Breathe — Provisional Through 2026

On May 25, 2020, George Floyd died under the restraint of Minneapolis police officers after Officer Derek Chauvin kept a knee on Floyd for minutes while Floyd was handcuffed and prone.

The killing was recorded.

That mattered.

Americans had seen police violence before. Black Americans had organized against it for generations. The names before Floyd were already numerous: Amadou Diallo, Oscar Grant, Eric Garner, Michael Brown, Tamir Rice, Freddie Gray, Philando Castile, Breonna Taylor, and many more.

The country did not enter 2020 innocent of the issue.

It entered 2020 under a different kind of pressure too.

The COVID-19 pandemic had closed workplaces, schools, churches, stadiums, theaters, restaurants, and large parts of ordinary public life. Tens of millions of Americans were experiencing isolation, unemployment, risk, fear, and an unusually intimate dependence on state rules. The disease itself was exposing racial and economic inequalities in work, housing, health, and vulnerability. People who could stay home were doing so. People called essential were often being asked to assume risks that better-paid people could avoid.

Then millions of people watched a man die in public custody over the course of minutes.

The video made distance difficult.

A teenager, Darnella Frazier, recorded the encounter. Bystanders pleaded with officers. Floyd said he could not breathe. The event did not arrive as an institutional summary after an internal review. It arrived as visible duration.

That distinction matters.

A photograph can capture an instant and leave the sequence uncertain. A short clip can make context disappear. The Floyd video forced viewers to remain with the event long enough to watch repeated opportunities for the officers to change course.

The first refusal was immediate: this cannot be treated as routine.

Protest began in Minneapolis and spread across the country. Demonstrations appeared in large cities, suburbs, small towns, and communities with very different racial and political histories. Black Lives Matter, founded years earlier, became the language through which many participants understood the moment, though the protests were never controlled by one organization or one set of demands.

The scale was extraordinary.

So was the argument inside it.

Some protesters demanded prosecution of the officers involved. Some demanded changes in use-of-force policy, training, discipline, qualified immunity, police-union contracts, or civilian oversight. Others argued that reform had failed and called for shrinking, defunding, or abolishing police institutions. Still others joined because the killing represented a broader refusal of racial hierarchy without endorsing every policy demand attached to the movement.

A mass protest is not a unanimous legislature.

Its unity is often narrower than its slogans suggest.

The shared no in 2020 was clearer than the shared yes.

No, the death could not disappear into administrative language.

No, the video could not be explained away as a private misunderstanding between officer and suspect.

No, police legitimacy could not rest entirely on police accounts of police conduct.

No, racial disparity could be treated as irrelevant merely because a policy was written in neutral terms.

That refusal changed institutions even where the movement's largest demands did not become law.

Chauvin was convicted in state court of murder and manslaughter. Other officers faced state and federal cases. The Justice Department later opened a pattern-or-practice investigation into the Minneapolis Police Department and concluded in 2023 that the city and department engaged in systemic violations of the Constitution and federal law.

The federal investigation was not a referendum on every slogan shouted in 2020.

It was something more institutionally useful: an official investigation asking whether the problem could be reduced to one officer.

The report concluded it could not.

That distinction is central to this chapter.

The American no is often directed at an individual act. Its deepest consequences arrive when refusal forces attention onto the system that made the act possible, tolerated it, obscured it, or repeated it.

Floyd's death became a global symbol because the evidence was both specific and capable of supporting a systemic question.

A specific man died.

Specific officers were present.

Specific bystanders objected.

Specific officers were later prosecuted.

Those facts do not by themselves prove every institutional theory attached to the movement.

They create the demand to test one.

What if the incident was not an exception to an otherwise sound institution?

The Justice Department investigation later examined force, discrimination, speech, behavioral-health response, accountability, and other practices. Its findings gave the protest an institutional afterlife independent of corporate statements, murals, slogans, and the emotional intensity of 2020.

That is important because movements can become trapped inside their own most visible week.

A store burns.

A statue comes down.

A march fills a bridge.

A mayor kneels.

A company posts a black square.

The images create a sense that everything is changing at once.

Institutions change more slowly.

Policies have definitions. Budgets have line items. Union contracts have clauses. Prosecutors have standards. Courts have jurisdiction. Police departments have training systems, supervisory structures, disciplinary histories, recruitment pipelines, dispatch practices, data systems, and cultures that survive one news cycle.

The distance between the speed of protest and the speed of administration became one of the defining tensions of 2020.

Cities reconsidered chokeholds, no-knock warrants, body-camera rules, use-of-force standards, school-police contracts, budget allocations, civilian review, public access to disciplinary records, traffic enforcement, and the role of armed officers in behavioral-health calls. Some jurisdictions passed laws. Some departments changed policy internally. Some changes were symbolic. Some were substantive. Some were challenged in court or contract negotiations. Some were later narrowed or reversed.

That mixed record should remain mixed.

A chapter about protest is not improved by pretending every reform worked.

The slogan that produced the largest internal argument was "defund the police."

For some activists, defunding meant reallocating selected police functions and money toward housing, mental-health response, violence prevention, youth programs, transportation, or other public services. For others it was a step toward abolition. Critics often heard or deliberately emphasized the most literal interpretation: reduce police capacity at a time when many residents remained deeply concerned about violent crime.

The ambiguity made the slogan powerful inside part of the movement and vulnerable outside it.

A good protest phrase does not have to be a policy memo.

A governing coalition eventually does.

This gap between mobilization language and administrative design became one reason the post-2020 politics moved so quickly from apparent consensus to conflict.

Major corporations issued statements. Sports leagues placed racial-justice language on courts and uniforms. Universities reviewed names, policing, hiring, curriculum, and campus practice. Publishers, museums, philanthropies, and news organizations revisited whose experience and scholarship they treated as central. Diversity, equity, and inclusion programs gained institutional attention and then became targets of political backlash.

The backlash was not incidental.

It was another measure of how far the protest had moved the agenda.

By the middle of the decade, politicians and institutions were not arguing about whether 2020 had happened. They were arguing about which changes it had justified, whether reforms had gone too far, whether organizations had substituted symbolism for competence, whether racial categories were being used too broadly, whether public safety had been neglected, and whether institutional responses to the protests themselves required reversal.

A movement can lose policy ground after changing political language.

Those are separate measurements.

Violent crime rose in many American cities during the pandemic period, though the causes were contested and varied across place and time. Police departments in some jurisdictions reported recruitment and retention difficulties. Residents in high-crime neighborhoods did not necessarily experience "police" and "safety" as opposite terms. Some wanted less abusive policing and more reliable protection at the same time.

National argument often failed to hold those demands together.

Activists sometimes treated calls for more policing as evidence of reaction.

Critics sometimes treated demands for accountability as hostility to public safety.

Local reality was harder.

A resident could want fewer unnecessary stops, no racial profiling, quicker emergency response, more homicide clearance, better de-escalation, fewer armed responses to mental-health crises, and serious consequences for an officer who lies or uses unlawful force.

That combination is not ideologically elegant.

It is institutionally specific.

One long-term consequence of 2020 may be that policing became less available as a single policy object. Different functions began to separate in public argument.

Traffic enforcement is not a homicide investigation.

A welfare check is not a robbery in progress.

A school officer is not a SWAT team.

Crowd control is not neighborhood patrol.

A department can be overpowered in one domain and under-capable in another.

The protests pushed some cities to ask which tasks require an armed officer and which were assigned to police largely because police were the public institution available twenty-four hours a day.

That is an architectural question, not merely a budget question.

The event changed evidence too.

The smartphone had already altered public witnessing before 2020. Ferguson made that obvious in 2014. Floyd made it unavoidable.

Recording police was not new. The Rodney King beating had been videotaped in 1991. What changed by 2020 was distribution. A witness no longer needed a television station to make an image national. The phone was camera, archive, transmitter, and address book at once.

That shifted power without equalizing it.

A video can reveal and still be ignored.

A video can circulate and still be misread.

A video can become evidence and still fail to produce accountability.

A camera records an angle, not omniscience.

But ubiquitous recording changes the cost of secrecy and the sequence of explanation.

Before the networked phone, an official statement might define the first widely heard account. Afterward, officials increasingly had to answer a public artifact they did not create.

This matters beyond policing.

Every institution with authority now operates in an environment where the governed may possess their own evidentiary system. Workers record managers. Passengers record airline staff. students record professors. Residents record public meetings. Soldiers record battlefields. Protesters record police, and police record protesters.

The politics of evidence has become reciprocal surveillance.

That brings its own dangers.

A person can be globally exposed before facts are tested. A clip can remove what happened thirty seconds earlier. Internet crowds can identify the wrong person. Platforms can reward outrage faster than correction. Public evidence can become public punishment without due process.

The lesson of 2020 is therefore not that citizen video is always right and official records are always wrong.

It is that no institution can safely assume it will control the first record.

The protests also changed the geography of solidarity.

Large demonstrations occurred far beyond the cities most associated with Black protest politics. Small towns held marches. Predominantly white communities held vigils. International demonstrations used Floyd's name to address policing and racism in their own countries.

A local death became a global political vocabulary.

That scale creates interpretive temptation. A protest can spread because people agree on the core grievance while disagreeing on almost everything else. Participation numbers do not translate automatically into support for a specific bill, budget cut, abolition program, or candidate.

This is one reason polling after mass mobilization can appear contradictory.

People can approve of a protest's moral claim and reject one of its policy slogans.

Movements often mistake attention for mandate because attention is the first scarce resource they have successfully acquired.

Governing requires a second conversion.

The movement also contained disorder.

Property destruction, arson, assault, opportunistic looting, and indiscriminate violence harmed people and communities, including communities the broader movement claimed to defend. The scale and authorship varied by place. Peaceful protesters, people committing crimes, organized activists, provocateurs, bystanders, police officers, journalists, residents, and opportunists could occupy the same blocks without belonging to the same project.

Police responses varied too, from restraint to force later criticized or challenged as excessive. Journalists and protesters documented uses of tear gas, projectiles, kettling, arrest, and physical force. Officers also faced thrown objects, fires, property destruction, and situations that became genuinely dangerous.

The existence of disorder cannot substitute for analysis.

Nor can the existence of official misconduct.

A movement this large contains contradiction.

The historical task is to identify which behavior belonged to which actor and what institutions changed because the event created pressure.

The criminal cases around Floyd provide one form of consequence.

Chauvin's state conviction mattered because police killings rarely produce an easy prosecution. The legal standard was not "did the protest demand punishment?" The state had to prove crimes to a jury under criminal law. Other officers faced federal and state proceedings addressing their conduct and duties.

That separation between protest and adjudication is essential.

The crowd can demand a case.

The crowd cannot be the court.

A justice system that prosecutes because a crowd is angry is dangerous.

A justice system that refuses to investigate because a crowd is angry is dangerous too.

Legitimate pressure opens the institution to scrutiny without replacing its evidentiary rules with applause.

This book's earlier chapters keep returning to that boundary. Haymarket becomes partly a story about whether fear of anarchism corrupted adjudication. Sacco and Vanzetti become an international protest because people doubted the neutrality of a criminal process. Ferguson forces a distinction between a movement's symbolic claim and separate federal findings. George Floyd produces a different configuration: unusually strong public video, a massive protest wave, criminal prosecutions, and a later systemic federal investigation.

The mechanisms reinforce one another without being identical.

The strongest challenger for the 2020s is January 6, 2021.

The attack on the United States Capitol was another spectacular American no directed at an institution claiming legitimate authority. It sought to interrupt the constitutional transfer of presidential power after the 2020 election. Participants ranged from people who believed false claims about election fraud to organized extremist groups and others who entered the Capitol amid the chaos. The event led to deaths, injuries, prosecutions, congressional investigation, and enduring argument over political violence, election legitimacy, presidential responsibility, and democratic transfer of power.

Why does it not win here, at least provisionally?

Because this book weights inherited protest architecture and institutional afterlife. January 6 is historically enormous, but its principal political object was a specific transfer of power and its long-term place in American protest history remains unusually entangled with electoral politics, criminal accountability, and the history of insurrection. The George Floyd mobilization had broader immediate diffusion across jurisdictions and institutions and altered multiple policy domains beyond the triggering case.

That judgment could change.

So could the field.

The Supreme Court's 2022 decision in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* produced large abortion-rights protests and a state-by-state political struggle over reproductive law. Labor organizing and strikes gained new visibility in several industries. Campus protests over the war in Gaza created conflicts over war, university governance, antisemitism, Islamophobia, encampment, policing, donor influence, and speech. Immigration and federal-enforcement conflicts continued to generate demonstrations. Later events in 2027, 2028, or 2029 may dwarf the cases already visible.

This is why the chapter title contains a date condition.

Provisional through 2026.

A later edition must rerun the ballot.

That requirement changes how the ending should sound.

It cannot say the decade's story is settled.

It can say what has already survived six years of consequence.

George Floyd's death did not begin the American argument over policing, race, or state violence.

It changed the argument's scale and vocabulary.

Police governance became a mainstream subject of municipal politics in places where it had been treated as specialized administration. Budgets, union contracts, disciplinary systems, traffic enforcement, behavioral-health calls, armed response, body-camera rules, and public records became ordinary political arguments rather than matters left only to departments and city attorneys.

The phrase "Black Lives Matter" became a reference point that institutions across American life had to answer, whether by adoption, rejection, reinterpretation, or backlash.

That did not create consensus.

It destroyed the option of pretending there was no argument.

The movement did not solve what it named.

Police killings continued. Racial disparities continued. Reform laws produced mixed results. Political coalitions fractured. Some departments changed policies while resisting deeper oversight. Some activists concluded that the moment had been absorbed by corporate statements and symbolic gestures. Some voters concluded that reformers had underestimated disorder and public demand for policing. Some reforms survived quietly after the national attention moved on.

The consequence is not a completed transformation.

It is a changed burden of proof.

After 2020, an institution defending the status quo had to answer evidence that millions of Americans had already seen and arguments they had already heard. Critics of the movement also had to answer the federal record, the criminal cases, and the difference between legitimate concern about crime and acceptance of unlawful policing.

The country had been told before.

In 2020 it was shown with unusual duration and distribution.

Through 2026, George Floyd and the protest movement that followed remain this book's provisional winner because they converted a long-running struggle over police violence and racial inequality into a national institutional crisis, forcing courts, cities, police departments, corporations, media organizations, voters, and federal investigators to argue publicly over what legitimate policing requires and who has authority to define it.

That is the current verdict.

The decade still gets a vote.
