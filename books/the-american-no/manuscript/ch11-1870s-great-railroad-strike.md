# Chapter 11 — 1870s: The Great Railroad Strike

The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 began with a wage cut and discovered a nation.

Railroads were the circulatory system of postwar industrial America. They moved freight, passengers, mail, soldiers, raw materials, and information. They linked regional markets into something increasingly national. They also concentrated enormous corporate power in companies whose financial decisions could reach workers across hundreds of miles.

When the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad announced another wage reduction in July 1877, workers in Martinsburg, West Virginia, stopped trains.

The stoppage spread.

Baltimore. Pittsburgh. Chicago. St. Louis. Louisville. Smaller towns along the lines. Workers on different railroads and in different crafts joined or sympathized. Crowds gathered. Militia units were called. In some places militia members hesitated to fire on strikers they knew. In others violent confrontation erupted. Rail yards burned. Federal troops were deployed. By the time the uprising was suppressed, the country had seen labor conflict on a scale it had never experienced before.

The strike belongs to the 1870s because it made industrial power national and labor resistance national in the same week.

Before railroads, a strike could shut one mill or one local trade. Railroads created a system in which interruption at one junction could affect commerce far away. The same network that gave corporations scale gave workers a new form of leverage.

Infrastructure is a promise to power and a vulnerability at the same time.

The more a society depends on a system, the more disruptive it becomes when the people operating that system stop.

This is why railroad workers occupied such a consequential position. They did not own the tracks, locomotives, depots, rolling stock, or freight. But the machinery did not move itself.

The Great Strike made that obvious.

It also unfolded during the long depression that followed the Panic of 1873. Workers had already endured layoffs and wage reductions. Railroad corporations faced intense competition and financial pressure. Executives described cuts as necessary for survival. Workers experienced them as another demand that employees absorb the cost of a crisis they had not created.

That argument is nearly permanent in labor history.

When an enterprise is prosperous, management often treats profits as returns to capital and executive decision-making. When the enterprise is distressed, workers are told sacrifice must be shared.

The strike asks who gets to define sharing.

In 1877, there was no mature federal labor-relations system for bargaining over the answer. National unions were weak after the depression. The law largely protected property and contract on terms favorable to employers. Workers had organizations and traditions of collective action, but nothing like the later institutional architecture of collective bargaining.

The result was improvisational and explosive.

In Martinsburg, workers physically prevented trains from leaving. State authorities tried to restore operations. West Virginia’s governor sought federal assistance. President Rutherford B. Hayes sent troops.

The use of federal soldiers established a pattern that would shadow major labor conflicts for decades: when a strike interfered with interstate commerce or became violent, the federal government’s most reliable early relationship to organized labor was often military.

Workers could interpret this as proof that government belonged to capital.

Officials could answer that rail traffic, property, mail, and public order were national responsibilities.

Both descriptions could be true at once.

The state does not need to be secretly owned by a corporation to act in ways that protect corporate operations. A government tasked with preserving commerce will often defend the infrastructure through which commerce flows. When workers target that flow, the state can appear neutral in principle and aligned in effect.

The Great Strike taught labor movements to pay attention to that difference.

It also exposed the limits of local policing in a national economy. A mayor or sheriff could not solve a work stoppage spreading across multiple railroad systems and cities. Governors could call militia, but militia were drawn from communities whose class sympathies could be uncertain. Federal troops offered a more dependable coercive force precisely because they were less entangled in local relationships.

Distance became a source of discipline.

That is a recurring feature of state power. Authorities often import personnel when local enforcers are considered unreliable, compromised, or too sympathetic to protesters. The British had done versions of this in colonial America. State governments would later use National Guard units. Corporations hired private detectives and guards who did not share neighborhood ties with strikers.

The protester’s solidarity can be countered by the authority’s distance.

Pittsburgh became one of the strike’s most violent centers. Local militia resistance and crowd hostility collided. Troops fired into crowds, killing and wounding people. Rage escalated. Railroad property and other buildings burned. The scale of destruction transformed a labor stoppage into an urban uprising.

Again the distinction between a movement and everything that happens around it becomes difficult.

Organized railroad workers had specific grievances. Crowds included people who were not railroad employees. Some participated out of sympathy, anger at inequality, opportunity, or hostility to authorities. Property destruction widened beyond a controlled bargaining tactic. Once violence escalated, the strike’s political meaning became vulnerable to being defined by fire.

Employers and officials could point to disorder as proof that force was necessary.

Workers could point to shootings as proof that authorities had converted an economic dispute into class war.

Escalation again became reciprocal evidence.

The Great Strike is important partly because ordinary Americans could now see something like a national class conflict.

Industrial capitalism had created fortunes, corporations, managers, wage workers, and financial networks on scales difficult to imagine at the founding. The post-Civil War economy celebrated development, rail construction, steel, finance, and continental integration. The strike showed that the same development produced people with shared grievances across state lines.

The worker was becoming a national political category.

That was new enough to frighten elites.

The Paris Commune of 1871 still haunted conservative imaginations. European socialism and labor radicalism were becoming reference points in American debate. Immigration brought political traditions from abroad. When railroads stopped and crowds confronted troops, some observers saw not a wage dispute but the possibility of social revolution.

This is another durable pattern: elites interpret disruptive protest through the scariest available foreign analogy.

The external label can clarify genuine ideological influence. It can also exaggerate coherence. The Great Strike did not operate under one national revolutionary command. It was a wave of local actions connected by railroad networks, news, shared conditions, and imitation.

The spread itself was the organization.

That form of diffusion matters.

Movements do not always require a central office to become national. A tactic can travel faster than a hierarchy. Workers in one place see that workers elsewhere have stopped, and the example changes what seems possible. News functions as permission.

In 1877, telegraph and newspapers accelerated that process. The railroad carried both the economy and the story of the economy stopping.

This combination created one of the first truly networked American protests.

The tactic moved along the infrastructure it disrupted.

Later movements would reproduce this pattern through sit-down strikes, campus occupations, antiwar demonstrations, civil-rights sit-ins, immigrant marches, and digitally coordinated protests. A local action becomes a template. Another city copies it. The movement gains scale without anyone issuing a universal order.

Authorities face a puzzle because arresting one leader does not stop imitation.

The Great Strike also shows that solidarity can expand beyond a workplace without becoming politically coherent.

In several cities, workers from other trades joined demonstrations or sympathy strikes. Unemployed people and neighborhood residents participated. The railroad grievance became a container for wider anger about wages, unemployment, wealth, and industrial power.

Broadening can make a movement stronger.

It can also make demands less precise.

A railroad company can negotiate a wage issue with railroad workers. It cannot negotiate “industrial capitalism is producing intolerable inequality” with a crowd containing thousands of people who have no representative structure.

This is the scale problem of protest.

As movements become more representative of diffuse grievance, they may become harder to bargain with.

Institutions often demand a leader who can deliver the movement. Movements often do not have one.

The absence can be democratic and dangerous at the same time.

The strike was ultimately defeated. Trains resumed. Troops and authorities reestablished control. Workers did not win a national settlement that transformed railroad labor overnight.

Yet calling the Great Strike a failure would misunderstand what it introduced.

It demonstrated the national disruptive capacity of labor. It accelerated elite interest in armories, militia reform, and methods for controlling urban unrest. It helped convince workers that local organization was insufficient against national corporations. It entered the political imagination as evidence that the “labor question” could no longer be contained within individual workplaces.

The labor question was really several questions.

Could industrial workers earn enough to live?

Who controlled hours and conditions?

What rights did workers have to organize?

Could employers replace strikers freely?

When did private conflict become public emergency?

Would government mediate or repress?

How much inequality could a republic tolerate before economic hierarchy became political instability?

The Great Strike did not answer these questions.

It made them national.

The episode also belongs immediately after the Draft Riots for a reason.

Both events involved working-class crowds, economic grievance, violence, state force, and fractured solidarity. But the target structure differed.

In 1863, racial terror redirected much of the New York crowd’s anger toward Black residents. In 1877, the central target was the railroad system and the corporations operating it, though the strike wave still occurred within a society divided by race, ethnicity, skill, and gender.

The contrast shows that class grievance does not determine its own politics.

Organization does.

A grievance must be given a target, a story, and a coalition.

The Great Strike pushed more of that anger upward toward employers and government protection of commerce. It therefore offered a stronger foundation for labor politics even though the movement remained fragmented.

The strike also raises the question of sabotage and property.

Railroad property was destroyed on a large scale in some places. To workers, railroads could symbolize corporate wealth and the power to dictate wages. To communities dependent on rail service, burning infrastructure imposed costs far beyond shareholders. To authorities, destruction justified military intervention.

Property violence can create immediate leverage while eroding the infrastructure a movement’s own communities need.

That tradeoff is not resolved by moral slogans.

A movement has to ask whether property is being used as a tool of oppression, whether disabling it is necessary, whether the damage is targeted, who bears the downstream cost, and whether the tactic advances the objective.

In 1877, much destruction emerged from escalation rather than disciplined national strategy.

That matters because uncontrolled destruction can allow opponents to select the movement’s most dramatic act as its whole meaning.

Fire is easier to photograph, draw, and remember than a wage ledger.

The public sees the burning depot and may never learn about the repeated wage cuts.

Effective protest therefore fights on two fronts: material leverage and narrative leverage.

The Great Strike often won the first temporarily and lost the second among elites terrified of disorder.

But working-class memory preserved another version.

The strike became part of a lineage of resistance that later organizers could use to argue that workers possessed power only when they acted together. The Knights of Labor would expand dramatically in the following decade. Industrial conflicts would become increasingly central to national politics. Haymarket, Homestead, Pullman, Ludlow, Lawrence, Flint—all belong to a world whose outline was visible in 1877.

The railroad strike was the rehearsal America could not pretend not to see.

The Library of Congress chronology makes the national sequence unusually visible. Workers in Martinsburg refused to let freight move, and the conflict traveled into Maryland, Pennsylvania, New York, and farther west as news and imitation spread. Hayes issued separate proclamations connected to West Virginia on July 18, Maryland on July 21, and Pennsylvania on July 23. The federal response itself had to follow the strike across jurisdictions.

That is a sign of scale more concrete than the word nationwide.

The federal government was not intervening once in one company dispute. It was repeatedly being asked to restore the functioning of a network whose private ownership crossed public boundaries. A corporation’s labor policy in one place had become a governance problem in several states.

The crisis therefore anticipated a question the United States would face repeatedly as corporations grew beyond the effective reach of local politics: which level of government can bargain with an employer whose operational geography is larger than the town or state in which a strike begins?

At first, the national government had a clearer answer for restoring trains than for mediating wages.

The asymmetry shaped labor memory. Federal troops could appear quickly when tracks were blocked; federal protection for organizing rights would take generations to become durable, and even then would remain contested. The sequence helped workers conclude that industrial citizenship lagged behind industrial scale.

The strike also arrived at an extraordinary political hinge. Reconstruction was ending. Hayes had taken office after the disputed election of 1876, and federal troops were being withdrawn from the former Confederacy as the national government retreated from enforcing Black civil and political rights in the South. Within months, federal force was being used decisively in northern labor conflict.

The comparison should not be flattened into a slogan that troops were simply “moved from protecting Black rights to protecting capital”; the deployments, commands, and legal contexts differed. But the juxtaposition is historically revealing. State capacity is never just a quantity. It is a choice about where capacity is spent.

A government can be strong in one domain and retreat in another.

For people living through 1877, those choices helped define what national authority was for.

The strike’s geographic spread also produced different local outcomes, which is another reason to resist a single heroic narrative. Pittsburgh experienced some of the worst bloodshed and destruction. Baltimore saw deadly clashes. Reading saw militia fire kill people. In St. Louis, a broader general strike briefly connected railroad workers with other trades and radical organizers. Chicago’s conflict had its own mix of workers, unemployed people, police, and militia.

A national movement can therefore be one event analytically and many events experientially.

The common mechanism was network interruption. The local politics determined how interruption escalated.

This distinction becomes crucial later in the book, especially with decentralized protests such as Seattle, Ferguson, and 2020. National labels create coherence after the fact. Participants encounter local police, local employers, local mayors, local newspapers, local racial structures, and local tactical cultures.

Scale does not erase place.

The Great Strike’s defeat also deserves precision. The Library of Congress notes that the initial wage cuts were not rolled back as an immediate national concession. State and federal force restored rail operation, many participants were jailed, and companies continued anti-union efforts. If this book measured importance by negotiated gains, 1877 would be a strange winner.

It wins because the strike changed what every side had to plan for next.

Railroads and governments invested more seriously in mechanisms for containing unrest. Workers saw the weakness of spontaneous solidarity without durable organization. Political observers saw that economic grievance could cross state borders as quickly as commerce did. Later unions inherited both the possibility and the warning.

This is protest as institutional education through defeat.

The strongest runner-up for the decade is Susan B. Anthony’s 1872 act of voting in Rochester and the prosecution that followed. Her deliberate test of citizenship and suffrage was legally focused, symbolically powerful, and part of a broader movement of women attempting to vote. It would fit this book’s interest in people acting as political members before law fully recognized them.

The railroad strike wins because it reveals a larger transformation in the country’s operating system. Industrial capitalism had become networked enough that a labor refusal could create a national emergency before a national labor institution existed to contain it.

That mismatch—national capital, local labor law, improvised state response—would define the next half century.

Its most lasting lesson was structural.

Industrial systems concentrate dependency.

That makes them efficient.

It also makes them protestable.

Every network has nodes. Every supply chain has chokepoints. Every service has workers without whom service stops. Corporations spend enormous energy reducing dependence on any individual employee through standardization, automation, replacement labor, subcontracting, and redundancy.

Workers respond by increasing collective dependence through organization.

The contest is partly an engineering problem.

Who can make whom more replaceable?

In 1877, railroad workers discovered that their geographic dispersion did not prevent common action because the railroad itself connected them.

The company had built the network.

The strike used it.

That is why the Great Railroad Strike is more than an early labor battle.

It is the decade when American protest learned to think at system scale.
