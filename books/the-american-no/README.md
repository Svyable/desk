*250 Years of Protest, One Decade at a Time*

America did not spend 250 years agreeing about change. It spent 250 years arguing about when **no** becomes powerful enough that institutions have to answer.

*The American No* chooses one defining protest for every decade from the 1770s through the unfinished 2020s and tells the story behind the choice. The selection is intentionally argumentative. “Most important” does not mean largest crowd, purest cause, most famous photograph, cleanest victory, or movement history later placed on the right side of a textbook. It means the protest that most changed the available language, tactics, institutions, coalitions, or limits of American dissent in its decade and beyond.

That rule produces a less comfortable history. The Boston Tea Party sits beside Shays’ Rebellion and the Whiskey Rebellion. Seneca Falls sits beside the New York Draft Riots, where resistance to conscription became racist terror. A disability-rights occupation sits beside a march A. Philip Randolph never had to hold because the threat itself extracted federal action. ACT UP sits beside Seattle, Ferguson, and the 2006 immigrant-rights marches. Protest is treated here as a political instrument, not a moral credential.

The 2020s selection is explicitly **provisional through 2026**. The decade is not over, and a later Desk edition must re-run the choice rather than pretending history has already closed the ballot.

## Editorial principle

The book can rank. It cannot pretend the ranking is fact. Every chapter should distinguish documented event history from the author’s judgment about why that event wins its decade. Runner-ups and counterarguments belong in the record. A protest’s historical importance does not certify its morality, and a movement’s moral force does not prove every factual claim made in its name.
