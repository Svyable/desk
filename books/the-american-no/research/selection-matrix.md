# Selection matrix — The American No

This matrix is the argument behind the table of contents. The winner is not presented as a measured historical fact. It is the current editorial judgment under the book’s five-part test: tactical inheritance, institutional consequence, coalitional consequence, language consequence, and boundary consequence.

| Decade | Current winner | Why it wins this draft | Strong runner-up / challenge | Main caveat to preserve |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1770s | Boston Tea Party | Converted a tax/authority dispute into targeted economic destruction whose punishment widened intercolonial resistance; created enduring repertoire and symbolism. | Boston Massacre protest; colonial nonimportation campaigns | Did not “cause” independence by itself; participants’ liberty claims coexisted with slavery and settler expansion. |
| 1780s | Shays’ Rebellion | Made debt, taxation, court closure, armed resistance, and the weakness of the Confederation part of the national capacity debate. | Pennsylvania Mutiny of 1783 | Important input into constitutional politics, not single cause of the Constitution. |
| 1790s | Whiskey Rebellion | First major test of whether the new federal government could enforce an unpopular national tax against organized resistance. | Fries’s Rebellion begins at decade’s end | Distinguish tax grievance, intimidation, organized resistance, and later myth; federal response was itself politically contested. |
| 1800s | Gabriel’s planned rebellion | Exposes what political refusal looks like when enslaved people are denied lawful citizenship and ordinary channels; produced fear and repression. | 1806 New York mechanics / labor actions | Planned uprising was thwarted before intended mass action; do not narrate the counterfactual as fact. |
| 1810s | Philadelphia Black anti-colonization mass meeting | Collective Black self-definition against colonization made belonging itself the protest demand. | Antiwar resistance during War of 1812 | Less visually famous; requires careful use of contemporaneous and later-reprinted resolutions. |
| 1820s | Pawtucket Strike | Early industrial strike showed how wage labor could convert the factory and production schedule into political leverage. | Working Men’s movement | Local scale; significance rests on tactical inheritance more than national immediate effect. |
| 1830s | Abolitionist mail campaign | Turned national communications infrastructure and censorship into the battleground; exposed sectional limits of free expression. | Anti-abolition petition fights / gag-rule resistance | Keep distinctions among federal policy, local mobs, postmasters, and Southern censorship. |
| 1840s | Seneca Falls Convention | Declaration of Sentiments gave the women’s-rights movement a portable constitutional/political text and durable organizational memory. | Lowell Female Labor Reform Association | Not the beginning of women’s activism and not an immediate suffrage victory. |
| 1850s | Anthony Burns protests | Federal enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Law became physically visible through mass protest, failed rescue, troops, and Burns’s return to slavery. | Christiana resistance; John Brown at Harpers Ferry | Harpers Ferry is a formidable late-decade challenger; Burns wins here for the collision of federal law and mass urban protest. |
| 1860s | New York Draft Riots | Demonstrates that protest can be historically consequential and morally atrocious; linked conscription, class grievance, wartime state power, partisan conflict, and racist terror. | Richmond Bread Riot; antiwar Copperhead resistance | Black New Yorkers were targeted; never sanitize the event into “working-class unrest.” |
| 1870s | Great Railroad Strike of 1877 | National rail network transmitted labor conflict across cities and revealed the industrial state’s dependence on force and infrastructure. | Reconstruction-era Black political resistance | “National” was uneven and decentralized; local events differed sharply. |
| 1880s | Haymarket | One violent rupture reshaped public understanding of the eight-hour movement, anarchism, labor radicalism, policing, and trial legitimacy. | Great Southwest Railroad Strike | Identity/intention of bomb thrower remains unresolved; do not merge bombing, police fire, trial, and movement into one factual claim. |
| 1890s | Pullman Strike | Fused company-town power, national rail boycott, federal injunction, troops, mail, and union strategy into a major state-labor confrontation. | Coxey’s Army | Multiple causal mechanisms; federal action was justified partly through mail/interstate commerce claims and contested by labor. |
| 1900s | Uprising of the 20,000 | Young immigrant women turned garment work into a mass strike and changed union power and public understanding of women workers. | 1902 anthracite coal strike | Settlement was partial and uneven; not all shops won equal terms. |
| 1910s | Bread and Roses / Lawrence textile strike | Multilingual immigrant workforce, women’s participation, strike logistics, child evacuation controversy, and national scrutiny made fragmentation itself an organizing problem. | 1913 Paterson silk strike; 1917 suffrage pickets | Famous “Bread and Roses” language has a complicated relationship to the specific strike; source slogans carefully. |
| 1920s | Sacco and Vanzetti protest movement | A criminal case became an international crisis of legitimacy around immigration, anarchism, class, prejudice, and justice. | Bonus Army is 1932, outside decade; 1929 Gastonia strike | Do not state innocence as settled fact; chapter is about protest and legitimacy. |
| 1930s | Flint Sit-Down Strike | Occupying the factory changed labor leverage and helped force General Motors to recognize the UAW as a bargaining force. | Bonus Army; 1934 West Coast waterfront strike | UAW recognition and later labor settlement came through broader organizing and New Deal context too. |
| 1940s | Randolph’s threatened March on Washington | A mass protest threat extracted federal action before the march occurred, proving credible mobilization can make cancellation a concession. | 1943 Detroit protest/riot context; wartime labor actions | Executive Order 8802 had multiple political causes; never claim Randolph alone produced it. |
| 1950s | Montgomery Bus Boycott | Long-duration mass refusal plus car pools, churches, litigation, fundraising, leadership, and local discipline created a reusable civil-rights machine. | 1957 Little Rock mobilization; lunch-counter movement begins 1960 | Rosa Parks was not a spontaneous lone actor detached from organizing networks. |
| 1960s | Birmingham Campaign / Project C | Deliberately created a crisis segregationists could not manage quietly; economic pressure, arrests, children, police violence, media, negotiation, and federal politics converged. | March on Washington; Freedom Rides; Selma; Stonewall; antiwar movement | No single 1960s winner is uncontestable. Preserve the movement ecosystem and avoid claiming Birmingham alone produced federal civil-rights law. |
| 1970s | Section 504 sit-in | Converted regulatory delay into a 25-day occupation and disability from individualized hardship into enforceable civil-rights obligation. | Antiwar movement; gay liberation; anti-nuclear protest | Section 504 was already law; the fight was over implementing regulations and enforcement. |
| 1980s | ACT UP | Fused disruptive protest with technical expertise and changed who had standing in drug approval, clinical research, and health-policy urgency. | Nuclear Freeze; anti-apartheid divestment | Activism changed the research/policy environment; it did not invent the biomedical breakthroughs. |
| 1990s | Seattle WTO protests | Made globalization’s political choices visible, built an unusual labor/environment coalition, and ended the era when trade rules could be narrated only as technical inevitability. | Los Angeles uprising; queer military/marriage activism | WTO member governments had serious internal disagreements; street protest did not single-handedly collapse the talks. |
| 2000s | 2006 immigrant-rights marches / A Day Without an Immigrant | Turned a population described as a border problem into a visible domestic constituency and linked immigration status to labor, family, school, church, and consumption. | Iraq War protests; Tea Party emergence at decade’s end | H.R. 4437 did not become law, but comprehensive reform also failed; importance rests on mobilization and political inheritance rather than legislative victory. |
| 2010s | Ferguson | A local police killing became a national argument connecting policing, race, municipal extraction, militarization, digital witnessing, and official narrative authority. | Occupy Wall Street; Standing Rock; #MeToo; Women’s March | Keep DOJ Wilson shooting findings separate from DOJ systemic Ferguson findings; famous slogan outran evidentiary record. |
| 2020s | George Floyd protests — **provisional through 2026** | Extraordinary national/global scale; converted police violence and racial inequality into a crisis that courts, cities, departments, corporations, media, and federal investigators had to answer. | January 6; post-*Dobbs* protests; Gaza-war campus protests; labor and immigration/federal-enforcement mobilizations | The decade is unfinished. Selection must be rerun before any edition claims a final 2020s winner. |

## Editorial use

A chapter should be revised when its runner-up can beat it under the stated test and the manuscript has no serious answer. The correct response is not to make the winner sound more certain. It is to deepen the comparison or change the winner.

The final 2020s row has a different rule: even a strong defense cannot convert an unfinished decade into a finished one. The label remains provisional until the calendar stops moving.
