# Coda — The 2027 Dashboard

A forecast becomes useful when somebody can check it without needing the forecaster in the room.

The bets in this book therefore need a dashboard.

Not a dashboard in the corporate sense, with fifty gauges arranged to make uncertainty look managed. A small set of observations that can tell us whether the structure of the market is moving in the direction the book expects.

The first number to watch is the **open–closed capability gap**.

Do not reduce it to one leaderboard position. Track how far the strongest openly available systems trail the strongest closed systems on tasks that people actually pay for: difficult coding, reliable tool use, multilingual reasoning, long-horizon work, multimodal tasks, and domain-specific enterprise evaluations.

The important measure is time.

How long does a meaningful closed-model advantage remain economically exclusive before an open alternative becomes good enough to substitute on the relevant workload?

If that interval keeps shrinking, the premium window is under pressure even if the closed frontier remains ahead. If it expands, the moving-moat argument is winning.

The second number is **cost per accepted outcome**.

Token prices are too easy to manipulate and too far from the user's actual objective. Measure total model and infrastructure cost for a task that passes the real acceptance condition. Include retries, verifier calls, tool failures, latency, human correction, and escalation to a stronger model.

This single measure can reveal whether cheap open inference is actually cheap or merely produces inexpensive attempts.

It can also reveal when an expensive frontier model earns its premium by finishing work cleanly.

The third measure is **routing share**.

How much serious AI traffic is dynamically assigned among models, providers, or locations rather than sent to one default system?

A market can contain many models and still behave like a monopoly if applications rarely switch among them. Routing is the mechanism that turns theoretical substitutes into continuous competition.

Watch enterprise gateways, agent frameworks, cloud catalogs, and internal model platforms. The telling question is whether model choice becomes policy and telemetry rather than a product-manager preference made twice a year.

The fourth measure is **derivative depth**.

Do not count only downloads of the original checkpoint. Count the ecosystem that grows beneath it: quantizations, fine-tunes, adapters, distillations, runtime integrations, language specializations, device bundles, training environments, and models that use the original as a teacher.

A model family with fewer headline downloads but more meaningful descendants may be the stronger piece of infrastructure.

This is especially important for Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi, and whatever families replace today's names. Infrastructure announces itself by becoming somebody else's starting point.

The fifth measure is **license friction**.

How often does a technically attractive model lose deployment because its commercial terms are unclear, custom, threshold-based, or incompatible with the intended service?

Watch enterprise allowlists, cloud catalogs, forks from the last permissive checkpoint, and whether model hubs begin exposing machine-readable rights as prominently as context length and parameter count.

Time-to-lawyer should be treated as a real deployment metric.

The sixth measure is **maintenance concentration**.

How many critical open-model paths depend on very few maintainers or one corporate sponsor?

Weights can be distributed while operational knowledge remains concentrated. Track the runtimes, quantization tools, model converters, evaluation frameworks, and package layers through which many families pass.

A healthy commons should have enough redundancy that one exhausted maintainer, acquisition, or abandoned repository cannot strand a large part of the ecosystem.

The seventh measure is **multi-hardware health**.

How quickly do important model releases become efficient on more than one hardware stack?

Time from model release to solid support on Nvidia, AMD, CPUs, Apple silicon, and other relevant accelerators is a practical measure of whether open weights are producing hardware competition or simply feeding one dominant execution environment.

The question is not equal market share.

It is whether alternatives remain warm enough to discipline the incumbent.

The eighth measure is **local share of useful work**.

Not the number of enthusiasts running giant models on desktops. Measure how often production applications route tasks to devices, workstations, private clusters, or sovereign infrastructure as part of an ordinary heterogeneous system.

The prediction is strongest when users stop thinking of this as “local AI” and simply experience software that chooses the right execution boundary.

The ninth measure is **neutrality evidence**.

If a central hub, cloud marketplace, router, benchmark, or runtime is vertically integrated, can outsiders tell whether competing models and hardware receive equivalent opportunity?

Watch deployment-support delays, ranking policies, sponsored placement, data-access boundaries, export tools, independent governance, and whether participants begin withholding contributions because the platform owner is also a competitor.

A neutrality dispute becomes economically meaningful when it changes behavior, governance, portability, or regulation.

The tenth measure is **Chinese-family normalization outside China**.

Ignore the argument conducted through flags and benchmark screenshots. Look for boring use.

Are Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi, or successor families embedded in Western and global applications, enterprise catalogs, local runtimes, cloud services, educational material, fine-tunes, and packages? Are they evaluated as ordinary technical options, even when policy excludes them from sensitive domains?

Normalization does not require geopolitical trust.

It requires technical use to become routine enough that origin is one procurement field among several rather than the entire conversation.

The eleventh measure is **private evaluation ownership**.

Do serious enterprises build and retain their own task distributions, failure cases, acceptance thresholds, traces, and corrections?

This is one of the book's quieter predictions. A company that owns excellent evaluations can compare models, negotiate prices, route traffic, and adopt new open systems quickly. A company that relies on vendor benchmarks remains dependent even when weights are available.

Evaluations are the measuring instrument that makes portability economically real.

The twelfth measure is **recoverability**.

Can an organization preserve useful operation after a provider, region, model family, or platform disappears?

A recovery drill should answer more than whether somebody saved the weights. Can the team reproduce the serving environment? Does it have the tokenizer, configuration, licenses, evaluation suite, fine-tune, traces, and operational knowledge? Can it move the workflow rather than merely possess the artifact?

This is the empirical form of credible exit.

The thirteenth measure is **open-model monetization**.

Where does money return to the organizations paying to create openly available capability?

Official inference, premium tiers, cloud revenue sharing, hardware demand, enterprise support, agent environments, fine-tuning, data services, managed distributions, and complementary applications all count.

If the leading open-model organizations cannot capture enough value somewhere in the stack to fund continued frontier work, openness may depend on strategic subsidy rather than a durable commercial equilibrium.

Subsidy can last a long time.

It should still be named.

The fourteenth measure is **teacher value**.

How often are strong models used to create the data, tests, traces, critiques, and environments that train or improve other models?

This will be difficult to observe because much of it happens privately. Look for disclosed distillation pipelines, synthetic curricula, teacher–student releases, generated evaluations, and smaller models whose capabilities clearly depend on stronger teachers.

The copy machine thesis becomes more important if the best model is valuable not only for the work it performs but for the systems it helps create.

The fifteenth measure is **scarcity half-life**.

Whenever a new bottleneck earns unusually high margins, start the clock.

How long until engineers, competitors, standards, capital, or agents attack it?

Expensive inference invites quantization and better serving.

Legal friction invites standardized metadata.

Deployment pain invites managed platforms.

Model selection pain invites routers.

Maintenance bottlenecks invite automation and funding.

Hardware concentration invites alternative backends and custom silicon.

The best businesses may still own scarce assets. The dashboard should distinguish scarcity that compounds from scarcity that is merely waiting to be automated.

These measures should not be collapsed into one score.

They can move in opposite directions.

The open–closed capability gap can widen while routine inference prices collapse. Chinese models can normalize while regulation tightens in sensitive sectors. Local execution can increase while clouds earn more money. Model licenses can become more restrictive while derivative ecosystems grow. Nvidia can strengthen its hardware position while open weights weaken the pricing power of individual laboratories.

That is not a failure of the framework.

It is the point.

The market is layered.

The dashboard exists to keep us from calling one layer the whole story.

At the end of 2027, the question should not be whether open source won.

There is no finish line at which the industry becomes open or closed.

The useful questions are narrower.

Did more users gain credible alternatives?

Did capability become easier to possess and adapt?

Did the price of ordinary intelligence fall?

Did new gatekeepers appear around the abundance?

Did the organizations that funded open models discover sustainable ways to capture value?

Did the frontier remain scarce enough to preserve large premiums?

Did the ecosystem become easier to leave without becoming impossible to depend on?

Those answers will tell us where the power moved.

That is the only scoreboard this book needs.
