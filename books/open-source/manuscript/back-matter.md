# Back Matter

This manuscript is a snapshot of a moving market, written in August 2026. Model names, benchmark positions, licensing terms, acquisition status, cloud agreements, and product availability can change faster than a conventional publishing cycle. The research directory therefore records the source vintage behind the factual claims and keeps 2027 forecasts separate from reported events.

A vocabulary note matters especially here. “Open source” is used in the title because it names a cultural movement, a software tradition, and the language the industry itself uses. When a specific AI release provides weights but not the full preferred form needed to reproduce and modify the system, the manuscript calls it **open-weight**. That distinction follows the spirit of the Open Source AI Definition rather than assuming that downloadable parameters alone settle the question.

Benchmarks are treated as evidence, not verdicts. Vendor-reported scores are identified as such in the research trail. Hub downloads are evidence of activity on a particular platform, not a complete measure of market share. Parameter counts describe architecture, not intelligence. The largest model is not necessarily the best model, and the highest-scoring model on a selected benchmark is not necessarily the most useful one in production.

The 2027 chapters are intentionally falsifiable. A forecast that cannot be wrong cannot teach us much when the year is over.

## How to score the 2027 bets

The easiest way to preserve a prediction is to remember only the part that came true.

That temptation is strongest in a fast market. If open-model prices fall but closed frontier prices rise, almost any forecaster can claim victory after the fact. If Chinese models gain adoption in one region and lose it in another, the original prediction can be silently narrowed to whichever geography looked best. If a platform faces criticism but no formal regulatory action, “neutrality fight” can be stretched until it includes an angry forum thread.

That is not forecasting. It is literary salvage.

A later edition of this book should therefore score the bets against observable evidence rather than tone.

The first test is **price anchoring**. Did capable open-weight systems create a real reference price for ordinary commercial inference? Evidence should include actual hosted pricing, self-hosting economics where available, enterprise routing behavior, and whether closed providers increasingly explain their premium through reliability, safety, tools, latency, or stronger capability. A fall in one promotional API rate is not enough. The question is whether credible substitutes forced the market to compare the premium with an open alternative.

The second test is **Chinese model normalization**. The relevant evidence is not whether a Chinese laboratory briefly tops a leaderboard. Look for boring adoption: derivatives, runtime support, managed hosting, enterprise approvals, package dependencies, fine-tunes, device bundles, educational material, and applications whose users barely notice the model's origin. Also record counter-evidence. Procurement bans, security findings, sanctions, license changes, weak independent evaluations, or a sharp resurgence of American and European open families would all weaken the prediction.

The third test is **neutrality conflict**. Score this only when a distribution or infrastructure layer becomes important enough that self-preference, ranking, access, compatibility, or governance produces a concrete response. A response might be published neutrality commitments, portability requirements, structural separation, an independent foundation, a meaningful fork, a regulatory inquiry, or a product change clearly tied to the dispute. General suspicion that a platform company prefers itself does not qualify.

The fourth test is **local inference as a property rather than a category**. Evidence should come from applications that route some work to devices, workstations, private clusters, or sovereign infrastructure while using clouds for other work. The bet is stronger if users experience one product while the execution plan crosses several model locations automatically. It is weaker if local inference remains mostly a hobbyist or privacy niche and serious applications continue to choose one centralized provider for nearly all useful work.

The fifth test is **monetization around the weights**. Watch whether open-model creators increasingly earn through official inference, premium tiers, cloud revenue sharing, enterprise services, agent environments, fine-tuning, data, hardware pull-through, or other complementary layers. The prediction should be marked down if leading open-model organizations continue to rely mainly on financing, strategic subsidy, or hope while downstream hosts capture the commercial value.

The scoring should preserve partial outcomes.

A bet can be directionally correct and materially overstated. It can be right in the United States and wrong in Europe. It can happen six months later than expected. A platform can remain neutral in practice while regulators still force formal commitments. A model family can become technically ubiquitous and commercially weak.

Use four labels rather than a binary victory lap:

**Hit.** The predicted mechanism is plainly visible in multiple independent signals.

**Partial.** The mechanism appeared, but scope, timing, or economic importance was meaningfully smaller than predicted.

**Miss.** The mechanism did not appear or the opposite pattern became stronger.

**Unresolved.** Evidence is genuinely insufficient, contradictory, or too private to score responsibly.

“Unresolved” is not a polite name for “miss.” It should be used sparingly.

## What counts as evidence

The evidence hierarchy for a later edition should remain similar to the one used here.

Primary artifacts come first when they can answer the question directly: model repositories, licenses, company documentation, standards, incident reports, financial filings, pricing pages, technical release notes, public governance documents, and source repositories. These sources are closest to the object being described, though they can still be selective or self-serving.

High-quality reporting matters for transactions, negotiations, financing, internal strategy, and events whose participants have not published complete accounts. Reporting should be dated and distinguished from a company's own announcement. A reported agreement is not a closed acquisition. A reported negotiation is not a signed contract.

Independent evaluation matters most when claims concern performance. Vendor benchmarks can explain what the vendor believes is important and how it wants the release perceived. They should not be converted into a universal ranking without outside evidence.

Community evidence can be especially useful for operational reality: whether a model is painful to deploy, whether a license frightens companies away, whether a runtime supports an architecture well, whether maintainers are overwhelmed, whether a supposedly portable system is portable in practice. Community evidence is noisy, so repeated patterns matter more than one dramatic complaint.

Private enterprise behavior is the hardest category because much of it never becomes public. A company can run an open model for billions of tokens after one download and leave almost no visible trace. This means public download statistics may undercount successful deployment precisely when the artifact becomes infrastructure. Later editions should resist pretending that the absence of public telemetry means absence of use.

## The claims most likely to age badly

Some statements in this manuscript are deliberately durable. The cost of copying weights differs from the cost of training them. Distribution layers can become valuable around portable artifacts. Open development requires more than source visibility. Local control changes bargaining power. Irreversible release has a different safety topology from hosted access.

Other statements are perishable.

The Nvidia–Hugging Face transaction status can change.

Model families can lose momentum quickly.

A permissive license can be replaced by a different license for the next generation.

A benchmark can become contaminated or irrelevant.

A cloud partnership can be announced, restructured, or abandoned.

A government can create a new procurement rule that changes adoption overnight.

A runtime can make a previously impractical model cheap enough to become ordinary.

The text should not be protected from those changes. The research trail exists so a future revision can say, with dates, which world this edition was describing.

## A note on “best”

The book avoids declaring a single best open model of 2026 because the category is underspecified.

Best at what?

Long-horizon coding?

Multilingual work?

Local inference?

Cost per accepted enterprise task?

Multimodal reasoning?

License simplicity?

Ease of fine-tuning?

Tool use?

Safety?

Hardware fit?

A model can win one of these and lose six others. The more models become routable components inside larger systems, the less useful one universal ranking becomes.

The same caution applies to companies. Nvidia can be extraordinarily strong at the hardware and software platform layer while a Chinese laboratory leads a particular open-model release cycle. A cloud can capture more economic value from an open model than the laboratory that trained it. A tiny runtime project can determine whether a giant model reaches a laptop.

The stack permits several winners because value creation and value capture occur at different layers.

That is the book's central claim and the claim that should receive the hardest future test.

## Revision covenant

If this manuscript receives a 2027 edition, the author should not silently replace failed forecasts with better ones.

Keep the old bets visible.

Score them.

Explain the misses.

Where the thesis was wrong, say so plainly. Where a mechanism appeared for a different reason than predicted, distinguish outcome from explanation. Where a transaction never closed, do not keep writing as though it did. Where a model family faded, preserve the fact that it once looked structurally important and ask what the market taught us.

A forecast is valuable because it creates a timestamped argument with reality.

Reality gets the final edit.

Research notes and source records live in `research/` beside this manuscript.
