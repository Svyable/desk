# The New Open Cartels

The word cartel usually arrives carrying a meeting room.

A few competitors sit around a table. Someone closes the door. Prices are discussed in sentences that should not exist. Market shares are divided. Customers become numbers on a page. The scandal, once discovered, has a satisfying shape because the coordination is visible.

That is not what this chapter is alleging.

The more interesting danger in an open-model economy is almost the opposite. Companies can compete intensely, publish code, release weights, cut prices, sponsor standards, and still produce a market whose practical choices narrow around a handful of shared chokepoints.

No secret room is required.

Everyone simply arrives at the same infrastructure.

This is one of the stranger consequences of abundance. When the thing at the center becomes easy to copy, coordination pressure moves outward. Thousands of models can exist while most of them are downloaded from the same few hubs, trained on the same families of accelerators, served through the same runtimes, reached through the same clouds, evaluated on the same leaderboards, wrapped in the same agent protocols, and discovered through the same developer surfaces.

The artifact is plural. The road system is not.

That is what I mean by the new open cartels.

The phrase is deliberately provocative and legally imprecise. I am not using cartel to claim unlawful collusion. I am using it to describe a structural possibility: an ecosystem that is open at the level users can see and concentrated at the levels that determine what gets built, distributed, optimized, and trusted.

The distinction matters because open source has trained us to look in the repository for freedom.

Can I inspect the code?

Can I copy it?

Can I modify it?

Can I run it without permission?

Those remain valuable questions. They are just no longer sufficient.

A model can be downloadable and still live inside a market where a small number of companies control the scarce resources required to use it economically.

The clearest example is compute.

Suppose a laboratory releases excellent weights under permissive terms. In principle, anyone can run them. In practice, the largest version may require expensive accelerators, large memory pools, fast interconnects, specialized kernels, careful quantization, and an inference stack maintained by people who know where the bottlenecks hide. The legal permission to run the model is real. The economic ability to run it at scale is uneven.

Openness does not abolish capital intensity.

Sometimes it makes capital intensity more valuable.

If every software company can obtain a strong model, more companies have a reason to buy the hardware needed to operate models. If every cloud can host the same weights, clouds compete on serving cost and availability. If agents make inference demand elastic, cheaper tokens can produce more total compute consumption rather than less.

This is the hardware-company dream hiding inside open weights.

Nvidia does not need every successful model to belong to Nvidia if successful models create demand for Nvidia-compatible computation.

That logic helps explain why the reported agreement to acquire Hugging Face for $12.9 billion matters beyond the price tag. Hugging Face became useful partly because it refused to be one model company's showroom. It was a place where competitors could coexist. A developer could begin with the task rather than with a vendor allegiance. The hub lowered the cost of comparison and substitution.

If the reported deal closes, that neutrality becomes a governance question rather than a branding assumption.

The issue is not whether Nvidia immediately starts hiding rival models. That would be crude and self-defeating. A distribution platform becomes more valuable when it contains the whole market. The subtler question is whether control of the hub can influence defaults, optimization, deployment paths, preferred hardware, promoted tooling, inference economics, enterprise relationships, and the data generated by developer attention.

A marketplace can remain open while its gravity changes.

This is why platforms are so powerful. They rarely need to prohibit alternatives. They can make one path smoother.

One click becomes three.

A default becomes an advanced setting.

An integration arrives six months earlier.

A model card has a deploy button for one infrastructure stack and a documentation link for another.

A benchmark dashboard highlights the metric where one family looks strongest.

A managed endpoint receives the best caching, the newest kernel, or the easiest billing.

None of these choices has to be malicious. Platforms make choices because software cannot contain infinite neutral surfaces. Every interface is a ranking of effort.

At scale, effort is market power.

The same pattern appears in cloud distribution.

Moonshot's reported discussions with Microsoft, Amazon, and Google over revenue sharing for Kimi K3 are interesting because they reveal an emerging bargain. The weights can be open enough to travel, yet the laboratory still wants compensation when giant clouds monetize the model. The cloud wants a popular model in its catalog because customers want choice. The model maker wants the cloud's enterprise relationships and global capacity. Neither side needs exclusivity for the relationship to become economically important.

Now repeat that across many models.

The major clouds become department stores for open intelligence.

They do not have to own the brands on the shelves. They own the building, the checkout, the customer account, the network, the capacity planning, the security review, and the invoice.

This is not new. Cloud computing has always turned open software into managed revenue. Linux is free in a sense that does not prevent clouds from making enormous amounts of money operating Linux servers. PostgreSQL can be open while database services become valuable businesses. Kubernetes can be open while control planes, support, networking, observability, and enterprise integration remain commercial.

AI intensifies the pattern because the open artifact itself is expensive to execute.

A text editor can run the same source code on a laptop. A frontier-scale model may require a small industrial system.

That creates a wide zone between permission and practical independence.

Runtimes occupy another chokepoint.

A model file is inert until software can load it, schedule it, allocate memory, dispatch kernels, manage attention, quantize weights, batch requests, cache prefixes, route experts, and translate the mathematical architecture into work the hardware performs efficiently.

The runtime is where theoretical openness meets the physics of a machine.

This layer is unusually powerful because performance differences can erase nominal hardware differences. A model that technically runs on many devices may run economically on only a few. A new quantization method can pull a model onto consumer hardware. A better kernel can reduce serving costs enough to change which provider wins a bid. Support for an architecture in a popular runtime can determine whether developers experiment with it at all.

The open-model ecosystem therefore creates incentives for runtime concentration.

Developers want a small number of tools that run many models.

Hardware makers want those tools optimized for their devices.

Clouds want predictable serving stacks.

Model labs want immediate support when they release a new architecture.

The more universal the runtime becomes, the more valuable compatibility becomes.

This is the same force that built CUDA's soft power.

A developer does not wake up wanting dependency. The developer wants the thing to work.

Enough individual decisions to make the thing work can produce a dependency nobody explicitly chose as a political project.

Standards behave similarly.

The best standards are liberating. They let components from different vendors interoperate. They reduce duplicated work. They create markets because buyers can replace one supplier without rebuilding the whole system.

But successful standards also concentrate influence around the organizations that define, implement, certify, extend, or interpret them.

An agent protocol can be open while the most important extensions are developed by a few companies. A model format can be documented while one implementation effectively decides what edge cases mean. An evaluation schema can be public while the datasets, hosting, or ranking surface remain concentrated.

Compatibility creates a commons and a capital asset at the same time.

This is why antitrust thinking becomes difficult in the open era.

Traditional instincts often look for high prices, restricted output, contractual exclusion, or explicit coordination. Open ecosystems can produce the opposite symptoms.

Prices fall.

Output explodes.

Code is published.

New entrants appear constantly.

Users report more choice than ever.

And yet the bargaining power underneath the abundance can still consolidate.

A market can be competitive in products and concentrated in infrastructure.

The distinction is familiar in other industries. Thousands of websites can exist while a few browsers dominate access. Millions of merchants can sell while a few payment networks settle transactions. Vast numbers of applications can be written while two mobile operating systems control distribution. A system can contain enormous creativity above a narrow waist.

The narrow waist is often efficient.

That is what makes it hard.

Nobody wants seventeen incompatible model formats, thirty-seven agent protocols, twelve package registries required for one application, and a bespoke inference runtime for every architecture. Standardization saves time. Central hubs reduce search costs. Large clouds spread fixed costs. Mature hardware ecosystems make development easier.

Concentration can be the residue of coordination value.

The policy question is not how to eliminate the narrow waist.

It is how to keep the narrow waist contestable.

Contestability is a more useful word than decentralization.

A system does not need every component to be equally distributed. It needs credible exit.

Can a model move between clouds without being economically crippled?

Can developers obtain the weights without the hub?

Can another registry mirror the metadata?

Can a runtime support alternative hardware without architectural punishment?

Can enterprises export fine-tunes, evaluations, prompts, traces, and policy configurations?

Can a competing tool implement the standard from the specification rather than reverse-engineering one vendor's behavior?

Can a project fork not just the code but enough of the development machinery to survive?

These are the questions that distinguish an open ecosystem from an open storefront.

Exit is expensive in every real system. The relevant issue is whether the cost of exit is created by genuine technical complexity or by avoidable control over interfaces, data, identity, or distribution.

The answer will rarely be pure.

Consider a model hub. A repository of weights can be mirrored. The community discussions, download statistics, popularity signals, security scanning, model cards, issue history, organization identity, deployment integrations, and developer habits are harder to reproduce. The code may travel more easily than the social graph.

That social graph is part of the product.

It is also part of the moat.

This is why acquiring a community platform is different from acquiring a model laboratory. A laboratory owns a stream of artifacts. A platform owns the place where artifacts become legible to a market.

The market may punish any owner that abuses this position. Developers are unusually capable of leaving when software becomes hostile. Open ecosystems have memory. Forks happen. New registries appear. Protocols route around obstruction.

But migration is not instant.

The fact that exit is possible does not mean control is weak.

A landlord can have power even when tenants are legally allowed to move.

The practical cost of moving is the power.

This is where the new open cartels differ from old monopoly stories. The scarce asset is often not ownership of the thing users want. It is ownership of the transition between things.

Model to hardware.

Model to cloud.

Model to runtime.

Model to application.

Repository to deployment.

Agent to tool.

Evaluation to purchase.

Identity to trust.

The seam can be worth more than either object it joins.

An ecosystem containing many interchangeable models may make the seam especially valuable because substitution increases traffic through the compatibility layer.

This sounds paradoxical until you think about railways.

If only one destination exists, a station has limited value. If hundreds of destinations exist, the station becomes a coordination point.

Open models multiply destinations.

The hub is the station.

The runtime is the gauge.

The accelerator platform is the track.

The cloud is the railway company.

The agent is the passenger that never sleeps.

The analogy breaks in important places, but it captures the economic direction: abundance in endpoints raises the value of shared routing infrastructure.

There is another concentration mechanism that receives less attention because it does not look like infrastructure.

Evaluation.

A market with thousands of models needs ways to decide which ones matter. Benchmarks, leaderboards, community rankings, arena results, coding suites, agent tasks, safety evaluations, and enterprise test harnesses become filters on abundance.

The filter can become a chokepoint.

If one benchmark becomes the shorthand for quality, labs optimize for it. If one arena becomes the prestige surface, release strategy bends toward it. If one enterprise evaluation vendor becomes the procurement standard, model providers implement around its categories.

Measurement begins as information and ends as an incentive system.

This is not corruption. It is feedback.

A ruler that everyone uses starts shaping the objects being built.

Open AI will therefore need plural evaluation more than closed AI did. A closed market with five major models is easy to compare manually. An open market with fifty thousand derivatives requires automated ranking, which increases the power of whoever designs the ranking.

The model commons can become governed by the scoreboard.

Data creates a related feedback loop.

A hub sees what developers download.

A cloud sees which models are actually used.

A runtime project sees which architectures create support pain.

A hardware vendor sees which kernels dominate compute.

An agent platform sees which models succeed on long-running tasks.

This operational data can be more strategically valuable than public benchmarks because it reveals revealed preference rather than stated preference.

The company sitting at a shared layer learns across competitors.

The competitors learn only from themselves.

That asymmetry can compound.

It is one reason vertically integrated infrastructure companies have an advantage in an open ecosystem. They do not need privileged access to model weights if they can observe the market formed around the weights.

The obvious response is to demand neutrality.

Neutrality sounds clean and often is not.

A platform must optimize something. Search results need ranking. Security scanners need policy. Deployment integrations require engineering priorities. Abuse controls need thresholds. Hardware support requires trade-offs. Every platform makes decisions that benefit some users more than others.

The better objective is legible governance.

Tell developers what is ranked and why.

Make sponsored placement obvious.

Publish compatibility requirements.

Separate safety enforcement from commercial preference where practical.

Provide export paths.

Document APIs well enough for competitors to interoperate.

Avoid contracts that turn technical convenience into artificial exclusivity.

Give important community constituencies a way to surface breakage before it becomes migration.

These are not guarantees of fairness. They are ways to make power observable.

Open source has always depended on power being observable.

A maintainer can reject your patch. The repository lets you see the decision. A project can change direction. The license tells you whether you can fork. A company can dominate development. The commit history lets others measure the dominance.

Visibility does not eliminate hierarchy.

It makes hierarchy contestable.

The AI layer complicates this because not every important decision leaves a commit.

Which model received faster inference support?

Which architecture got optimized first?

Which provider received favorable commercial terms?

Which safety classifier blocked which deployment?

Which benchmark was weighted in search ranking?

Which model was recommended to an enterprise buyer?

These choices may live in private operational systems even when the weights are public.

The next generation of open-infrastructure governance will need to care about these invisible decisions.

The strange outcome is that the more important open AI becomes, the more it will resemble a regulated utility in some layers and a bazaar in others.

The bazaar builds models.

The utility distributes them.

This is not necessarily bad.

Utilities exist because some infrastructure benefits from scale. The mistake would be to confuse scale efficiency with a permanent right to control the ecosystem.

A healthy open market can tolerate powerful hubs if new hubs can emerge, powerful runtimes if specifications remain implementable, powerful hardware platforms if software can migrate, and powerful clouds if workloads remain portable enough that customers can negotiate.

The objective is not to flatten every mountain.

It is to keep passes through the mountains open.

This is where Chinese open-model competition becomes especially consequential.

DeepSeek, Qwen, Z.ai, Kimi, and other labs do more than add models to a leaderboard. They increase the number of credible suppliers at the model layer. That weakens any attempt to monopolize intelligence through model ownership alone.

But their success may strengthen the shared infrastructure around them.

Every new model uploaded to the same hub adds gravity to the hub.

Every architecture optimized for the same accelerator stack adds gravity to the stack.

Every deployment sold through the same clouds adds gravity to the clouds.

Pluralism at one layer can create concentration at the next.

The West-versus-China framing therefore misses part of the story. American infrastructure companies may profit from Chinese model innovation. Chinese labs may depend on American distribution. European enterprises may use Chinese weights on American-designed chips inside sovereign data centers. An open artifact can cross political boundaries even while the surrounding infrastructure remains geopolitical.

This produces alliances that look absurd if you think companies are countries.

Companies are not countries.

They are incentive machines operating inside countries.

A cloud may lobby for restrictions on a competitor in one context and seek to host that competitor's model in another because customers want it. A hardware company may support hundreds of model labs because every successful lab expands compute demand. A model company may release weights that help competitors because ubiquity improves its ecosystem position.

Open source makes these cross-cutting incentives visible.

It also makes policy clumsier.

A government can restrict chips at a border. It is harder to restrict an idea that has already been downloaded.

A regulator can review a merger. It is harder to measure the influence of a default setting across thousands of open projects.

An antitrust authority can examine exclusive contracts. It is harder to decide when a popular open standard has become an exclusionary moat.

The legal categories were built around ownership of products. The open AI economy will repeatedly create power through ownership of coordination.

This is why I expect the phrase open source to become less politically comforting in 2027.

For years it implied an escape hatch from concentrated technology power. Often it still does. Open models give startups leverage against API vendors. Local inference gives enterprises leverage against clouds. Forkable software protects developers against abandonment. Transparent weights can enable research that closed systems prevent.

But open source can also be the feedstock for a highly concentrated infrastructure business.

The code is free.

The factory is not.

The road is not.

The marketplace is not.

The trust layer is not.

The maintenance labor is not.

The lesson is not cynicism.

It is accounting.

Whenever something becomes free, ask what bill grew more important.

Whenever a platform promises openness, ask what cannot be exported.

Whenever a standard wins, ask who can change it.

Whenever a hub becomes indispensable, ask what realistic fork means at the level of community and operations rather than source files.

Whenever a hardware ecosystem becomes universal, ask whether an alternative can reach adequate performance without asking the incumbent for permission.

Whenever a cloud sells every model, ask whether the customer owns enough of the surrounding system to move.

These questions are more useful than demanding purity because no serious AI system will be pure.

The most open model still runs on somebody's machine.

The most neutral hub still has a ranking algorithm.

The most portable runtime still makes assumptions about hardware.

The most decentralized community still develops informal elites.

The goal is not to eliminate dependency.

Civilization is dependency arranged well.

The goal is to avoid dependencies that silently become sovereignty.

That is the political economy hidden inside the next phase of open AI.

The first phase was about access: can I get the model?

The second was about capability: is the open model good enough?

The third is about control: now that everybody can get a good model, who owns the machinery that decides how the good models reach the world?

The answer will not be one company.

That would make the story too simple.

It will be a small number of heavily connected layers, each open enough to attract participation and concentrated enough to collect rent.

They will compete with one another. They will partner with one another. They will fund the projects that threaten them and acquire the projects that complement them. They will describe themselves as ecosystems because ecosystem is a warmer word than infrastructure empire.

Some of them will deserve the warmth.

The test is whether the ecosystem can survive the departure of its gardener.

If it cannot, it was never quite a commons.

It was a very generous estate.
