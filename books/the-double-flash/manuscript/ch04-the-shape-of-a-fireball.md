# The Shape of a Fireball

A nuclear fireball is brightest when it is least simple.

The phrase "double flash" makes the optical signature sound like two camera strobes separated by darkness. The actual physics is continuous. Energy deposited in air creates an expanding fireball and shock front. The earliest radiation escapes rapidly. As the shocked air becomes opaque, the apparent brightness falls. Expansion weakens and cools the shock; the hot interior becomes visible again; luminosity rises into a broader second maximum and then decays.

For the Vela program, that temporal behavior was gold. Yield changes the scale. Altitude changes the atmosphere through which the fireball develops. Clouds, geometry, sensor response, and background light alter the recorded curve. But the distinctive dip between two maxima offered a way to discriminate a nuclear explosion from many ordinary optical transients.

The cleverness of the bhangmeter was not that it recognized "nuclear light." It recognized dynamics.

This distinction has become more important, not less, in the age of automated classification. A still image of an explosion can be ambiguous. Time is often the extra dimension that exposes mechanism. A jet engine and a rocket can have similar bright plumes in one frame but different acceleration profiles. A seismic impulse and an earthquake can overlap in frequency content but differ in source physics. A biological signal and an electrical artifact may occupy the same spectrum but evolve differently after perturbation.

The Vela designers encoded mechanism into time.

That is why Event 747 was alarming. Its optical trace had the canonical feature the system existed to notice.

Yet "canonical" became the battlefield.

The second pulse did not look equally strong to the two bhangmeters. The mismatch raised concern that something other than a distant atmospheric event was being seen. For a remote source illuminating the spacecraft from effectively the same direction, two comparable sensors should exhibit a relationship constrained by their orientations and calibration. If one reported a different second-pulse amplitude, perhaps the light field close to the spacecraft was irregular. Debris, reflection, or some local contamination could do that.

This was not a trivial objection. Instruments fail by leaving fingerprints.

Suppose a camera sees a bright object but adjacent pixels respond strangely. A real external scene may still be responsible, but the anomaly lowers confidence. Suppose two microphones hear the same blast, yet one waveform carries echoes impossible for the environment. You investigate the microphone. Sensor disagreement is not bureaucracy; it is evidence.

The difficulty is that nuclear signals are not ideal mathematical curves either.

Later work by Christopher Wright and Lars-Erik De Geer revisited the declassified optical record and argued that the anomaly emphasized by the Ruina panel was less damning than it appeared. They noted that confirmed nuclear detections also showed background modulation during the second pulse, though with timing differences, and challenged the physical plausibility of the proposed meteoroid-reflection scenario. Their 2017 analysis concluded that the double flash remained consistent with a nuclear explosion and that the meteoroid hypothesis had not been demonstrated with a convincing model.

That paper is important, but it deserves the same discipline as the older panel.

A peer-reviewed reanalysis is not a time machine. The authors did not recover the bomb. They did not produce an independently attributable fallout sample. They reweighted the optical evidence using declassified material and modern understanding of spacecraft impact dynamics. The result changes the plausibility landscape. It does not close the case.

This is what good historical science often looks like.

A new instrument can resolve an old sample. A declassified document can expose an assumption. A physical model can show that a previously plausible explanation is geometrically awkward. Old evidence is not static if the inferential machinery around it improves.

The Vela flash is therefore not one question frozen in 1979. It is a sequence of model comparisons.

Model A: atmospheric nuclear explosion.

Model B: meteoroid impact on or near the satellite, producing reflective debris.

Model C: some other natural optical transient.

Model D: sensor or electronics malfunction.

A strong analysis asks what each model predicts for every relevant observation, including the awkward ones.

For a nuclear model, the double-pulse timing should fall within physically plausible ranges for yield and atmospheric burst conditions. Detector ratios should be explainable by calibration, geometry, background modulation, or known system behavior. Other channels—acoustic, hydroacoustic, radionuclide, electromagnetic—should be sought with predictions tied to a candidate location and yield. If they are absent, that absence must be penalized in proportion to expected detectability.

For a meteoroid model, one must specify more than "spacecraft get hit." The impact geometry, debris velocities, reflective areas, sunlight angles, sensor fields of view, duration of illumination, and two-pulse structure must jointly make sense. The model should explain why two optical detectors saw the particular pattern and why the signal resembled the nuclear class strongly enough to trigger the alert.

For an electronics fault, correlated output on two separate sensors requires a common mechanism or common interference path. It should leave some relation to spacecraft electrical behavior, telemetry, or known failure modes.

For a natural event on Earth, the optical history and location constraints need a candidate phenomenon capable of producing the signature at the observed intensity and timescale.

The power comes from forcing alternatives to pay rent.

"Could be" is cheap. Almost any historical anomaly can be surrounded by possibilities. The useful question is how much independent machinery each possibility needs.

This is Occam's razor in a form engineers recognize. Not the childish version—choose the story with the fewest words—but the accounting version: how many unsupported auxiliary assumptions must be introduced to rescue the model from each inconvenient datum?

A nuclear interpretation has one enormous advantage. The sensor was designed specifically for that phenomenon, and the signal resembled the intended signature.

It has one enormous disadvantage. The expected corroboration was not decisive.

A meteoroid interpretation has the reverse profile. Meteoroid impacts on spacecraft are physically real, and an aged satellite is vulnerable to local optical artifacts. But the model must reproduce a signal unusually similar to a nuclear double flash without a direct observation of the proposed impact or debris.

This symmetry is why people talk past one another.

One side treats the intended purpose of the detector as nearly dispositive: if a smoke alarm rings and you smell smoke, start with fire. The other treats the absence of corroboration and sensor anomaly as evidence that the smoke alarm itself may be the source of confusion.

Both instincts are reasonable. The answer depends on likelihood ratios, not temperament.

The optical trace also raises a deeper issue about expert pattern recognition.

Weapons laboratories had scientists who had seen data from real nuclear tests. Their visual and technical familiarity with double-pulse signatures mattered. Experts can notice structure that a generic statistic misses. But expertise is vulnerable to anchoring. A person trained to recognize nuclear signatures may overweight nuclear resemblance. A panel convened specifically to investigate false positives may overweight anomalies.

The cure is not to eliminate judgment. It is to externalize it.

Define measurable features. Compare against known positives and known negatives. Blind reviewers where possible. Separate detection from attribution. Archive raw data. Preserve dissent. Do not let a final confidence label erase the distribution underneath it.

Modern systems can do this far better than 1979 systems because storage is cheap and computation is abundant. Yet the institutional temptation to compress remains. Leaders do not want a hundred-dimensional posterior distribution. They want an answer before the meeting.

Nuclear.

Not nuclear.

Maybe.

The word *maybe* is often treated as analytical failure. It can be analytical honesty. The failure begins when maybe is used to avoid specifying what would move the answer.

Event 747 gives us a concrete way to ask.

If the detector mismatch was the strongest optical objection, what degree of mismatch was seen in confirmed nuclear events under comparable background conditions? If the meteoroid explanation was physically plausible, what impact geometries generated synthetic traces matching the two sensors? If neither dataset existed in adequate form, what uncertainty should have been assigned to the conclusion?

Those questions are more valuable than asking which panel had the smarter physicists.

The fireball, if there was one, lasted seconds.

The shape it left in argument is still expanding.
