# A Verdict With Four Columns

The Vela incident does not need one more person saying what they think happened.

It needs an accounting system.

The public debate has accumulated enough confidence language to stock a warehouse: likely, probable, consistent with, almost certainly, inconclusive, most likely non-nuclear, ninety-percent-plus, whitewash, mystery, test. These words came from different institutions answering different questions with different evidence at different times.

Put them in one paragraph and they create fog.

Put the evidence into columns and the structure emerges.

Column one is verified, declassified, or directly documented.

Vela 6911 recorded the double-pulse optical event on September 22, 1979. The event triggered U.S. national-security investigation. A CIA-sponsored scientific group found the signal consistent with an atmospheric nuclear explosion while noting anomalies and recommending more collection. The White House convened a panel chaired by Jack Ruina. That panel ultimately judged a non-nuclear explanation more probable. The Department of Energy reported an unusual Arecibo ionospheric disturbance as potentially corroborative. A December 1979 interagency memorandum said the nature and origin of the event could not be determined with certainty and examined possible actors under a nuclear-test assumption. President Carter privately wrote that scientists were increasingly convinced of an Israeli explosion. South Africa did in fact build a small nuclear arsenal and later dismantled it. The IAEA inspected the dismantlement and found no indication that the declared program had not been terminated.

These facts do not all point in one direction.

That is why they are useful.

Column two is credible but incomplete evidence.

The hydroacoustic material described through declassified correspondence and later technical analysis belongs here. So do the Australian sheep iodine-131 measurements as evidence of environmental radioactivity whose source attribution remains contested. Some intelligence reports about South African naval activity and readiness belong here, depending on provenance. Technical reanalyses of the optical signature belong here when they argue plausibility rather than produce new direct observations.

Column three is disputed allegation.

A specifically Israeli test.

A joint Israeli-South African test.

A South African test assisted by Israel.

Named or unnamed retrospective claims of participation without independent event-specific confirmation.

Claims that the Ruina panel deliberately falsified its conclusion under political pressure.

These propositions may be true. They have not crossed the documentary and physical threshold needed to state them as fact.

Column four is synthesis.

A clandestine tester may have optimized for deniability rather than invisibility.

The Carter administration's policy incentives may have raised the practical threshold for public attribution.

Sensor heterogeneity is more important than simple redundancy.

The event illustrates how secrecy can create an evidentiary ecology in which real disagreement looks like conspiracy from outside.

Modern multi-phenomenology monitoring would likely constrain the event much more strongly.

These are arguments built from the record. They should be labeled as such.

The four-column method sounds almost embarrassingly simple. It prevents several common errors.

First, it stops documentation from laundering underlying claims. A CIA document can be verified while the human-source allegation inside it remains uncertain.

Second, it stops later proven capability from proving earlier action. South Africa's real arsenal raises plausibility but does not identify Event 747.

Third, it stops official conclusions from becoming observations. The Ruina panel's judgment is a documented fact about the investigation, not a direct measurement of a meteoroid.

Fourth, it stops speculation from hiding behind passive voice. "It is believed" becomes "Carter wrote that scientists were increasingly convinced," which restores provenance.

Once the columns are built, we can finally ask for a verdict.

My own assessment is narrower than the title of the case has encouraged.

The public record supports a higher probability of a nuclear event than the old shorthand "meteoroid reflection" suggests. The optical signature was detected by a system designed for nuclear double flashes. A technically serious early review found it consistent with an atmospheric explosion. Later reanalysis has challenged the spacecraft-impact explanation on physical grounds. Arecibo, hydroacoustic reporting, and radionuclide arguments provide possible independent corroboration. The known existence of South Africa's weapons program and Israel's nuclear capability makes a clandestine regional test historically plausible.

That is not enough to identify a perpetrator beyond responsible dispute.

The absence of decisive real-time fallout remains significant. The satellite was old. The two optical sensors did not behave with perfect self-consistency. The principal corroborating streams are incomplete, retrospective, or non-unique. Much event-specific attribution evidence remains circumstantial. No publicly available operational record from Israel or South Africa has emerged that demonstrates a joint test.

So the disciplined conclusion is asymmetric.

The nuclear-explosion hypothesis deserves to be treated as a live and arguably leading interpretation of Event 747, not as a debunked relic.

The Israeli or Israeli-South African attribution remains less secure than the event-level nuclear inference.

This ordering matters.

People often want the most specific conclusion first because names are satisfying. But evidence usually supports nested claims at different strengths.

There was an optical event: very high confidence.

It was external to the satellite: high but not absolute confidence.

It was a nuclear explosion: substantial but contested confidence.

It was conducted by South Africa and/or Israel: plausible, historically grounded, but materially less certain.

It was deliberately covered up by the Carter administration: possible institutional-selection story, not established conspiracy.

A single word like "Vela" hides this ladder.

The ladder also helps specify what future evidence could move each level.

For the external-versus-spacecraft question, recover detailed sensor calibration histories, spacecraft anomalies, micrometeoroid-impact data, and quantitative optical simulations.

For nuclear-versus-natural, recover or declassify raw hydroacoustic records, full atmospheric-sampling data, Arecibo analysis, and environmental radionuclide records. Reconstruct them with modern propagation models.

For attribution, search operational archives: naval movements, nuclear custody records, partner communications, diagnostic equipment, personnel travel, weather support, special budgets, and intelligence intercepts.

For cover-up claims, examine panel drafts, White House communications, evidence-access decisions, and differences between private assessments and public statements.

A good historical mystery should contain its own research program.

Otherwise it is entertainment.

There is also a moral advantage to calibrated confidence.

False accusation in nuclear affairs is not harmless. Naming a country as the perpetrator of a secret nuclear test is a serious historical claim. It can distort public understanding of proliferation, alliances, and legal obligations. Historians should not use "everyone knows" as a substitute for evidence.

False exoneration is not harmless either. If a clandestine test occurred and political convenience helped keep it unresolved, treating the semi-official natural explanation as settled rewards ambiguity and hides a failure of the nonproliferation regime.

The duty runs both directions.

This is why the four columns are better than a jury metaphor. A criminal trial forces acquittal or conviction under a defined burden of proof. History often has no equivalent terminal state. Evidence can remain strong enough for scholarly judgment and too weak for categorical attribution.

That does not mean we shrug.

It means we publish the gradient.

There is a phrase intelligence analysts use when evidence supports a proposition but significant uncertainty remains: confidence level. The phrase is easy to bureaucratize into meaningless labels. Used properly, it is an ethical commitment to separate what you think from how hard the world pushed you there.

Event 747 deserves that courtesy.

The flash was brief.

Our certainty should not be.
