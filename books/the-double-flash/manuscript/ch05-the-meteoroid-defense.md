# The Meteoroid Defense

The most consequential meteoroid in Cold War history may never have existed.

That is not a joke about the Vela panel. It is a statement about explanatory status.

A meteoroid impact on a spacecraft is an ordinary physical event. Small particles strike satellites. Hypervelocity impacts excavate material, create plasma, damage surfaces, and throw debris. Sunlight reflected from debris can enter optical instruments. No new physics is required. No secret navy is required. No illegal nuclear test is required. As an alternative class, the mechanism is respectable.

But a respectable class is not the same thing as a demonstrated event.

The distinction became blurred because the Ruina panel's conclusion acquired the authority of official closure. Over time, summaries often compressed the panel's reasoning into something like: the Vela flash was probably caused by a meteoroid hitting the satellite. The sentence sounds as though investigators found an impact crater, reconstructed a debris cloud, and reproduced the bhangmeter traces.

They did not.

The panel's task was probabilistic. It was looking for a non-nuclear explanation capable of accounting for an anomalous optical record in the absence of decisive corroboration. Its members reviewed a large archive of Vela signals and identified non-nuclear events with some similar features. They considered how impacts and reflected light might produce a false alarm. The final judgment favored a non-nuclear origin.

That is evidence about a technical assessment.

It is not physical recovery of the culprit.

This difference matters because hypotheses have a tendency to harden after they enter official prose. A conjecture begins as "possibly." A panel says "more likely." A press account says "scientists conclude." An encyclopedia says "caused by." Forty years later the speculative scaffolding has disappeared.

The reverse hardening happens too. A possible nuclear test becomes "the Israeli bomb" in retelling, with uncertainty stripped away in the other direction.

Vela is unusually good at teaching this pathology because both camps can point to real documents.

To evaluate the meteoroid hypothesis fairly, start with what it gets right.

First, the satellite was old. Vela 6911 had been in orbit for roughly ten years, beyond the clean opening phase of a mission. Space hardware ages in a radiation environment and accumulates damage. A local spacecraft event therefore deserves a higher prior probability than it would on a pristine platform launched yesterday.

Second, meteoroid and debris impacts are real. Hypervelocity impacts can eject fragments and generate transient optical effects. Engineers designing space sensors must treat them as part of the environment.

Third, the detector mismatch in Event 747 invites a local explanation. If two instruments looking outward report an external event differently, one possibility is that the illumination near the spacecraft was not uniform. A debris cloud or glint could create angular structure impossible for a distant fireball.

Fourth, the lack of unambiguous fallout weighs against a nuclear event. The non-nuclear model does not need to explain missing radioactive debris because it predicts none.

These are substantial points.

Now charge the hypothesis for what it needs.

A random impact must occur at the right moment, with the right geometry relative to the Sun and the bhangmeters, creating reflective material with the right angular distribution and brightness history. The reflected-light sequence must generate not just any transient but a two-pulse temporal structure sufficiently similar to a nuclear signature to activate the detection logic. It must illuminate both sensors in the observed fashion while producing the detector asymmetry that motivated the local-event argument. It must do this without leaving an independently recognized spacecraft anomaly that clinches the impact.

None of these requirements makes the event impossible. Together they make the phrase "a meteoroid hit it" analytically incomplete.

Wright and De Geer's later reanalysis focused on this gap. They examined spacecraft geometry and argued that much ejecta from a hypervelocity collision would be carried away from the optical sensors' fields of view, particularly given the satellite's solar-panel-dominated surface. They also argued that the panel had not presented a quantitative model showing how impact debris would reproduce the measured double flash. Their conclusion was not that meteoroids cannot create optical artifacts. It was that this specific artifact explanation was weaker than its official afterlife suggested.

The history of science is full of these orphaned alternatives.

An explanation is proposed because the favored model has an anomaly. The alternative survives because it is possible, not because it is tested. Over time, the anomaly becomes associated with the alternative so strongly that people forget the alternative never passed its own experimental burden.

This happens in medicine. A patient's symptoms do not fit diagnosis A, so diagnosis B is floated. Later records list B as though a confirmatory test occurred.

It happens in intelligence. A radar track is inconsistent with a known aircraft, so "instrument artifact" enters the assessment. Years later, artifact becomes the official cause despite no reproduction of the failure.

It happens in anomalous research constantly. Skeptics are right to demand that extraordinary claims pay an extraordinary evidentiary cost. But conventional alternatives also need mechanisms. "Could be mundane" is a useful warning, not a completed analysis.

The right standard is symmetrical mechanism.

If the bomb hypothesis must explain detector ratios, the meteoroid hypothesis must explain the double pulse.

If the bomb hypothesis must explain absent fallout, the meteoroid hypothesis must explain any independent acoustic or ionospheric traces that align with the event.

If the bomb hypothesis requires a clandestine operation by a capable state, the meteoroid hypothesis requires a coincident local spacecraft event with specific optical geometry.

Neither gets to treat the other's difficulty as its own proof.

There is another reason the meteoroid defense became attractive. It offered a category of cause with no perpetrator.

Natural explanations are diplomatically cheap.

That does not make them false. Nature is indifferent to diplomatic budgets. But when two explanations have political costs differing by orders of magnitude, analysts should become unusually careful about how uncertainty is expressed.

A meteoroid means no treaty violation, no nuclear newcomer, no need to accuse Israel or apartheid South Africa, no sanctions debate, no intelligence failure to explain, no destabilizing precedent. A clandestine test creates all of those problems at once.

The existence of those incentives has been used by critics to argue that the White House panel was predisposed toward a natural explanation. The accusation can itself become lazy. Scientists do not automatically bend because their conclusion is convenient. A panel can reach a politically convenient result for sound reasons. The correct way to test the allegation is to inspect method: selection of comparison events, modeling assumptions, treatment of dissent, access to corroborating intelligence, and how strongly the final language follows from the data.

Motives belong in an evidentiary audit, not in place of one.

The same applies to those who prefer the nuclear interpretation. A clandestine Israeli-South African test is narratively powerful. It fits later knowledge that South Africa built weapons. It fits Israel's opaque nuclear posture. It offers secret cooperation, remote seas, intelligence conflict, and a possible cover-up. Those features make the story compelling enough to create their own bias.

A story can be true and still benefit from being irresistible.

This is why the spacecraft deserves more attention than the suspected governments in the first half of the case. If the optical record can be plausibly generated by local debris, attribution becomes irrelevant. If the local-debris model fails under realistic geometry, the nuclear hypothesis regains weight before we ever mention Pretoria or Jerusalem.

Physics should get first refusal.

What experiment could have helped?

In an ideal world, investigators would have taken a flight spare or geometrically faithful model of Vela 6911, simulated hypervelocity impacts on representative surfaces, illuminated the ejecta under relevant solar angles, and measured what the bhangmeter positions would see. They could have varied impact location, particle mass, velocity, surface composition, and orientation. A distribution of synthetic traces could then be compared against Event 747.

Such a program might have been impractical, expensive, or insufficiently diagnostic in 1979. But writing down the experiment clarifies what the hypothesis claims.

Today, one could go further. Modern spacecraft impact models, ray tracing, high-speed photometry, Monte Carlo debris distributions, and archived sensor characteristics could define the range of optical artifacts expected from impacts. The key output would not be a cinematic recreation. It would be a likelihood distribution: among plausible impacts, how often do two detectors see a nuclear-like double pulse with Event 747's timing and amplitude relationships?

That number would transform the argument.

It might vindicate the Ruina panel.

It might bury the meteoroid theory.

It might show that both camps have been pretending the data are more discriminating than they are.

Historical mysteries often survive because no one identifies the experiment that would make them less mysterious.

Vela's meteoroid is not interesting because rocks in space are exotic.

It is interesting because a mundane hypothesis can become culturally immune to testing once it has been assigned the job of making an inconvenient event go away.
