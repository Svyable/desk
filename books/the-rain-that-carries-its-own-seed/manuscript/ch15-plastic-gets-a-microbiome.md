# Plastic Gets a Microbiome

Microplastic is often discussed as if its environmental identity were fixed at manufacture.

A polyethylene fragment leaves a product, enters soil or water, becomes airborne, and remains a polyethylene fragment in our mental inventory. In nature it weathers, oxidizes, adsorbs organics, accumulates salts and acquires a microbiome. Its surface becomes history.

Cloud physics cares about the surface that exists now.

A 2024 *Nature Communications* study showed that weathering can alter the ice-nucleation activity of microplastics. Then a 2026 *Environmental Science & Technology* paper by Carrie Carpenter and colleagues added *Pseudomonas syringae* to the problem. They grew strains with different ice-nucleating capacities on polystyrene and polyethylene particles and compared freezing behavior.

The composite particles froze warmer.

Across the tested systems, biofilm colonization by *P. syringae* increased median freezing temperatures by roughly six and a half degrees Celsius relative to uncolonized plastics, while polymer type and biological state influenced the details. Even a noncanonical ice-nucleating strain could alter some particle behavior when abundant on the surface.

This is exactly the kind of result that attracts a bad headline.

'Plastic is changing the weather.'

The experiment does not show that.

It shows that microplastic ice-nucleating properties are not independent of biological colonization and that composite particles can behave differently from either clean polymer or suspended cells under laboratory freezing assays.

Atmospheric relevance still depends on size, abundance, aerosolization, residence time, cloud incorporation and temperature.

The tested particles included sizes larger than many long-lived atmospheric aerosols. Laboratory biofilms may differ from thin environmental coatings. The fraction of airborne microplastics carrying ice-active *P. syringae* is unknown. A warmer median freezing temperature does not tell us how many cloud droplets contain such particles.

Those caveats should make the result more interesting, not less.

We have spent decades dividing aerosols into natural and anthropogenic bins. Biological colonization erases that division at the particle surface.

A plastic particle can become a scaffold for microbial ice nucleation.

That raises several testable questions. Does weathering make colonization more likely? Do bacterial ice-nucleating proteins remain active after aerosolization? Are biofilm-coated microplastics enriched in precipitation relative to clean particles? Does a polymer surface stabilize protein assemblies or alter their orientation? Does exposure to ultraviolet light destroy the biological contribution faster than the polymer contribution?

The composite object invites molecular work and field work at once.

It also adds a new evolutionary context. *P. syringae* did not evolve on polyethylene. If its ice-nucleating machinery functions well on plastic, that is exaptation in the bluntest sense: ancient molecular machinery encountering a new planetary surface.

The consequences need not be beneficial to the bacterium.

Plastic could increase atmospheric residence by changing particle aerodynamics. It could shield cells from radiation. It could carry them to unsuitable habitats. It could increase ice incorporation. It could simply be a dead-end substrate.

The same uncertainty applies to weather.

Anthropogenic particles may enhance, suppress or redistribute biological ice activity depending on conditions. Pollutants can also damage ice-nucleating proteins. The next chapter turns to that opposite interaction.

For now, the 2026 experiment matters because it prevents a static view of atmospheric material.

Particles age biologically.

The atmosphere is full of interfaces that have been touched by life.

If cloud models classify particles only by original material, they can miss the surface that water actually meets.

This is not unique to plastic. Dust acquires coatings. Sea spray contains organics. Soot ages. Pollen ruptures. Bacteria exude polymers. The ice nucleus is often a composite archive.

Microplastic makes the principle visible because the anthropogenic origin is unmistakable.

A particle invented within living memory can enter a biological process old enough to predate animals.

That juxtaposition is the sort of thing this book is looking for.
