# What a Cloud Actually Needs

A bacterium cannot make a cloud from a blue sky.

That boundary should be printed on every popular account of bioprecipitation.

Cloud formation requires thermodynamics and dynamics: air must reach saturation, usually through cooling associated with ascent or mixing. Water vapor condenses on cloud condensation nuclei. Droplets form. Temperature, humidity, vertical motion and aerosol population evolve together. Only after supercooled liquid exists does an ice-nucleating bacterium get to perform its famous trick.

This seems elementary. It is also where causal language goes wrong.

If biological ice nuclei alter the phase of an existing cloud, saying they 'make rain' is comparable to saying a spark makes gasoline. The spark can be decisive without supplying the fuel.

Meteorology supplies the potential energy and water.

Ice nucleation changes a transition.

Mixed-phase clouds are especially relevant because liquid droplets and ice coexist below zero. The saturation vapor pressure over ice is lower than over liquid water. Under suitable conditions, ice crystals can grow at the expense of supercooled droplets. This Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen process is one route by which initial ice can amplify. Riming adds another, as ice collects supercooled droplets. Aggregation builds larger particles. Falling ice can melt into rain below the freezing level.

The importance of the first ice depends on regime.

In some clouds, homogeneous or abundant mineral nucleation will eventually produce plenty of ice regardless. In others, warm-active biological particles could advance glaciation. A few minutes or a few hundred meters of altitude can change where precipitation begins, how long condensate remains aloft and how cloud radiative properties evolve.

The atmospheric leverage is therefore temporal as well as numerical.

An ice nucleus that acts early can matter more than many nuclei that act later.

This is why freezing spectra are more informative than a yes/no label. An organism with activity beginning near minus three degrees occupies a different niche from mineral dust activating mainly below minus fifteen. As temperature falls, the competitive field changes.

There is a simple way to overstate this. Take the warm activation temperature of *P. syringae*, place it next to the statement that ice is important for precipitation, and imply dominance.

Missing are concentration and encounter probability.

A highly efficient biological nucleus that appears once per cubic meter may lose to a less efficient mineral particle present millions of times more often. Cloud droplets also have to contain or contact the active particle. Aerosol size influences activation as a cloud droplet. Some ice-nucleating material may remain interstitial rather than entering droplets.

The path from aerosol to ice crystal is not automatic.

Nor is every ice crystal precipitation.

It can sublimate. It can remain small. The cloud can evaporate. Updrafts can retain particles. Warm layers can change phase. A storm's dynamics may overwhelm microphysical perturbations.

These caveats are not defensive prose. They identify the measurements needed to estimate effect size.

One wants vertical profiles of biological and mineral INPs, activation spectra, cloud-droplet residual chemistry, ice-crystal residuals, meteorology, radar evolution and precipitation. Ideally one also wants models run with and without observed biological nuclei, constrained by the same dynamics.

The model cannot serve as proof merely because it produces a rainfall difference. Microphysics schemes contain assumptions. Biological INP parameterizations are uncertain. A model is most useful when it predicts observables beyond the variable used to tune it.

For example, if adding measured warm-active biological nuclei produces earlier glaciation, the model should also predict where ice appears vertically, how droplet number changes, what radar signatures emerge and whether precipitation timing shifts. Aircraft data can then challenge the whole chain.

This is how a hypothesis about invisible bacteria becomes meteorology.

The cloud also imposes a biological filter. Droplets are acidic or chemically complex. Oxidants are present. Ultraviolet exposure changes with cloud depth. Freeze-thaw cycles stress membranes. Some cells may be metabolically active. Others become fragments. Proteins may remain active after viability is lost.

Atmospheric ice nucleation therefore lives at the boundary between particle physics and organismal state.

A dead bacterium can still be meteorologically active.

That sentence is fatal to simplistic evolutionary stories and valuable to atmospheric ones.

If the protein persists, cloud effects need not depend on cell survival. If the ecological hypothesis requires viable deposition, however, persistence of dead activity is not enough.

A cloud needs the right physics.

A life cycle needs a living beneficiary.

The two overlap only part of the time.
