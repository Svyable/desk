# Dust, Soot, Fungi, Pollen, Bacteria

The atmosphere does not hold a contest called bacteria versus nothing.

Every claim for biological ice nucleation occurs inside a crowded field of mineral dust, soot, sea spray, organic particles, fungal spores, pollen fragments, plant debris and secondary aerosols. The important question is rarely whether a bacterial particle can nucleate ice. It is whether it changes which particle wins first under a particular cloud condition.

Mineral dust has enormous advantages of abundance and durability. Desert sources can inject vast quantities into the atmosphere and transport them across continents and oceans. Many minerals nucleate ice effectively at sufficiently cold temperatures. Dust therefore sets a formidable baseline for any claim that biological material controls cloud glaciation globally.

Biology's advantage is warmth.

Some biological ice nuclei activate at temperatures above minus ten degrees Celsius, with the most efficient bacterial systems acting far warmer. That gives them access to a regime where many mineral nuclei remain inactive.

This temperature separation is why relative rarity does not automatically imply irrelevance.

Fungi complicate the category further. Spores and fungal macromolecules can nucleate ice. Some are emitted in enormous numbers. Pollen grains can release subpollen particles and macromolecules much smaller than the original grain. Plant material can contribute ice-active organic matter. Marine biology can add ice-nucleating material to sea spray.

The biological atmosphere is not synonymous with bacteria.

A 2023 Communications Earth & Environment study pushed this point in a different direction by showing that abundant biomolecules, including RuBisCO, can catalyze ice nucleation under laboratory conditions. The atmospheric importance of any one biomolecule remains a question of source and concentration, but the result undermines the intuition that ice activity requires a specialized microbial adaptation.

There may be many biological roads to an ice-like water structure.

This creates an attribution problem. Heat treatment, filtration and chemical digestion can classify some ice nuclei as likely biological, but these are operational definitions. Heat-sensitive activity suggests proteinaceous material but does not name a species. Filtration separates size fractions but particles aggregate. DNA sequencing describes organisms present in a sample, not necessarily the molecules responsible for freezing.

One Scientific Reports study of rainwater found efficient ice-nucleating activity that was not readily explained by the known abundance of canonical ice-nucleating bacteria, suggesting submicron or otherwise unrecognized sources.

That kind of result is healthy for the field.

If every warm freezing event were assigned to *Pseudomonas syringae*, bioprecipitation would become a just-so story. Unknown nuclei force mechanism back into the laboratory.

Competition also changes with aerosol aging. Mineral particles acquire coatings. Biological particles interact with pollution. Proteins denature or aggregate. Sea salt changes water activity. Organic films can expose or block active sites. Freeze-thaw cycling can fragment particles.

The 'ice nucleus' sampled at a detector may be a composite object with history.

This becomes crucial in polluted environments where anthropogenic and biological categories merge. A microplastic colonized by bacteria is both. A soot particle coated in biological organic matter does not respect our source taxonomy. A fungal fragment attached to dust may behave differently from either particle alone.

Cloud microphysics sees surfaces, not grant categories.

The strongest bioprecipitation experiments therefore need competition built in. It is not enough to introduce bacteria into ultrapure water and show warm freezing. Add realistic mineral dust concentrations. Add sea salt. Add organic coatings. Vary temperature. Measure which particles actually appear inside ice residuals.

Then manipulate the biological fraction while holding dynamics fixed.

This is harder than a clean assay and much closer to the atmospheric claim.

There is also a geographical dimension. In dusty regions, mineral INPs may dominate. Over vegetated, humid landscapes with low dust loading, biological warm-active particles may have more room to matter. Over oceans, marine biological particles enter a different regime. Snowy mountain environments can mix long-range dust with local biological sources.

A global mean may hide the places where biology is consequential.

That is why 'Does biology affect precipitation?' is badly posed.

Where? At what temperature? In competition with what? During which season? At what aerosol loading? In which cloud type?

The more specific questions become, the less sensational the subject sounds.

That is usually a sign of progress.

The bacterium does not need to defeat dust everywhere.

It only needs a niche in phase space.
