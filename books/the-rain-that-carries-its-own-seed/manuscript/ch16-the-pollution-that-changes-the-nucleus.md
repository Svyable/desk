# The Pollution That Changes the Nucleus

If biological ice nucleation were a rigid property, pollution would merely change how many particles reached a cloud.

It is not rigid.

The active machinery is chemical. Proteins aggregate. Membranes change. Surfaces adsorb molecules. Oxidants attack residues. Surfactants alter interfaces. Persistent pollutants can enter biological membranes and bind proteins.

In 2026, laboratory work on perfluorinated organic acids reported that PFOS and PFOA could disrupt bacterial ice-nucleating protein aggregates and reduce freezing efficiency across tested ice-nucleation classes. The proposed mechanisms involved interactions with membranes and proteins rather than a simple toxic-kills-bacterium story.

This points in the opposite direction from colonized microplastics.

One anthropogenic influence can enhance a composite particle's ice activity. Another can impair the molecular assembly that makes a bacterium unusually efficient.

There is no single pollution effect.

This is important because environmental narratives like symmetry. Natural biology has a feedback; pollution perturbs it. The actual system is likely to contain sign changes. A pollutant can increase aerosol number while decreasing biological potency. Warming can increase microbial growth while reducing mixed-phase cloud opportunities at some altitudes. Land-use change can increase dust and reduce vegetation simultaneously.

The resulting precipitation response may be nonlinear.

The PFAS result is laboratory evidence, not a weather observation. Atmospheric concentrations at bacterial surfaces may differ from exposure conditions. Cells in biofilms behave differently from cultures. Other organic films may protect ice-nucleating proteins. The study nevertheless establishes a mechanistic possibility: modern chemical exposure can reach down to the molecular architecture of an ancient ice-nucleation trait.

That is enough to justify field measurement.

One could sample *P. syringae* and other biological INPs across gradients of PFAS contamination, urban pollution and agricultural chemistry. Measure pollutant burdens in cells or particles, ice-nucleation spectra, protein aggregation and viability. Compare atmospheric isolates with surface populations. Use controlled exposures to reproduce field differences.

If pollution systematically shifts activation temperature, cloud models would need a state variable for biological aging rather than a fixed species coefficient.

That sounds excessive until one remembers that atmospheric chemistry already tracks aging for inorganic and organic aerosols because it matters for hygroscopicity and radiative properties.

Biological particles deserve the same seriousness if their contribution is consequential.

This chapter also complicates evolutionary inference. A trait selected under ancient atmospheric chemistry is now operating in a novel chemical environment. If modern pollutants impair ice nucleation, present-day field measurements may underestimate historical potency. If anthropogenic surfaces enhance activity, the opposite may occur.

We cannot assume the modern atmosphere is a neutral laboratory for evolutionary hypotheses.

The Anthropocene changes both sources and interfaces.

There is a broader epistemic lesson here. Environmental effects are often inferred from concentration alone. More bacteria means more biological ice nucleation. More plastic means more plastic INPs. More pollution means more cloud effect.

Function can change independently of count.

A billion inactive cells are not equivalent to a million strongly active ones. An abundant particle population coated into irrelevance may matter less than a sparse warm-active population. The correct unit is not just particles per cubic meter but an activity spectrum over temperature and state.

This is why freezing assays remain central even in an age of sequencing.

DNA can tell us who is there. It cannot by itself tell us how water freezes around them today.

The nucleus has physiology.

That sentence feels odd because we are accustomed to treating atmospheric nuclei as inert objects. Biology refuses the convenience.

A bacterium carried into a cloud brings a recent history of temperature, nutrients, radiation, pollutants and growth conditions. Its ability to organize ice reflects that history.

Weather may therefore encounter not a species but a phenotype.
