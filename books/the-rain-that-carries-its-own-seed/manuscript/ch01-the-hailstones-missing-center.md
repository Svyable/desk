# The Hailstone's Missing Center

A hailstone is a layered archive of indecision. Liquid water remained liquid below zero, then some small thing convinced part of it to become ice. More water arrived. The particle rose and fell through a cloud, collecting layers whose texture recorded temperature, liquid water content, collision history and time. By the time it struck the ground, the initiating event was buried near the center.

In the 1960s, asking what occupied that center was not an eccentric question. Cloud physicists needed to understand heterogeneous ice nucleation because the atmosphere does not usually wait for pure water to freeze homogeneously near minus forty degrees Celsius. Mixed-phase clouds contain supercooled droplets at much warmer temperatures. Something provides a surface on which the ordered lattice of ice can get started.

Mineral dust was an obvious candidate. Soot, clay and other aerosols could do it. Yet some collected material froze water at temperatures surprisingly close to zero. That efficiency was the anomaly.

Gabor Vali and Russell Schnell reconstructed this path in 2024, looking back on work that began with hail and ended in microbiology. Exceptionally active nuclei turned up in soils rich in decaying vegetation. Then in decaying leaves. Then in plankton-rich ocean water. The pattern was awkward for a story in which atmospheric ice nuclei were principally bits of inert mineral.

The phrase biological ice nucleus sounds obvious now because we have inherited the result. It was not obvious then. A dead fragment of biological material might have unusual surface chemistry. A living cell might simply carry mineral contamination. A macromolecule might be responsible. A population of microbes might be incidental. Each possibility implied a different atmospheric story.

This chapter matters because the discovery did not begin with a theory that bacteria control weather. It began with measurements that would not behave.

That is one of the cleanest origins a strange field can have.

Schnell and Vali's retrospective is unusually useful because it also corrects a common narrative error. Discoveries of this kind are often retold as a single eureka moment. The actual path was distributed across meteorology, plant pathology, microbiology and field sampling. Different investigators were asking different questions. The convergence is stronger evidence than a single laboratory announcing a grand synthesis because each group had reasons to mistrust the other's assumptions.

Plant pathologists did not need bacteria to matter in clouds. Cloud physicists did not need a leaf pathogen to explain crop injury. The organism became interesting because the same physical trait answered two unrelated problems.

The first problem was warm ice.

Biological ice-nucleating particles can be remarkably efficient at temperatures above minus ten degrees Celsius. That is meteorologically important because the population of particles capable of triggering freezing generally changes sharply with temperature. A particle that can act at minus five is not merely a slightly better particle than one that activates at minus twenty. It enters a different part of the cloud's history.

Warm-temperature activity also created a measurement trap. If one samples rain or snow and finds biological ice nucleators, did they cause precipitation or were they merely collected by it? The direction of causality is not printed on the particle.

This problem will recur through the book. Presence in precipitation is Class 1 if the organism is cultured and identified. Participation in ice nucleation can be Class 1 in a controlled droplet assay. Causation of a particular cloud's precipitation is a much stronger statement.

The center of a hailstone does not solve that problem by itself.

But the hailstone taught atmospheric science to look for a trigger small enough to disappear inside the event it helped begin. That is the conceptual move worth keeping. Weather contains thresholds. A threshold process can be influenced by a minority component whose mass is negligible compared with the final storm.

A seed crystal does not have to weigh as much as the ice it organizes.

This is where weak versions of the hypothesis remain plausible even if grand versions fail. Biological ice nucleators do not need to dominate atmospheric aerosol mass to matter in a warm mixed-phase cloud. They need to be present in the right droplets, at the right temperature, before competing ice-nucleating particles activate.

The word right is doing all the work.

Concentration matters. Vertical placement matters. Cloud regime matters. Droplet chemistry matters. Biological state matters. The aggregation state of ice-nucleating proteins matters. Mineral dust may be so abundant that bacteria add little. In other clouds, warm-active biological material could have disproportionate leverage.

This is why global statements are dangerous. 'Bacteria make rain' compresses an atmospheric competition into a slogan.

The more interesting sentence is conditional: in some clouds, at some temperatures, a biological particle may trigger freezing before an inorganic competitor would have done so.

If that initial ice then grows by vapor deposition, riming or collision and eventually contributes to precipitation, the bacterium has participated in a chain much larger than itself.

We can test every link.

That is the standard this book will use. Start with the nucleus. Refuse to skip directly to the storm.
