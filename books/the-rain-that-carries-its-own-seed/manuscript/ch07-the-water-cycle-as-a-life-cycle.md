# The Water Cycle as a Life Cycle

A diagram of the water cycle normally contains arrows between ocean, atmosphere, cloud, precipitation, river and land. Add *Pseudomonas syringae* and the arrows become potential habitat transitions.

This was the deeper significance of the 2008 work linking the bacterium to rain, snow, alpine waters, wild plants and crops. The organism was not simply 'airborne.' It appeared across connected freshwater environments in ways consistent with repeated movement through the same hydrological network humans use to teach elementary geography.

The word cycle invites overstatement, so it helps to specify what is known.

Cells have been isolated from multiple water-associated habitats. Genotypes connect environmental and agricultural populations. Ice-nucleation-active strains occur among them. Snow populations can be strongly enriched for warm-active ice nucleation. Rain can deposit viable cells. Surface water can carry them onward. Plants can amplify populations.

Those are pieces of a cycle.

What is not directly demonstrated is that an individual lineage routinely completes the entire circuit because its ice-nucleation phenotype increases atmospheric return.

That stronger claim requires fitness evidence.

Ecologists are used to this distinction. Finding a bird on two islands proves dispersal occurred, not why. Finding a gene associated with a trait proves neither that the trait is adaptive in the current environment nor that it caused the dispersal. Historical contingency and hitchhiking are always available.

The atmospheric version is harder because the habitat is moving and short-lived.

A cloud is not a pond in the sky. Droplets form, collide, evaporate and freeze. Air entrains and detrains. Radiation changes temperature. Turbulence moves particles across microenvironments. Residence time may be hours rather than generations.

For bacteria, this could be less a habitat for growth than a corridor with biochemical activity.

Some atmospheric microbiology studies have documented metabolic activity in cloud water, but activity does not imply robust population growth. The evolutionary benefit of an ice-nucleating trait could therefore come from transport rather than reproduction aloft.

One attractive model is 'suicidal' at the cellular scale but beneficial at the lineage scale only if enough viable cells survive. Ice formation can expose a cell to mechanical and osmotic stress. Yet if freezing causes a hydrometeor to precipitate sooner, the alternative might be prolonged UV exposure and desiccation followed by deposition in a less favorable place.

The fitness comparison is not survival versus death.

It is one bad atmospheric itinerary versus another.

This suggests experiments more interesting than measuring freezing curves. Compete active and inactive strains through complete transport cycles. Start from plant canopies or water surfaces. Aerosolize under controlled conditions. Expose cells to radiation and humidity regimes. Form clouds. Allow mixed-phase processing. Collect precipitation and residual aerosol separately. Measure viability and subsequent colonization success.

The experiment is technically difficult because it tries to preserve ecology across scales.

That is precisely why the hypothesis has survived largely as inference.

There is also a population-genetic question. If ice nucleation is strongly advantageous in atmospheric cycling, alleles supporting the phenotype should show patterns consistent with selection in lineages frequently using that route. But the machinery has costs. Large proteins must be produced and assembled. Expression may be regulated. The phenotype varies within *P. syringae*. A trait with atmospheric benefit should not be uniformly present if only some ecologies reward it.

Variation is therefore not an embarrassment. It may contain the test.

The water-cycle hypothesis also broadens the meaning of plant disease. A crop epidemic does not begin at the field boundary. Irrigation water, rain and snow can connect plant pathogens to regional hydrology. Agricultural management can alter surface populations that later become aerosol. Conversely, wild landscapes can seed agricultural environments.

This reciprocity is more plausible than a one-way story in which weather delivers pathogens from an abstract sky.

The sky has sources.

Forests, crops, lakes, soils and oceans continually emit particles. Raindrop impact can eject new aerosols from surfaces. Wind can strip microbes from leaves. Bubble bursting can launch marine material. Fire can redistribute biological remnants. Every atmospheric sample is downstream of a landscape.

Bioprecipitation proposes that the downstream process can feed back on the source.

The correct null hypothesis is not 'biology has no effect on weather.' Biology already affects aerosol composition and surface fluxes. The question is narrower: does biological ice-nucleating material change precipitation enough, often enough, for a feedback to matter ecologically or climatically?

That question can fail in degrees.

The effect might matter to individual clouds but not regional rainfall. It might matter regionally only in certain seasons. It might matter for snowfall more than rain. It might affect initiation timing without changing total precipitation. It might be important before abundant mineral dust arrives and irrelevant afterward.

A cycle can be real without being dominant.

That is a useful sentence to carry into every feedback argument in Earth science.
