# The Forest Above the Forest

Stand above a forest and the canopy looks like a boundary.

For atmospheric biology it is a source surface.

Leaves exchange gases, emit volatile organics, release pollen and fungal spores, host bacterial populations and intercept particles arriving from elsewhere. Wind shakes material loose. Rain splashes it upward. Drying films fracture. Insects and birds disturb surfaces. A forest extends into the air as chemistry and particulate matter long before one reaches a cloud.

This airborne extension is sometimes called the aerobiome. The word risks making a dynamic mixture sound like a stable ecosystem, but it captures something useful: the atmosphere contains biological material with sources, transformations and destinations.

Ice-nucleating organisms occupy a special position because their effect is conditional on phase.

A leaf population of *Pseudomonas syringae* can become aerosol. Some cells or fragments reach cloud-relevant altitudes. If they enter supercooled droplets and remain active, they can alter freezing. Precipitation can then return biological material to the same or another forest.

The forest is both source and sink.

This is the landscape scale at which bioprecipitation becomes environmentally consequential rather than merely microbiologically interesting.

Morris and colleagues have argued that biologically active landscapes may participate in precipitation feedbacks through ice-nucleating aerosols. Observations show that biological INP abundance can correlate with vegetated sources and climatic zones. Numerical work can produce plausible sensitivities.

But forests also alter weather through much larger fluxes: evapotranspiration, surface roughness, sensible heat, boundary-layer development and volatile-organic aerosol formation. Any microbial ice effect sits inside those stronger pathways.

Attribution is therefore brutal.

If forest removal reduces rainfall, the result does not isolate biological ice nuclei. It could arise from reduced moisture recycling or circulation changes. If reforestation increases warm-temperature INPs, that does not prove those particles changed precipitation.

The microbial pathway needs a discriminating signature.

Temperature provides one. Biological INPs often dominate the unusually warm end of the freezing spectrum. If a landscape perturbation changes warm-active INPs without comparable changes in dust, cloud glaciation timing at warmer mixed-phase temperatures could respond in a characteristic way.

Molecular source markers provide another. Specific ice-nucleation genes or proteins might link cloud residuals to vegetation. Isotopic signatures and back trajectories can constrain origin. Aircraft can compare air entering and leaving forested regions.

A strong field campaign would follow an air mass rather than merely sample a site.

Measure the forest surface microbiome. Measure emitted aerosols. Measure boundary-layer profiles. Sample cloud water and ice residuals. Record precipitation. Repeat across seasons and after rainfall. Compare with adjacent land covers.

The campaign would be expensive.

It would also answer more than a decade of diagram-based argument.

There is a colonial history of rainfall narratives that makes caution useful. People have repeatedly claimed that forests attract rain, that cultivation makes rain, that deforestation inevitably creates desert, or that particular land practices control regional climate with simple directionality. Some versions contain pieces of real physics. Others became policy myths.

Bioprecipitation could join that tradition if it becomes a slogan detached from effect size.

A forest does not need bacteria to influence rainfall. Bacteria do not need to control rainfall to be interesting.

The strongest book keeps both truths at once.

There is nevertheless something conceptually beautiful about a canopy releasing particles capable of changing the phase of the water that may later return. Beauty is allowed in science. It just has to pay rent in prediction.

Here is one prediction: after controlling for meteorology and mineral aerosol, landscapes with strong sources of warm-active biological INPs should show systematic enrichment of those particles in cloud ice and precipitation compared with matched landscapes lacking such sources.

Here is another: suppressing the biological activity while preserving aerosol mass should shift initial ice to colder temperatures.

These are not impossible experiments.

The forest above the forest is measurable.
