# The Gram-Positive Surprise

For years, the bacterial ice-nucleation story had a molecular protagonist so persuasive that it threatened to become a monopoly.

*Pseudomonas syringae* and related Gram-negative bacteria carried a large membrane-associated protein whose repetitive architecture could explain unusually warm freezing. Once a field has a famous mechanism, new observations tend to be interpreted through it. Find ice-active bacteria in precipitation and the first question becomes whether they have the familiar protein.

Then an organism from precipitation refused the script.

The 2017 Virginia precipitation survey found ice-nucleation-active isolates belonging to *Lysinibacillus*, a Gram-positive genus not previously known for the canonical bacterial ice-nucleation phenotype. The activity behaved differently. It was associated with nanometer-scale material, survived treatments that would have destroyed an ordinary protein explanation, and appeared to be secreted rather than embedded in a Gram-negative outer membrane.

A second mechanism had entered the weather story.

Follow-up work published in *The ISME Journal* in 2022 traced the phenotype in *Lysinibacillus parviboronicapiens* to biosynthetic machinery involving a polyketide synthase and a non-ribosomal peptide synthetase. The active product appeared associated with secreted particles resembling chains of extracellular structures rather than the huge ice-nucleating protein assemblies known from *Pseudomonas*.

This was not merely taxonomic novelty.

It changed what atmospheric sampling had to look for.

If ice nucleation can be carried by secreted nanoscale material, viable-cell counts underestimate the biological inventory. A parent organism can remain on a surface while active particles travel. Filtration thresholds that remove whole bacteria may leave nucleating material behind. Genetic screens limited to the canonical ice-nucleation gene can miss function entirely.

The atmosphere may contain biological ice activity whose source organism is no longer attached.

That possibility creates a strange ecological asymmetry. A secreted particle can influence freezing without itself reproducing. Any benefit must return indirectly to the lineage that made it. If the particle helps precipitate water onto a landscape containing its producer, a local ecological feedback is conceivable. If it travels far away and falls elsewhere, the benefit becomes diffuse. If it merely changes cloud phase with no meaningful ecological consequence, it is atmospheric chemistry produced by life rather than adaptation for weather.

The distinction cannot be solved from biosynthesis alone.

Still, multiple biological mechanisms weaken one objection sometimes made against bioprecipitation: that the phenomenon is an oddity of one plant pathogen. Specialized ice nucleation has evolved or emerged in more than one biological context. Fungi, pollen and bacterial secretions add still more routes.

Convergence does not prove atmospheric selection.

The same physical problem can be useful in many environments. Ice formation matters to frost injury, overwintering, dispersal and food structure. A molecule can have atmospheric consequences without those consequences explaining its origin.

But convergence does change prior plausibility. If diverse biological systems repeatedly produce efficient ice nuclei, biology is not an accidental contaminant in the atmospheric ice-nucleating pool. It is a source class with multiple molecular strategies.

That recognition also complicates climate models. Parameterizing biological INPs as a fixed bacterial concentration misses active fragments and secreted material. Parameterizing all warm-temperature ice activity as biological risks the opposite error because inorganic particles can also be efficient under some conditions.

The Gram-positive surprise is therefore a lesson in unknown unknowns with an experimental handle.

One can mutate the biosynthetic genes. One can fractionate secreted particles. One can compare freezing spectra. One can expose particles to heat, enzymes and radiation. One can track their survival in aerosol chambers.

The weather-scale question begins again at the molecule.

There is a pleasing historical symmetry here. The original discovery of biological ice nuclei began because atmospheric particles were more efficient than expected. Decades later, even within the biological category, researchers found an efficient nucleus that did not fit the expected mechanism.

Nature had kept a spare trick.

The danger for this book would be to turn every new mechanism into evidence for a grand feedback. The better lesson is methodological: do not equate a famous organism with a functional class.

If the atmosphere is selecting particles by what they do to water, taxonomy is secondary.

The cloud asks only whether a critical ice embryo can form.
