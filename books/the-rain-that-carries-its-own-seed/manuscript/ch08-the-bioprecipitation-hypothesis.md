# The Bioprecipitation Hypothesis

The strongest version of this book can be drawn with five arrows.

Vegetation and other surfaces produce microorganisms or biological material. Some becomes airborne. A subset nucleates ice efficiently in clouds. That ice contributes to precipitation. Precipitation returns the organisms or their relatives to habitats where biological production resumes.

A circle appears.

Circles are dangerous in science because our eyes supply causation for free.

Cindy Morris and colleagues have argued that observations and modeling make a bioprecipitation feedback plausible, especially for biological particles capable of catalyzing ice near zero compared with many mineral nuclei. The evolutionary speculation is particularly interesting: if ice-nucleation-active bacteria have been associated with terrestrial vegetation for long periods, precipitation-mediated dispersal could have influenced the trait's history.

Plausible is the correct word.

The hypothesis gains strength from independent facts. Biological ice nuclei exist. Some are extremely efficient. Vegetated landscapes emit bioaerosols. Ice-active bacteria occur in precipitation. Some strains are enriched in snow. Precipitation benefits terrestrial microbial habitats. Atmospheric residence is hazardous. Ice can participate in precipitation formation.

None of these facts alone closes the circle.

The missing quantity is feedback gain.

How much does the biological input alter precipitation relative to the counterfactual atmosphere? How much does that altered precipitation change biological abundance or dispersal? Does the increase produce enough additional ice-nucleating aerosol to matter on the next pass?

A feedback can exist mathematically and still be negligible.

This distinction is often lost when Gaia-like language enters environmental science. There is no need to invoke planetary self-regulation, intention or homeostasis. A bacterium can gain from a local transport mechanism without 'the biosphere' regulating rainfall. Forests can alter aerosols without optimizing climate. Evolution acts on reproductive success under local constraints, not on a planetary objective function.

The bioprecipitation hypothesis is most interesting when stripped of cosmic purpose.

Imagine a lineage on leaves. Wind lofts some cells. Most die or settle randomly. Ice-active variants, under certain cloud conditions, enter growing ice more readily or trigger freezing earlier. Some precipitate onto new vegetation sooner. If that pathway modestly improves expected colonization, selection can favor the trait even if the meteorological effect on total regional rainfall is tiny.

That is an evolutionary bioprecipitation mechanism without climate regulation.

Now scale upward. Suppose biologically active landscapes emit enough warm-temperature ice nuclei that mixed-phase clouds glaciate earlier. If this shifts precipitation efficiency or location, vegetation can influence its own hydrological environment through aerosol biology. That is a stronger ecological feedback.

Now scale again to climate. Changes in cloud phase affect radiation as well as precipitation. Biological aerosol sources could in principle influence cloud lifetime or albedo. This is stronger still and much harder to attribute.

The mistake is treating these scales as one claim.

They should be separate hypotheses with separate defeat conditions.

The lineage-level dispersal hypothesis fails if active and inactive cells have indistinguishable atmospheric fates under realistic conditions. The cloud-level hypothesis weakens if biological nuclei are almost always outnumbered or activated after more abundant competitors. The regional feedback weakens if cloud changes do not propagate into meaningful precipitation differences. The climate hypothesis requires effects persistent and large enough to survive aggregation across meteorological regimes.

This hierarchy is useful because negative results at one level do not erase lower levels.

A bacterium can be an excellent ice nucleus even if bioprecipitation is climatologically trivial.

Conversely, regional biological effects could arise from dead macromolecules even if no living bacterium gains a dispersal advantage.

The word bio does not guarantee the word adaptive.

There is one more complication. The atmosphere is changing. Land use alters vegetation and microbial sources. Agriculture changes canopy composition. Air pollution changes aerosol chemistry. Microplastics add new surfaces. Climate warming changes cloud phase regimes. A biological feedback measured today may differ from one operating before industrialization.

Thus even proving a mechanism does not grant a timeless coefficient.

This is not a reason to abandon the hypothesis. It is why the field deserves better measurement.

The bioprecipitation idea is one of those scientific propositions that becomes more interesting as it becomes less mystical. The question is not whether forests 'call the rain.' It is whether biological particles measurably alter the statistics of ice initiation in cloud populations, and whether that alteration changes the transport and persistence of the organisms producing them.

Those are hard questions.

They are also ordinary enough to answer.
