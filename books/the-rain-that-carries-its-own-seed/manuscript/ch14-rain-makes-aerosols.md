# Rain Makes Aerosols

The water cycle is usually drawn downward at precipitation and upward at evaporation.

The surface has another upward arrow.

When raindrops strike soil, leaves and water films, they can generate tiny droplets and particles that become airborne. Anyone who has smelled earth after rain has encountered chemistry mobilized by the impact. Aerosol physics shows that splash and bubble processes can also eject material back into the atmosphere.

In 2025, a *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres* study reported that raindrop impact can emit ice-nucleating particles. The result matters for bioprecipitation because it supplies a physical recycling mechanism that does not require dry windy conditions.

Rain can help reload the atmosphere with particles capable of influencing later freezing.

Again, a loop appears.

And again, the loop is not automatically important.

The emitted particles may be mineral, biological or mixed. Their concentrations and vertical transport determine whether they ever reach cloud base. Boundary-layer turbulence, surface wetness, canopy structure and subsequent precipitation all control fate. A particle lofted one meter is not yet a cloud nucleus.

But the mechanism changes how we think about the timing of biological aerosol emission.

A rain event can simultaneously deposit microbes and mechanically generate new aerosols from the wetted surface. The hours after precipitation may therefore contain a distinct aerosol population created by the storm itself.

This resembles the smell of petrichor but with cloud microphysics attached.

The ecological possibilities are interesting. Suppose rain deposits *P. syringae* onto vegetation. Cells colonize leaves. Later drops strike those surfaces and eject bacteria or protein-bearing fragments. Convection lifts some material into developing clouds. The causal chain now contains a direct precipitation-to-emission step.

To call this a positive feedback requires numbers.

How many active particles are emitted per square meter per millimeter of rain? What fraction is biological? How rapidly does activity decay? How high are they transported? How often does a second cloud system arrive before removal?

The atmosphere forgets quickly.

Aerosol lifetimes, boundary-layer mixing and precipitation scavenging can break loops as easily as they create them.

The 2025 result is especially useful because it suggests experiments at ecosystem scale. One can collect aerosols above controlled surfaces during simulated rainfall, measure ice-nucleating spectra, sequence biological material and compare sterile, natural and manipulated microbial communities. One can distinguish splash from bubble bursting and dry aerosolization. One can examine leaves, litter, soil and microplastic-contaminated surfaces separately.

Then one can ask whether active particles survive a realistic upward journey.

This is the kind of work that could move bioprecipitation from a diagram to a budget.

The chapter also exposes a semantic trap. If precipitation emits ice nuclei that later participate in clouds, is the atmosphere causing biology to affect atmosphere, or biology causing atmosphere to recycle biology?

Causal ownership becomes meaningless in a coupled system.

Better to track fluxes.

Earth science improves when verbs such as causes and controls are replaced, where possible, by rates and sensitivities. The rain emits X active particles. Y percent remain active after Z hours. A fraction reaches cloud level. Under a given temperature distribution, they increase ice number by some amount. The cloud's precipitation response follows from a model constrained by observations.

That chain can be wrong at identifiable places.

A story about rain seeding itself cannot.

The most interesting possibility is that the emitted INPs are not the same objects that arrived. Rain may stimulate biological growth, fragment plant material, mobilize microbial products or coat anthropogenic particles. The next aerosol generation could have different ice activity from the deposited one.

Cycles transform their cargo.

This becomes important in the Anthropocene, where surfaces contain polymers, combustion particles, fertilizers and altered microbial communities. A feedback that evolved on vegetation may now operate through composite particles that did not exist historically.

The rain that hits a modern city or farm does not encounter a preindustrial surface.

It encounters chemistry we have added.
