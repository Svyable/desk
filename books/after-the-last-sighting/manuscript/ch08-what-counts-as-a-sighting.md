# What Counts as a Sighting

A sighting is not a unit.

This causes trouble because databases like units. A spreadsheet wants a row: date, location, witness, description. Once entered, a hunter's fleeting glimpse through rain and a museum specimen can both become one record. Count the rows and it looks as though the animal was seen twice.

Nobody doing competent extinction analysis actually believes those records are equivalent, but popular hidden-animal arguments often behave as though volume can substitute for quality. Five hundred reports sound more impressive than five. The crucial questions are how the reports were generated, what fraction are independent, and what kind of error can produce them.

The ivory-billed woodpecker literature makes this problem explicit. Solow and colleagues' 2012 analysis distinguished records classified as certain from uncertain sightings rather than simply counting everything. A later paper by Lee and coauthors modeled certain and uncertain reports as processes with different rates, including false sightings after extinction. ([Solow et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21967229/); [Lee et al.](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2664.12144).) The statistical details matter, but the conceptual move is more important: evidence quality has to enter the model.

For this book, I use four working classes.

Class one is evidence that directly establishes a relevant fact with strong provenance: a specimen deposited in an accessible collection; a diagnostic DNA sequence from material with documented custody and appropriate contamination controls; a clear photograph or video whose raw file, time, location, and morphology can be independently assessed; a peer-reviewed analysis whose data support the stated claim; an official document establishing that an agency conducted a search or reached a conclusion. Class one does not mean every interpretation of the item is true. A government report is class-one evidence that the government wrote the report, not that every biological claim inside it is correct.

Class two is credible but incomplete evidence. A trained biologist reports a brief view of a plausible animal. A camera captures a shape with some diagnostic features but insufficient resolution. A trackway has appropriate dimensions but no recoverable DNA. A sound resembles a target call but overlaps known species. A local hunter provides detailed habitat information that predicts later findings. This evidence can substantially justify search. It usually cannot carry species existence alone.

Class three is disputed allegation. The evidence has unresolved provenance, strong ordinary alternatives, heavy processing, missing originals, contradictory metadata, or dependence on testimony that cannot be checked. Again, disputed does not mean false. It means the item should not be silently promoted because it is exciting.

Class four is speculative synthesis. This is where hypotheses live: perhaps a reported animal is an undescribed bear population, a known species outside range, a surviving lineage, or a cultural composite. Speculation is allowed to be adventurous if its evidentiary label remains attached.

The classes are not moral ranks. A sincere witness can produce class-two evidence. A liar can accidentally produce a class-one photograph if the photograph really shows the target animal. Institutions can generate class-three claims. The classification attaches to the support for a proposition, not the virtue of the source.

Independence then becomes the next problem.

Ten witnesses standing together are not ten independent sightings. Ten online reports after a viral video may share a perceptual template. Three camera angles synchronized to one event can be more independent in some respects than three human witnesses because each sensor has separate optics, but they still share the same location and target. Two DNA laboratories splitting the same contaminated extract do not create independent genetic confirmation. Multiple analyses of the same low-resolution image can create the appearance of replication while preserving the original ambiguity.

This is why evidence matrices should record lineage.

For each claim, ask: what physical event generated the evidence? What transformations occurred between event and analyst? Who handled the file or sample? Which observations share a source? Which failure modes are common? What alternative explanation could produce all channels at once?

A famous anomalous case often looks stronger after being atomized into "multiple lines of evidence." The reverse can be true. If witness testimony led researchers to place a recorder at a site, and the researchers then interpreted an ambiguous sound through the witness expectation, the two lines are causally linked. If a third line is a news report quoting both, the archive has three citations and one underlying anomaly.

Real species can also produce correlated evidence, of course. A saola walking past a camera may leave tracks and DNA in the same place. Correlation does not invalidate evidence. It changes how much new information each channel adds.

There is also negative evidence, which is routinely classified badly. "No animal was seen" is not a single class either. A fifteen-minute walk through thousands of square kilometers is nearly worthless as absence evidence. Ten thousand calibrated camera-nights across high-suitability habitat, with high detection rates for comparable species and no target detections, can be substantial. A laboratory that fails to amplify target DNA without a positive control tells us little. A validated assay that detects one target molecule in spiked controls but repeatedly returns negative field samples tells us more.

The amount of information in a null result depends on expected detection if the animal were present.

Witness credibility also needs to be decomposed. Expertise can improve identification, but experts have priors and expectations too. Military pilots are excellent observers of aircraft behavior in domains they know; that does not make them ornithologists at dusk. Hunters may be excellent at recognizing local mammals but poor at estimating distance in a startling encounter. A child's report may contain a diagnostic observation an adult interviewer later edits out. A person's occupation is not a scalar credibility score that can be multiplied by mystery.

Good interview practice can preserve more of the raw observation. Ask the witness to describe before naming. Record exact words. Separate perceived size from distance estimate. Ask what was visible and what is inferred. Avoid showing candidate images until an unaided description is complete. Record weather, light, duration, angle, motion, and familiarity with confusing species. Capture uncertainty instead of forcing confidence.

The final discipline is temporal. Evidence quality can degrade or improve after the event. Memory drifts. Files get recompressed. Metadata disappear. But new comparative data can also clarify old records. A museum skin misidentified in 1920 can become diagnostic after genetics improves. An unknown call can be compared with a newly built acoustic library. Old photographs can be rescanned from negatives.

A sighting is therefore not a point. It is an object with a history.

The hidden-animal field will become more scientific when it stops asking how many sightings exist and starts asking what kind of machine could have produced the sighting record we have.
