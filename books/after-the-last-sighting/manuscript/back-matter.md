# Back Matter

This book is about a narrow epistemic problem with surprisingly broad consequences: how to reason about animals that might exist when direct observation is sparse, disputed, or absent.

The useful distinction is between biological hiddenness and evidentiary insulation. Real elusive species have reasons they are hard to detect: low density, inaccessible habitat, nocturnality, deep water, arboreality, small body size, limited range, or weak interaction with common sensors. Those reasons can be modeled. An insulated claim is different. It changes behavior after every failed search so that no result can lower belief.

The chronology below is not exhaustive. It marks the events and methods that carry the manuscript's argument.

1936 — The last captive thylacine dies in Hobart, Tasmania. Post-1936 survival reports continue without universally accepted physical confirmation.

1938 — A living coelacanth is recovered off South Africa, transforming a lineage known from fossils into a living biological population.

1944 — The last universally accepted U.S. observation of an ivory-billed woodpecker occurs in Louisiana's Singer Tract.

1961 — The specimen later named Attenborough's long-beaked echidna is collected in the Cyclops Mountains; the species then disappears from scientific record for decades.

1976 — A previously unknown large shark becomes entangled with a U.S. Navy research vessel's sea anchor off Hawaii and is later described as the megamouth shark.

1992 — Vietnamese and WWF surveyors encounter unusual bovid skulls and horns in Vu Quang; the saola enters formal zoology.

1993 — Andrew Solow publishes "Inferring Extinction from Sighting Data" in Ecology, helping formalize how sighting gaps can inform extinction inference.

2004 — Kubodera and Mori photograph a living giant squid in deep water near the Ogasawara Islands.

2005 — Cornell-led researchers and U.S. officials announce evidence interpreted as rediscovery of the ivory-billed woodpecker in Arkansas; the identification becomes the center of a long scientific dispute.

2012 — Solow and colleagues publish a Bayesian analysis incorporating uncertain ivory-bill sightings and report substantial support for extinction under their model.

2013 — Smithsonian researchers describe the olinguito after integrating museum specimens, DNA, historic records, and field observations; the animal had been misidentified in collections and zoos.

2014 — Sykes and colleagues publish genetic testing of hair samples attributed to anomalous primates; most resolve to known mammals, while surprising Himalayan bear results draw follow-up criticism and study.

2019 — A University of Otago-led environmental-DNA survey of Loch Ness reports no evidence for giant reptile hypotheses and abundant eel DNA.

2021 — The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposes delisting the ivory-billed woodpecker and twenty-two other species due to extinction; the ivory-bill decision is later held for additional review.

2023 — Twenty-one species from the 2021 U.S. proposal are delisted as extinct, while the ivory-bill remains under review. In the same year, an Oxford-led expedition photographs Attenborough's long-beaked echidna after more than sixty years without a scientific record.

2025–2026 — De-extinction projects and debates make species identity itself a new evidentiary problem: reconstructed organisms, surviving lineages, and reintroduced proxies can no longer be treated as interchangeable meanings of "the animal is back."
