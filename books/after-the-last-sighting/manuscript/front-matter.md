# After the Last Sighting

There is an awkward interval between the last animal and the declaration that there are no animals left.

The animal may already be gone. It may be alive in a drainage nobody sampled, a ridge nobody climbed, a cave whose entrance is hidden by nettles, a patch of forest that was logged on three sides but not the fourth. The last museum skin may have been misidentified. The last photograph may show the wrong species. The witness may be right about what she saw and wrong about what it was. A government may spend money searching for an animal that does not exist because extinction is politically difficult to pronounce. A conservation group may keep hope alive because hope protects habitat. A cryptid hunter may keep the same claim alive because every failed expedition can be converted into proof of cleverness.

Those are not the same epistemic situation, although they can look remarkably similar from the road.

This book is about animals at the boundary of evidence: species rediscovered after long absences, species declared extinct only after decades of uncertainty, large animals unknown to formal science until surprisingly recently, and famous alleged animals that have never crossed the evidentiary threshold from story into zoology. The point is not to turn conservation biology into cryptozoology or cryptozoology into conservation biology. The useful collision is methodological. Conservation science has spent the last several decades learning how to treat non-detection as information without pretending it is omniscience. Occupancy models explicitly separate presence from detection. Extinction-inference methods ask what a run of sightings and silences can actually support. Environmental DNA can detect organisms without seeing them, while bringing its own contamination and false-inference problems. Camera traps can watch continuously for months and still miss a rare animal. Museum genomics can reveal that a species was sitting in a drawer for a century under the wrong name.

The book's central proposition is simple enough to be dangerous: a hidden-animal claim becomes scientifically useful when the search states in advance what amount of failure to detect would count against the claim.

That does not mean a single null survey proves extinction. It means absence has to be earned. If the claimed animal is a breeding population of large terrestrial mammals in a bounded landscape, then camera density, home-range estimates, trackability, food requirements, eDNA persistence, carcass production, roadkill exposure, acoustic behavior, and survey duration can be converted into a detection burden. If the claim concerns a deep-sea animal that rarely approaches sampled depths, the burden is different. If the claim concerns a migratory bird easily confused with a common relative, witness reports need different treatment again.

Four evidence classes are kept separate throughout. Class one is verified, documented evidence: specimens, independently interpretable photographs, genetic sequences with adequate custody, official search records, peer-reviewed analyses, and well-provenanced archival material. Class two is credible but incomplete evidence: qualified witness reports, ambiguous imagery, indirect sign, or research claims that are serious enough to investigate but do not independently establish the animal. Class three is disputed allegation: sightings or extraordinary interpretations that lack independent confirmation, have unresolved provenance, or compete strongly with ordinary explanations. Class four is speculative synthesis: the hypotheses this book constructs about search design, evidentiary thresholds, and what a modern science of hidden animals could look like. Class four is not smuggled upward by typography.

The phrase "cryptid" is used sparingly because it carries too much furniture. Some alleged animals are ordinary species seen outside accepted range. Some are misidentifications. Some are folklore. Some are hoaxes. Some may represent undescribed taxa. Some once-unconfirmed animals did become specimens, and when they did, zoology did not need a special metaphysics for them. The saola became a bovid. The olinguito became a procyonid. The coelacanth became a living population rather than a fossil endpoint. The work changed categories because the evidence changed.

That is the standard here. Not ridicule. Not belief. Category change earned by evidence.
