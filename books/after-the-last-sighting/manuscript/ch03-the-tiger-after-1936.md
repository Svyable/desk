# The Tiger After 1936

The last captive thylacine died in Hobart in September 1936. The date has become so clean in cultural memory that it obscures what followed: decades in which people continued to report seeing striped, dog-shaped animals in Tasmania and occasionally on the Australian mainland, while organized searches repeatedly failed to produce a specimen, a body, a clear photograph, or genetic evidence from a living population.

The thylacine is the cryptid problem with the mythology removed. It was real. It was photographed and filmed. Its anatomy is known. Its habitat and prey were not imaginary. Its extinction was historically recent, driven through a period of European settlement in which bounties, habitat change, disease hypotheses, persecution, and small-population dynamics overlapped. If someone had seen a surviving thylacine in 1940, that would not have required rewriting mammalian evolution. Even a sighting in 1960 would have meant a remnant population had outlasted formal records by a few decades. A sighting now would be much harder to reconcile with the accumulating silence, but it would still concern an ordinary marsupial carnivore, not a supernatural beast.

That makes the case unusually useful. We can ask how evidence should decay without arguing about whether the proposed animal could ever have existed.

Thylacine reports have a familiar structure. A driver sees a long stiff tail crossing a road. A bushwalker sees stripes on the rear half of an animal. Someone captures a few seconds of distant video. A photograph shows an animal whose proportions become diagnostic only after enough enlargement. Each report can be sincere. The landscape contains dogs, fox-like forms, wallabies seen oddly, shadows, motion blur, and the strange geometry of brief encounters. The problem is not that people lie. It is that perception is a lossy instrument and rare-animal searches select for the moments when it fails interestingly.

When sightings are publicized, the process changes. People learn the canonical thylacine features: stiff tail, stripes, sloping back, unusual gait. That can improve recognition if the animal survives. It can also shape ambiguous perception. The sighting record is no longer a sequence of independent measurements drawn from a fixed process. It is a cultural system in which reports alter future reports.

This is one reason extinction inference is difficult. Andrew Solow's 1993 Ecology paper, "Inferring Extinction from Sighting Data," helped formalize the obvious intuition that a widening gap after a last sighting can contain information. ([Solow 1993](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2307/1940821).) Later work has added declining sighting rates, uncertain records, false sightings, and Bayesian priors. A 2014 Journal of Applied Ecology paper by T. E. Lee and colleagues explicitly modeled records containing both certain and uncertain sightings and emphasized that uncertain reports can continue after extinction because false-positive reports do not stop when the species does. ([Lee et al. 2014](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2664.12144).)

That last sentence should be engraved above every cryptid forum.

After extinction, true sightings go to zero. False sightings do not.

If an animal is famous, false sightings may increase after extinction. The species becomes a search template. The thylacine is especially vulnerable because its image is iconic and the claim carries emotional reward. A witness who sees an odd canid in Tasmania has a culturally available interpretation that a witness in Ohio does not. The archive can therefore exhibit a long tail of reports that looks superficially like biological persistence.

The opposite error is also real. Dismissing all late sightings because false reports exist can erase genuine survival. Extinctions do not happen at the date of the last specimen. The last specimen is the last specimen we happened to collect. Roberts and Solow's famous 2003 Nature analysis of dodo records estimated that the species may have persisted until around 1690, almost three decades beyond the last confirmed 1662 sighting. ([Roberts & Solow 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14628039/).) The exact estimate depends on assumptions, but the conceptual lesson is robust: last observation and extinction are different events.

So where should the thylacine sit?

The first evidence class contains the pre-extinction specimens, films, skins, government records, bounty history, and the known death of the Hobart animal. It also contains documented search efforts that failed to find surviving animals. Those failures are facts even though the inference drawn from them can differ.

The second class contains credible but incomplete post-1936 reports, particularly those by observers with relevant experience and records made before a story became widely contaminated by publicity. They justify targeted search if habitat and chronology fit. They do not become specimens because the witness is impressive.

The third class contains the enormous field of disputed photographs, videos, mainland reports, and claims whose provenance or morphology does not survive independent scrutiny. Some may be true. "Disputed" is not a synonym for false. It is a statement about evidentiary state.

The fourth class contains the synthesis: if a breeding population survived far beyond 1936, what traces should it have generated? Here the animal's ordinary biology becomes our friend. A population must contain multiple individuals, not one immortal survivor. Individuals eat, defecate, breed, die, move, cross tracks, leave hair, encounter roads, and occupy territories. Population persistence for ninety years implies generations. A breeding population large enough to avoid immediate demographic collapse cannot be arbitrarily small forever.

This lets search design leave the realm of adventure.

A serious modern thylacine program would divide plausible habitat into cells and preregister survey intensity. Cameras would be placed using explicit movement and habitat models rather than only where sightings cluster. Hair snares and scat collection would use contamination controls. Environmental DNA sampling would target watercourses with hydrological models that estimate transport. Putative tracks would be photographed with scale and cast where possible. Acoustic sensors might be useful only if a vocalization model could distinguish target sounds from dogs and other fauna. Local witnesses would be interviewed before being shown canonical images, and reports would be scored by predeclared criteria. All raw negative data would be published alongside positives.

Most important, the search would declare what a null result changes.

For example: if a three-year camera network covers a specified fraction of modeled high-suitability habitat, with detection probability calibrated on Tasmanian devils and other mammals of comparable movement scale, how much does the probability of an extant breeding population fall after zero diagnostic images? If eDNA samples repeatedly fail under validated sensitivity, how much additional weight should that add? If claimed sightings remain concentrated along roads while cameras in adjacent habitat remain empty, does that pattern fit animal movement or human observation?

A cryptid culture dislikes these questions because they risk ending the story. Conservation culture should like them because search resources are finite and extinction declarations have consequences.

The thylacine also exposes a newer complication: de-extinction. In the 2020s, genome sequencing and gene-editing companies began presenting the thylacine not only as a lost animal to search for but as a candidate for biological reconstruction. A 2026 review involving University of Melbourne and Colossal-affiliated researchers described extinct-genome assembly, editing systems, stem-cell approaches, and reproductive technologies as a developing conservation toolkit. ([Hutchison et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1262/jrd.2026-002).) The capabilities are real research domains; the leap from reconstructed traits to restored species remains contested.

Soon we may have to distinguish three animals: the historic thylacine, any undiscovered surviving thylacine, and an engineered organism intended to approximate the extinct form. That is not wordplay. The first is documented. The second is a survival hypothesis. The third is a biotechnology project.

The tiger after 1936 has therefore become more than an extinction story. It is a test of whether we can keep longing from altering categories. If a thylacine survives, find it. If it is extinct, admit what the search says. If an engineered proxy is eventually born, describe exactly what was built. Hope is allowed. Taxonomy still needs receipts.
