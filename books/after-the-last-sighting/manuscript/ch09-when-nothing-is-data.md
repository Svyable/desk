# When Nothing Is Data

There is a specific kind of bad faith available to anyone studying elusive phenomena: design a search that cannot falsify the claim, then describe every outcome as interesting.

If the animal is photographed, the animal exists. If it is not photographed, the animal is unusually intelligent, rare, migratory, subterranean, nocturnal, sensor-shy, or just outside the frame. If DNA is found, confirmation. If DNA is not found, the assay was inadequate. If witnesses cluster in one area, hotspot. If they do not, wide range. If old reports stop, secrecy or population decline. If they continue, persistence.

No observation is allowed to reduce belief.

That is not research. It is narrative insulation.

Ecologists cannot afford this luxury because they routinely need to infer presence from imperfect surveys. Occupancy modeling emerged in part to separate two probabilities that casual observation confuses: the probability a species occupies a site and the probability the survey detects it given that it is there. Dennis MacKenzie and collaborators helped establish the modern framework in the early 2000s; subsequent USGS summaries emphasize that detection–nondetection data are useful precisely because methods model imperfect detection rather than assuming every occupied site will produce a sighting. ([USGS overview](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/inference-occupancy-and-occupancy-dynamics).)

The mathematics can be elaborate. The intuition is not.

Suppose a camera has a 20 percent chance of detecting a target during one independent sampling interval if the target is present. One miss means little. Ten truly independent misses are more informative. If the detection probability really remained 0.2, the chance of missing all ten while the target is present would be 0.8 to the tenth power, about 10.7 percent. Twenty misses would push it to about 1.15 percent. The calculation is simplistic because real samples are not perfectly independent and detection probability varies, but it shows why "absence of evidence" is not a permanent shield.

Expected evidence accumulates.

This principle can be used without pretending we know exact numbers. A search can define ranges. If a population of twenty large mammals occupies a bounded reserve and comparable mammals trigger cameras every few hundred camera-days, then years of dense camera coverage should matter. If the target is a tiny cave salamander with unknown microhabitat and the survey samples three accessible pools, non-detection matters much less.

The difficulty is estimating detection probability without having the animal.

Proxy species can help. Body size, movement rate, habitat use, density, and sensor interaction can be bracketed using known animals. A hypothetical unknown primate might be compared with bears, humans, deer, and known great apes across different traces: camera triggers, hair, scat, tracks, road crossings, acoustic activity. No proxy is perfect, so the model should show sensitivity across a wide range rather than hide uncertainty in one authoritative number.

Search design should also distinguish local absence from global extinction. A river eDNA survey can say something about the sampled watershed, not the entire continent. A camera grid can constrain the landscape it actually covers. One of the pathologies of both skeptical and credulous rhetoric is geographic inflation. A failed search in one swamp becomes "the bird is extinct everywhere"; a report in one swamp becomes "the species survives."

The spatial claim should match the spatial data.

Extinction-inference models face an analogous problem in time. Andrew Solow's 1993 work formalized how the timing of sightings can inform extinction inference. Later reviews, including a 2015 Methods in Ecology and Evolution paper by Boakes and colleagues, compared multiple approaches and their assumptions. ([Boakes et al. 2015](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/2041-210X.12365).) The models differ, but they share a conceptual commitment that hiddenness is not free. As the gap after the last reliable observation grows, belief in persistence should usually change unless there is a reason detection conditions also changed.

This is where post-extinction false sightings become dangerous. If uncertain sightings keep arriving at a roughly constant rate after a species is gone, an analyst who treats every report as true can push inferred extinction indefinitely forward. Lee and colleagues' 2014 work explicitly modeled false sighting rates for this reason. A famous species can generate a cultural background rate of observations independent of biological presence.

The proper response is not to discard witnesses. It is to estimate the background.

How often do observers report the target in places where it is known to be absent? How often do they misidentify a common analog under controlled conditions? How many reports spike after media coverage? Can fake target images be introduced into a blinded classification task to estimate analyst error? Can acoustic detectors be tested against known non-target sounds before field deployment?

Anomalous research needs control groups more than it needs better adjectives.

Imagine a hidden-animal observatory designed with the same seriousness as a medical diagnostic study. Before the search begins, investigators publish the target morphology, habitat model, survey grid, camera settings, eDNA primers, contamination procedures, witness-interview script, positive controls, negative controls, and statistical plan. They specify what counts as diagnostic. They also specify a "defeat condition": a result after which the current population hypothesis will be downgraded.

The defeat condition is not "we will never look again." Science does not require theatrical finality. It might say: if three seasons of calibrated camera coverage across 70 percent of modeled high-use corridors produce zero diagnostic detections while comparable large mammals are detected at expected rates, the hypothesis of a resident population above N individuals falls below a predeclared probability threshold. A different hypothesis—transient animals, smaller population, different habitat—would need new evidence rather than inheriting the old claim automatically.

This is how null results become cumulative rather than disposable.

The system also needs a way to recover from being wrong. Attenborough's echidna appeared after many blank images. A good model would not have declared metaphysical impossibility after the first week. It would have updated. When the last memory card produced photographs, the posterior moved violently in the other direction because the observation was highly diagnostic.

That is exactly what we want: a search architecture capable of surprise.

Falsifiability is sometimes caricatured as hostility to wonder. In hidden-species work it is the opposite. A claim that can lose can also win. If no conceivable null result counts, then a positive result merely joins a story that was already guaranteed to continue. If the search could have killed the hypothesis and instead produces a specimen, the discovery has teeth.

Nothing becomes data only after we decide what we expected to see.
