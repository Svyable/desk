# The Last-Sighting Protocol

A hidden animal should be given a fair chance to exist.

That means the search has to be designed so it can fail.

The protocol I would use is not complicated enough to deserve a brand name. It is a sequence of commitments that prevents the mystery from changing shape every time the evidence disappoints us.

First, define the animal before deployment. Not the mythology—the biological claim. Estimated body size. locomotion. diet. likely habitat. breeding population or transient individual. geographic range. terrestrial, arboreal, aquatic, subterranean. expected vocalization. known analogs. If proponents disagree, preserve multiple hypotheses rather than blending them into a creature that can do everything.

Second, define the claim's evidence classes. A diagnostic specimen or independently replicated genomic signal may be class one. A clear multi-frame image with raw metadata can approach class one if morphology is genuinely diagnostic. Expert testimony, ambiguous imagery, tracks without DNA, and unusual calls are class two. Unprovenanceable files, hearsay, and evidence with strong unresolved ordinary alternatives are class three. Proposed mechanisms and identity stories remain class four.

Third, build a confounder library before looking at target data. Photograph and record known species under the same bad conditions expected in the field. Obtain bear hair, deer tracks, pileated woodpecker flight video, eel wakes, depending on the case. Test analysts blind. Measure how often the known world is called anomalous.

Fourth, map search effort. A camera-nights total without geography is weak. We need habitat cells, deployment dates, sensor height, field of view, trigger settings, downtime, bait, weather, and maintenance. eDNA samples need volume, location, hydrology, extraction method, controls, and assay validation. Acoustic deployments need microphone response, noise environment, classifier performance, and false-alarm rates.

Fifth, preserve witness reports prospectively. The interviewer records unaided descriptions before showing images or naming candidate species. Distance and size estimates are separated. Exact words are retained. Reports receive timestamps and locations. Publicity exposure is recorded because it affects the background rate. Witness identity can remain confidential while metadata needed for analysis are preserved.

Sixth, establish custody before the exciting sample arrives. Field kits contain sterile tubes, gloves, scales, rulers, sample labels, tamper-evident seals, and instructions. A suspicious hair or tissue is split where possible between independent repositories. Raw image files are hashed at ingestion. Exact locations of threatened species can be escrowed rather than publicly dumped.

Seventh, preregister positive criteria. What would actually count as diagnostic? A single blurry thermal figure should not become decisive after the expedition merely because nothing better appeared. If the standard is DNA across two independent laboratories plus repeat camera detection, write that down first.

Eighth, preregister negative updates. This is the difficult part. The team states what a null season means for each population hypothesis. Detection probability will be uncertain, so use ranges. If a resident population of at least fifty large mammals would have a 90–99 percent chance of producing one of the monitored traces under the calibrated system, zero detections should sharply reduce that hypothesis. A smaller or different-habitat model may survive, but it starts as a new model with a lower prior, not as an automatic escape hatch.

Ninth, publish misses. Every blank camera grid, failed PCR run with valid controls, and unconfirmed witness follow-up belongs in the record. The denominator is part of the evidence.

Tenth, design the search to be scientifically useful if the animal is absent. Survey known biodiversity. improve occupancy maps. collect invasive-species data. document local ecological knowledge with consent. build acoustic libraries. validate eDNA assays. The project should not need the mystery to survive in order for the science to have value.

Now imagine applying this to three different claims.

For the ivory-billed woodpecker, the target is a historically documented species with a known confusing relative. Cameras and autonomous acoustic sensors can be placed in high-suitability bottomland forest. The confounder library is rich because pileated woodpeckers can be recorded extensively. Historic ivory-bill audio and morphology exist. The search aims to establish contemporary persistence, not species identity from scratch.

For thylacine, the target is also historically documented, but the claim concerns a breeding mammal population after nine decades without universally accepted evidence. Camera networks, roadkill systems, scat genetics, and habitat models should produce quantitative upper bounds on plausible population size. Any engineered or captive-derived future thylacine proxy would require genetic exclusion before a wild detection could be interpreted as historic persistence.

For Bigfoot, the challenge is greater because no authenticated biological reference specimen exists. There is no target genome. eDNA therefore cannot simply search a known Bigfoot sequence. The strongest molecular route would be repeated unassigned primate-like sequences with independent custody and phylogenetic analysis, followed by targeted physical search. The population model must account for calories, breeding, deaths, distribution, and sensor exposure. A continent-wide sighting map should not be treated as one population hypothesis unless ecology supports it.

The protocol would be even stricter for Loch Ness because the habitat is bounded. Water sampling, sonar, biomass estimates, prey ecology, and air-breathing requirements can constrain large-animal hypotheses differently from an open ocean claim. The 2019 eDNA survey already belongs in the evidence base. A future hypothesis has to update from it, not restart from zero.

There is no universal number of blank cameras after which an animal becomes extinct. The protocol is not a machine for manufacturing certainty. It is a way to stop ignorance from being infinitely reusable.

The most important output may be a probability distribution rather than a discovery. "No breeding population larger than roughly X is supported in the surveyed habitat under these detection assumptions" can be a major scientific result. It tells future searchers where not to spend another decade. It can also expose the remaining blind spots where surprise is still plausible.

And surprise should remain plausible.

The coelacanth existed while textbooks lacked it. The megamouth shark existed before a Navy anchor found it. The olinguito lived in zoos under the wrong label. The saola left horns in houses. Attenborough's echidna waited until the last memory card.

Those stories do not teach us to believe every animal story. They teach us to make our disbelief vulnerable to evidence.

That is the bargain.

A witness says an animal is there. A scientist says show me where the claim can fail. They build the search together. Cameras go up. samples go cold. code is preregistered. blank seasons count. strange results get harder tests instead of louder adjectives. The animal is permitted to become ordinary if it is real.

After the last sighting, we do not know exactly when an animal becomes absent.

We can know when our own uncertainty has stopped being disciplined.

The difference is enough to build a science.
