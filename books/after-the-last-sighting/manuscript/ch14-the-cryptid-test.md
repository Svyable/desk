# The Cryptid Test

At some point the book has to say Bigfoot.

Not because North America's alleged large upright primate is the most scientifically promising hidden animal. It is useful because the claim has accumulated almost every kind of evidence that can remain ambiguous: eyewitness testimony, footprints, hair, sound recordings, photographs, videos, thermal images, anecdotal ecology, Indigenous stories translated into the category, and an enormous amateur search culture. What it has not accumulated is an authenticated body, diagnostic recent fossil, independently verified genome, or unambiguous repeatable population record.

The question is how to say that without cheating.

Ridicule is not a method. "Everyone knows Bigfoot is fake" explains nothing. Hoaxes exist, some famous footprint evidence has been entangled with hoax claims, and many photographs are weak, but the existence of fraud does not logically explain every report. A serious analysis needs to ask what a North American great ape hypothesis predicts.

Begin with population biology.

A species persisting across centuries cannot be one animal. A viable population of large primates requires breeding individuals distributed in space. Population size cannot be chosen arbitrarily small because demographic and genetic stochasticity become severe. The animals would need substantial calories. They would defecate, shed hair, leave tracks, sleep somewhere, produce juveniles, die, and interact with other species. If their range spans the Pacific Northwest or much of the continent as sighting maps sometimes imply, the number of expected interfaces with roads, hunters, cameras, forestry, fires, search-and-rescue operations, and wildlife surveys increases enormously.

None of this makes persistence impossible. It creates a footprint budget.

The hair record is instructive. In 2014, Bryan Sykes and colleagues published a systematic mitochondrial-DNA survey of thirty hair samples attributed to anomalous primates including yeti, almasty, Bigfoot, and sasquatch. Most resolved to known extant mammals. Two Himalayan samples produced a surprising match in the assayed fragment to a Pleistocene polar-bear sequence, an interpretation later challenged in the literature on sequence grounds; the original authors themselves described the result cautiously and subsequent genetic work has connected alleged yeti material strongly to known Himalayan bears. ([Sykes et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4100498/); [Edwards & Barnett 2015 critique](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25520353/).)

The important part for Bigfoot is less exotic. Samples submitted as physical traces of an anomalous primate yielded known animals rather than a new primate lineage.

That does not prove Bigfoot absent. It changes how we should treat future hair claims. A new sample needs strong custody and independent replication. If it yields ordinary bear DNA, the result should stay negative rather than being explained away as the wrong hair from the right encounter. If it yields an unusual primate sequence, multiple loci and independent laboratories should follow before publicity.

Loch Ness offers a different control. In 2019 the University of Otago eDNA team reported broad sampling of the loch without evidence for a giant reptile lineage and with abundant eel DNA. The popular headline became "Nessie might be a giant eel," which was more definite than the science. The useful conclusion is that a large modern environmental survey found no molecular support for several famous zoological hypotheses. ([University of Otago](https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/newsroom/otago-scientist-reveals-loch-ness-monster-could-be-a-giant-eel).)

Again, the mystery does not evaporate. It gets narrower.

This is the cryptid test: does new evidence force the hypothesis to occupy less space, or does the hypothesis expand to swallow every result?

If eDNA shows no reptile, a serious plesiosaur hypothesis weakens. If the response is that the monster sheds no DNA, lives mostly in hidden caves, enters from the sea through unknown tunnels, and appears only when cameras fail, the hypothesis has been immunized. It can still be told. It is no longer doing much scientific work.

Bigfoot has developed similar escape routes. The animal is said to bury its dead, avoid cameras, understand human technology, move silently, inhabit cave systems, migrate vast distances, or possess unusual sensory abilities. Some of those traits are biologically possible in isolation. Together they are often introduced after a failed detection, which makes them ad hoc.

A testable model should choose behavior before the next search.

Suppose proponents specify that a resident population uses a particular watershed, travels riparian corridors, is roughly human-sized or larger, eats a mixed omnivorous diet, and has no supernatural abilities. We can estimate camera placement, eDNA sampling, hair snares, acoustic monitoring, and expected tracks. Witness reports can be collected prospectively. The search can run for multiple seasons. At the beginning, proponents and skeptics agree on what counts as a strong positive and what degree of non-detection lowers the population estimate.

This would be enormously more interesting than another expedition built for television.

The same framework can be applied to less famous claims: orang pendek in Sumatra, alleged surviving thylacines, unknown canids, giant snakes, lake animals, relict hominins. The prior differs by habitat and biology. Sumatra contains dense tropical forest and known primates; North American temperate forests contain extraordinary recreational and sensor coverage. A small nocturnal carnivore in New Guinea is not equivalent to a breeding giant ape in Oregon. "Cryptid" conceals the ecological difference.

There is also a positive criterion. Real hidden animals should eventually generate evidence that becomes boring.

The saola has diagnostic skulls and genetic identity. The olinguito has museum series. the megamouth has type material. the coelacanth has populations. the giant squid has tissue and live observations. Attenborough's echidna has camera-trap images in a known range. None relies on one sacred artifact.

A cryptid that crosses into zoology should lose some of its charisma. That is a feature.

I am not interested in proving that people should stop looking. Search is cheap enough in many cases, and the same cameras, samples, and interviews can discover ordinary rare species or improve conservation. What I want is a search that can disappoint its sponsors.

The test is not whether an animal sounds ridiculous. Nature has already made a platypus, a megamouth shark, a goblin shark, a giant squid, an aye-aye, and a two-pound carnivore hidden in zoo collections. Appearance has no vote.

The test is whether the claim behaves like an animal when instruments arrive.
