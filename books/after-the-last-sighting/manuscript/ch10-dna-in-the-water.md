# DNA in the Water

Environmental DNA is almost too perfect for the hidden-animal imagination.

An organism can be detected without being captured, photographed, or even present at the exact instant of sampling. Cells, mucus, feces, scales, gametes, and other biological material enter water, soil, snow, air, or a touched surface. Genetic material is extracted. A targeted assay asks whether a sequence associated with the species is present; metabarcoding or broader sequencing approaches ask what taxa the sample contains. Suddenly the forest and the lake seem capable of remembering their inhabitants.

The danger begins exactly where the method becomes poetic.

DNA is physical material. Physical material moves.

A positive detection can come from a living animal at the site. It can also come from upstream transport, wastewater, predator feces, fish bait, food, laboratory contamination, field contamination, index hopping, sample mix-up, or a reference sequence that does not uniquely identify the taxon at the resolution claimed. A negative can occur because the animal was absent, because it shed little DNA, because water diluted the signal, because UV and heat degraded it, because inhibitors blocked amplification, because the primer did not bind well, or because the sample missed the relevant microhabitat.

This is why the 2021 USGS-associated paper "What do you mean by false positive?" is so useful. Darling, Jerde, and Sepulveda distinguished false-positive detection in a sample from false-positive inference that a taxon actually occupies a site. They also noted a base-rate problem that applies far beyond genetics: when true positives are expected to be rare, even a low error rate can produce a large proportion of apparent positives that are false. ([USGS publication record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/what-do-you-mean-false-positive).)

Cryptid research lives almost entirely in low-base-rate territory.

If you test ten thousand ordinary lake samples for a mythical reptile using an assay that has even a tiny contamination or misclassification rate, the one exciting positive may be exactly the result that needs the most suspicion. That is emotionally backward. We are trained to scrutinize boring data and celebrate anomalies. Low-prevalence search requires the reverse: the rarer the expected target, the more a positive needs replication and orthogonal confirmation.

Negative eDNA evidence can also be badly abused. A 2017 USGS evaluation of eDNA for the rare giant gartersnake found that the method failed to detect target DNA in most laboratory samples and all field samples even at sites with known populations. The authors pointed to the snake's biology and environmental conditions as likely contributors and concluded that further development was needed before the approach could reliably detect the species. ([USGS report](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/evaluation-efficacy-using-environmental-dna-edna-detect-giant-gartersnakes-thamnophis).)

That is a devastating control for anyone who wants to say "no DNA, no animal."

An assay has to earn its negative power.

Before using eDNA to search for an anomalous animal, we would want a validation ladder. Can the assay identify authenticated tissue from the target or its closest known relatives? If the target itself is unknown, what sequence is being searched for? Can the method detect a proxy species with similar shedding ecology at known sites? What water volume is required? How does temperature affect persistence? How far can DNA travel in that hydrology? What are extraction blanks, field blanks, and positive controls doing? Are replicates independent? Are the analysts blinded to which samples came from alleged hotspots?

The question becomes harder for an undescribed species because there may be no reference genome. Metabarcoding can return sequences that do not match databases cleanly, but "unknown sequence" does not mean "unknown large animal." Most biodiversity is microbial or small. Reference databases are incomplete. Sequencing error and poorly represented taxa create novelty all the time.

A useful anomalous result would therefore need morphology to catch up with molecules. Suppose repeated water samples from one drainage contain a vertebrate mitochondrial sequence that cannot be assigned to known regional species, appears in independent extractions, survives negative controls, and clusters phylogenetically near primates. That would be genuinely interesting. It still would not prove Bigfoot. It would justify intensified sampling, capture-independent search, and perhaps a paper about an unassigned vertebrate lineage. The name should arrive after the organism.

The Loch Ness eDNA project in 2018–2019 is a nice case of a famous mystery meeting a modern method without producing a miracle. A University of Otago-led team collected 250 water samples throughout the loch and generated a large sequence dataset. The university reported no evidence for a plesiosaur-like reptile in the eDNA and a great deal of eel DNA; project leader Neil Gemmell said the data could not exclude very large eels, but the study did not discover "monster DNA." ([University of Otago](https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/newsroom/first-edna-study-of-loch-ness-points-to-something-fishy).)

This result should not be exaggerated in either direction. The study did not prove that every reported Nessie sighting was an eel. It did not prove no large animal could ever have been present in the loch. It did apply a broad biological sampling method to the contemporary system and fail to find genetic evidence for the most extravagant reptile hypotheses while confirming abundant known taxa. That updates the case.

Cryptid culture has trouble with updates that are neither confirmation nor final annihilation. It prefers verdicts.

Science often produces narrower statements. The assay did not detect. The controls behaved. Under these assumptions, this lineage is unlikely above this abundance. Additional sampling would change the bound. That language feels weak until you realize it is exactly what allows evidence to accumulate.

Environmental DNA is particularly powerful when paired with other sensors. A camera event can trigger targeted water or soil sampling. A hair snag can produce nuclear DNA with stronger individual-level information. Predator scat can reveal prey taxa. Acoustic detections can identify times and places for sampling. A putative eDNA hit can be followed by independent laboratories using different loci.

The important word is paired.

No instrument should become a new oracle. Hidden animals are a chain-of-custody problem before they are a sequencing problem. If a sample attributed to a sensational animal passes through a collector's freezer, a documentary production, several mailing envelopes, and a non-blinded laboratory before yielding a strange sequence, the molecular sophistication at the end does not repair the provenance at the beginning.

DNA can be precise about the wrong thing.

A bear hair can be identified beautifully. A contaminated tube can be sequenced exquisitely. A mislabeled sample can produce a perfect genome. The highest-resolution instrument in the chain cannot increase the resolution of an event it no longer knows how to locate.

The promise of eDNA is still enormous. Rivers integrate landscapes. Snow records tracks invisibly. Cave sediment can preserve vanished fauna. Air sampling may extend ecological surveillance into another medium. The hidden-animal question is finally becoming less dependent on luck with a camera.

But the method's greatest contribution may be philosophical. It forces us to write down what connects a molecule to an animal.

That bridge is where mystery either becomes biology or falls apart.
