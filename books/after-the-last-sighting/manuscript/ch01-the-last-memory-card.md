# The Last Memory Card

For almost four weeks in 2023, more than eighty cameras in the Cyclops Mountains of Indonesian New Guinea failed to photograph the animal the expedition had come to find. Attenborough's long-beaked echidna had been recorded by science only once, in 1961. It was not a giant animal. It was nocturnal, burrowing, shy, geographically restricted, and built to look like a zoologist had assembled it while short of matching parts: spines, long snout, digging feet. Local people had continued to report it. Biologists had indirect signs. None of that was the photograph.

The photograph arrived at the end. The Oxford-led expedition reported that the final memory card, checked on the last day, contained the first camera-trap images of the species. The university's account is unusually useful because it preserves the failed search surrounding the success: eighty-plus cameras, repeated mountain ascents, four weeks, no visible animal until the end. The result is not a fairy tale about persistence. It is a lesson in detection probability. A species can be present while a competent search repeatedly produces zeroes. The zeroes are not meaningless. They tell us that this particular animal, in this particular terrain, under this particular deployment, was difficult to detect. Then one detection changed the state of knowledge. ([University of Oxford, 10 November 2023](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2023-11-10-found-last-bizarre-egg-laying-mammal-finally-rediscovered-after-60-years-1).)

This is the first problem with the phrase "absence of evidence." It is usually used as a conversational solvent. Someone presents a claim. Someone else observes that no specimen has been produced. The claimant replies that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The sentence is logically correct in the abstract and scientifically lazy in practice. The question is how much evidence we should have expected if the thing were there.

A tiger in a closet and a bacterium under Antarctic ice do not generate the same evidentiary footprint. Neither do a breeding population of eight-foot primates in a temperate forest and a handful of deep-diving beaked whales in an ocean basin. The expected evidence depends on abundance, body size, movement, habitat, reproductive rate, mortality, interaction with roads and humans, acoustic behavior, diet, scat production, shed DNA, carcass persistence, and the amount and quality of searching. Once those variables become even roughly estimable, silence gains weight.

Conservation biology had to learn this because it repeatedly faces decisions that cannot wait for metaphysical certainty. A species may be listed, delisted, protected, declared extinct, or targeted for expensive searches. Managers need to know whether a failure to detect an animal means the population is absent, rare, avoiding the survey method, or merely unlucky. Modern occupancy analysis therefore treats ecological presence and observation as separate processes. The species can occupy a site while a camera, trap, microphone, observer, or DNA sample fails to detect it. The U.S. Geological Survey's summaries of occupancy modeling emphasize exactly this problem: occurrence data are useful only when imperfect detection is modeled rather than quietly assumed away. ([USGS overview](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/inference-occupancy-and-occupancy-dynamics).)

The trick is that detection technology does not abolish uncertainty. It rearranges it.

A camera trap sees when an animal passes through the field of view and triggers the sensor. The camera may be badly placed. Vegetation may trigger it thousands of times and exhaust power. Bait may attract one species and repel another. A 2022 USGS-linked study of Allegheny woodrats found camera detection probability roughly 1.7 times that of live traps, and baited cameras outperformed unbaited cameras. That is not a universal coefficient. It is the point. Detection is a property of an animal-method-place interaction, not of the animal alone. ([Thorne et al. 2022](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/comparison-two-detection-methods-a-declining-rodent-allegheny-woodrat-virginia).)

Environmental DNA has the same structure. Water, soil, snow, or surface swabs can contain fragments of an organism without the organism being seen. This is powerful enough to feel magical, which is precisely when discipline matters. DNA can move. It can contaminate equipment. Reference databases can misassign sequences. A laboratory false positive at the sample level is not identical to a false inference that the species inhabits the sampled site. USGS researchers John Darling, Christopher Jerde, and Adam Sepulveda made this distinction explicit in a 2021 paper whose title asks the right question: "What do you mean by false positive?" ([USGS publication record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/what-do-you-mean-false-positive).)

The hidden-animal problem is therefore not solved by choosing the fanciest instrument. It is solved, or at least made honest, by designing a system in which each kind of evidence has a known way to fail.

The 2023 echidna rediscovery is a good opening because nobody needed to inflate it. The animal had a prior scientific specimen. The habitat was plausible. Local knowledge was geographically specific. The team generated indirect sign. The camera evidence was interpretable. A mammalogist confirmed the identification. The claim was not that an impossible beast had crossed categories. It was that a known species, unrecorded by scientists for six decades, still occupied a difficult landscape. The surprise lived inside ordinary biology.

That distinction matters because rediscoveries are constantly recruited into arguments they cannot carry. A coelacanth is found and someone says science was wrong about sea monsters. A large shark unknown to science becomes proof that any large unknown vertebrate could remain hidden anywhere. An echidna appears on the last memory card and failed searches lose all meaning. This is bad inference disguised as wonder.

The right lesson is narrower and more interesting. Search failure is conditional. An animal can survive long periods without formal detection when the ecology and the search regime make that plausible. But the same mathematics that keeps us humble about a missing echidna can make us increasingly skeptical about a repeatedly undetected animal that should be easier to find.

Imagine two claims. In the first, perhaps a few dozen small nocturnal mammals occupy steep tropical mountain forest with little road access. In the second, several thousand large terrestrial mammals occupy heavily recreated forest crossed by roads, hunters, cell phones, trail cameras, forestry operations, genetic surveys, and winter snow. Both can be called elusive. The word hides the difference. A serious science of hidden animals would not.

It would ask what an animal must do to remain alive. It would turn metabolism into expected prey consumption, reproduction into expected juveniles, mortality into carcasses, movement into crossings, habitat into surveyable area, and time into opportunities for detection. It would not assume all of those traces are recoverable. It would estimate how often they should be recoverable if the proposed population exists.

This book begins with a last memory card because the card contains both hope and a limit. The animal was there. Most of the cameras did not know it. Eventually one did. The challenge is to know, before opening the card, what another blank image should mean.
