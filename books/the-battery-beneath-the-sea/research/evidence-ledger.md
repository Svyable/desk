# Evidence Ledger

## Class 1 — verified / documented

- Sweetman et al. (2024) reported increasing dissolved oxygen in benthic chamber experiments in CCZ nodule fields.
- The paper reported ex situ incubations, poison treatments, nodule-only tests, and surface potential measurements up to about 0.95 V.
- The authors framed seawater electrolysis as a hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.
- Nature Geoscience added an Editor's Note on April 8, 2026 stating that aspects of the article were under concern.
- Downes et al. (2025) published a detailed peer-reviewed critique.
- Historical mining-disturbance experiments show biological impacts persisting for decades.
- NOAA's 2026 regulatory process includes active U.S. applications for polymetallic-nodule exploration and commercial recovery.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- A real positive oxygen flux may have occurred in at least some original chambers.
- Nodules have heterogeneous electrochemical potentials and chemically active manganese/iron oxide surfaces.
- Deployment disturbance could expose reactive surfaces and generate transient chemistry.
- Mining-related removal or burial of nodules can alter habitat and benthic biogeochemistry over long periods.

## Class 3 — disputed / unconfirmed

- Polymetallic nodules generate ecologically meaningful oxygen at the abyssal seafloor.
- Seawater electrolysis driven by nodule `geobatteries` explains the reported signal.
- Omitted/negative-control behavior fully explains the original observation as apparatus artefact.
- Dark oxygen materially changes the global oxygen cycle or the origin-of-life story.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis

- Nodule fields may host intermittent redox microcircuits activated by disturbance rather than continuous oxygen generation.
- The policy-relevant unit may be the loss of experimental baselines rather than a proven oxygen service.
- Deep-sea reference areas should be treated partly as long-duration scientific controls, not only conservation set-asides.
