# 2. The Plain Nobody Sees

The Clarion-Clipperton Zone is an industrial prospect hidden by depth.

Across a vast swath of the northeast Pacific, thousands of metres below the surface, potato-sized polymetallic nodules sit partly exposed on fine sediment. They accumulate metal oxides on nuclei over geological time. Manganese and iron dominate, with economically interesting nickel, copper and cobalt mixed into the mineral architecture.

Abyssal plains are easy to mistake for emptiness because their life is small, slow and badly lit. That visual error has policy consequences. Nodules themselves are hard substrate in a landscape dominated by soft sediment. Remove them and the effect is not merely subtraction of ore; it is subtraction of habitat that does not regrow on human political timescales.

The old disturbance experiments should make this concrete. At DISCOL in the Peru Basin, a plough-harrow was towed repeatedly across a nodule field in 1989. Twenty-six years later, biological effects remained evident. A 2025 Nature study of another historical test-mining disturbance found biological impacts still persistent after roughly four decades, despite partial recolonization.

This does not answer whether commercial mining should occur. It removes one comforting premise: that abyssal disturbance can safely be treated as short-lived because the surface ocean is dynamic.

The dark-oxygen controversy lands here. If nodules have an unrecognized electrochemical or ecological function, mining calculations are missing a term. If they do not, the controversy still reveals how thin the baseline can be when commercial decisions arrive.

The seafloor is therefore doing two jobs at once. It is a possible resource and an incomplete experiment.
