# 14. Forty-Four Years in a Plough Track

The mining debate does not need dark oxygen to be serious.

Historical disturbance experiments already show that abyssal ecosystems remember mechanical intervention for decades. Twenty-six years after DISCOL, altered faunal patterns remained visible. A 2025 Nature study found persistent biological impacts roughly forty-four years after an old test-mining experiment, even while some groups had begun to re-establish.

This matters because uncertainty is often presented asymmetrically. Advocates may say dark oxygen is too uncertain to affect policy. Opponents may say the possibility alone justifies prohibition. Both can miss the stronger point: long-lived physical and ecological disturbance is independently documented.

Dark oxygen therefore should not carry the whole moral burden of deep-sea mining.

If the phenomenon disappears under replication, the case for careful environmental baselines remains. If it survives, the baseline becomes more complicated.

A good book should be able to lose its most sensational fact without losing its argument.
