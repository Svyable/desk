# 3. A Chamber at Four Kilometres

A benthic chamber is a wonderfully simple idea until it is sent four kilometres down.

Enclose a known patch of seabed. Measure dissolved oxygen through time. Infer net flux from the trajectory. The elegance hides an engineering fact: the chamber creates a new micro-environment while claiming to observe the old one.

Deployment can disturb sediment. A bow wave can uncover nodule surfaces. Trapped gas can behave differently under pressure. Water mixing changes. Temperature is low. Sensors have response functions. A lid turns an open boundary condition into a closed one. What looks like a flux from nature may partly be a flux created by the act of measurement.

Sweetman et al. themselves invoked disturbance when discussing a possible declining dark-oxygen rate: lander deployment might expose electrochemically active nodule surfaces that later passivate. That is physically interesting, but it also means the apparatus is no longer a neutral witness. The putative mechanism may depend on the measurement event.

There is nothing scandalous about this. Every detector couples to what it detects. The question is whether that coupling is modeled and controlled.

For dark oxygen, the decisive experimental object is not `the chamber`. It is a family of chambers that differ in exactly one thing: nodules present or absent, disturbed or undisturbed, vented or sealed during descent, sensor type A or B, live sediment or sterilized matrix, real rock or inert replica. Replication means reproducing the effect while making the apparatus change its costume.
