# Front Matter

There is a peculiar category of scientific problem in which the experiment and the policy clock run at different speeds.

Polymetallic nodules take geological time to grow. The oxygen claim associated with them is two years old. A mining permit can move faster than either.

In July 2024, Andrew Sweetman and colleagues reported something that should make any marine biogeochemist sit forward: oxygen concentration rose inside dark benthic chambers on the abyssal seafloor. They did not report photosynthesis. They proposed that metal-rich nodules might participate in an electrochemical process, perhaps something like a natural battery. The phrase became irresistible: dark oxygen.

Then the controls became the story.

Critics argued that the mechanism could not pay its thermodynamic bill, that prior chamber studies in nodule provinces had not shown the effect, that hydrogen expected from water splitting was not demonstrated, and that control incubations omitted from the paper complicated the attribution to nodules. In April 2026 Nature Geoscience attached an Editor's Note saying concerns about aspects of the article were under consideration.

That does not prove the original result false. It changes what a responsible person may claim.

This book keeps four ledgers. Documented observations are one thing. Credible but incomplete interpretations are another. Disputed claims remain disputed. Speculation is allowed, but must wear its own clothes.

The timing matters because the Clarion-Clipperton Zone is not merely an experimental site. It is a mineral province. Nodules contain manganese, nickel, copper and cobalt. Mining proposals are advancing while the ecology remains incompletely described. We may therefore be approaching a historically strange act: changing the apparatus before deciding whether the signal was real.
