# Chapter 14 evidence audit — long-end buybacks

Checked September 9, 2026.

## Why this matters

Chapter 14, **The Long Bond Test**, treats the long end of the Treasury curve as a test of both price and market capacity: can the government sell duration at a tolerable yield, and can the financial system hold that duration through volatility without repeated emergency intervention?

Treasury's 2024–2026 buyback program adds an important complication. The long end is not a passive arena in which issuance meets a fixed stock of market-making capacity. Treasury can now buy back less-liquid off-the-run securities to support secondary-market liquidity while continuing to issue new benchmark debt. That does not abolish duration supply or refinancing risk, but it can improve the plumbing through which that supply is absorbed.

## Fresh primary-source update

On August 19, 2026, Treasury announced that, effective **September 9, 2026**, the maximum size of liquidity-support buyback operations in the 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year nominal coupon sectors would rise from $2 billion to **at least $4 billion per operation** for the remainder of the refunding quarter, through November 4, 2026.

Treasury said the change reflected demand for greater long-end liquidity support and the volume of high-quality offers it had been receiving in those sectors.

Source:
https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0607

This is a market-structure fact, not evidence that Treasury faces a failed-auction problem. It is also not debt cancellation. Treasury is retiring particular outstanding securities while financing needs continue through the ordinary issuance program.

## What the program is designed to do

Treasury describes regular buybacks as serving two different purposes:

1. **Liquidity support** — provide a predictable opportunity to sell less-liquid off-the-run securities back to Treasury, allowing intermediaries to recycle balance-sheet capacity into additional market making.
2. **Cash management** — use cash inflows to smooth Treasury's cash balance and reduce otherwise sharp variation in bill issuance.

Treasury officials have emphasized that liquidity-support buybacks are discretionary and price-sensitive rather than guaranteed purchases at any offer. In 2025 remarks reviewing the program, Treasury reported that it had often bought less than the stated maximum when offers were not sufficiently attractive.

Source:
https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0267

The Inter-Agency Working Group on Treasury Market Surveillance similarly described the buyback program as a market-resilience measure intended to improve off-the-run liquidity and free intermediary balance sheet for additional market making.

Source:
https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy2593

## The strongest counterargument to the chapter's current framing

If Chapter 14 reads the long bond chiefly as a referendum on investor willingness to absorb duration, it can understate how much sovereign debt management can change the **liquidity characteristics** of that duration after issuance.

A larger stock of long bonds does not mechanically imply a thinner or more brittle market. Regular buybacks can remove some less-liquid off-the-run inventory, improve dealer balance-sheet turnover, and preserve benchmark liquidity even while gross issuance remains large. Central clearing, broader direct buyback access, electronic trading, and other structural reforms may reinforce the same point.

This weakens any simple inference from "more long-duration supply" to "less resilient long-duration market."

## The counter-counterargument

Buybacks do not make the fiscal wall disappear.

Treasury has repeatedly said the program is small relative to the debt stock. In 2025, officials noted that buybacks to date had changed weighted-average maturity only by a few weeks, and that increasing long-end buyback frequency would affect it by only days, all else equal.

Source:
https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0267

The correct distinction is therefore:

- **issuance and debt service** determine how much duration taxpayers ultimately finance;
- **buybacks and market-structure reforms** can change how easily particular securities circulate and how much dealer/intermediary capacity is consumed in the process.

A more liquid market can absorb a large refinancing cycle better. It cannot turn a large debt stock into a small one or convert an expensive long-term funding rate into a cheap one.

## Claim boundary for a future manuscript pass

The chapter can safely make the narrower claim that the long-bond test has at least three dimensions:

1. **price** — what yield investors require to hold long nominal duration;
2. **capacity** — whether natural and leveraged buyers can absorb issuance without destabilizing feedback loops;
3. **liquidity architecture** — whether Treasury and regulators can keep off-the-run and benchmark markets functioning well enough that issuance scale does not itself create avoidable intermediation stress.

The September 2026 buyback expansion is evidence that Treasury is actively managing the third dimension. It is not evidence that the first two have ceased to matter.

## Disconfirming evidence / falsification

This interpretation would weaken if:

- later Treasury evaluation found no material liquidity benefit from long-end buybacks;
- market participants used the operations in ways that reduced rather than increased intermediation capacity;
- the program grew large enough to become a material maturity-management or quasi-monetary tool rather than a liquidity-support program;
- repeated long-end auction weakness persisted despite improved secondary-market liquidity, showing that the binding problem was price or fundamental demand rather than market plumbing;
- or Treasury materially changed the program's stated purpose.

## Editorial target

A future Chapter 14 pass should avoid the formula that "duration has to live somewhere" becoming a complete explanation. Duration still has to live somewhere, but the **ease with which it moves between holders** is now an explicit object of Treasury policy.

That makes the long-bond test more interesting: the government is not merely choosing how much duration to issue. It is also engineering some of the market infrastructure that determines how costly that duration is to intermediate.
