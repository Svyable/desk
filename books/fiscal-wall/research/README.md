# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Fiscal Wall: The Great Refinancing* asks what happens when the world's deepest sovereign bond market must continuously refinance a historically large debt stock while the federal government is still running large primary deficits and interest rates are materially above the near-zero regime that shaped the previous decade.

The strong crisis claim—that roughly $10 trillion comes due at once and the United States therefore faces an imminent funding failure—is too crude. The useful claim is narrower and more testable:

> The United States is entering a period in which unusually large gross refinancing flows, substantial new borrowing, a higher effective interest-rate regime, changing Federal Reserve balance-sheet policy, and evolving Treasury-market structure can make fiscal and market outcomes more sensitive to auction demand and maturity choices than they were during the era of cheap money.

The number often described as a "$10 trillion refinancing wall" must be decomposed. It can refer to Treasury securities maturing within a year, a maturity bucket that includes a very large stock of bills; gross auction rollovers, which count repeatedly reissued short-term bills; or the combination of maturities and new deficit financing. Those are not interchangeable measures. The manuscript should never present $10 trillion as $10 trillion of new cash borrowing.

## Current anchor points

- Treasury Fiscal Data reported debt held by the public of roughly $32.31 trillion on August 27, 2026 and total public debt outstanding of roughly $40.08 trillion.
- Treasury's August 3, 2026 financing estimate projected $739 billion of privately held net marketable borrowing for July–September 2026 and $628 billion for October–December 2026, explicitly excluding ordinary rollovers from the net borrowing concept.
- Treasury's August 5, 2026 quarterly refunding statement said current nominal coupon and FRN auction sizes were expected to remain in place for at least the next several quarters, with bills absorbing seasonal and unexpected financing variation.
- CBO's February 2026 baseline projected a $1.9 trillion federal deficit in FY2026, debt held by the public equal to 101 percent of GDP, and net interest outlays above $1.0 trillion. It projected debt held by the public at 120 percent of GDP in 2036 and net interest outlays of about $2.1 trillion.
- The Federal Reserve's July 29, 2026 implementation note maintained a 3.5–3.75 percent federal-funds target and directed the New York Fed to purchase short Treasury securities as appropriate to maintain ample reserves while rolling over Treasury principal payments.
- The SEC's Treasury clearing rule is scheduled to reach its cash-market compliance date on December 31, 2026 and repo compliance date on June 30, 2027, adding a major structural transition to the market during the refinancing cycle.

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should preserve evidence that weakens the thesis.

- Large maturities do not imply a failed auction. Treasury rolls debt continually as part of ordinary sovereign debt management.
- Short-term bills create rollover frequency but also serve a deep global demand for safe, liquid collateral and cash-management assets.
- A higher interest bill can stabilize or fall if inflation and policy rates decline faster than debt grows.
- A longer maturity profile can reduce refinancing risk but can also lock in expensive term premiums for taxpayers.
- The Federal Reserve is not simply "gone" as a buyer; its operating framework and reserve-management purchases can alter demand for short Treasuries even when policy is not quantitative easing.
- Central clearing can create implementation costs while ultimately increasing netting and reducing counterparty exposures.
- Strong nominal GDP growth can make a very large nominal debt stock easier to carry relative to national income.
- The United States retains institutional, legal, currency, collateral, and market-depth advantages that distinguish it from ordinary emerging-market debt crises.

## Evidence lanes

1. Treasury debt stock, maturity composition, auction sizes, weighted average maturity, bill share, and buybacks.
2. Federal deficits, primary deficits, net interest, debt-to-GDP, and sensitivity to interest rates.
3. Primary dealer balance sheets, auction intermediation, repo, leverage, hedge-fund basis trades, and central clearing.
4. Federal Reserve balance-sheet policy, SOMA holdings, reserve management, standing repo facilities, and Treasury remittances.
5. Foreign official and private demand, reserve currencies, dollar invoicing, safe-asset demand, and exchange-rate implications.
6. Historical comparisons: postwar U.S. debt, 1970s inflation, Volcker disinflation, 1994 bond selloff, 2008, March 2020, 2022 UK gilts, and other episodes where sovereign-market plumbing mattered.
7. Policy paths: fiscal consolidation, growth, inflation, maturity extension, bill reliance, financial repression, entitlement reform, tax changes, and combinations thereof.

## Source discipline

Prefer Treasury, CBO, Federal Reserve, New York Fed, SEC, GAO, CRS, IMF, BIS, OECD, original academic research, and contemporaneous market data. Distinguish stocks from flows, gross from net borrowing, face value from market value, debt held by the public from gross federal debt, bills from coupon securities, and auction allotments from secondary-market positions. Record vintages. Do not treat a forecast as an observed outcome. Never quote a market participant without a verifiable source. For every major causal claim ask: what evidence would make this false?
