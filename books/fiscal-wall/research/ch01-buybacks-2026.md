# Chapter 1 — Treasury buybacks as liquidity support

## Claim under test

Chapter 1 says Treasury buybacks can improve liquidity in older securities and thereby relieve one kind of pressure in a growing Treasury market. That claim is directionally sound, but the mechanism is worth separating from deficit finance: liquidity-support buybacks rearrange which outstanding securities the market must intermediate; they do not erase the government's debt or eliminate the need to finance deficits.

## Fresh primary-source evidence

On August 19, 2026, the U.S. Treasury announced that beginning September 9 it would at least double the maximum size of liquidity-support buyback operations in the 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year nominal coupon sectors, from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation. Treasury said the change followed feedback from the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee and market participants and was intended to provide more meaningful liquidity support in less-liquid, off-the-run securities.

Source: U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Treasury Announces Increased Sizes of Nominal Long-End Liquidity Support Buybacks Beginning September 9,” August 19, 2026.
https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0607

The timing is useful for the manuscript because it turns a generic policy option into an observed 2026 debt-management choice. Treasury is not merely discussing buybacks as a theoretical market-structure tool; it is increasing their scale specifically in older long-end securities where liquidity support is the objective.

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

This should not be written as evidence that buybacks solve the fiscal-wall problem. Treasury's liquidity-support buybacks exchange cash for selected outstanding securities and are part of debt management, not debt cancellation. They can improve trading conditions or reduce fragmentation in particular off-the-run issues while leaving the government's overall financing requirement intact. The August announcement also concerns maximum operation sizes, not a guarantee that every operation will purchase the maximum amount or that the change will materially lower Treasury's borrowing cost.

A stronger version of Chapter 1's argument is therefore narrower: as the debt stock grows, Treasury can adapt the market machinery as well as the maturity mix. Buybacks are one tool for making an enormous inventory easier to trade and intermediate. Their success should be judged by liquidity, execution, and market-functioning evidence—not by whether they make deficits disappear.

## Editorial implication

The existing Chapter 1 sentence, “Treasury buybacks can improve liquidity in older securities,” remains accurate and appropriately restrained. A future manuscript pass could earn more specificity by briefly noting that Treasury doubled the maximum size of long-end liquidity-support operations in September 2026, but only if the example improves narrative flow rather than turning the chapter into a policy inventory.
