# Postwar debt-decomposition audit — 2026-09-14

## Scope

This note audits the post-World War II U.S. example used in Chapters 16 and 17 of *Fiscal Wall*. The manuscript is right that the debt burden fell without a household-style repayment of the wartime principal. The risk is a cleaner inference: that ordinary economic growth did most of the work and therefore provides a close analogue for the present soft-landing case.

## What the historical record establishes

CBO's historical series puts debt held by the public at 106 percent of GDP in 1946 and 23 percent in 1974. Over the same period the nominal stock did not disappear: CBO reports that debt held by the public rose from about $242 billion in 1946 to $283 billion in 1970. The denominator therefore mattered enormously, but “the denominator grew” does not identify how much came from real growth, inflation, fiscal balances, or unusually low effective borrowing costs.

Source:
https://www.cbo.gov/publication/56309

Federal Reserve History supplies an institutional fact that should remain visible in any postwar analogy. Beginning in 1942, the Fed maintained a 3/8 percent peg on short Treasury bills and implicitly capped long-term Treasury bond yields at 2.5 percent in support of wartime finance. The conflict over continuing that regime after the war ended with the Treasury-Federal Reserve Accord in March 1951. The early postwar debt path therefore began under a monetary/debt-management arrangement that is not comparable to today's independent inflation-targeting regime.

## The stronger decomposition

Julien Acalin and Laurence Ball's 2023 NBER working paper, *Did the U.S. Really Grow Out of Its World War II Debt?*, directly tests the common growth-only story. Their counterfactual removes primary surpluses and the real-interest-rate distortions associated with surprise inflation and the pre-Accord peg. Under that counterfactual, debt/GDP falls from 106 percent in 1946 to about 74 percent in 1974 rather than the observed 23 percent.

Source:
https://www.nber.org/papers/w31577

That result is useful because it puts the manuscript's own multi-mechanism account under pressure. It implies that the sentence “the economy grew around it,” if left unqualified, invites exactly the simplification the surrounding book otherwise rejects. Real growth mattered, but this decomposition assigns a large share of the decline to primary surpluses, surprise inflation, and the early interest-rate peg rather than to growth outrunning an undistorted interest rate by itself.

## Strongest counterargument / boundary

The Acalin-Ball result is a counterfactual decomposition, not an observed partition of history. Its quantitative shares depend on assumptions about the undistorted interest rate, surprise inflation, fiscal balance, and the maturity structure of the debt. It should not replace the manuscript's multi-causal account with a new monocausal claim that “financial repression did it.” CBO's simpler historical accounting is compatible with several decompositions: nominal GDP grew much faster than the debt stock, but that arithmetic alone cannot tell us which policy or macroeconomic mechanism deserves causal credit.

There is also a present-day boundary. The United States today has inflation-indexed debt, a much more globally integrated capital market, different financial regulation, an independent Federal Reserve with an explicit price-stability mandate, and a fiscal path characterized by persistent projected primary deficits. The postwar episode can demonstrate that a large debt ratio can fall without nominal debt being paid down. It cannot establish that today's debt ratio will fall through growth alone or that recreating the 1940s rate regime would be costless or desirable.

## Revision implication

A future prose pass should preserve the vivid 106-to-23-percent historical movement but replace the clean “grew around it” shorthand with a more exact claim: postwar America diluted the wartime debt burden through a combination of real growth, inflation, primary fiscal adjustment, and—especially in the early period—an interest-rate regime designed to hold Treasury financing costs down. The strongest soft-landing lesson is therefore not that growth automatically rescues a high-debt sovereign. It is that debt ratios can bend when several favorable mechanisms reinforce one another.

That revision would also sharpen the transition from Chapter 16 to Chapter 17. Chapter 16 can keep growth as the least painful favorable mechanism while acknowledging that the historical precedent was not growth-only; Chapter 17 can then explain why durable escapes have generally been combinations rather than miracles.
