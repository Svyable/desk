# The Buyers at the Table

Every Treasury security has two lives.

In one life, it is a liability of the United States government.

In the other, it is an asset belonging to somebody else.

The fiscal wall is usually described from the first perspective. Trillions mature. Deficits continue. Interest costs rise. Treasury has to issue more debt.

The second perspective changes the picture.

For the securities to exist, somebody must want to hold them.

This does not mean one mysterious pool of “bond vigilantes” sits across the table from Washington deciding whether America deserves another loan. The Treasury investor base is too broad for that story. Different buyers want different maturities for different reasons, face different regulations, use different funding structures, and react differently when yields move.

The market clears because those motives overlap.

Understanding the buyer base is essential because the phrase *who will buy all the debt?* sounds more frightening than it is and less interesting than the real question.

The real question is:

Who will buy which debt, at what maturity, through which balance sheet, at what yield, and with what consequences for the rest of the financial system?

Start with money-market funds.

A money-market fund is not trying to beat the stock market. It is trying to preserve principal, provide liquidity, and earn a short-term yield while operating within strict portfolio rules. Government money funds are natural buyers of Treasury bills and participants in repo backed by government securities.

For them, a large supply of bills can be welcome.

During parts of the post-pandemic period, money funds accumulated enormous assets while the supply of Treasury bills fluctuated because of debt-limit constraints, fiscal cash management, and Federal Reserve operations. When Treasury issues more bills at attractive yields, money funds have a direct use for them.

This is one reason analysts who look only at the total federal debt can underestimate the elasticity of short-term demand.

A four-week bill is not competing for the same portfolio slot as a thirty-year bond.

The money fund wants the bill precisely because it will mature soon.

That same preference explains why heavy reliance on bills does not solve the long-run financing problem. Money funds can absorb short-duration supply, but they do not give taxpayers thirty years of fixed-rate funding. Treasury obtains liquidity and buyer depth by accepting rapid repricing.

Next come banks.

Banks hold Treasuries for several reasons. The securities are liquid, high quality, useful in liquidity portfolios, and central to collateralized funding. Banks also act as dealers, market makers, custodians, and repo intermediaries. Their relationship with Treasury debt is therefore broader than simple investment.

A bank may want more Treasuries and still be unwilling to devote unlimited balance sheet to them.

This distinction matters.

After the global financial crisis, regulation changed how banks measure capital, leverage, and liquidity. Those reforms made the banking system safer in important respects, but they also placed a price on balance-sheet use. A low-risk asset can still consume scarce leverage capacity. A repo transaction secured by Treasuries can still occupy a balance sheet.

When Treasury supply grows, the question is not merely whether Treasuries are safe enough for banks.

It is whether holding or intermediating the next dollar is attractive relative to other uses of capital.

That is a price question.

Higher yields can make the asset more appealing. Wider dealer spreads can compensate intermediation. Regulatory changes can alter the balance-sheet cost. Central clearing can increase netting. But there is no rule that says bank capacity expands automatically with federal borrowing.

Pension funds approach the table from the opposite end of the maturity spectrum.

A pension plan has liabilities extending decades into the future. It owes payments to people who may still be receiving benefits long after today's Treasury secretary has retired. Long-duration government bonds can be useful because they help match those liabilities.

Higher long-term yields can improve the economics of that match.

This is one of the ironies of the fiscal wall.

The government dislikes paying five percent instead of two.

A pension fund with future obligations may be delighted to lock in five percent on a high-quality asset.

The same interest rate is a cost to one balance sheet and an opportunity to another.

This is why rising Treasury yields can attract their own demand.

It is also why a market selloff is not the same thing as buyers disappearing. As bond prices fall and yields rise, some investors who were uninterested at lower yields enter the market. The adjustment can be violent, but it contains a stabilizer.

Insurers share some of the pension logic.

A life insurance company collects premiums today and expects to make payments over many years. It needs assets that can support those liabilities. Treasuries provide duration, liquidity, and a low-credit-risk reference point, though insurers also invest in corporate bonds, mortgages, and other assets to earn additional spread.

When safe yields rise, insurers can meet return requirements with less credit risk than they needed when rates were near zero.

This can strengthen demand for government debt.

It can also reveal losses on older low-coupon bonds.

A holder that bought a thirty-year Treasury at a very low yield may be sitting on a substantial mark-to-market loss after rates rise. If the holder can keep the bond and fund itself patiently, the loss may be economically tolerable. If liabilities become unstable or collateral must be posted, duration losses can become a liquidity problem.

Again, the security can be safe while the holder is fragile.

Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds add another layer.

They hold Treasuries for individuals and institutions that want duration, diversification, liquidity, or a particular index exposure. Their flows can be procyclical. In a selloff, investors can redeem shares and force portfolio adjustments. In a rally, inflows can increase demand.

The fund is not a permanent buyer.

It is a conduit for the preferences of its investors.

Households themselves have become more visible Treasury buyers when yields rise.

For years, a saver could look at a bank deposit and see almost no interest. When bill yields became competitive with or higher than many deposit rates, Treasury securities suddenly became dinner-table finance. Brokerage platforms made them easy to purchase. TreasuryDirect had long offered direct access. Interest in bills and savings products rose as households rediscovered that cash could earn something.

This matters at the margin.

A broad household base can absorb meaningful issuance, especially at the short end.

But retail participation should not be romanticized. The Treasury market is measured in tens of trillions. Households are one buyer class among many, and their appetite is sensitive to relative yields, convenience, taxes, and alternatives.

Corporations hold Treasuries for cash management too.

Large companies can have billions in temporary liquidity. They need assets that are secure, liquid, and easy to pledge or sell. Treasury bills and money-market instruments serve that need. A company awaiting an acquisition, tax payment, capital expenditure, or payroll cycle may care less about the government's fiscal trajectory than about having a safe place to park cash for ninety days.

This is another source of what economists call convenience yield.

Treasuries provide services beyond interest.

They settle easily.

They are accepted as collateral.

They satisfy regulatory and institutional requirements.

They serve as benchmarks.

They can be converted into cash quickly in normal markets.

An investor may therefore accept a lower yield on a Treasury than on another asset with similar expected cash flows because the Treasury is more useful.

That usefulness is part of the United States' fiscal advantage.

Foreign buyers bring a different set of motives.

Foreign central banks hold dollar reserves to manage exchange rates, provide foreign-currency liquidity, intervene in markets, and maintain confidence. Sovereign wealth funds, banks, insurers, corporations, pension funds, and private investors abroad also hold Treasuries.

Some are return-seeking.

Some are reserve managers.

Some need dollars because global trade and finance are dollar-heavy.

Some buy Treasuries because alternatives of comparable scale do not exist.

Foreign demand is often discussed as though China or Japan can decide one morning to “stop financing America.” The reality is more distributed. Large official holders can reduce positions, and the composition of reserves can change over time, but the dollar system creates continuing demand from many actors whose motives are not geopolitical charity.

The United States pays for that privilege in other ways. A strong dollar can hurt exporters. Global demand can hold U.S. yields lower than they otherwise would be, encouraging more borrowing. Reserve-currency status creates obligations and vulnerabilities as well as benefits.

But foreign demand remains a structural part of the buyer table.

Then there are hedge funds.

Hedge funds complicate the question *who owns the debt?* because they may own a Treasury security without wanting its economic duration.

Consider a basis trade.

A hedge fund sees a small price difference between a cash Treasury and a related futures contract. It buys the cash security, often financing it in repo, and sells the futures contract. The intended profit is the convergence of the prices, not a long-term view that Treasury yields will fall.

The trade can use substantial leverage because the price difference is small and the underlying security is considered safe.

This creates demand for cash Treasuries.

It also creates a funding dependency.

If repo rates jump, margin requirements rise, volatility spikes, or the price relationship moves against the trade, leveraged positions can be unwound quickly. The investor who looked like a buyer of Treasuries becomes a seller.

This was one of the dynamics examined closely after March 2020.

The lesson is subtle.

Demand is not a substance measured only in dollars.

It has a stability profile.

A pension fund buying a thirty-year bond to match a liability behaves differently from a hedge fund buying the same security as one leg of a leveraged arbitrage. Both show up as buyers. They do not provide the same kind of market depth under stress.

Primary dealers sit at the table in yet another role.

They are not end investors in the ordinary sense. They are intermediaries with obligations and incentives to participate in Treasury auctions and make markets. They warehouse securities before distributing them to clients. They finance positions. They connect hedge funds, asset managers, foreign institutions, banks, and other buyers to Treasury supply.

When an auction is weak, dealers may be awarded a larger share.

That can be misread as evidence that nobody wanted the debt.

More precisely, it can mean the intermediation system had to absorb more inventory at the clearing price.

The question becomes how easily dealers can distribute it.

The Federal Reserve is the most misunderstood buyer of all.

When the Fed buys Treasuries for monetary policy or reserve-management purposes, it changes the amount and composition of securities held by the private sector. But it is not a conventional investor deciding that the yield is attractive. Its motives are policy motives.

That distinction matters because a Treasury market dependent on ever-larger central-bank purchases to keep yields below market-clearing levels would represent a different fiscal regime from one in which private demand willingly absorbs supply.

At the same time, saying “the Fed is no longer buying” can be false or oversimplified. Balance-sheet policy changes over time. The Fed can reduce some holdings, roll principal, reinvest into different maturities, or purchase short Treasuries to maintain ample reserves without engaging in a new round of quantitative easing.

The buyer table moves.

So does the supply.

This is why aggregate ownership statistics are snapshots, not destiny.

Suppose foreign central banks reduce their Treasury share.

That does not necessarily mean yields must explode. Domestic money funds can buy more bills. Pensions can buy more duration. Households can move from bank deposits. Banks can alter portfolios. Higher yields can attract global private investors. The adjustment can occur through price and composition.

Suppose money funds shrink because investors move into risk assets.

Treasury can issue fewer bills and more coupons over time, though long-term yields may need to rise to attract duration buyers.

Suppose bank regulations make dealer intermediation expensive.

Central clearing, balance-sheet reform, or greater participation by nonbanks can change the channel.

The market is adaptive.

That adaptability is the strongest argument against simple refinancing panic.

It is also the reason the wall should be analyzed as a system rather than a fixed capacity number.

There is no known point at which “the world runs out of money” for Treasuries.

There is a price at which capital moves.

The constraint is what that price does to everything else.

If long-term Treasury yields rise enough to attract buyers, mortgage rates can rise with them. Corporate borrowing becomes more expensive. Equity valuations can fall as the discount rate increases. Municipalities pay more to build. Banks experience mark-to-market losses. The dollar can strengthen, tighten global financial conditions, and pressure foreign borrowers with dollar debts.

The buyer problem can therefore solve itself in the Treasury market while creating stress elsewhere.

This is one of the central paradoxes of sovereign finance.

The United States can always make its debt more attractive by paying more.

The fiscal wall begins when paying more is itself the problem.

A healthy buyer base has several qualities.

It is diverse.

It contains investors with different time horizons.

It does not depend excessively on leverage.

It can absorb securities without forcing dealers to warehouse too much inventory.

It includes buyers that value Treasury liquidity and collateral services, not only yield.

It can expand when prices move.

And it does not require monetary policy to be subordinated to debt service.

By those standards, the Treasury market remains extraordinarily strong.

No other sovereign issuer has the same combination of scale, liquidity, currency importance, legal infrastructure, collateral use, and institutional demand.

That advantage should not be confused with an unlimited credit line at a fixed price.

The buyers at the table are not refusing to eat.

They are reading the menu.

As the portions grow, the price matters more.

One way to make this buyer base less abstract is to imagine a single day on which the same Treasury security passes through several motives without changing its CUSIP.

A dealer receives part of a new note at auction because client demand did not absorb the entire amount directly. For a few hours, the security is inventory. The dealer finances it, perhaps in repo, and hedges the rate risk.

Later that day an asset manager buys some of the note because it needs to match the duration of a benchmark index. For the fund, the Treasury is not a political judgment. It is part of a mandate.

A hedge fund buys another block and sells a futures contract against it because the relative price is attractive. For that fund, the note is one leg of a trade.

A foreign institution buys another piece because it has dollar cash that must remain liquid and high quality. A household acquires a smaller amount through a brokerage because the yield is attractive relative to a bank account.

By settlement, one security has become four different economic objects.

Inventory.

Benchmark exposure.

Collateralized arbitrage.

Savings.

Ownership data compress those motives into one total.

Market resilience depends on keeping them distinct.

This matters because demand can look deep while being more correlated than it appears. Ten funds can be separate legal entities and still react to the same margin call. Five banks can have different shareholders and still face the same leverage constraint. Hundreds of money funds can own bills and still shift in the same direction when relative yields change.

Diversity of names is not always diversity of behavior.

A strong Treasury market needs both.

The distinction became visible in March 2020. The Federal Reserve's later analysis and market discussion focused partly on the way leveraged Treasury positions and a broad dash for cash interacted with dealer intermediation. The point is not that hedge funds caused the entire episode. Many investors sold for different reasons. The lesson is that holders who appear stable in normal times can become synchronized when funding conditions change.

The safest collateral can be sold because it is the thing that still has a buyer.

This turns one common intuition upside down.

People assume a safe asset must rally in every crisis.

Often Treasuries do. In a conventional recession with falling inflation, investors seek safety and expect easier Federal Reserve policy. Bond prices can rise while risk assets fall.

But in a generalized demand for cash, even safe assets can be liquidated. If enough participants need dollars immediately, a Treasury security becomes valuable partly because it can be sold. Liquidity produces selling as well as buying.

A buyer base therefore has to be judged through stress, not only ownership.

Which holders are funded patiently?

Which are funded overnight?

Which have liabilities that can run?

Which face variation margin?

Which are required by mandate to rebalance?

Which will buy more when yields rise?

Which will be forced to sell at exactly that moment?

This is the difference between *capacity* and *elasticity*.

Capacity is how much an investor can hold.

Elasticity is how much more it will hold when the price changes.

The Treasury market depends on both.

A pension fund may have huge capacity but a specific duration target. A money fund may be highly elastic to bill supply but have no mandate for ten-year notes. A household may enter quickly when bills yield five percent and leave just as quickly when bank deposits catch up. A foreign reserve manager may hold large stocks for institutional reasons but adjust slowly rather than chase yield.

The marginal buyer can therefore change by maturity and by regime.

That is why the question *who buys?* cannot be answered with a pie chart.

Pie charts show yesterday's holders.

Debt management needs tomorrow's marginal demand.

Treasury International Capital data offer one view into foreign flows, but even those data require caution. Country attribution can reflect custody rather than ultimate beneficial ownership. Official and private demand differ. Long-term securities and short-term instruments can move in opposite directions. A foreign institution can reduce Treasuries while increasing other U.S. assets.

In June 2026, Treasury reported substantial net foreign purchases of long-term U.S. securities alongside a positive overall TIC flow. The snapshot did not prove foreign demand would remain strong forever. It did demonstrate why one country's Treasury holdings cannot be treated as a referendum on the entire dollar system.

Capital crosses borders for portfolios, not headlines.

Currency hedging adds another layer.

A Japanese or European investor does not experience a Treasury yield in the same way a dollar-based investor does. If the institution hedges the currency exposure back home, the cost of that hedge can erase or enhance the apparent yield advantage. Interest-rate differentials, cross-currency basis, regulation, and balance-sheet treatment all matter.

A five-percent Treasury can be attractive to an American saver and unattractive to a hedged foreign investor at the same time.

This makes foreign demand price-sensitive in more dimensions than the coupon alone.

Reserve managers are different again. They may tolerate a lower expected return because liquidity and intervention capacity matter more. A central bank that needs a large pool of dollar assets cannot simply replace Treasuries with a smaller, less liquid market because another government bond yields twenty basis points more.

This is the convenience yield in institutional form.

Scale itself creates demand.

The United States benefits from a network effect: Treasuries are useful partly because everybody already knows how to trade, clear, finance, hedge, value, and regulate them. That network cannot be replicated quickly by a competitor. It is one of the strongest answers to predictions that reserve demand will vanish suddenly.

Network effects are durable.

They are not indestructible.

A network loses value if trust, liquidity, or legal predictability erodes. Debt-limit brinkmanship matters because it introduces doubt into the one attribute the market is designed not to debate. Market dysfunction matters because a safe asset that cannot be exchanged smoothly in stress is less useful collateral. Inflation credibility matters because a nominal promise is only as stable in real terms as the currency behind it.

The buyer base is therefore partly a referendum after all—not on a party or one budget, but on institutional usefulness.

That usefulness gives Treasury pricing power.

The fiscal wall tests how much.

There is a common argument that the United States can always rely on domestic buyers because every Treasury liability is somebody's asset. At the level of accounting, that is true: a security cannot exist without a holder. At the level of economics, it evades the question.

The holder is determined by price.

If the federal government issues another trillion dollars of debt, somebody will end the process owning it. The meaningful question is what portfolio changes were required to make that ownership happen.

Did households save more?

Did banks reduce other assets?

Did pension funds sell corporate bonds?

Did foreign investors shift from another currency?

Did hedge funds add leverage?

Did the Federal Reserve change the composition of public liabilities?

Did yields rise enough to reduce private investment elsewhere?

Accounting guarantees a holder after the fact.

It does not guarantee a painless clearing price before the fact.

This distinction is the heart of crowding out.

Government borrowing does not mechanically remove one dollar from a fixed pool and destroy one dollar of private investment. Financial systems create credit, foreign capital moves, central banks alter reserves, and saving responds to rates. But a larger government claim can change relative prices. Higher safe yields make marginal private projects less attractive. Banks and investors compare the Treasury with corporate credit, mortgages, municipal bonds, equities, and cash.

The government can win the auction by offering a better return.

The rest of the economy receives that price signal too.

This is why a strong Treasury auction can coexist with weaker private financing conditions.

The buyer problem may be solving itself exactly as markets are designed to solve it.

That is not evidence the fiscal problem vanished.

It is evidence the price moved.

The most reassuring outcome over the next decade would not be one buyer class taking a heroic share of issuance.

It would be a broad migration.

More bills absorbed by money funds and households when short yields are attractive.

More long bonds absorbed by pensions and insurers when duration becomes useful.

Foreign demand remaining substantial even if its share changes.

Banks and dealers intermediating without balance-sheet stress.

Hedge funds providing relative-value demand without leverage becoming a destabilizing single point of failure.

Central clearing increasing netting rather than merely moving liquidity pressure into margin calls.

The Federal Reserve remaining able to manage reserves and market functioning without becoming the fiscal buyer of last resort.

That would look boring.

Boring is the standard.

A diversified buyer base is successful when no one needs to be called a savior.

The Treasury market has spent decades building exactly that kind of depth.

The fiscal wall asks whether it can scale faster than the government's need to use it.
