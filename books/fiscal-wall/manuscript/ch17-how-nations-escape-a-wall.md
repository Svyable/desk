# How Nations Escape a Wall

There are only a few ways to reduce a debt burden.

The political vocabulary makes them sound more numerous than they are.

Grow faster.

Tax more.

Spend less.

Pay creditors back in money worth less than expected.

Hold borrowing costs below economic growth for long enough that the denominator does the work.

Restructure or default.

Sell assets.

Change the maturity structure and timing of payments.

Most real fiscal adjustments combine several.

The argument begins to go wrong when one tool is advertised as painless.

Growth is the closest thing to a gentle exit.

If the economy becomes more productive, incomes rise without requiring a corresponding rise in hours worked or inflation. Federal revenue generally increases. The debt ratio can fall even if nominal debt does not. Higher productivity can support real wages, investment, and living standards at the same time the fiscal burden becomes easier to carry.

This is why almost every fiscal coalition eventually invokes growth.

The disagreement is about how to produce it.

Tax reform can improve incentives at the margin while raising or lowering revenue depending on design. Infrastructure can reduce bottlenecks but can also become expensive patronage. Research can generate enormous spillovers but is difficult to target. Education can raise human capital but not every dollar of educational spending has the same return. Immigration can expand the labor force and tax base while creating distributional and political pressures. Regulatory reform can increase supply but can also remove protections whose value becomes visible only after they are gone.

Growth policy is policy, not a wish.

Even successful growth reforms may be insufficient by themselves if the primary deficit is large and structural.

A one-time productivity surge can improve the path without closing it.

That brings us to taxes.

A government can stabilize debt by collecting more revenue.

The United States has room to do so in a mechanical sense. Federal revenue as a share of GDP has varied over time and remains below the combined tax burden of many other wealthy countries. But tax capacity is not the same as political willingness or economic cost.

Different taxes have different effects.

A broader consumption tax can raise large sums with a wide base but is often criticized for burdening lower-income households unless paired with rebates or credits.

Higher income-tax rates can increase revenue but alter work, investment, avoidance, and the timing of income.

Corporate taxes can raise revenue while influencing investment location and financing choices.

Property and wealth taxes raise different administrative and constitutional questions.

Carbon taxes can price an externality while changing energy costs.

Reducing tax expenditures can raise revenue through what looks economically like spending restraint even though it occurs through the tax code.

The fiscal wall does not tell us which tax is best.

It tells us that “tax the other person” is rarely a complete solution at the scale of the long-run gap.

Large adjustments usually reach a broad base eventually.

Spending restraint is equally unavoidable in the menu and equally complicated in practice.

The federal budget contains discretionary programs that Congress votes on annually and mandatory programs driven largely by eligibility and formulas. The largest long-run spending pressures are not confined to small agencies that can be eliminated without touching broad constituencies.

This is why campaigns against waste, fraud, and abuse are simultaneously worthwhile and insufficient.

Every government should reduce improper payments, procurement failures, duplicate programs, and ineffective spending. A billion dollars saved is still a billion dollars.

The arithmetic of structural deficits measured in trillions requires confronting larger categories too.

Retirement benefits.

Health spending.

Defense.

Tax subsidies.

The age at which benefits begin.

The formulas by which they grow.

The revenues dedicated to them.

The prices government pays for medical care.

These choices are difficult because the programs are popular for reasons.

People plan around them.

A retiree cannot rebuild a career because a spreadsheet changed.

A patient cannot defer treatment until the budget improves.

A country cannot decide it no longer needs defense because interest rates rose.

This is why timing matters as much as direction.

A change announced decades before it takes full effect is different from an abrupt cut.

Gradual eligibility reforms can protect people near retirement while changing the path for younger workers who have time to adjust.

Health reforms can focus on prices, incentives, competition, and delivery rather than mechanically reducing access.

Defense reform can target procurement and strategy rather than pretending every dollar is interchangeable.

Fiscal adjustment is design work.

The third route is inflation.

Governments throughout history have discovered that nominal debt can be reduced in real terms if the currency loses purchasing power faster than creditors expected.

This is a tax that does not require an explicit vote.

It is also unreliable and regressive in complicated ways.

Unexpected inflation benefits borrowers with fixed nominal debt and hurts creditors. It reduces the real value of some government obligations. It can also push nominal interest rates higher, raise indexed benefits and TIPS principal, distort tax brackets, weaken real wages, and damage investment.

If investors come to expect inflation as a fiscal strategy, they demand compensation in advance.

The trick stops working.

The refinancing wall accelerates that problem because short bills and maturing notes reset quickly. Inflation can erode yesterday's fixed-rate debt while making tomorrow's borrowing more expensive.

A one-time surprise is not a sustainable debt-management plan.

The fourth route is financial repression.

The term describes a family of policies that channel domestic savings toward government debt at yields below what a freer market might otherwise require. Historically, this has included interest-rate ceilings, capital controls, reserve requirements, directed credit, and regulations that create captive demand.

The postwar reduction of advanced-economy debt burdens included periods in which regulated financial systems and moderate inflation helped keep real government borrowing costs low.

It is tempting to imagine a modern version.

Require banks to hold more Treasuries.

Encourage pensions to own government bonds.

Use regulation to make alternatives less attractive.

Keep policy rates below inflation.

Let nominal GDP grow around the debt.

Some of these policies can have legitimate non-fiscal purposes. Banks should hold liquid safe assets. Pensions should match liabilities. Central banks sometimes keep real rates negative because economic conditions warrant it.

The line between prudence and repression depends on intent, design, and constraints.

A system that forces private savers to subsidize government borrowing can reduce explicit fiscal pain while imposing hidden costs through lower returns and distorted capital allocation.

It is an exit.

It is not a free one.

The fifth route is maturity management.

Treasury can extend duration, increase bills, issue floating-rate notes, expand TIPS, conduct buybacks, and adjust auction sizes.

These tools can reduce refinancing peaks and support market liquidity.

They cannot repair the primary budget.

Maturity management changes the path along which the wall is encountered.

It does not change the amount of real resources government ultimately consumes.

The sixth route is asset sales.

Governments own land, buildings, mineral rights, spectrum, enterprises, loans, and other assets. Selling or leasing them can raise cash and improve efficiency if the private sector can use them better.

A one-time asset sale does not solve a recurring deficit unless the asset was itself generating less value than the proceeds can create elsewhere.

Selling the furniture to pay the electric bill works once.

That does not mean all government assets should remain government assets. It means balance-sheet transactions should not be mistaken for structural reform.

The seventh route is default or restructuring.

For the United States, deliberate default on dollar-denominated Treasury debt would be an extraordinary act of self-harm. The government has taxing capacity, monetary sovereignty, and an asset whose value is partly derived from the belief that nominal payment is unquestioned.

A restructuring could reduce the legal debt burden at the cost of damaging the financial system built around Treasuries.

Banks, money funds, pensions, insurers, foreign reserves, derivatives, and collateral arrangements all rely on the securities.

The United States would be attacking its own financial infrastructure.

This is why default should be treated as a tail outcome, not the natural conclusion of a large debt number.

The more relevant danger is an accidental payment disruption around the debt limit or operational failure.

Even that would be costly because it undermines the very privilege that makes the debt manageable.

The eighth route is the one rarely named because it sounds like doing nothing.

Wait.

If the primary deficit is modest, growth is healthy, and the effective interest rate remains below nominal growth, a high debt ratio can decline gradually without a dramatic fiscal package. Time itself becomes the adjustment mechanism.

This was part of the U.S. postwar experience.

But waiting is a policy only when the arithmetic is favorable.

If debt grows faster than the economy, waiting is simply compounding.

The fiscal wall asks which side of that line the United States occupies over the next decade.

Historical cases are useful because they show combinations.

After World War II, the United States reduced its debt ratio through rapid nominal growth, periods of inflation, primary fiscal restraint relative to wartime spending, and a regulated financial system. No single lever did the work.

The political conditions were unusual. War spending collapsed. The country had enormous productive advantages. Demographics were favorable. The financial system was more controlled.

The case proves that a giant debt can shrink without a giant repayment.

It does not prove the same mix is available now.

Canada's 1990s adjustment combined expenditure restraint, institutional changes, growth, and favorable interest-rate developments. The country entered the period under different monetary, demographic, and federal structures from the United States.

Sweden rebuilt fiscal institutions after a severe banking and fiscal crisis in the early 1990s. Its reforms helped create stronger budget discipline, but the country is much smaller and operates inside a different welfare-state bargain.

The United States itself moved from large deficits in the early 1990s to federal budget surpluses at the end of the decade through a mix of policy, strong growth, capital-gains revenue, lower defense spending after the Cold War, and favorable demographics.

Few forecasters at the beginning of the period projected the exact outcome.

Fiscal paths can bend.

They can bend the other way too.

Wars happen.

Recessions happen.

Pandemics happen.

Tax laws change.

New programs are created.

Interest rates surprise.

This is why a credible exit from the wall should not aim only for one deterministic baseline.

It should rebuild fiscal capacity for bad futures.

A nation escapes a refinancing wall when it no longer needs every future to be favorable.

That is a higher standard than stabilizing debt in the median forecast.

Suppose a fiscal plan balances only if interest rates fall two percentage points, productivity booms, unemployment stays low, and no recession occurs for ten years.

That is not resilience.

It is a parlay.

A stronger plan can survive a recession, a war, or a period of higher rates without entering an explosive debt path.

This is where gradual adjustment has an advantage.

Small changes made early compound in the helpful direction.

A modest improvement in the primary balance reduces borrowing.

Less borrowing reduces future interest.

Lower expected supply can reduce risk premiums at the margin.

Stronger confidence can support investment.

The feedback loop reverses.

The same compounding that makes delay expensive makes early progress valuable.

The danger is demanding so much adjustment immediately that the economy contracts and the politics collapse.

A fiscal reform that cannot survive one election is not a reform.

This is why durable bargains usually distribute pain.

Broad tax bases rather than symbolic levies on tiny groups.

Gradual entitlement changes rather than sudden benefit cuts.

Protection for low-income households.

Spending reviews that distinguish investment from consumption.

Rules that preserve emergency flexibility.

Growth reforms that make supply more responsive.

Budget processes that expose long-run costs before programs are enacted.

No constituency gets everything.

That is what makes the bargain credible.

The United States has not yet been forced into such a bargain by markets.

That is an advantage.

Countries that adjust during crisis pay an additional tax: urgency.

They sell assets when prices are low.

They cut spending during recessions.

They raise taxes when households are already weak.

They accept financing terms set by frightened investors or external institutions.

They lose the ability to phase changes compassionately.

The fiscal wall is a warning precisely because the United States still has options.

Its auctions clear.

The dollar remains central.

The economy is productive.

The tax system can raise more or less depending on political choice.

The maturity structure can be managed.

The Federal Reserve retains credibility.

The investor base is vast.

None of those advantages should be confused with permission to wait forever.

They are the resources with which an adjustment can be made before it becomes compulsory.

How nations escape a wall is therefore less dramatic than how they hit one.

They improve the denominator.

They narrow the primary gap.

They manage time.

They preserve monetary credibility.

They keep the market liquid.

They spread adjustment across groups and years.

And they do enough early that the next crisis can still be financed.

The best escape is the one nobody later calls an escape.

It becomes ordinary budget policy again.

The Social Security trust-fund calendar illustrates why early adjustment has a quality that late adjustment cannot buy.

The 2026 Trustees Report projected that the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund would be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032 under the intermediate assumptions, with the combined OASDI funds reaching depletion later if the funds were hypothetically combined. The legal and policy details matter, and Congress can change them. The relevant point here is time.

A retirement-system change announced years before a financing shortfall can be phased. Workers far from retirement can adjust saving and labor decisions. Revenue changes can be introduced gradually. Benefit formulas can protect people with little ability to change plans. A broad negotiation can distribute the adjustment across cohorts.

The same arithmetic addressed at the last possible moment becomes more abrupt.

There are fewer years over which to spread the change.

This is true of the fiscal wall more generally.

Time is a policy instrument before it becomes a financing constraint.

A government that begins adjustment while auctions are orderly can choose sequencing. It can protect recession-sensitive programs. It can distinguish current beneficiaries from younger cohorts. It can pair revenue with spending changes. It can invest in administrative capacity before changing tax bases or health-payment systems.

A government adjusting after yields spike or market access narrows has fewer choices.

The size of the eventual accounting adjustment may be similar.

The human experience is not.

This is why the most durable fiscal changes often look unimpressive at first.

A quarter-point change in a payroll tax introduced over several years.

A benefit formula altered for future cohorts rather than current retirees.

A slower growth rate in a tax expenditure.

A health-payment reform that compounds savings instead of announcing a giant nominal cut.

A budget rule that forces permanent policies to reveal permanent financing.

No one measure rescues the republic.

Several measures that survive can change the trajectory.

The Canadian adjustment of the 1990s is useful for this reason, though it should not be converted into a U.S. recipe. Canada combined spending restraint with institutional and political changes, benefited from an improving interest-rate environment, and operated in a different federal structure and economic setting.

The lesson is not *cut spending like Canada*.

It is that fiscal arithmetic can change when policy, institutions, and macroeconomic conditions reinforce one another.

Sweden offers a different version. A severe banking and fiscal crisis helped generate reforms to budget institutions and fiscal rules. Again, the country is smaller and the political settlement is different. The relevant lesson is that rules can improve durability after a coalition has decided what it is willing to constrain.

Rules cannot substitute for the coalition.

A numerical ceiling that the political system repeatedly suspends creates less discipline than a modest process that lawmakers actually respect.

The United States in the 1990s demonstrates the danger of assigning too much causal credit to one policy. The federal balance moved from deficit to surplus through a mix of tax and spending decisions, strong growth, lower defense spending after the Cold War, favorable demographics, and unusually strong revenue from asset markets.

A different economy would have produced a different result from the same laws.

This is the recurring lesson of fiscal history.

Policy matters.

Context matters.

The exit is a combination.

Growth deserves special skepticism because it is the favorite painless solution of every coalition.

A sustained productivity boom would transform the wall. More real output means a larger tax base without the same inflationary erosion. It can improve living standards while making debt smaller relative to national income.

But a fiscal plan should distinguish between growth that is assumed and growth that is caused.

Writing a higher GDP number into a forecast does nothing.

Policies that plausibly raise supply—productive infrastructure, research, labor-force participation, immigration rules, competition, faster permitting where appropriate, education that actually builds useful skill, reliable energy—can help. Their returns are uncertain, distributed, and often slow.

Some will fail.

Growth is a portfolio too.

The same caution applies to tax cuts advertised as self-financing. Lower marginal rates can improve incentives in some contexts, but the evidence does not support treating every tax cut as revenue-neutral through growth. The scale, base, timing, and starting rates matter.

A serious exit counts the revenue loss that remains after behavioral effects rather than declaring the behavioral effect complete in advance.

Spending has a parallel problem.

A dollar called investment is not automatically productive.

A dollar called waste is not automatically removable without consequence.

The classification has to survive evidence.

This is why adjustment requires institutions capable of evaluation rather than only slogans about size.

Health spending deserves particular attention because the long-run federal budget is shaped not merely by how many people receive care but by the price and intensity of that care. Reducing health-cost growth without reducing useful health can improve the fiscal path in a way that a blunt benefit cut cannot.

That is hard policy.

Payment incentives, provider competition, drug prices, administrative cost, preventive care, and technology interact. Savings estimates are uncertain. Stakeholders adapt.

The difficulty does not make the category optional.

It makes simplistic promises less credible.

Defense poses an equally uncomfortable tradeoff. A country cannot choose its security environment from a budget spreadsheet. Cutting military spending can create fiscal room and strategic risk. Increasing it can be necessary and fiscally costly.

This is one reason a durable adjustment has to preserve a contingency margin rather than optimizing every dollar around a peaceful baseline.

The budget is partly a portfolio against bad futures.

A nation escapes the wall when it stops needing a perfect future to make the portfolio work.

Financial repression is tempting precisely because it appears to avoid visible bargaining. If regulations or central-bank policy hold real government borrowing costs below growth, the debt ratio can improve without one explicit tax bill labeled *debt reduction*.

History shows that this can be powerful.

It also shows the cost is distributed through savers, financial institutions, capital allocation, and inflation.

In a globally mobile modern system, strong repression can encourage capital to seek alternatives, raise political conflict, and weaken the very financial network that makes Treasuries cheap.

The modern United States has more to lose from degrading market openness than a closed postwar system did.

That does not mean prudential regulation should be weakened to preserve investor freedom. It means prudential rules should be justified by prudential goals rather than quietly turned into a captive-financing program.

The line matters most when fiscal pressure rises.

Default remains the least plausible voluntary exit because it destroys the asset that makes all the other exits easier. A Treasury security is not just a promise to pay. It is collateral, reserve asset, benchmark, liquidity instrument, and settlement convention.

Restructuring that promise would impose losses throughout the same institutions the government needs to keep financing the economy.

The United States has far cheaper options before it reaches that one.

That fact is reassuring.

It is also an argument for using the cheaper options while they are still choices.

The difference between adjustment and crisis is often not the amount of sacrifice.

It is who chooses the timing.
