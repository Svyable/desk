# How Nations Escape a Wall

There are only a few ways to reduce a debt burden, although political language makes them sound more numerous. A country can grow faster, collect more revenue, spend less, reduce the real value of what it owes through inflation, hold borrowing costs below growth for long enough that the denominator does some of the work, sell assets, restructure obligations, or change the timing of the debt so adjustment has more room. Most successful episodes use several of these at once.

The argument becomes unserious when one lever is sold as painless.

Growth comes closest. If productivity rises, incomes and tax receipts can increase without the same rise in hours worked or inflation, and a given stock of debt becomes smaller relative to the economy supporting it. That is why nearly every fiscal coalition eventually discovers growth. The disagreement begins when the slogan has to become policy.

Tax reform can improve incentives or damage them depending on what is taxed, at what rate, and from what starting point. Infrastructure can remove bottlenecks or become expensive patronage. Research can generate large spillovers without producing them on a congressional budget schedule. Immigration can expand the labor force and tax base while creating real distributional and political pressures. Regulatory reform can increase supply and also remove protections whose value becomes visible only after they are gone. Education can raise human capital, but calling a dollar educational does not establish its return.

Growth policy is policy, not a wish. A sustained productivity boom would change the fiscal problem substantially. It should not be written into the solution before it happens.

The same discipline applies to taxes. The United States can collect more revenue in a mechanical sense, but the design matters. A broad consumption tax can raise large sums and burden lower-income households unless offset. Higher income or corporate taxes affect behavior, avoidance, investment, and timing. Reducing tax expenditures can raise revenue through provisions that often function economically like spending. No tax is free of incidence simply because somebody else is named on the form.

At the scale of the long-run gap, narrow promises usually become broad choices. A serious plan should count the revenue that remains after plausible behavioral effects rather than declare every favored tax increase or tax cut self-financing in advance.

Spending has the same problem. Waste, fraud, improper payments, failed procurement, and obsolete programs should be attacked because a billion dollars saved is still a billion dollars. They are not, by themselves, a solution to structural deficits measured in trillions. The large categories eventually enter the argument: retirement benefits, health spending, defense, tax subsidies, benefit formulas, eligibility ages, and the revenues dedicated to them.

Those categories are difficult because people have built lives and institutions around them. A retiree cannot rebuild a career because a spreadsheet changed. A patient cannot defer necessary care until the budget improves. A country cannot choose its security environment because interest rates rose. The question is therefore not only what changes, but how much time people have to adjust.

The 2026 Social Security Trustees Report makes the value of time unusually concrete. Under the intermediate assumptions, the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund was projected to be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032, with a later date for the combined OASDI funds if they were hypothetically combined. Congress can change that path. The point here is not to prescribe a particular reform. It is that a change made years before a financing shortfall can be phased across cohorts, revenue sources, and benefit formulas. The same arithmetic confronted at the last possible moment becomes more abrupt because fewer years remain over which to spread it.

Time is a policy instrument before it becomes a financing constraint.

Inflation offers another route, and a dangerous temptation. Unexpected inflation reduces the real value of fixed nominal debt. Creditors receive the promised dollars and discover that the dollars buy less. But the refinancing cycle limits the trick. Bills roll quickly, notes mature, TIPS principal adjusts, indexed benefits respond, and investors who expect inflation demand compensation in new yields. Inflation can cheapen yesterday's fixed-rate promise while making tomorrow's borrowing more expensive.

That makes deliberate inflation a poor escape strategy for a country whose financing advantage depends heavily on trust in the currency and the Treasury market. The first surprise may transfer wealth. A policy of surprises is eventually priced.

Financial repression is a quieter version of the same temptation. Historically, governments have used interest-rate ceilings, capital controls, reserve requirements, directed credit, and regulations that channel domestic savings toward public debt. In the postwar period, combinations of regulated finance, inflation, growth, and primary fiscal restraint helped several advanced economies reduce large debt burdens.

Modern prudential rules can also create demand for government securities for legitimate reasons. Banks need liquid assets. Pensions need duration. Central banks need instruments with which to conduct policy. The line matters when regulation is shaped primarily to make government borrowing cheaper by restricting investors' alternatives. A policy can reduce the visible coupon while shifting costs into savers' returns, capital allocation, currency confidence, or financial openness.

The United States has more to lose from damaging an open, liquid Treasury market than a more closed financial system would. Its market structure is part of the asset.

Debt management can buy time without repairing the primary budget. Treasury can change the bill share, extend duration, issue floating-rate notes or TIPS, alter auction sizes, and use buybacks to improve liquidity and cash management. Each move redistributes uncertainty. More bills make refinancing frequent and the budget more sensitive to short rates. More long bonds reduce rollover frequency while asking investors to absorb more duration, often at a higher yield. Floating-rate notes postpone principal maturity without fixing the coupon. TIPS share inflation risk differently.

These are useful tools precisely because there is no maturity mix that wins in every future. They change when pressure arrives and who bears it. They do not create the taxes, spending restraint, or productive capacity needed to close a structural fiscal gap.

Asset sales are similarly real and limited. Governments own land, buildings, spectrum, enterprises, loans, mineral rights, and other assets. Selling or leasing an asset can make sense when another owner can use it better or when the public no longer has a reason to hold it. A one-time sale does not cure a recurring deficit unless the transaction improves the continuing balance sheet rather than merely converting property into cash.

Default or restructuring belongs on the list because it is mathematically an exit and, for the United States, economically one of the worst available. Treasury securities are not merely claims on tax revenue. They are reserve assets, collateral, benchmark rates, liquidity instruments, and components of banks, funds, pensions, insurers, derivatives, and payment systems. Deliberately impairing those obligations would reduce the legal debt burden by damaging the infrastructure that makes the remaining debt unusually easy to finance.

The more relevant U.S. danger is an accidental payment disruption around the debt limit or an operational failure. Even a short disturbance would attack a quality investors normally do not have to price into Treasuries: uncertainty over nominal payment when the government has the economic capacity to pay.

There is one route that sounds like doing nothing: wait. Waiting can work when the primary deficit is modest, nominal growth is healthy, and the effective interest rate remains low enough relative to growth that the debt ratio falls or stabilizes without a dramatic fiscal package. It was part of the postwar U.S. story. Waiting is not a policy when the arithmetic runs the other way. Then it is compounding.

History is useful because it shows combinations rather than miracles. After World War II, the United States did not write one giant check to retire the debt. Rapid nominal growth, periods of inflation, the end of wartime spending, primary fiscal restraint relative to the war years, favorable demographics, and a more regulated financial system worked together. Canada in the 1990s combined expenditure restraint, institutional change, growth, and favorable interest-rate developments. Sweden's early-1990s crisis was followed by reforms to budget institutions and fiscal rules. The United States moved from large deficits in the early 1990s to surpluses late in the decade through policy changes, strong growth, lower defense spending after the Cold War, favorable demographics, and unusually strong revenue from asset markets.

None is a U.S. recipe for the 2020s. Canada and Sweden are smaller countries with different political and monetary arrangements. Postwar America occupied a different demographic and industrial position. The 1990s benefited from conditions that cannot be legislated back into existence. The useful fact is narrower: fiscal paths can bend when policy and macroeconomic conditions reinforce one another, and rules become durable only after a political coalition has decided what it is willing to constrain.

That is why numerical rules by themselves are weak medicine. A ceiling repeatedly suspended is less constraining than a modest process lawmakers expect to follow. A ten-year reform scheduled entirely for someone else's Congress has little value if markets and voters assume it will be repealed. Durability is part of the arithmetic.

Early adjustment helps because it compounds in the favorable direction. A smaller primary deficit means less new debt. Less new debt means less future interest. A more credible supply path can reduce part of the premium investors demand at the margin. The effects are not guaranteed and should not be exaggerated, but the mechanism is real.

Late adjustment carries an additional tax: urgency. Governments in crisis sell assets when prices are poor, tighten policy during recessions, raise taxes when households are weak, and accept financing terms set by frightened markets or outside institutions. Even when the eventual accounting adjustment resembles what an earlier plan might have required, the human experience can be much harsher because the timing is no longer chosen.

This is the strongest argument for gradualism, not an argument for timidity. Changes made too slowly can be overtaken by interest compounding. Changes made too abruptly can damage growth and destroy the coalition needed to sustain them. A fiscal reform that survives matters more than an elegant package that collapses after one election.

That tends to mean distributing the adjustment. Broad bases rather than symbolic levies on tiny groups. Changes to retirement and health systems that give people time to adapt. Protection for households least able to absorb abrupt losses. Spending reviews that distinguish programs by evidence rather than by whether advocates call them investment or waste. Growth policies whose returns are tested rather than assumed. Enough contingency room that a recession, war, pandemic, or security emergency does not immediately break the plan.

The budget is partly a portfolio against bad futures. A nation has escaped the wall when it no longer needs every future to be favorable for the portfolio to work.

That is a higher standard than balancing a median forecast. A plan that succeeds only if rates fall two percentage points, productivity booms, unemployment stays low, and no major emergency occurs is a parlay. A more resilient path can absorb at least some disappointment without converting every shock into a new debt regime.

The United States still has a luxury many countries receive only after losing it: choice. Its auctions clear. The dollar remains central. The investor base is vast. The economy is productive. Treasury can manage maturity. The tax and spending systems can be changed through ordinary politics. The Federal Reserve retains credibility and tools that can protect market functioning without being assigned the job of hiding fiscal costs.

Those advantages are not evidence that adjustment can be postponed forever. They are the resources with which adjustment can be made before markets or a legal deadline dictate the sequence.

That is what an escape looks like in practice. Not one heroic lever and not a clean repayment of the national debt, but a combination that improves the primary balance, protects productive growth, manages refinancing risk, preserves monetary credibility and market liquidity, and leaves enough room for the next event nobody forecast.

The difference between adjustment and crisis is often not the amount of sacrifice. It is who chooses the timing.
