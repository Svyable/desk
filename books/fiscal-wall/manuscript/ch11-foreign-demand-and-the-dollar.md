# Foreign Demand and the Dollar

A country that borrows in the world's dominant reserve currency has a privilege no household analogy can capture.

The United States can issue a security in New York and find natural buyers in Tokyo, London, Zurich, Singapore, Riyadh, Seoul, Toronto, Frankfurt, and dozens of other financial centers. Some buyers want yield. Some want liquidity. Some need collateral. Some manage national reserves. Some hedge dollar liabilities. Some simply need an asset large enough that billions of dollars can move without transforming the market around them.

This global demand is one reason the Treasury market became the Treasury market.

It is also why arguments about foreign financing so often go wrong.

The frightening version is easy to tell.

America depends on foreigners to fund its deficits.

Foreign countries become tired of American fiscal irresponsibility.

They dump Treasuries.

Interest rates spike.

The dollar collapses.

The fiscal wall arrives.

Each step can happen under some conditions.

The chain is not automatic.

Foreign holdings are the result of an international monetary system, not a favor extended to Washington.

To see why, begin with trade.

The dollar is used far beyond U.S. borders. Commodities are often priced in dollars. International companies borrow in dollars. Banks fund dollar assets. Central banks hold dollars to intervene in currency markets and provide liquidity during stress. Global investors measure performance against dollar assets. Cross-border contracts are written in dollars even when neither party is American.

A world that uses dollars needs places to hold dollars.

Bank deposits are one option.

Agency securities are another.

Corporate bonds and equities offer more risk.

Treasury securities provide an unmatched combination of size, liquidity, legal clarity, maturity diversity, and perceived credit quality.

The reserve currency and the Treasury market reinforce each other.

This is the machinery behind what has often been called the dollar's “exorbitant privilege.”

The phrase should not imply that the benefit is free.

A reserve currency issuer must provide assets to the world.

Persistent foreign demand can make it easier for the United States to run current-account deficits and borrow cheaply. That can support consumption and investment. It can also contribute to a stronger currency than exporters might prefer and reduce market discipline on fiscal policy.

Privilege can encourage dependence on the privilege.

The fiscal wall tests how durable the bargain is.

Foreign official institutions are the most politically visible holders.

China's Treasury holdings became a recurring source of anxiety as its reserves grew in the 2000s. Commentators asked whether Beijing could use the portfolio as a weapon. Japan has long been another very large holder. Oil exporters, Asian reserve managers, and other central banks accumulated Treasuries through periods of intervention and reserve growth.

The weapon story has an obvious problem.

Selling a reserve asset changes the seller's portfolio too.

If a central bank sells Treasuries for dollars, it now holds dollars unless it uses them to buy something else. If it sells dollars for another currency, the exchange rate moves. If the transaction is large enough, it can appreciate the alternative currency and reduce the value of the seller's remaining dollar assets. The set of alternative sovereign markets large enough to absorb very large reserve shifts is limited.

This does not make diversification impossible.

It makes it a portfolio problem rather than a button marked *hurt America*.

Reserve managers can reduce the Treasury share gradually. They can buy gold. They can hold more euros, yen, sterling, Canadian dollars, Australian dollars, or other reserve assets. They can shift maturity. They can hold deposits or agency securities. Some countries can encourage bilateral trade in their own currencies.

At the margin, these choices matter.

The dollar's share of disclosed global reserves has declined over decades from earlier peaks, even while the dollar remains dominant. Gold purchases by central banks have attracted attention. Sanctions have made some governments more conscious of the geopolitical risk of holding reserves inside Western financial systems.

A serious fiscal-wall analysis should not dismiss these trends.

It should put them in scale.

Reserve currency status is not a winner-take-all trophy awarded permanently to one country.

It is a network.

Networks can erode slowly.

They are also sticky.

An alternative reserve asset needs more than a large economy behind it. It needs deep and open capital markets, reliable institutions, a large supply of safe assets, convertibility, predictable law, and a willingness to let foreigners hold claims on the domestic economy at scale.

The euro has many of these qualities but does not have one unified federal sovereign bond market comparable in size and institutional simplicity to Treasuries. Japan has a large government bond market but a different economic and currency role. China's economy is huge, but capital controls and institutional structure limit the renminbi's reserve role relative to its economic weight. Gold is nobody's liability, which is precisely its attraction, but it does not provide the same yield, settlement infrastructure, or capacity for modern collateral markets.

The dollar does not remain dominant because every alternative is bad.

It remains dominant because replacing an international monetary network is harder than criticizing it.

Private foreign demand is at least as important as official reserve management.

A Japanese insurer can buy Treasuries because U.S. yields are attractive relative to domestic bonds after considering currency hedging costs.

A European bank can hold Treasuries as liquid assets.

A sovereign wealth fund can use them as the defensive part of a diversified portfolio.

A multinational company can park dollars in bills.

A foreign hedge fund can trade the Treasury basis.

A pension fund can seek duration.

These institutions react to price more than politics.

That can make foreign demand surprisingly elastic.

Suppose U.S. yields rise because Treasury issues more debt.

All else equal, higher yields make Treasuries more attractive to global investors.

But all else rarely stays equal.

If the rise reflects stronger U.S. growth, the dollar may strengthen and capital may flow in.

If it reflects inflation fears, foreign investors may worry about real returns.

If it reflects fiscal risk and a weaker dollar outlook, currency losses can offset the yield advantage.

If hedging dollars back into the investor's home currency becomes expensive, a nominally high Treasury yield can disappear after the hedge.

The foreign buyer sees a different yield from the American buyer.

Currency hedging is the hidden variable in many international Treasury flows.

An investor in Japan does not necessarily care about a five percent Treasury yield if the cost of hedging dollars into yen consumes most of it. Another investor may choose not to hedge and accept exchange-rate risk. A reserve manager may have dollar liabilities or policy reasons that make the hedge unnecessary.

This diversity keeps foreign demand from behaving as one block.

It also means the Treasury cannot assume that a higher yield always attracts the same amount of foreign money.

Relative policy rates matter.

Volatility matters.

Currency expectations matter.

Domestic opportunities abroad matter.

One of the biggest changes in the global environment is Japan.

For decades, Japanese interest rates were extraordinarily low. Japanese institutions looked abroad for yield, including to U.S. fixed-income markets. As Japan's own monetary regime evolves and domestic yields become more attractive, the relative incentive to send capital overseas can change.

This does not imply a mass liquidation of Treasuries.

It means one source of global savings can become less mechanically outward-looking.

Europe faces another set of changes.

Greater defense spending, energy investment, and infrastructure needs can increase European sovereign issuance. If more high-quality euro-denominated debt becomes available, global investors have more alternatives to Treasuries.

Again, competition is not abandonment.

The United States is entering a refinancing cycle in a world where other borrowers are asking for more capital too.

This raises the importance of the term premium.

A global investor choosing among long-dated sovereign bonds is deciding where to bear duration. More public borrowing across advanced economies can require higher real yields or term premiums if the supply of desired savings does not rise in proportion.

The old idea of a global savings glut may weaken at the same time fiscal borrowing needs grow.

Or it may not.

Aging populations can increase demand for safe retirement assets. Wealth can continue accumulating. Regulatory systems can require liquid government securities. Risk aversion can rise. Productivity gains can increase global saving.

The balance between safe-asset supply and demand is not predetermined.

That uncertainty is why foreign ownership statistics should be read cautiously.

A decline in the foreign share of Treasuries can occur even while foreigners buy more in dollar terms if domestic holdings grow faster. A country can reduce reported Treasury holdings while shifting to custodial locations that obscure beneficial ownership. Official and private categories can move for technical reasons. Valuation changes can affect reported positions.

The Treasury International Capital system and other official data are essential, but no single monthly release reveals geopolitical intent.

Flows require context.

The dollar itself can behave in ways that confound crisis intuition.

When global risk rises, investors often seek dollars and Treasuries.

This happened during the global financial crisis and again in parts of the pandemic shock. The country at the center of a financial disturbance can sometimes see its currency strengthen because the world needs its safe assets.

This is one of the strangest advantages of issuing the reserve currency.

American stress can create demand for American money.

That pattern is not guaranteed.

A crisis specifically about U.S. inflation credibility, institutional breakdown, or deliberate repudiation could produce different flows. If investors came to fear that the real value of dollar claims would be systematically diluted, demand for alternatives could accelerate.

The distinction is between a global risk-off event and a dollar-confidence event.

Treasuries tend to benefit from the first.

The second is the tail risk fiscal pessimists worry about.

How would we know the difference was beginning?

Not from one country selling.

Not from one bad auction.

Not from a one-month drop in foreign holdings.

A structural loss of confidence would likely appear across several markets.

Long-term real yields and term premiums might rise without a corresponding improvement in growth expectations.

The dollar could weaken even as U.S. yields rose.

Foreign reserve composition could move away from dollar assets persistently.

Treasury auctions might require larger concessions across maturities.

Inflation compensation could become less anchored.

Private foreign demand could weaken even after adjusting for hedging costs.

Alternatives could gain market depth and collateral status.

No single indicator would announce *reserve currency decline*.

Networks fail by losing convenience one connection at a time.

The United States has agency in this process.

Reserve status depends partly on economics and partly on institutions.

Deep markets matter.

Open capital accounts matter.

The rule of law matters.

Central-bank credibility matters.

Political stability matters.

Reliable payment of government obligations matters.

A debt-limit accident would therefore be more damaging than its immediate cash amount suggests. The entire value proposition of Treasury securities rests on being the asset investors do not have to wonder about in nominal payment terms.

Using that certainty as leverage in domestic politics imposes a cost on an international public good the United States itself benefits from.

Likewise, inflation policy matters because reserve managers care about real purchasing power.

A modest amount of inflation is normal in a fiat-currency system. Persistent surprise inflation functions as a tax on nominal creditors. If it becomes a strategy rather than an outcome, global holders can demand more yield or diversify.

The dollar's privilege is therefore a form of trust capital.

Trust capital can finance a remarkable amount.

It should not be treated as free equity.

There is a bullish reading of foreign demand that deserves respect.

The world's financial system continues to grow. More trade, more wealth, more derivatives, more regulated balance sheets, and more collateralized finance can create more demand for high-quality liquid dollar assets. The United States may issue more Treasuries and discover that global demand expands with supply because the securities themselves make markets easier to operate.

Safe assets can create their own uses.

There is a bearish reading too.

If debt grows faster than the economic capacity behind it, if inflation remains volatile, if political institutions repeatedly threaten payment, and if alternative financial networks deepen, investors may require a larger premium for holding U.S. duration. The dollar can remain dominant while the fiscal advantage of dominance shrinks.

These outcomes are not mutually exclusive.

The dollar can keep first place and become less special.

That may be enough to matter.

The fiscal wall does not require a dramatic end to dollar hegemony.

A rise of fifty or one hundred basis points in the long-run financing premium on a debt stock of this size would be consequential even if every central bank still called the dollar its primary reserve currency.

The question is not whether foreigners abandon America.

It is whether the international monetary system continues to subsidize American time at the same price.

For decades, the answer was unusually favorable.

The refinancing cycle will ask again.

The privilege contains a paradox that is easy to miss from inside the United States.

The world wants dollar assets partly because the United States supplies so many of them.

A small country with pristine public finances cannot create a reserve market merely by having no debt. It may lack enough securities for central banks, dealers, derivatives users, and global institutions to hold at scale. Liquidity requires outstanding supply. Benchmark markets require repeated issuance. Collateral networks require a large stock.

Some public debt is not merely tolerated by the dollar system.

It is infrastructure for the dollar system.

This complicates the intuition that every additional Treasury security necessarily weakens reserve status. More supply can deepen the market and satisfy global demand for safe assets. The danger appears when supply expands faster than the convenience value and economic capacity supporting it.

The fiscal wall therefore involves a balance rather than a maximum.

Too little safe collateral can make global finance less efficient.

Too much duration at the old price can require a higher yield.

This is close to the old dilemma that reserve-currency countries face between supplying international liquidity and maintaining confidence in the liabilities being supplied. The modern dollar system is far more complex than the fixed-exchange-rate world in which the original dilemma was framed, but the tension survives in a new form.

The world wants dollar claims.

The world also wants those claims to remain reliably valuable.

Foreign demand can finance persistent U.S. external deficits because dollars sent abroad through trade and investment do not disappear. They become somebody's asset. A foreign exporter paid in dollars can exchange them through a bank, invest them, or ultimately help create a chain that ends in U.S. securities. Current-account deficits and capital inflows are two sides of international accounting.

This is why slogans about foreigners “lending America its own money” miss the network. Dollars are liabilities of the U.S. monetary system circulating through global balance sheets. Foreign institutions choose among American assets because the currency itself is heavily used.

The Treasury market sits at the safe end of that menu.

That connection creates a stabilizer during ordinary diversification.

Suppose a reserve manager sells a Treasury note and buys a U.S. agency security. Treasury's direct foreign ownership falls, but the dollar system has not been abandoned.

Suppose it buys a U.S. corporate bond. The private issuer receives the financing instead of Treasury.

Suppose it buys a euro-area government bond and sells the dollars. The exchange rate may move, making dollar assets more attractive to another investor.

Portfolios rebalance through prices.

The system is not a referendum with one ballot per country.

This is why reported country holdings require humility. A Treasury held through a custodian in Belgium or the United Kingdom may ultimately belong to an institution elsewhere. Securities can be lent. Derivatives can create synthetic exposure. Official reserve managers can hold assets through accounts that do not map neatly onto political borders.

The data are good enough to reveal large trends and too imperfect to support conspiracy theories about one monthly change.

The June 2026 Treasury International Capital release is useful precisely as a snapshot rather than a prophecy. It showed sizable net foreign purchases of long-term U.S. securities and positive net capital flows in that month. The observation does not prove that foreign demand will absorb any future issuance at any yield.

It does show that the story of a world already refusing American assets was false at that moment.

The future question is marginal.

What happens to the next hundred billion dollars of duration?

A foreign buyer compares it with domestic bonds, other currencies, private credit, equities, and the cost of hedging. If domestic yields in Japan rise, the comparison changes. If European governments issue more debt for defense and infrastructure, the comparison changes. If U.S. inflation falls while real yields remain attractive, the comparison can move back toward Treasuries.

The dollar's reserve role sets the table.

Relative returns still decide many of the seats.

Sanctions add a harder dimension.

The ability of the United States and its allies to immobilize or restrict access to assets inside their financial jurisdiction demonstrated that reserve holdings have political as well as market risk for certain governments. Some official institutions have responded by emphasizing gold or non-dollar settlement arrangements.

That reaction is rational from their perspective even if the alternatives are less liquid.

The fiscal question is whether geopolitical diversification becomes broad enough to raise the price Treasury pays.

It does not need to dethrone the dollar.

A reserve manager that shifts five percent of a portfolio away from Treasuries still holds ninety-five percent of the original position. If enough large institutions make similar marginal shifts while U.S. issuance grows, the long-term demand curve can change.

Marginal changes matter on a thirty-trillion-dollar base.

The reverse can happen too. Geopolitical instability can increase demand for the most liquid dollar assets among institutions that do not fear U.S. sanctions. A fragmented world can make the dollar network more valuable to some users and less attractive to others.

There is no single geopolitical sign on Treasury demand.

This is another reason the reserve-currency debate should be separated from the refinancing debate even though they interact.

Reserve status is slow-moving institutional capital.

Refinancing is a continuous market price.

The first can remain stable while the second becomes more expensive.

That is probably the more realistic fiscal risk.

Imagine the dollar remains the world's leading reserve currency in 2035. Treasuries remain the premier collateral asset. Foreign central banks still hold enormous dollar reserves. No competitor has built a market with comparable scale.

Yet investors require a larger term premium because U.S. debt supply is higher, global investment opportunities are better, inflation uncertainty is greater, and foreign institutions have more alternatives.

Nothing dramatic happened to dollar hegemony.

The federal interest bill is still much larger.

This is why the book does not need a reserve-currency collapse to make the wall consequential.

The privilege can erode in price before it erodes in rank.

A fifty-basis-point increase in the structural financing premium sounds minor beside predictions of currency collapse. Applied gradually across trillions of dollars of refinancing, it is not minor to a budget.

The quieter outcome is more plausible and more useful to monitor.

Watch whether foreign buyers continue to take long duration when hedging costs are unfavorable.

Watch whether reserve managers diversify gradually or abruptly.

Watch whether the dollar rises or falls when long Treasury yields rise.

Watch whether foreign demand shifts from official to private institutions.

Watch whether alternative sovereign markets become deeper and more standardized.

Watch whether Treasury liquidity remains exceptional during stress.

Reserve status is not a binary variable.

It is a collection of advantages.

The United States can lose some and remain dominant.

It can preserve them and still pay more.

The fiscal wall lives in that difference.
