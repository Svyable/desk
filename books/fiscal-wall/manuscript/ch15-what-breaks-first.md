# What Breaks First

A fiscal crisis is easier to imagine than to sequence.

The popular version begins with a number.

Debt reaches a threshold.

Investors panic.

Treasury cannot refinance.

The dollar falls.

The Federal Reserve prints money.

Inflation rises.

Something breaks.

Real crises rarely respect the order of a paragraph.

They move through whichever part of the system is least able to absorb the next shock.

That is why the useful question is not *when does America hit the wall?*

It is *what would fail first if the refinancing system came under pressure?*

The answer may not be the federal government's ability to make a coupon payment.

The United States has tools that make a conventional foreign-currency sovereign crisis unlikely. It issues in dollars. It has a central bank capable of providing liquidity. Treasury securities sit at the center of domestic and global finance. The tax base is enormous. The legal and institutional capacity to change policy exists.

The weaker links are often one step away from the sovereign.

Market liquidity.

Dealer balance sheets.

Leveraged funds.

Banks holding duration.

Pension hedges.

The political debt limit.

Inflation expectations.

The exchange rate.

Private credit markets that reprice off Treasuries.

The fiscal wall becomes dangerous when stress in one link forces decisions that worsen another.

Consider the least dramatic scenario first.

Nothing breaks.

Auctions continue clearing. Investors demand a little more yield as supply rises. The average interest rate on federal debt increases. Net interest consumes more revenue. Congress responds slowly with some combination of taxes, spending restraint, and growth measures. The debt ratio stabilizes at a high level.

This outcome can be painful without being a crisis.

It may also be the most likely form of adjustment.

A wall experienced at walking speed feels like a hill.

Now add a weak auction.

Treasury sells a large long-dated issue. Demand at the prevailing when-issued price is poor. The auction tails several basis points. Dealers take more than expected. Long yields rise across the curve.

This is not a failed funding event.

The government has borrowed.

The price changed.

If subsequent auctions stabilize, the event becomes another data point.

If the pattern repeats, investors begin asking whether the supply of duration is outrunning the natural buyer base.

Term premiums rise.

Mortgage rates rise with them.

Corporate borrowing costs increase.

Equities revalue.

The economy slows at the margin.

Tax receipts soften.

The fiscal outlook worsens slightly.

More borrowing is required than expected.

This is the first adverse loop.

It does not require panic.

Price does the work.

Now add dealer constraints.

A large auction leaves dealers with inventory while volatility is elevated. Balance-sheet capacity is already occupied. Hedge-fund clients reduce positions. Repo financing becomes more expensive. Dealers widen bid-ask spreads and reduce the size they are willing to quote.

Market depth falls.

Treasuries still trade, but a given order moves price more.

An asset manager needing cash sells into a thinner market.

Yields jump.

Other investors sell because volatility limits are breached.

The move feeds itself.

This resembles the mechanics of March 2020 more than a sovereign default.

The Federal Reserve has tools for this problem.

Repo operations can provide financing.

The standing repo facility can backstop eligible counterparties.

The Fed can purchase Treasuries to restore market functioning.

Those interventions can stop a liquidity spiral without changing the fundamental fiscal path.

That distinction is crucial.

A central bank can fix market plumbing faster than it can fix a primary deficit.

If the plumbing breaks once during an extraordinary shock, intervention is a sign of resilience.

If it breaks repeatedly under ordinary issuance because private balance-sheet capacity cannot handle the supply, the fiscal problem has entered market structure.

Now add leverage.

A hedge fund has financed a large Treasury basis position in repo. The cash bond and futures contract have moved apart. Volatility has risen. The fund receives margin calls from futures counterparties while repo lenders demand more collateral.

The trade may still be profitable at convergence.

It can fail before convergence because liquidity is required now.

The fund sells Treasuries to raise cash.

Other funds running the same strategy do the same.

Dealers receive more inventory while their own balance sheets are constrained.

Yields rise further.

Margin calls increase.

The system discovers that a buyer class absorbing Treasury supply in calm markets was actually a leveraged intermediary whose demand reversed under stress.

Again, the federal government has not missed a payment.

The financing ecosystem has become procyclical.

Now add a bank.

The bank holds a portfolio of long fixed-rate securities purchased when yields were low. Rates have risen. The securities are still money-good at maturity, but their market value has fallen.

If deposits are stable, the bank can wait.

If depositors leave, the bank needs liquidity. Selling securities crystallizes losses and reduces capital. Borrowing against them can provide time, but counterparties and depositors may question solvency if the loss is large enough.

This was one of the lessons of the 2023 regional bank failures.

The trigger was not federal credit risk.

It was duration and funding mismatch.

A government that issues more long debt to protect taxpayers from refinancing risk can therefore increase the amount of duration the private system has to place somewhere. Pensions and insurers may be ideal holders. Banks with flighty funding may be less ideal.

The location of the duration matters.

Now add the debt limit.

Unlike the previous scenarios, this stress is legal and political rather than market-generated.

Congress has authorized spending and taxes that produce a deficit, but the statutory limit prevents Treasury from issuing enough debt to finance the obligations once extraordinary measures and cash are exhausted.

The Treasury market begins pricing the possibility that a particular bill maturing around the projected date will be delayed. Money funds avoid vulnerable maturities. Yields on those securities rise relative to nearby bills. Market infrastructure prepares for an event it was designed never to see.

This is the strangest way the fiscal wall could break because it is self-imposed.

The United States can have the economic capacity to pay and still create payment uncertainty through law.

A technical default or delayed payment, even if quickly cured, could damage the assumption that Treasury securities are operationally riskless. The immediate dollar amount might be small relative to the debt stock. The institutional cost could be larger.

Now add inflation.

Suppose the government responds to rising interest costs with large new deficits while the economy is already near capacity. Suppose investors begin to believe future fiscal policy will remain expansionary and the Federal Reserve will eventually be pressured to accommodate it.

Long-term inflation compensation rises.

The dollar weakens.

Long yields rise even as growth expectations do not.

Treasury's financing cost increases.

Higher interest enlarges deficits.

The Fed faces a choice between tighter policy, which worsens near-term fiscal costs, and easier policy, which may validate inflation fears.

This is the fiscal-dominance scenario.

It is much more serious than a weak auction because the central bank's normal stabilizer becomes part of the conflict.

The United States is not in that state merely because debt is high or interest is expensive.

The diagnostic sign would be a loss of confidence that monetary policy is willing and able to preserve price stability despite fiscal pressure.

Institutional credibility is the circuit breaker.

Now add the currency.

A weaker dollar can help exporters and raise the dollar value of foreign earnings. It can also increase import prices and add to inflation. Foreign investors holding unhedged Treasuries can suffer currency losses that exceed their coupon income.

If the dollar falls because the Federal Reserve is easing into a healthy disinflation, the move may be benign.

If it falls because investors are reducing exposure to U.S. assets while long yields rise, the signal is more concerning.

The combination of a falling currency and rising long-term yields is a classic warning in countries facing confidence crises.

For the United States, reserve-currency demand makes the mechanism less direct.

Global stress often strengthens the dollar.

That is why a true dollar-confidence event would represent a regime change rather than ordinary risk-off behavior.

Now consider private borrowers.

Treasury can pay whatever yield the market requires more easily than almost any private borrower because it can tax and issues the currency. A homeowner cannot. A leveraged company cannot. A commercial property owner cannot. A city cannot print dollars. A startup cannot compel revenue.

If Treasury yields rise to attract enough buyers, private borrowing costs rise too.

The risk-free rate is the foundation beneath credit.

A five-percent Treasury plus a three-percent corporate spread is eight-percent borrowing.

A higher mortgage rate changes the monthly payment on the same house.

A municipal project that worked at three percent may not work at six.

A private-equity deal modeled on cheap leverage can fail to refinance.

The federal financing problem can therefore break private balance sheets before it breaks Treasury.

This is perhaps the most plausible near-term failure sequence.

The government keeps borrowing.

The price required for that borrowing tightens financial conditions.

Weak private borrowers discover they cannot refinance.

Defaults rise.

Banks take losses.

Growth slows.

Tax revenue falls.

The deficit increases through automatic stabilizers and weaker receipts.

Treasury issues more debt into the slowdown.

The Federal Reserve cuts rates if inflation permits, relieving the system.

If inflation does not permit, the policy tradeoff becomes harder.

The wall has moved from Washington into the economy and back again.

This sequencing explains why crisis prediction based on one debt threshold is so unreliable.

The system has buffers.

Higher yields attract buyers.

The Fed provides liquidity.

Banks hold capital.

Treasury can change issuance composition.

Congress can change policy.

The dollar draws safe-haven flows.

Private borrowers can deleverage.

Those buffers can stop a chain at many points.

A crisis occurs when several buffers fail or conflict at once.

This is what happened in many historical financial crises. One vulnerability was tolerable. Two interacted. Then the response to the second worsened the first.

The 2022 U.K. gilt episode combined a fiscal surprise, a sharp long-yield move, leveraged pension strategies, collateral calls, and central-bank intervention during an inflation fight.

March 2020 combined a global dash for cash, dealer constraints, leveraged Treasury trades, mutual-fund selling, and an economic emergency.

The 2008 financial crisis combined mortgage losses, leverage, short-term funding, opaque securitization, and institutional distrust.

The lesson is not that the United States is about to repeat any one of them.

It is that financial systems break through interaction.

The fiscal wall supplies one large source of pressure: persistent issuance and repricing.

The rest of the system determines whether that pressure remains ordinary.

What should we watch?

Auction performance across time, not one sale.

Treasury market depth and bid-ask spreads.

Dealer inventories and balance-sheet capacity.

Repo rates relative to policy benchmarks.

Leverage in relative-value trades.

Central-clearing transition metrics.

Bank unrealized losses and funding stability.

The term premium and long-term real yields.

Inflation expectations.

The dollar's behavior relative to yields.

Foreign and domestic buyer composition.

Federal interest relative to revenue.

The primary deficit.

Debt-limit politics.

No single indicator is the wall.

Together they reveal whether the system is absorbing the load.

There is a final scenario worth including because it is easy to overlook.

What if the thing that breaks first is political patience?

Markets may continue functioning. Inflation may remain contained. The dollar may remain strong. Auctions may clear.

But interest expense can become large enough that voters and legislators refuse the mix of taxes and spending required to stabilize debt. One coalition rejects benefit changes. Another rejects tax increases. Another rejects defense cuts. Another rejects inflation. Every group is individually rational from its own perspective.

The aggregate budget becomes impossible to reconcile.

That is not a market crisis.

It is a governance crisis.

The fiscal wall may therefore be reached in Congress long before it is reached at a Treasury auction.

The country still has money.

It has run out of mutually acceptable choices.

That is why the most important buffer is not the standing repo facility or the Federal Reserve balance sheet.

It is the ability of a political system to adjust before markets force the timing.

If that ability holds, nothing may need to break.

If it fails, the first break could appear almost anywhere.

The sequence becomes easier to understand if we distinguish four kinds of failure that are often collapsed into one word: price failure, liquidity failure, balance-sheet failure, and policy failure.

Price failure is the mildest and most common. It is not failure in the sense of a market ceasing to function. It means the price required to clear has moved far enough to break somebody's business model or political assumption.

A Treasury yield rises from four to five percent. The auction works. A commercial property whose financing only penciled at three and a half no longer refinances. A venture project with distant cash flows is postponed. A municipality delays a bond-funded project. A homeowner stays in an existing house because the new mortgage payment is unattractive.

The sovereign is funded.

The old private price regime has failed.

This form of adjustment can do most of the economic work of a fiscal wall without producing one dramatic day.

Liquidity failure is faster. A Treasury security still has a fundamental value, but the market cannot move the required quantity at normal spreads. Dealer quotes become small. Repo becomes expensive. Funds sell into falling prices because they need cash rather than because they dislike the asset.

A liquidity failure can be reversible in hours or days if a central bank or end buyers provide balance sheet.

That is why emergency intervention in 2020 did not imply a permanent fiscal bailout. The Fed was restoring the ability to trade the benchmark asset, not declaring a fair-value yield for the next decade.

Balance-sheet failure happens when mark-to-market losses, funding outflows, or margin calls overwhelm an institution. The asset can be sound while the holder is not. Silicon Valley Bank and the U.K. LDI episode are different examples of the same broad principle: duration risk becomes dangerous when liabilities or collateral demands are less patient than the assets.

Policy failure is the slowest and potentially the most consequential. It occurs when institutions know what tradeoffs are required and cannot execute them without making another objective worse.

The Federal Reserve can restore Treasury liquidity, but inflation may prevent broad easing.

Treasury can shorten maturity, but the budget becomes more rate-sensitive.

Treasury can extend maturity, but the private sector demands a large term premium.

Congress can cut deficits, but poorly timed tightening can worsen a recession.

Congress can delay adjustment, but the interest bill compounds.

A system becomes dangerous when these failure types overlap.

Imagine a long-bond selloff begins as price discovery. Yields rise enough to trigger margin calls on leveraged positions. That becomes a balance-sheet problem. Forced sales widen bid-ask spreads and reduce market depth. That becomes a liquidity problem. The Fed wants to intervene, but inflation remains above target and elected officials accuse it simultaneously of bailing out investors and causing the deficit through high rates. That becomes a policy problem.

Nothing in this sequence requires Treasury to miss a coupon.

The wall has become systemic before it becomes a credit event.

The U.K. gilt episode is useful because it shows how quickly these categories can stack. Long yields moved after a fiscal announcement. Leveraged pension strategies faced collateral calls. Forced selling threatened market functioning. The Bank of England bought long gilts temporarily while emphasizing the operation's financial-stability purpose rather than a reversal of the broader inflation fight.

The lesson is not that the United States is Britain.

It is that a central bank can find itself protecting a sovereign bond market from a liquidation dynamic while refusing to validate the underlying price that triggered the liquidation.

That distinction would be harder in the United States because Treasuries are woven into more markets and because the scale is much larger.

A Treasury shock can move SOFR, futures, mortgage hedges, corporate spreads, bank portfolios, foreign exchange, and the collateral posted against derivatives. The first visible break could occur in a market far from the auction that started the move.

This is why off-the-run liquidity matters.

The newest Treasury issues trade most actively and often enjoy the deepest liquidity. Older securities remain enormous in aggregate but can trade less frequently. During stress, the difference between an on-the-run benchmark and an older off-the-run bond can widen. A holder needing to sell a less-liquid issue may face a larger concession than the headline benchmark yield suggests.

Treasury's buyback program can support the functioning of these older securities, but buybacks do not eliminate the stock. They help manage liquidity inside it.

A wall made of trillions of securities is only as strong as the less visible pieces when everyone wants cash.

Settlement is another quiet failure point.

A Treasury trade involves not only a buyer and seller but clearing, custody, cash, securities delivery, collateral substitution, and operational systems. Central clearing can reduce counterparty risk and improve netting, but it also concentrates operational importance. A cyber incident, processing error, or liquidity shortfall at a critical node could temporarily impair the market without changing one fiscal forecast.

This is not a reason to expect operational catastrophe.

It is a reminder that scale makes boring infrastructure a macroeconomic variable.

The fiscal wall is unusual because the government's financing machine and the financial system's collateral machine are the same machine.

That coupling creates resilience in normal times. A Treasury security is easy to own precisely because it is easy to finance, hedge, pledge, and settle.

The same coupling creates transmission in stress. A problem in one function spreads because the security is used everywhere.

The most important question in a stress episode is therefore not simply *are Treasury auctions clearing?*

It is whether the price change is being absorbed by investors who can hold it, or transmitted into institutions that must react immediately.

A pension fund buying more after yields rise can stabilize the market.

A leveraged fund facing margin can destabilize it.

A bank with stable funding can hold duration through a mark-to-market loss.

A bank with fleeing deposits cannot.

A foreign reserve manager can use volatility as an opportunity to rebalance.

A currency-hedged foreign investor may find the same yield unattractive because hedging costs have moved.

The identity of the marginal balance sheet determines the next link in the chain.

This is why a real warning system would look for co-movement rather than a magic threshold.

A weak auction by itself is noise.

A weak auction plus persistent dealer inventory plus widening repo spreads plus declining market depth is more informative.

Add rising margin requirements and forced selling, and the signal strengthens.

Add a falling dollar, rising inflation compensation, and political pressure on the Federal Reserve to cap yields, and the regime has changed materially.

The wall is interaction.

That is also the source of reassurance.

Because there is no single fuse, there are many places to interrupt the sequence.

Higher yields recruit new buyers.

Standing repo provides secured liquidity.

Central clearing can improve netting.

Banks can hold more capital and liquidity.

Treasury can adjust issuance gradually.

Congress can improve the fiscal path.

The Fed can separate market-functioning tools from its policy stance.

The dollar can attract global safe-haven demand.

A system with multiple buffers can absorb a large shock without reaching the end of the chain.

That is why the most useful question is not which disaster is inevitable.

It is which buffer is weakening before the others are forced to carry its load.
