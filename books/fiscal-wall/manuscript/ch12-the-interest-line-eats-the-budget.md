# The Interest Line Eats the Budget

A government budget is a list of promises competing for the same tax base.

Some promises are explicit.

A Social Security benefit formula.

A Medicare payment.

A defense contract.

A federal salary.

A grant to a state.

A tax credit.

Others are inherited.

Interest is the clearest example.

Congress does not sit down each January and ask whether paying interest on Treasury securities seems like a worthwhile new program. The payment follows from obligations already created. Past Congresses authorized spending and taxes. Treasury borrowed the difference. Debt managers chose maturities. The Federal Reserve and the economy helped determine the rate environment. Investors bought the securities.

Then time passed.

The coupon arrived.

This is what makes interest politically different from almost everything else in the budget.

It has no natural constituency demanding a ribbon cutting.

It produces no road, school, aircraft, laboratory, hospital, or retirement check in the year it is paid.

It is the cost of financing things that already happened.

By 2026, that cost had become one of the largest lines in the federal budget.

The Congressional Budget Office projected net interest outlays above one trillion dollars for the fiscal year. Under its February 2026 baseline, net interest continued rising through the decade, reaching roughly $2.1 trillion by 2036.

A projection is not a bill.

Rates can be lower than expected. Growth can be stronger. Fiscal policy can change. Inflation and revenue can surprise. Congress can alter the path of major programs and taxes.

But the baseline reveals the mechanism.

Debt is no longer an abstraction whose carrying cost can be ignored because rates are near zero.

Interest now competes visibly with the rest of government.

The word *competes* needs care.

The federal budget is not a fixed household envelope. Congress can raise taxes, cut taxes, increase spending, reduce spending, or borrow more. A one-dollar increase in interest does not mechanically force a one-dollar cut in education or defense that afternoon.

The competition happens through the intertemporal budget constraint.

If revenues and primary spending remain unchanged, higher interest increases the deficit.

A larger deficit requires more borrowing.

More borrowing creates more future interest.

Eventually, either revenues, primary spending, inflation, growth, or the debt ratio has to absorb the difference.

Interest is the line that turns delay into compounding.

To see this clearly, separate the **primary deficit** from the total deficit.

The primary deficit is the gap between government revenues and non-interest spending. The total deficit adds net interest.

This separation answers an important question.

Is the government borrowing mainly because current programs and taxes do not balance, or because it is paying for accumulated debt?

In the mid-2020s, the answer was both.

Large primary deficits continued while interest costs rose.

That combination is more difficult than either problem alone.

If the primary budget were balanced, the government could still see debt rise temporarily from interest, but stabilizing the debt ratio would be easier.

If interest rates were near zero, large primary deficits could accumulate for longer before debt service became painful.

With both primary deficits and high interest costs, the fiscal system feeds itself.

This is the arithmetic behind debt dynamics.

It is not a forecast of catastrophe.

A country can run deficits for long periods while its economy grows. The relevant question is whether debt grows faster than the income and revenue available to support it.

Debt-to-GDP is one imperfect way to express that relationship.

CBO projected debt held by the public at around 101 percent of GDP in 2026 and 120 percent by 2036 under current-law assumptions.

The ratio matters because national income is the broad base from which federal revenue ultimately comes.

But it should not be worshipped.

A debt ratio does not tell you maturity.

It does not tell you who holds the debt.

It does not tell you the currency composition.

It does not tell you the government's ability to tax.

It does not tell you the real interest rate, the growth rate, or the productivity of the spending financed.

Japan has sustained a far higher public debt ratio than many countries that experienced crises at much lower levels. Emerging markets that borrow heavily in foreign currency can face constraints the United States does not. Institutional credibility and monetary sovereignty matter.

Debt-to-GDP is a map scale, not a cliff.

The interest-to-revenue ratio can be more immediate.

If a growing share of federal receipts is committed to interest, less revenue is available for current programs before new borrowing begins. This is not because bondholders are morally prior to citizens. It is because maintaining Treasury creditworthiness is part of the infrastructure that lets the government finance every other commitment.

A country that casually repudiates interest payments does not save the coupon and move on.

It changes the price and availability of future credit, damages financial institutions holding the debt, disrupts collateral markets, and undermines the safe asset used throughout the economy.

The budget therefore treats interest as a claim that cannot be wished away.

Who receives the money?

This question complicates the politics.

Federal interest is not burned.

It becomes income.

A retired household owns a Treasury note through a mutual fund.

A pension fund receives coupons to pay beneficiaries.

A money-market fund earns bill income for savers.

A bank receives interest on a security in its liquidity portfolio.

An insurer uses bond income to support policy claims.

A foreign central bank receives dollars.

The Federal Reserve receives Treasury interest on its holdings, though the consolidated fiscal effect depends on the Fed's own interest expenses and remittances.

Interest payments redistribute resources from taxpayers and future borrowers to creditors.

Some creditors are the same taxpayers in another role.

This is why comparing net interest with a program like defense or Medicaid can illuminate scale without proving that interest is social waste.

Interest is the price paid to obtain resources earlier.

The question is whether the earlier use was worth the price.

Debt used to finance an emergency can prevent deeper economic loss.

Debt used to build productive infrastructure can raise future income.

Debt used to cut taxes can support incentives or consumption depending on design and conditions.

Debt used to postpone hard choices can simply move the burden.

The coupon does not remember the purpose.

It arrives all the same.

This is one reason interest changes political accountability.

A legislature can inherit the bill for policies it opposed.

A president can enter office with much of the fiscal path already determined by prior law, demographics, and accumulated debt.

Voters can demand current benefits while blaming current officials for the cost of financing previous ones.

The lag between borrowing and interest makes causality politically inconvenient.

When rates were low, this lag was forgiving.

Suppose Congress added a trillion dollars to the debt at a one-percent average cost. The annual interest was roughly ten billion dollars before compounding and maturity effects.

At five percent, the same trillion implies roughly fifty billion.

The political decision can be identical in principal and radically different in carrying cost.

This is why the fiscal wall is not just about how much America borrowed.

It is about the regime into which the borrowing rolls.

The federal budget has another feature that makes adjustment difficult: the largest programs are popular and structurally embedded.

Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, defense, veterans' benefits, and other major commitments cannot be changed casually. Their beneficiaries are real people and institutions with plans built around existing law. Tax expenditures are similarly embedded in household and business behavior. Mortgage deductions, retirement incentives, health exclusions, business provisions, and credits develop constituencies even when they do not appear as spending lines.

Interest grows into a budget that is already politically spoken for.

This is the difference between fiscal capacity and accounting capacity.

Accounting says the government can issue another Treasury security.

Fiscal capacity asks how many future claims the political system can honor without raising taxes, reducing benefits, accepting inflation, or crowding out useful public investment.

A country can retain market access while losing fiscal freedom.

That may be the more likely manifestation of the wall.

Imagine Congress debating a new scientific program with a cost of twenty billion dollars a year.

In a low-debt, low-interest environment, the argument can focus mostly on whether the program is useful and how it should be financed.

In a high-interest environment, another fact sits in the room: hundreds of billions of additional revenue are already being absorbed by debt service relative to an earlier baseline.

The science program has not become less valuable.

The opportunity cost has become more visible.

The same applies to defense.

The United States may face legitimate needs to modernize weapons, support allies, build shipyards, secure supply chains, and prepare for new forms of conflict. Those requirements do not disappear because interest expense rises.

They become harder to finance.

The same applies to climate adaptation, infrastructure, border security, child benefits, public health, and every other priority.

A rising interest line is ideologically neutral.

It crowds the agenda of both parties.

This is why fiscal politics eventually becomes a coalition problem rather than a bookkeeping problem.

One side can propose cutting domestic discretionary spending.

Another can propose raising taxes on high incomes or corporations.

The arithmetic may show that neither approach, by itself, reaches the scale of long-run adjustment if major mandatory programs and broad revenue remain untouched.

One side can promise growth.

Another can promise enforcement against waste, fraud, and abuse.

Both can contribute.

Neither becomes a trillion-dollar fiscal plan simply because the slogan is attractive.

The wall forces scale discipline.

A serious fiscal adjustment begins by admitting the size of the numbers.

That does not require one dramatic austerity package.

Gradual changes can be more durable and less economically damaging.

A retirement-age change phased in over decades affects expectations differently from an overnight benefit cut.

A tax reform that broadens the base can raise revenue with different incentive effects from simply increasing statutory rates.

Health-policy changes can target cost growth rather than benefits mechanically.

Public investment that raises productivity can improve the denominator instead of only shrinking the numerator.

Debt management can smooth refinancing risk.

The Federal Reserve can maintain price stability, protecting the real value of the tax base and the credibility of the currency.

The exact mix is political.

The requirement for a mix is arithmetic.

Higher interest also changes how quickly mistakes compound.

Suppose the government enacts a policy that permanently increases the primary deficit by one hundred billion dollars per year.

In the first year, the direct deficit effect is one hundred billion.

If financed with debt, the next year begins with that additional principal carrying interest. The second year's borrowing includes the new primary deficit plus the interest on the first year's debt. Over time, the cost grows.

At very low interest rates, the compounding is slow.

At higher rates, it becomes more material.

This changes the option value of delay.

A fiscal reform postponed for five years can cost substantially more than the sum of five annual primary deficits because the larger debt stock carries interest thereafter.

The same logic makes early overreaction dangerous.

Cutting productive public investment in the name of deficit reduction can lower future growth and tax revenue, making the debt ratio harder to stabilize. Raising taxes during a deep recession can worsen the downturn. Austerity is not automatically prudence.

The problem is not that the government must balance the budget every year.

The problem is that persistent structural deficits leave less room to borrow when borrowing is most valuable.

Fiscal capacity is a reserve.

You want it in a war.

You want it in a pandemic.

You want it after a financial crash.

You want it when a hurricane destroys a city or a technological opportunity requires public investment.

A high interest burden uses part of that reserve before the emergency begins.

This is the deepest cost of the fiscal wall.

Not default.

Not one failed auction.

Not a cinematic day when investors refuse to lend.

The cost is that the future becomes pre-allocated.

More tax revenue arrives already owed to yesterday.

More political debate begins with less room.

More monetary tightening feeds into fiscal costs.

More emergency borrowing starts from a higher base.

The interest line eats the budget slowly enough that a country can spend years pretending it is still deciding what to have for dinner.

By the time the plate looks empty, most of the meal has already been ordered.
