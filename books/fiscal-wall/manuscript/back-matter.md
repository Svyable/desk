# The Bond Market That Bit Back

In 1994, the United States learned how quickly a bond market can change its mind without producing a sovereign crisis.

The episode is remembered as the bond-market massacre.

The name is melodramatic and useful.

Treasury kept borrowing. The dollar remained the dollar. The federal government did not default. Yet bond investors suffered large losses as yields rose sharply across the developed world, leveraged positions failed, and institutions discovered that a long period of falling rates had encouraged more duration risk than they realized.

The parallel to the fiscal wall is not that 1994 will repeat.

The contrast is more instructive.

The federal debt burden was much smaller relative to the economy than it is today. Net interest was important but did not sit on top of a public debt stock measured around the size of annual GDP. The modern Treasury market is larger, more electronic, more globally integrated, and more deeply connected to leveraged nonbank finance.

If a 1994-style rate repricing occurred against the current balance sheet, the same market move would have a different fiscal echo.

The story began with an economy strengthening after the early-1990s recession. Inflation had come down substantially from the previous decade, and many investors were positioned for continued benign rates. The Federal Reserve then tightened monetary policy in a series of moves that surprised a market accustomed to a different path.

Bond yields rose.

Prices fell.

The losses spread beyond Treasury desks because duration was embedded throughout finance.

Mortgage markets were hit. Leveraged investors suffered. Orange County, California, later became one of the most famous casualties after a portfolio built with leverage and interest-rate exposure produced enormous losses. Global bond markets sold off as the change in U.S. rates and positioning transmitted abroad.

The episode reminds us that rate risk does not require bad credit.

It requires time.

A fixed-rate bond can make every promised payment and still generate a severe loss for an investor who must mark it to market or sell early.

This is basic bond math.

It becomes systemic when institutions have built business models around the assumption that rates will not move much.

The near-zero era created a similar temptation at a larger scale.

When safe yields are tiny, investors search for alternatives.

They buy longer maturities.

They add leverage.

They accept less liquidity.

They compress credit spreads.

They justify high asset prices with low discount rates.

If low rates persist long enough, the behavior stops feeling like a trade and starts feeling like the financial environment itself.

Then the environment changes.

The rate increases after 2022 produced a much larger version of this repricing across global markets. Bond portfolios lost value. Banks held securities far below par. Mortgage activity slowed. Commercial real-estate finance tightened. Equity valuations adjusted. The 2023 regional-bank failures made duration risk visible outside trading floors.

Why include 1994 when a more recent rate shock already exists?

Because 1994 occurred without the extraordinary pandemic, without a banking system recovering from a once-in-a-century shutdown, and without a giant fiscal emergency program.

It was a reminder that markets can create their own discontinuity when expectations change.

The fiscal wall may not require a macroeconomic disaster to become expensive.

A revision in the expected path of rates can be enough.

Suppose investors enter a year expecting the Federal Reserve to cut policy rates several times. Treasury leans on bills partly because short financing is expected to become cheaper. Asset managers add duration because they expect bond prices to rise. Hedge funds finance relative-value positions. Mortgage investors hedge for a falling-rate environment.

Then inflation data remain firm.

The Fed delays cuts.

Expected short rates rise.

Long yields rise too.

The term premium widens because Treasury supply is heavy.

The trades built around the earlier forecast have to move.

The government has not changed one spending law.

Its expected financing path has become more expensive.

This is the price-of-time problem in its purest form.

A large debt stock makes forecast errors costly even when the fiscal policy itself is unchanged.

The 1994 episode also shows why a rate rise can be economically ambiguous.

The Federal Reserve tightened because the economy was strengthening and it wanted to prevent inflation from returning. Higher yields were partly a sign of vigor. The subsequent expansion continued for years. By the end of the decade, the federal budget moved into surplus.

A bond selloff can therefore be painful for investors and healthy for macroeconomic credibility.

This is an important counterweight to fiscal alarmism.

If long yields rise because productivity growth is stronger, investment opportunities are better, and the Federal Reserve is preserving price stability, the government may pay more interest while collecting more revenue from a larger economy.

The fiscal effect cannot be read from the bond chart alone.

The problem becomes sharper when rates rise without corresponding growth.

That is why the decomposition matters.

Expected short rates.

Expected inflation.

Real growth.

Term premium.

Supply.

Liquidity.

All can move the long bond.

A fiscal wall is not diagnosed by the number five appearing before a percent sign.

It is diagnosed by what the five percent represents and how long it persists.

The 1994 lesson extends to debt management.

A borrower who knows rates can move abruptly values maturity diversification.

Treasury cannot predict the next surprise better than the market with confidence. A portfolio spread across bills, notes, bonds, TIPS, and floating-rate instruments prevents one forecast error from repricing everything at once.

This is the sovereign equivalent of not financing a thirty-year asset with an overnight loan.

The analogy is imperfect because Treasury can roll overnight liabilities in its own currency market under conditions no private borrower can replicate. The risk principle still holds.

Frequency creates exposure.

The other 1994 lesson is that financial institutions adapt after being hurt.

Risk systems improved. Derivatives practices evolved. Market participants learned more about convexity and leverage. Regulation changed. A painful episode became part of the institutional memory.

That memory can fade.

Every generation of traders eventually learns the same bond lesson in a new instrument.

In 1994 it appeared in leveraged bond portfolios and derivatives.

In 2008 it appeared through mortgage credit and funding.

In 2019 it appeared in repo plumbing.

In 2020 it appeared in Treasury liquidity and leveraged basis trades.

In 2022 it appeared dramatically in U.K. pension leverage.

In 2023 it appeared in U.S. bank duration and deposit funding.

The asset changes.

The interaction between time, leverage, and liquidity survives.

The fiscal wall adds the government's own balance sheet to that interaction.

When the outstanding debt was smaller, a market rate shock changed the value of securities and the cost of new borrowing.

When the debt stock is above thirty trillion dollars and large maturities roll each year, the shock enters future budgets on a much larger base.

This does not make the present system more fragile in every sense.

Treasury has a broader investor base.

Federal Reserve facilities are more developed.

The market has deeper electronic trading.

Regulators collect more data.

Central clearing can improve netting.

Banks hold more capital than before the global financial crisis.

Resilience has improved in some channels.

The fiscal sensitivity has increased in another.

That tradeoff is the book in miniature.

The United States has built a stronger financial system around a larger public balance sheet.

We do not know in advance which change will dominate in the next shock.

What would a modern 1994 look like?

It might begin not with the Federal Reserve surprising investors with hikes, but with a change in the term premium.

Suppose inflation is stable and policy rates are expected to drift lower, yet long yields rise because investors demand more compensation for duration and supply. Mortgage rates stay high despite easier monetary policy. Treasury finds bill financing cheaper than long bonds and is tempted to stay short. The yield curve steepens.

Markets call it a bear steepener.

The budget experiences it as a choice between current interest expense and future refinancing risk.

That would be a fiscal-wall event even without a crisis.

Or the surprise could come from growth.

Suppose artificial intelligence lifts productivity faster than expected. Investment booms. Real rates rise because profitable opportunities are abundant. Long yields increase. Treasury pays more, but GDP and revenue grow faster as well.

The same bear market in bonds can coexist with an improving fiscal ratio.

That is why every historical analogy must preserve multiple explanations.

A rate shock is not a moral.

It is a repricing.

The task is to ask what was repriced.

1994 also offers a political lesson.

Bond markets acquired an almost mythic reputation in the 1990s as a force capable of disciplining elected governments. The phrase *bond vigilantes* suggested investors would punish fiscal excess with higher yields.

The metaphor is too personal.

Most investors are not enforcing a doctrine.

They are managing risk.

If expected inflation rises, they want more yield.

If supply rises relative to demand, price adjusts.

If the central bank tightens, short-rate expectations change.

If fiscal policy improves, term premiums can fall.

The market disciplines nobody intentionally. It reprices claims.

Politicians experience the repricing as discipline because the cost is real.

This distinction matters for democratic legitimacy.

A government should not outsource fiscal policy to traders.

It should also not assume it can set the price at which traders hold thirty years of nominal risk.

The market and the electorate answer different questions.

The electorate decides what government should do.

The market decides what financial claims are worth at the margin.

The fiscal wall is where those answers must coexist.

If the United States adapts, a future bond selloff can remain what 1994 largely was: a painful market event inside a functioning fiscal and monetary system.

If it does not, the same kind of repricing can have a much larger budgetary consequence.

That is the difference scale makes.

The bond market bit back once before.

The bite healed.

Today the balance sheet it would bite is far larger.
