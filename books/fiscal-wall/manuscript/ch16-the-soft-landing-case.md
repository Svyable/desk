# The Soft Landing Case

A book about a fiscal wall should be able to explain why the wall might never feel like one.

That is not a concession to optimism.

It is a requirement for honest analysis.

The United States can refinance trillions of dollars without a market crisis. It has done so repeatedly. Treasury auctions can remain orderly. Interest rates can decline. Growth can outrun expectations. Fiscal policy can improve gradually. Market infrastructure can become more efficient. Higher yields can create exactly the demand required to absorb larger supply.

The bearish case is easy to dramatize because its variables reinforce one another.

More debt.

Higher rates.

More interest.

Larger deficits.

More debt.

The soft-landing case has reinforcing variables too.

Start with inflation.

If inflation continues moving toward price stability, the Federal Reserve can reduce policy restraint without compromising credibility. Short rates fall. Treasury bills and floating-rate notes reset lower. Newly issued two- and three-year notes price at lower yields. Over time, the government's average interest cost stops rising as quickly and can eventually decline.

The refinancing wall becomes cheaper while the same securities still mature.

This is the first reason a maturity total is not destiny.

The amount rolling can be large and the cost of rolling can improve.

Now add growth.

Suppose real productivity rises faster than the baseline.

Artificial intelligence improves business processes. Energy becomes more abundant. Investment in factories, data centers, transportation, and grids raises productive capacity. New firms grow. Workers become more productive. Labor-force participation improves or immigration supports employment. Nominal GDP expands faster than expected without an equal rise in inflation.

Federal revenue rises with the economy.

The debt stock may continue growing in dollars while stabilizing or falling relative to GDP.

A larger economy can carry a larger nominal debt.

This is not magical “growth will save us” rhetoric. The arithmetic requires scale. A modest productivity improvement helps; it does not automatically erase structural deficits measured in several percentage points of GDP. Growth is one component of an adjustment.

It is also the least painful component because it expands the denominator instead of requiring one group to surrender a claim.

The soft-landing case therefore depends heavily on whether public and private investment raise future productive capacity rather than merely increasing demand.

Debt used to build infrastructure that lowers logistics costs can improve the fiscal path differently from debt used for consumption with no lasting return.

Tax cuts that raise work, investment, and entrepreneurship can partly pay for themselves, though claims of full self-financing often exceed the evidence.

Research spending can produce enormous returns, but not on a schedule Congress can count precisely in a ten-year budget window.

The composition of the deficit matters.

Now add demand for safe assets.

The global financial system may want more Treasuries than pessimists assume.

Banking regulation requires high-quality liquid assets. Central banks need reserves. Money-market funds need short safe instruments. Pensions and insurers need duration. Derivatives markets need collateral. Corporations need cash-management assets. Households have rediscovered Treasury bills as safe yield.

As financial wealth grows, demand for safe assets can grow with it.

This creates an important possibility.

The United States can issue more debt without a proportional rise in yields because the convenience services provided by Treasuries become more valuable as the system grows.

Treasury debt is not merely a claim on future taxes.

It is also infrastructure for private finance.

That does not make unlimited issuance free. A convenience yield can shrink as supply rises. But the demand curve may be much deeper than a simple savings model suggests.

Higher yields strengthen it further.

Consider a pension fund that could not earn enough on safe assets when the ten-year Treasury yielded two percent. At four or five percent, the same fund may happily shift out of riskier credit. An insurer can match liabilities more comfortably. A retiree can buy a ladder of Treasuries. A foreign investor can find U.S. yields attractive relative to alternatives.

The government pays more.

The buyer base becomes broader.

This is the stabilizing side of price.

Now add market reform.

The Treasury market that struggled in March 2020 is not frozen in time.

Central clearing can increase netting and reduce redundant balance-sheet use. Greater transparency can help regulators and participants see leverage and exposures. Expanded access to clearing can distribute intermediation. Treasury buybacks can improve liquidity in older securities and support cash management. Federal Reserve facilities can provide a backstop to repo stress. Bank-capital rules can be calibrated to preserve resilience without making low-risk Treasury intermediation unnecessarily scarce.

Each reform has tradeoffs.

Together they can increase the quantity of Treasury supply the market can process without violent price moves.

This is the infrastructure version of growth.

The debt market itself can become more productive.

Now add maturity management.

Treasury does not need to choose between all bills and all thirty-year bonds. It can move gradually.

If bill demand is strong and long-term term premiums are elevated, the government can use bills as a temporary buffer while keeping coupon issuance regular.

If long-term demand strengthens, it can extend maturity over time.

If TIPS or FRNs attract distinct investors at reasonable cost, they can diversify the portfolio.

If buybacks improve off-the-run liquidity, benchmark issuance can become more efficient.

A diversified maturity profile can turn a wall into a long ramp.

Now add fiscal adjustment.

The United States does not need to balance the budget next year to improve expectations.

Markets price trajectories.

A credible set of gradual policies that stabilizes the debt ratio over time can matter before the full cash savings arrive. Changes to taxes, health spending, retirement programs, discretionary priorities, and enforcement can be phased in. Rules can be designed with triggers or long implementation periods. Growth-enhancing reforms can accompany consolidation.

The word *credible* is doing important work.

A ten-year promise Congress is expected to reverse in year two has little economic value.

A reform that survives elections because it distributes adjustment broadly can be more valuable than a dramatic package that collapses politically.

The soft landing is a governance achievement.

There are historical precedents for large fiscal adjustments without sovereign crisis.

Canada in the 1990s reduced large deficits through spending restraint and policy changes in a favorable external environment. Sweden reformed fiscal institutions after its early-1990s crisis. The United States improved its fiscal balance dramatically in the 1990s through a combination of policy, growth, lower defense spending after the Cold War, and revenue performance.

None of these episodes is a template.

The demographics, monetary regimes, exchange-rate systems, starting debt, and political conditions differ.

They demonstrate that fiscal trajectories can change faster than long-run baselines assume.

A baseline is current law plus economic assumptions.

Politics can surprise in both directions.

The soft-landing case also benefits from a property unique to nominal debt.

Time can shrink its burden if nominal income grows.

Suppose the debt stock rises by three percent a year while nominal GDP rises by five. The debt ratio falls even though the government never writes a giant principal check.

This was part of the postwar U.S. experience.

Inflation contributed, but so did real growth and fiscal balances. The debt was not “paid off” in a household sense. The economy grew around it.

The same route remains available in principle.

The challenge is achieving enough nominal growth without relying on high inflation that raises future borrowing costs and damages real incomes.

Healthy nominal growth is real growth plus modest stable inflation.

That is a softer path than explicit default, surprise inflation, or severe austerity.

What about the Federal Reserve?

The soft-landing case does not require the Fed to cap Treasury yields.

It requires monetary credibility.

If investors trust the central bank to preserve price stability, long-term inflation risk can remain contained even while fiscal debt is high. That reduces one component of the term premium. If inflation falls, the Fed can lower short rates on macroeconomic grounds, indirectly reducing Treasury financing costs without subordinating policy to the budget.

Independence becomes a fiscal asset.

This is counterintuitive because a central bank willing to raise rates can make the current interest bill worse.

Over a longer horizon, credibility can make long-term borrowing cheaper than a politically controlled central bank that promises low rates but cannot anchor inflation expectations.

Cheap nominal rates are not the same as cheap real financing.

Trust is cheaper.

The dollar adds another stabilizer.

During global stress, investors often seek dollar assets and Treasuries. The United States can therefore experience a recession in which deficits rise sharply while Treasury yields fall because safe-haven demand increases and the Fed eases policy.

This happened in 2008 and, in a more complicated market-functioning environment, during 2020.

A future downturn could again reduce refinancing costs even as it worsens the primary deficit.

That tradeoff can buy time for adjustment.

The reserve-currency system is not guaranteed forever, but it does not disappear because debt crosses a round number.

Network effects are durable.

The soft-landing case also asks whether pessimists mistake gross issuance for net burden.

A ten-trillion-dollar maturity flow can look impossible if imagined as ten trillion dollars of fresh savings that must be created.

In reality, much of the principal returned to holders of maturing Treasuries is available for reinvestment. The same money can roll. Short bills can turn over repeatedly. Gross auction volumes can be immense while the incremental stock grows by a much smaller amount.

This reduces the scale of the new-buyer problem.

It does not eliminate it because deficits add supply and investors can change portfolios. But the difference between gross and net is central to any plausible soft landing.

The government is not finding ten trillion strangers with ten trillion new dollars.

It is asking a mature investor base to roll a large portfolio while adding new debt at the margin.

The price of that margin matters more than the headline total.

There is a final stabilizer: politics dislikes visible pain.

Usually that sentence explains why reform is difficult.

It can also explain why reform eventually happens.

When net interest starts crowding priorities favored by both parties, the coalition for fiscal adjustment broadens. Defense advocates do not want debt service squeezing the Pentagon. Progressives do not want it squeezing social programs. Tax cutters do not want it forcing future rate increases. Governors do not want federal retrenchment shifted onto states. Younger voters do not want a larger share of taxes devoted to past borrowing.

Interest can turn fiscal policy from an ideological hobby into a shared constraint.

The resulting compromise will not look pure to anyone.

That may be its strength.

A durable fiscal settlement is likely to combine measures that each coalition dislikes and values: some revenue, some spending restraint, some entitlement adjustment, some growth policy, and protections for people least able to absorb abrupt changes.

The market does not require perfection.

It requires confidence that the debt grows more slowly than the country's capacity to support it over time.

The soft landing is therefore not one event.

It is a sequence.

Inflation falls enough for rates to normalize.

The economy grows enough to support revenue.

Treasury manages maturity without making a giant rate bet.

Market infrastructure expands with supply.

Safe-asset demand remains deep.

Congress improves the primary balance gradually.

The Fed preserves credibility.

The dollar retains its network role.

No step has to be spectacular.

That is why the outcome receives less attention than crisis forecasts.

Success in debt management looks like nothing happening.

Auctions clear.

Repo works.

Interest stabilizes.

The debt ratio bends.

A new recession arrives and the government still has room to respond.

The wall remains on charts and disappears from daily conversation.

This is not an impossible future.

It may be the base case.

The reason to study the wall is not because catastrophe is certain.

It is because a soft landing has requirements, and requirements are easier to meet before they become emergencies.

The most important feature of a soft landing is timing.

The country does not need every variable to improve at once. It needs improvement to arrive before the lagged repricing of the debt stock overwhelms the benefit.

Imagine inflation continues declining and the Federal Reserve lowers short rates over the next few years. Bills reprice first. Floating-rate notes follow their reference rates. Two- and three-year issuance becomes cheaper. The old low-coupon ten-year notes continue to mature, but they no longer roll into the peak rate regime. The average federal interest rate still rises for a period because yesterday's cheap debt is being replaced, yet it peaks at a lower level than a simple extrapolation of today's yield curve would suggest.

The wall has not disappeared.

The rate regime moved before the whole portfolio reached it.

This is one of the strongest reasons to avoid multiplying today's yield by today's debt and calling the result the future interest bill.

The Treasury portfolio is a moving average of market history.

If the history improves soon enough, the average turns before every security has crossed the worst point.

Now add a modest improvement in the primary balance.

The effect can be larger than the first-year savings suggest because fewer new securities need to be issued, which means fewer future coupons. A gradual package that reduces annual primary deficits by an increasing amount over time can change the debt path through both the direct budget effect and the interest avoided on debt that was never issued.

This is compounding in the helpful direction.

It is also why credibility can matter before the cash savings are complete. If investors believe the future supply path has improved, term premiums and auction concessions can respond before the last statutory provision takes effect.

The market discounts policy as well as cash flows.

The risk, of course, is that promises are reversible.

A reform scheduled ten years in the future has little value if every participant expects the next Congress to repeal it. Soft landings therefore depend on institutional and political durability rather than numerical ambition alone.

A smaller package that survives can matter more than a giant package written to expire politically.

The same logic applies to growth.

A productivity improvement does not need to be a technological miracle to help. A few tenths of a percentage point sustained over a decade changes the level of output materially. More output supports wages, profits, and tax receipts. It also makes a given stock of nominal debt smaller relative to the economy.

But growth has to be real.

Inflation can raise nominal GDP and tax receipts, yet if it simultaneously raises Treasury yields, indexed benefits, and uncertainty, the apparent denominator improvement can be partly or fully offset.

The soft landing requires productive expansion with inflation contained enough that investors do not demand a permanent inflation premium.

This is why the artificial-intelligence boom, energy investment, factory construction, grid expansion, and infrastructure spending matter fiscally only through outcomes rather than headlines.

A data center built with borrowed money does not improve debt sustainability merely because it is called investment. It has to raise future productive capacity enough to justify the resources consumed. The same is true of public infrastructure.

Composition is not a moral label.

It is an empirical question about return.

Safe-asset demand offers another route that is easy to understate because it does not look like reform.

Suppose household and institutional financial wealth keeps growing. The world needs more collateral. Regulations continue to create demand for high-quality liquid assets. Pensions want more duration as they mature. Money funds remain large. Foreign reserve managers retain the dollar as their primary reserve asset.

The Treasury market can grow into a larger debt stock.

The relevant comparison is not today's buyer base against tomorrow's supply. It is tomorrow's buyer base against tomorrow's supply.

A larger financial system can absorb a larger sovereign market.

This does not guarantee the same yield. It means capacity is not fixed.

Market structure can expand that capacity further. If central clearing allows dealers to net offsetting Treasury and repo exposures more efficiently, the same amount of bank capital can intermediate more gross activity. If margin and liquidity requirements are handled well, a reform that initially looks technical can reduce the amount of balance sheet required per dollar of Treasury turnover.

This is an underappreciated soft-landing mechanism.

The government does not only adjust the numerator.

The market that carries the numerator can become more efficient.

A successful clearing transition through the end of 2026 and mid-2027 would therefore matter beyond compliance. It would demonstrate that the Treasury market can scale its infrastructure alongside issuance.

The same is true of buybacks and liquidity management. Improving the tradability of off-the-run securities can reduce the friction investors demand compensation for. Better data on leverage and positions can help regulators identify concentrated risks before they become forced sales. Federal Reserve facilities can make secured funding more reliable without fixing the long-term yield.

The wall becomes less dangerous when the market's shock absorbers become stronger.

There is a useful distinction between a fiscal soft landing and a market soft landing.

The fiscal version is debt stabilizing relative to the country's capacity to support it.

The market version is Treasury supply being absorbed without repeated dysfunction or an accelerating financing premium.

The United States needs both.

A fiscally improving country could still suffer a market accident if leverage and liquidity are badly arranged.

A perfectly functioning Treasury market could finance an unsustainable fiscal trajectory for a surprisingly long time.

The outcomes reinforce one another but are not identical.

This gives us a way to recognize progress without demanding perfection.

A soft landing would not require debt to fall in nominal dollars.

It would not require net interest to return to the cheap-money levels of the 2010s.

It would not require the thirty-year yield to return to two percent.

It would not require every auction to stop through.

It would require the dangerous derivatives to improve: the growth rate of debt relative to GDP, the growth of interest relative to revenue, the amount of repeated market intervention, and the size of the compensation required for marginal duration.

The direction matters more than nostalgia.

One year of improvement would not settle the question either. Fiscal trajectories can reverse quickly through recession, war, tax changes, or new programs. The point of a soft landing is to rebuild margin for those surprises.

If the debt ratio stabilizes only because every economic assumption comes in favorably, the country has not rebuilt resilience.

If it can absorb a recession or security emergency and remain on a recoverable path, it has.

This is the difference between a forecast that works and a balance sheet that works.

The soft landing is ultimately about option value.

The government does not need to eliminate borrowing. It needs borrowing to remain available for things worth borrowing for.

That means preserving room before the next event that no baseline contains.
