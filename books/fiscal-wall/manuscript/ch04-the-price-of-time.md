# The Price of Time

Interest is the price of waiting.

That definition is almost too simple for modern finance, but it is a useful place to begin. A lender gives up the use of money today in exchange for a promise of more money later. The extra amount has to compensate for several things at once: the loss of immediate use, expected inflation, uncertainty about repayment, the possibility that better opportunities appear, and the discomfort of committing capital for a period that cannot be known in advance.

For the United States Treasury, credit risk in the ordinary corporate sense is unusual. The federal government issues debt in dollars and controls the currency in which its nominal obligations are paid. That does not make Treasury securities riskless in every dimension. Inflation can erode purchasing power. Long-term yields can rise, reducing market values. Political conflict can create debt-limit accidents. Liquidity can deteriorate in moments of stress. But the market generally treats Treasury credit risk differently from that of a company or household.

The more important variable in the fiscal wall is time.

A four-week bill and a thirty-year bond can both be obligations of the same government. They expose the government and the investor to almost opposite risks.

With the bill, the investor gets money back quickly. Its market value has little time to move. The government, however, must return to the market almost immediately and accept whatever rate exists then.

With the thirty-year bond, the government fixes most of its nominal coupon cost for a generation. The investor bears decades of inflation and duration risk. If market yields rise after purchase, the bond's price can fall sharply. The government has bought insurance against refinancing risk by transferring interest-rate risk to the investor.

Insurance has a price.

That price is embedded in the yield curve.

The yield curve is simply the market's schedule of interest rates across maturities, but it carries far more argument than the phrase suggests. A two-year yield reflects expectations about Federal Reserve policy, inflation, growth, and near-term uncertainty. A thirty-year yield incorporates expectations over a much longer horizon and compensation for holding duration through futures no one can forecast precisely.

Economists often separate a long-term yield into expected future short rates plus a term premium. The decomposition is not directly observable. Different models produce different estimates. The concept is still useful.

If investors believe the Federal Reserve will keep short-term rates high, long yields can rise because expected future short rates rise.

If investors become less willing to hold long-duration bonds even without changing their central forecast for policy, the term premium can rise.

For Treasury, those two paths can look similar at the auction window: long-term borrowing becomes more expensive.

For diagnosis, they are different.

A rise driven by stronger expected growth is not the same signal as a rise driven by inflation fear. A rise driven by investors demanding compensation for fiscal uncertainty is not the same as a rise driven by an economy performing better than expected. A yield is a price produced by many motives. It does not arrive with an explanation attached.

This matters because the fiscal argument often treats interest rates as though they were an external weather system.

Rates go up. The government pays more.

True, but incomplete.

The rate the government pays is partly a judgment about the future that government policy helps create. Fiscal policy can influence growth, inflation, the supply of bonds, expectations about future taxation, and the risk premium investors demand. Monetary policy responds to the economy produced partly by fiscal choices. The maturity structure of the debt determines how quickly those rates enter the budget.

The price of time is endogenous to the world the borrower helps make.

For years after the financial crisis, that world offered unusually cheap time.

The Federal Reserve's policy rate remained near zero for extended periods. Inflation was subdued. Global savings were abundant. Demographics, regulatory demand for safe assets, central-bank reserve accumulation, and repeated episodes of risk aversion all supported demand for government bonds. The Fed's large-scale asset purchases removed substantial quantities of duration from private hands.

The consequence for the federal budget was easy to underappreciate.

A larger debt stock could be carried at a lower average rate.

Imagine a landlord who buys a second building with a larger mortgage, yet refinances both loans at half the old interest rate. The amount owed rises while the monthly interest bill barely moves. Anyone looking only at the payment may conclude that leverage is less important than earlier generations believed.

The conclusion lasts until the mortgage resets.

Sovereign finance is not a landlord's mortgage, but the timing problem is related. The federal government does not refinance its entire debt stock at once. It carries a portfolio of maturities. The average interest rate paid on that portfolio is therefore a memory of past market conditions.

When rates rise, the marginal rate changes first.

The average rate follows.

This delay is one reason fiscal deterioration can feel surprisingly slow and then surprisingly persistent.

Suppose market rates jump in a single year. Only the securities that mature, reset, or are newly issued immediately carry the new cost. The old fixed-rate notes and bonds keep paying their old coupons. Budget interest rises, but not as though the whole debt stock had been refinanced overnight.

If market rates stay high for several years, more of the portfolio turns over. Each maturity replaces an old coupon with a new one. The average rate climbs toward the prevailing market rate.

The same delay works in reverse after rates fall.

This is the central temporal feature of the fiscal wall.

It is not only about the level of interest rates. It is about the persistence of those rates relative to the maturity profile.

A brief spike can be painful at the margin and leave much of the portfolio untouched.

A moderate rate sustained for years can transform the government's interest bill.

That is why forecasts of federal interest expense are so sensitive to assumptions about rates. A few tenths of a percentage point, applied across a debt stock measured in tens of trillions and compounded over years, becomes real budget money.

Yet it would be a mistake to assume that lower rates are always fiscally better in a broader sense.

Why did rates fall to emergency levels in 2008 and 2020?

Because the economy was in trouble.

A recession can reduce tax revenue, increase automatic stabilizer spending, require emergency programs, and enlarge the deficit even as Treasury yields fall. Conversely, higher yields can accompany stronger growth and higher nominal incomes, which increase tax collections.

The carrying cost of debt cannot be separated from the economy carrying it.

This is where the relationship between interest rates and growth enters.

If the nominal economy grows faster than the effective interest rate on government debt, the debt burden can become easier to manage relative to income, all else equal. If interest costs persistently outrun growth, stabilizing the debt ratio generally requires a stronger primary balance.

The famous shorthand is *r minus g*: the interest rate minus the growth rate.

The shorthand is helpful and dangerous.

There is no single federal interest rate. Treasury has a portfolio. There is no guaranteed growth rate. Nominal GDP fluctuates. Inflation lifts nominal GDP while also changing rates and spending. Primary deficits matter. Maturity matters. One year of favorable arithmetic does not guarantee a decade of it.

Still, the basic insight explains why the same debt ratio can be comfortable in one regime and constraining in another.

A country growing at six percent nominally while paying three percent on its debt has more room than one growing at three percent while paying five.

The United States spent much of the post-financial-crisis period in a world where the cost of borrowing was strikingly low relative to historical norms. The political system adapted to that world.

Budgets rarely announce the assumptions that become habits.

A highway project is evaluated with a discount rate.

A pension liability is valued with a discount rate.

A venture investment is justified against the return available elsewhere.

A home price depends partly on the mortgage payment buyers can afford.

A stock price depends partly on the rate used to discount future earnings.

Government choices are also shaped by the price of time, even when the connection is indirect.

Low rates make delay cheaper.

A difficult fiscal reform can be postponed because the interest penalty appears manageable. An emergency can be financed without immediate tax increases. A tax cut can be enacted without a visible funding offset. A spending program can be expanded while the debt-service line remains contained.

None of these decisions follows mechanically from rates. Politics decides. But cheap time reduces the near-term cost of saying yes.

Higher rates do not automatically produce discipline either.

They can produce denial.

The interest line rises because of past decisions, while the political benefits attached to major programs remain current. No legislator receives applause for paying a coupon. Voters see the roads, benefits, tax rates, salaries, hospitals, defense programs, and transfer payments. Interest is an invoice for choices that may have been made by people no longer in office.

This makes it politically peculiar.

Interest is mandatory in an economic sense before it is mandatory in a budgetary classification. If the United States intends to maintain the full faith and credit of the federal government, it does not treat coupon payments as an optional appropriation to be trimmed during negotiations.

The price of time becomes the price of preserving trust.

That trust is enormously valuable.

Because investors believe Treasury obligations will be honored, the government can borrow at yields far below those available to risky private borrowers. Because Treasuries are widely accepted as collateral, they can be sold in size. Because the dollar is the dominant reserve currency, global institutions hold dollar assets for reasons beyond simple yield maximization.

A sovereign that damages that trust would not merely pay more on one bond.

It would damage a financial infrastructure built over generations.

This is why debt-limit brinkmanship is so strange from a financing perspective. Congress can debate spending and taxes legitimately. It can choose deficits or surpluses. But once obligations have been incurred, threatening the payment mechanism does not retroactively undo the spending decision. It introduces uncertainty into the asset whose exceptional value rests partly on the absence of such uncertainty.

Time has a political price as well as a financial one.

The term premium offers a useful window into that price because it can move when the market becomes less comfortable holding long government promises.

Long bonds ask an investor to trust not one Congress, one president, or one Federal Reserve chair, but a succession of them.

What will inflation be in twenty years?

What will the tax system look like?

How large will the debt be?

Will productivity surge?

Will retirement and health programs be reformed?

Will wars or climate disasters require emergency borrowing?

Will another financial crisis push rates back toward zero?

A thirty-year Treasury bond contains all of those uncertainties and more, compressed into a price.

Investors do not need to predict the answers correctly. They need compensation for being wrong.

When uncertainty rises, duration can become expensive even if short-term policy rates are expected eventually to fall.

This creates a tempting response for the borrower.

Stay short.

If long-term yields contain a large term premium, why lock them in? Issue more bills. Wait for the Federal Reserve to cut. Refinance later at lower rates.

That strategy may save money.

It may also turn the government into a giant floating-rate borrower.

If inflation persists or rates rise further, the savings disappear. The portfolio reprices quickly. The next Treasury secretary inherits a maturity wall built by the previous one.

The opposite temptation is to extend aggressively.

Lock the rate.

Push maturities out.

Buy certainty.

That may prove wise if rates rise. It may look expensive if rates fall. Long issuance also asks private investors to absorb more duration at a moment when the Federal Reserve may be holding or shedding less of it than during the quantitative-easing era.

Debt management therefore cannot be reduced to guessing the next move in rates.

A government should not run a trillion-dollar duration trade based on a forecast that would embarrass a hedge fund if it were wrong.

The objective is broader: regular and predictable financing at the lowest expected cost over time, subject to prudent risk.

The phrase *over time* does most of the work.

A cheap bill today can be expensive over repeated rollovers.

An expensive bond today can become cheap insurance in retrospect.

A Treasury security is a contract about time.

The fiscal wall is what happens when enough of those contracts reach their next decision point while the price of time has changed.

There is no reason to assume the new price remains high forever.

Inflation can fall. The Federal Reserve can cut rates. Productivity can rise. Global demand for safe assets can strengthen. A recession can drive investors toward Treasuries. Fiscal reform can reduce expected supply and lower risk premiums.

There is also no reason to assume the old price returns simply because it was comfortable.

The near-zero period was historically unusual. Demographics can change. Global investment demand can rise. Fiscal deficits can remain large. Inflation risk can demand compensation. The Federal Reserve can choose a smaller balance sheet relative to the economy. Investors can require more yield to absorb duration.

The central uncertainty is not whether rates will be three, four, five, or six percent next year.

It is whether the political and fiscal system has adapted to a world in which the cost of postponement is visible again.

For a long time, America could borrow from the future at a price that made the future feel far away.

The refinancing cycle brings it closer.

The price of time can be decomposed further by asking a lender a blunt question: what exactly would make you demand another one percentage point?

There is no universal answer.

A money-market fund might demand it because the Federal Reserve lifted the overnight rate. A thirty-year investor might demand it because expected inflation rose. Another might care less about inflation than about the quantity of duration arriving in the market. A foreign buyer might compare the Treasury yield with the cost of hedging dollars back into euros or yen. An insurer might care about the yield relative to the liability it needs to match. A leveraged relative-value trader might care about the spread between a cash Treasury and a futures contract, not the outright level at all.

A sovereign yield is a common price assembled from different private calculations.

That is why the phrase *the bond market thinks* should be used sparingly.

The bond market does not possess one mind. It is a clearing mechanism for institutions with different clocks, regulations, funding arrangements, liabilities, and forecasts. A rise in yield means the previous price no longer balanced those motives. It does not tell us which motive changed without further evidence.

The distinction matters enormously for policy.

Suppose ten-year yields rise because expected real growth improves. New factories are being built. Productivity is accelerating. Private investment opportunities are more attractive, so savers demand a higher real return to hold government bonds instead. Treasury pays more, but federal revenue may also rise as the economy becomes richer.

That is a very different fiscal future from one in which ten-year yields rise because inflation expectations become unanchored.

The same bond price can carry opposing economic stories.

Now suppose short-rate expectations are falling but the ten- and thirty-year yields remain stubbornly high. The curve steepens. Analysts begin talking about term premium.

That phrase can become a convenient container for whatever is unexplained, but the underlying question is real: how much compensation do investors require for holding a long nominal promise rather than repeatedly rolling short ones?

Supply can be part of that compensation.

If Treasury asks private investors to absorb more long-duration bonds at the same time the Federal Reserve owns a smaller share of the market than during peak quantitative easing, the private sector may require a lower bond price—therefore a higher yield—to hold the duration. This can happen without anyone doubting that the principal will be paid.

The fiscal consequence is uncomfortable because it weakens a popular hope.

Policymakers can imagine that once the Federal Reserve begins cutting short rates, the government's financing problem automatically improves. It does at the short end. Bills and floating-rate notes get cheaper relatively quickly. But if long rates remain elevated because term premium, supply, or inflation uncertainty stays high, extending maturity remains expensive.

The government is then offered cheapening short-term time and expensive long-term insurance.

That is a real debt-management dilemma.

There is no formula that resolves it because the right answer depends on futures that have not occurred.

This is where the history of 1994 is useful. The United States did not face a sovereign funding crisis. The Federal Reserve tightened as the economy strengthened, bond yields rose sharply, and investors who had become comfortable with a benign rate environment discovered how much duration they owned. Losses spread through institutions and leveraged strategies even though Treasury credit remained unquestioned.

A rate shock can therefore be a financial event before it is a fiscal event.

On today's larger public balance sheet, it can be both.

The investor marks the bond down immediately.

The government pays the higher rate only as debt rolls.

The financial system experiences the shock in market value; the budget experiences it in cash flow with a lag.

This asymmetry explains why the early years of a rising-rate regime can look deceptively survivable to fiscal policymakers. Market participants have already absorbed losses. Homebuyers have already seen mortgage rates change. Banks have already marked securities lower or watched unrealized losses grow. But the Treasury interest bill is still partly protected by old coupons.

The government gets the news slowly.

That slow transmission is a form of insurance, but it can also become a political trap. Because the full cost is delayed, fiscal policy can remain unchanged during the very period when early adjustment would be easiest.

Then more debt rolls.

The interest line rises after the rate shock that caused it has faded from public attention.

This is one reason the fiscal wall is a problem of timing rather than merely a problem of levels.

A government facing a one-year emergency rate spike can ride through much of it with a diversified maturity profile. A government facing a decade of moderately higher real rates cannot. The calendar gradually converts the temporary protection of fixed coupons into the new steady-state cost.

The difference between a spike and a regime is therefore more important than the difference between four and five percent on one afternoon.

The postwar era offers another lesson about the price of time: a negative real rate can transfer wealth quietly.

If the government pays three percent on a fixed nominal bond while inflation runs at five, the investor loses purchasing power even though every dollar arrives on time. The government's debt burden falls in real terms. Economists studying postwar debt reductions have described combinations of inflation, regulated financial institutions, and negative real rates as forms of debt liquidation or financial repression.

The phrase can be abused. Not every period of negative real rates is a deliberate fiscal strategy, and not every requirement that banks hold safe assets is repression. Still, the episode reveals a basic feature of nominal debt.

The price of time has a real and a nominal component.

A nominally cheap bond can be expensive to the investor if inflation is high.

A nominally expensive bond can be tolerable to the government if nominal growth is even stronger.

This is why *r minus g* needs to be handled with care. The relevant interest rate is the effective rate on the debt stock, not simply today's ten-year yield. The relevant growth rate should be consistent with the nominal or real measure used. Primary deficits can overwhelm a favorable interest-growth differential. And market rates are not guaranteed to remain below growth simply because they did for a period.

A useful thought experiment is to imagine three futures with the same debt stock.

In the first, nominal GDP grows five percent, the effective interest rate settles near three, and the primary deficit narrows. The debt ratio can stabilize or fall without heroic austerity.

In the second, nominal GDP grows four percent, the effective interest rate approaches four, and the primary deficit remains large. Debt dynamics become much more dependent on annual fiscal choices.

In the third, growth disappoints while effective rates stay high. The government borrows to cover both the primary gap and a rising interest bill. The arithmetic begins feeding on itself.

No threshold separates these futures cleanly.

They are paths.

The maturity profile determines how quickly the country moves from one path to another when market rates change.

This is why debt management is best understood as national duration management.

Treasury cannot know the future yield curve. It can choose how much exposure taxpayers have to being wrong about it.

Short debt says: we will accept tomorrow's price tomorrow.

Long debt says: we will pay today's price to avoid that uncertainty.

Floating-rate debt says: we will keep the principal maturity but let the coupon follow short rates.

Inflation-protected debt says: we will share inflation risk with the investor rather than ask the investor to price all of it in advance.

Each instrument divides uncertainty differently.

The government's objective is not to win every retrospective rate forecast. It is to keep any one forecast error from dominating the public balance sheet.

That is what prudent duration does.

The fiscal wall is therefore not evidence that Treasury should have issued only thirty-year bonds during the cheap-money era. Had it done so, taxpayers might have locked in more low rates, but the long-bond market may not have absorbed the volume without a higher term premium. Liquidity across the curve would have changed. Short safe assets demanded by money funds and collateral markets would have been scarcer. Treasury would have placed an enormous duration bet of its own.

Hindsight always offers a free maturity strategy.

Debt managers operate without it.

The useful question in 2026 is not why they failed to know the future.

It is whether the portfolio they built gives the country enough time to adjust now that the price of time is no longer negligible.

That is the hidden asset inside a long maturity profile: not cheap money, but decision time.

And decision time is worth most before it is needed.
