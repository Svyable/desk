# When the Fed Is No Longer the Same Buyer

For much of the 2010s, any discussion of Treasury demand eventually reached the same institution.

The Federal Reserve.

After the financial crisis, the Fed bought large quantities of Treasury and agency securities through a series of asset-purchase programs. In 2020, it did so again at extraordinary speed as the pandemic disrupted markets and the economy.

The purchases became familiar enough to acquire a political shorthand.

The Fed is financing the government.

The Fed is printing money.

The Fed is suppressing yields.

The Fed is monetizing the debt.

Each phrase points toward something real and then risks collapsing several different mechanisms into one accusation.

To understand the fiscal wall, the distinctions matter.

The Federal Reserve is part of the United States government, but it is not the Treasury. Congress created it with a monetary-policy mandate and a degree of operational independence. Treasury decides how to finance federal cash needs within the legal and policy framework. The Fed sets monetary policy and manages the supply of reserves in pursuit of its statutory goals.

The two balance sheets meet in the same securities.

That is where the confusion begins.

When the Federal Reserve buys a Treasury note in the secondary market, it pays by creating reserve balances in the banking system. The private sector holds fewer Treasury securities and more reserve balances. The consolidated public sector still has a liability outstanding, but the form and duration of that liability have changed.

A ten-year note held by a private pension fund locks in a coupon paid outside the government for ten years.

A ten-year note held by the Federal Reserve is an asset of the central bank. The Fed pays interest on reserve balances and other interest-bearing liabilities at rates that can change quickly. When the income it receives on its securities exceeds its expenses, the residual is normally remitted to Treasury after the Fed covers its costs and statutory requirements.

From a consolidated perspective, the central bank has transformed some long-duration public debt into a shorter-rate exposure.

This is one reason quantitative easing can reduce duration available to private investors and put downward pressure on longer-term yields.

It is also why the fiscal consequences of later rate increases are not captured simply by looking at the coupons on Treasuries held in the Fed's portfolio.

After the Federal Reserve raised short-term rates sharply in the 2020s, its interest expense on reserve balances and overnight reverse-repurchase liabilities increased. The income from older lower-yielding securities did not rise with it. Remittances to Treasury stopped. The Fed recorded a deferred asset representing future earnings that would be retained before remittances resumed.

Nobody defaulted.

No appropriation was required.

The economic benefit Treasury had previously received from Fed remittances disappeared for a period.

This is a hidden channel through which the price of short-term money enters the public balance sheet.

It also demonstrates why central-bank holdings are not free financing.

The Fed can create nominal money. It cannot create real resources without consequence.

If a central bank buys government debt persistently to hold yields below the level consistent with price stability, and if the economy is already operating near capacity, the result can eventually appear through inflation, currency pressure, distorted risk-taking, or loss of confidence. The exact path depends on circumstances. The constraint does not vanish because the buyer can create reserves.

That does not mean every Federal Reserve purchase is fiscal dominance.

Context and purpose matter.

In March 2020, the Treasury market itself was malfunctioning. Investors sold Treasuries for cash. Dealer balance sheets were strained. Market depth collapsed. The Fed's rapid purchases helped restore functioning while monetary policy was also being eased in response to a severe economic shock.

In other periods, asset purchases were explicitly used as a monetary-policy tool when the policy rate was near its effective lower bound. By removing duration from the market and signaling an accommodative stance, the Fed sought to lower broader borrowing costs and support the economy.

Reserve management is different again.

An ample-reserves operating framework requires enough reserve balances in the banking system that the Federal Reserve can control short-term interest rates without forcing banks to scramble for reserves each day. As the economy and financial system grow, the Fed may need to purchase assets simply to maintain an appropriate reserve supply.

Those purchases can include Treasury bills or other short Treasury securities.

In July 2026, the Federal Reserve's implementation note directed the New York Fed to purchase Treasury bills and, if needed, other Treasury securities with three years or less remaining maturity to maintain an ample level of reserves. It also continued to roll over Treasury principal payments and reinvest agency principal payments into bills under the policy then in force.

That is buying.

It is not the same thing as quantitative easing designed to push down long-term yields.

This distinction matters enormously for the fiscal-wall narrative.

A careless story says the Fed bought trillions before, stopped, and therefore private markets must suddenly replace the entire central bank.

The actual transition is more gradual.

The Federal Reserve's balance sheet changes through maturities, reinvestment policies, asset purchases, reserve demand, currency growth, and policy decisions. The composition matters as much as the total. A shift from holding more long-duration securities to purchasing bills for reserve management changes the amount of duration private investors must absorb even if the balance sheet remains large.

Treasury supply and Fed portfolio choices therefore interact.

Suppose Treasury issues more long bonds while the Fed is no longer buying long bonds.

Private investors must absorb more duration.

The clearing price may require a higher term premium.

Suppose Treasury issues more bills while the Fed is purchasing bills for reserve management and money funds have strong demand.

The short end may absorb supply more easily.

Suppose the Fed cuts policy rates.

Bill yields and floating-rate Treasury costs can fall quickly, while long yields may move less if inflation or fiscal concerns keep the term premium elevated.

Monetary policy changes the refinancing map without being designed to solve Treasury's financing problem.

This institutional separation is valuable precisely because the temptations run in the opposite direction.

A heavily indebted government naturally prefers low rates.

An inflation-fighting central bank may need high rates.

If monetary policy is forced to keep borrowing costs low because fiscal authorities cannot tolerate the interest bill, economists call the resulting risk fiscal dominance.

The term can be overused.

A central bank that considers financial conditions is not automatically dominated by the fiscal authority. Government debt is part of the transmission mechanism of monetary policy. Severe Treasury-market dysfunction can threaten credit creation and the broader economy. The Fed can respond to market functioning without promising taxpayers permanently cheap funding.

The line is crossed when preserving government financing conditions becomes the binding constraint on price stability.

The fiscal wall makes that line more important because the government's sensitivity to rates is increasing.

Consider the political optics.

Net interest exceeds a trillion dollars. A large portion of federal debt is rolling. The Treasury announces hundreds of billions in quarterly net borrowing. Long-term yields rise. Elected officials complain that the Federal Reserve is making the deficit worse.

From a narrow cash perspective, they are right.

Higher policy rates eventually increase federal interest costs.

From a monetary-policy perspective, the complaint reverses causality. If inflation requires restraint, lowering rates simply to reduce Treasury's bill may prolong inflation and ultimately make borrowing more expensive.

A government cannot reliably inflate away a debt problem while simultaneously demanding the central bank preserve the purchasing power of its currency.

The conflict is not theoretical.

American history contains a famous institutional settlement around it.

During and after World War II, the Federal Reserve supported ceilings on Treasury yields to help finance the war and postwar debt. As inflation pressure grew, the arrangement became increasingly contentious. The Treasury-Federal Reserve Accord of 1951 restored greater independence to monetary policy.

The exact postwar regime cannot be transplanted into the present. The financial system, reserve framework, fiscal programs, and global capital markets are completely different.

But the principle survives.

Debt management and monetary policy have different objectives for a reason.

A high-debt era tests whether those objectives can remain separate when their consequences collide.

There is another misunderstanding worth clearing away.

The Fed cannot make Treasury debt disappear by owning it.

The central bank could, in principle, hold a security to maturity and reinvest the proceeds. From the Treasury's perspective, the principal payment is made to the Fed. From the consolidated government's perspective, liabilities have been rearranged. If the money created to purchase the debt remains in the system, the public sector still owes something—reserve balances, currency, or another form of central-bank liability.

The relevant question is not whether the government “owes itself.”

It is what liabilities the private sector holds, what return they pay, how liquid they are, and what macroeconomic consequences follow.

Currency pays no explicit interest but is held voluntarily for transactions and savings.

Reserve balances pay an administered rate to eligible institutions under the current framework.

Treasury securities pay market yields and are held across the world.

Changing the mix can change fiscal sensitivity and monetary conditions.

It cannot repeal the real budget constraint.

This is where Modern Monetary Theory debates, conventional fiscal analysis, and market practice often talk past one another.

It is trivially true that the United States cannot be forced to run out of its own currency in the same way a household can run out of dollars.

It is also trivially true that creating more nominal claims cannot guarantee more doctors, engineers, energy, semiconductors, housing, soldiers, food, or productive capacity.

The ultimate constraint is real resources and the purchasing power of the currency.

Treasury refinancing sits between the nominal and the real.

Auctions determine the yield required for investors to hold nominal claims.

The Federal Reserve influences short-term rates and financial conditions.

Fiscal policy determines a large part of the quantity of claims being created.

Inflation and growth determine what those claims mean in real terms.

No institution controls all four.

That dispersion of authority is frustrating and protective.

It prevents the Treasury secretary from ordering the central bank to fund deficits at a preferred rate.

It prevents the Federal Reserve from deciding tax and spending policy.

It forces Congress to own the primary budget.

And it lets markets express a price for duration that neither institution can fully dictate in normal times.

The system can still bend under stress.

In a severe market-functioning event, the Fed may need to buy Treasuries aggressively. The standing repo facility can lend against Treasury collateral. Supervisors can adjust temporary constraints. Treasury can modify issuance. International central-bank swap lines can address dollar funding strains.

These are signs of institutional capacity, not necessarily signs of failure.

The dangerous inference is that because the tools exist, supply never matters.

A fire department makes a city safer.

It does not make fireproof construction unnecessary.

The Fed's presence makes the Treasury market more resilient.

It does not make fiscal arithmetic optional.

The mid-2020s are unusual because the market has to learn the difference between a central bank that will protect functioning and one that will protect a particular yield.

Those promises are not the same.

A market-functioning promise says: if forced sales, funding stress, or operational breakdown threatens the core Treasury market, the central bank can provide liquidity.

A yield promise says: if investors demand more compensation for inflation, fiscal risk, or duration, the central bank will prevent the price from adjusting.

The first is compatible with monetary independence.

The second can become a form of fiscal subordination if sustained against the inflation outlook.

Investors will watch for signs that the distinction is weakening.

They will watch Federal Reserve communications.

They will watch the maturity of reserve-management purchases.

They will watch the Treasury's issuance mix.

They will watch whether long yields rise while short rates fall.

They will watch inflation expectations.

They will watch political attacks on central-bank independence.

And they will watch the Fed's own income statement, not because accounting determines policy, but because it illustrates how the high-rate environment has already altered the public balance sheet.

The refinancing wall is therefore not a story about the Fed disappearing.

The Federal Reserve is still in the room.

It is simply not the same buyer, for the same reason, at every point in the cycle.

That is healthier than an automatic backstop to fiscal policy.

It is also more demanding.

Private markets must clear at prices private investors accept.

Treasury must manage maturity without assuming the central bank will rescue a poor duration bet.

Congress must confront interest costs without treating monetary policy as a free refinancing desk.

And the Federal Reserve must preserve both market functioning and price stability in a financial system whose benchmark asset is being issued on a historic scale.

The roles are separate.

The wall is where they meet.
