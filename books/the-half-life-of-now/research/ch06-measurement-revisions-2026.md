# Chapter 6 evidence note — measurement clocks and revisions

Checked September 8, 2026 for Chapter 6, “The Dashboard and the Street.”

## Primary sources

### BLS Current Employment Statistics preliminary benchmark — March 2026

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, “Current Employment Statistics Preliminary Benchmark (National) — March 2026,” released August 28, 2026.

https://www.bls.gov/news.release/prebmk.nr0.htm

Relevant boundaries:

- preliminary national benchmark revision to total nonfarm employment for March 2026: **−79,000 (−0.1%)**
- total private employment preliminary benchmark revision: **−178,000 (−0.1%)**
- annual benchmark revisions over the prior ten years had an absolute average of **0.2%** of total nonfarm employment
- the benchmark compares the sample-based CES estimate with more comprehensive employment counts derived primarily from state unemployment-insurance tax records filed by nearly all employers
- the preliminary benchmark is not immediately incorporated into the official CES time series; the final benchmark is scheduled for February 2027 with the January 2027 Employment Situation release
- BLS explicitly notes that the benchmark compares two independently derived employment counts, each with its own sources of error

### BLS final benchmark — March 2025

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, “CES Benchmark Announcement,” last modified February 11, 2026.

https://www.bls.gov/web/empsit/cesbmk.htm

Relevant boundaries:

- March 2025 total nonfarm payroll employment was revised downward by **862,000 (−0.5%)**, not seasonally adjusted, from the previously published level
- after BLS’s stated data reconstruction for selected banking series, the benchmark error was **−861,000 (−0.5%)**
- BLS describes annual benchmarking as a standard re-anchoring of sample-based employment estimates to more comprehensive UI tax-record counts
- over the prior ten years, absolute benchmark revisions averaged **0.2%**, with a range from less than 0.05% to 0.3%

## Editorial use

The chapter uses the contrast between the relatively small March 2026 preliminary revision and the much larger March 2025 final revision to show that a statistic has a measurement clock. Early estimates can be useful precisely because they arrive before exhaustive administrative data; later revisions are part of the measurement process rather than evidence that the original estimate was fraudulent or worthless.

The chapter deliberately does **not** say that 79,000 or 862,000 jobs “disappeared” when BLS published the revision. The underlying employment had already occurred; the statistical estimate was being re-anchored.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

Revisions can be rhetorically abused to imply that official statistics are generally unreliable. The BLS record cuts against that inference. The March 2026 preliminary national revision was only 0.1%, and the ten-year average absolute benchmark revision reported by BLS was 0.2%. The stronger lesson is not “do not trust estimates,” but that users should retain the distinction between preliminary estimates, later revisions, and final benchmarked series.

The reverse caution also matters: a small aggregate national revision does not imply that every detailed industry, state, or local estimate is equally precise. BLS notes that more detailed series can have larger percentage revisions because sampling error is greater at finer levels.
