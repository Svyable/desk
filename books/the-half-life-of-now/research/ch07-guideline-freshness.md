# Chapter 7 research note — freshness without novelty worship

## Claim under test

Chapter 7 argues that clinical guidance and drug information are time-stamped products of an evidence process. The useful claim is not that medicine should always adopt the newest finding. It is that high-consequence medical knowledge needs explicit update mechanisms because evidence, populations, safety signals, and feasible practice can change after a guideline or label is issued.

## Primary evidence

### WHO living mpox guideline — May 2025

The World Health Organization published *Clinical management and infection prevention and control for mpox: living guideline, May 2025* on June 2, 2025. WHO describes the document as a living guideline that incorporates new evidence to update recommendations dynamically. The guideline-development group evaluates interventions when WHO judges that sufficient evidence exists to support a recommendation, and the process considers resource implications, acceptability, feasibility, equity, and human rights in addition to the evidence itself.

This is a useful concrete boundary for the manuscript. A living guideline is not a stream of instant reactions to every new paper. It is an institutional mechanism for shortening update latency while retaining an evidence threshold and a formal recommendation process.

Source: World Health Organization, *Clinical management and infection prevention and control for mpox: living guideline, May 2025*, published June 2, 2025: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09434

### FDA orlistat labeling change — June 2026

On June 10, 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved changes to the Drug Facts label for over-the-counter alli (orlistat) to warn about rare kidney injury and kidney-stone risks. FDA said it re-evaluated the safety signal using adverse-event reports and the medical literature covering the period from the drug's February 9, 2007 approval through December 31, 2023. The agency identified 12 relevant cases, while explicitly noting limitations in spontaneous-report data, missing clinical details, and alternative patient risk factors.

This case sharpens the chapter's claim that a drug label is not the endpoint of knowledge. Post-market evidence can change the information judged important enough to place in labeling years after approval. It also supplies the chapter's own countercase: the evidence was not treated as mechanically decisive. FDA recorded uncertainty and competing explanations while still deciding the signal warranted a labeling change.

Source: U.S. Food and Drug Administration, *FDA Approves Labeling Changes for Over-the-Counter (OTC) Weight Loss Drug alli (Orlistat) to Warn of Risk of Kidney Stones and Kidney Injury*, June 10, 2026: https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-approves-labeling-changes-over-counter-otc-weight-loss-drug-alli-orlistat-warn-risk-kidney

## Strongest counterargument

Faster updating can make clinical systems worse when early evidence is noisy, unstable, or difficult to implement. A living-guideline model therefore does not support the proposition that every new study should alter practice immediately, and a post-market safety signal does not prove causation merely because a regulator acts on it. Both sources actually support the manuscript's narrower formulation: freshness has to be governed by evidence quality, consequence, reversibility, and the decision being made.

## Editorial implication

A later manuscript pass should prefer these concrete mechanisms over generic references to "new studies" and "updated labels." The strongest version of Chapter 7 is not a complaint that medicine moves slowly. It is an argument that medicine has to manage two risks at once: stale authority and premature certainty. Living guidance and post-market labeling are institutional attempts to keep those risks in tension.
