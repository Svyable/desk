# Chapter 14 evidence note — temporal authority

Checked 2026-09-09 for Chapter 14, “The Memory of Machines.”

## Primary / authoritative evidence

### CFPB: reporting clocks for accurate negative information

Current Consumer Financial Protection Bureau guidance, last reviewed September 2, 2026, says credit reporting companies can generally report negative account-payment information for up to seven years; bankruptcies can remain for up to ten years. The CFPB separately notes that a reporting company may retain information in its files after it is no longer ordinarily reported.

Sources:

- https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-information-stay-on-my-credit-report-en-323/
- https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/is-it-possible-to-remove-accurate-but-negative-information-from-my-credit-report-en-1249/

This is useful to the chapter because it provides a concrete institutional distinction between **preserving a historical record** and **continuing to expose that record to a class of present decisions**.

### CFPB / FTC: the seven-year period is not a universal right to forgetting

The FCRA framework contains important exceptions. CFPB guidance notes circumstances in which ordinary time limits do not apply, and bankruptcy can be reported longer than most other negative information. CFPB and FTC material on background screening likewise shows that different record types and decision contexts can receive different treatment.

Sources:

- https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_fair-credi-reporting-background-screening_2024-01.pdf
- https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/tenant-background-checks-and-your-rights

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

Aggressive forgetting is not inherently pro-freshness. Long-lived records can remain highly relevant to fraud detection, medicine, security, legal accountability, scientific reproducibility and institutional memory. Some retention is legally required; some archives exist precisely because interested parties would prefer inconvenient evidence to disappear. The chapter should therefore argue for **temporal authority**, not indiscriminate deletion: preserve evidence where justified, but make its current status, provenance and permitted decision weight explicit.

The FCRA example itself supplies the boundary. Seven years is not a universal scientific estimate of when information becomes irrelevant. It is a legal rule for particular consumer-reporting contexts, with exceptions. The manuscript must not generalize that duration to recommender systems, medical records, criminal records, AI memory or other domains.

## Editorial consequence

The chapter was rebuilt around a narrower proposition: machines create a freshness problem when cheap retrieval and copying allow historically accurate information or inference to retain present-tense authority after its relevance has weakened. The new opening uses consumer reporting as a concrete example, then distinguishes record accuracy from inference freshness, preservation from decision weight, and durable evidence from durable authority.
