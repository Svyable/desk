# The Half-Life of a Fact

Some facts seem to carry their own permanence.

Paris is in France. Water contains hydrogen and oxygen. A triangle drawn on a flat plane has three sides. The year 1969 came before 1970. We learn statements like these and store them without worrying that Tuesday may invalidate them.

Other facts arrive already running toward expiration.

The flight leaves from Gate 32. The pharmacy closes at nine. The price is $18.40. The patient is taking this medication. The company employs 4,200 people. The server is healthy. The road is open. The candidate is leading the poll. The wildfire is moving east. The bank balance is sufficient.

We call all of these things facts, but they do not behave the same way in time.

That linguistic flattening creates trouble. A database field does not announce whether it is closer to geography or to a gate assignment. A sentence in a report does not naturally reveal whether it should be trusted for ten years or ten minutes. A model may treat a person's age and a person's current employer as neighboring features even though one changes predictably and the other can change overnight.

The half-life of a fact is the period over which its decision value declines because the world it describes can change.

The phrase is metaphorical. Radioactive decay has mathematical regularity. Facts do not. Some remain stable until a single event invalidates them. Some degrade gradually. Some become less reliable as they move away from the moment of observation. Some are durable in one context and perishable in another.

A person's date of birth is durable as a historical fact. The person's age is derived from it and changes continuously. Their mailing address may be stable for years, then suddenly wrong. Their physical location may change by the minute. Their professional skills can grow, decay, or become newly relevant as technologies shift. Their political preference may be stable for a decade and change after one event. Their relationship status can be both legally categorical and emotionally ambiguous.

One human record contains many clocks.

Computer networks have a useful concept for this: time to live. A system may cache information for a specified period, then refresh it. The cached answer is not assumed to be eternally valid merely because it was valid when received. The system is designed around the possibility of change.

Human institutions often cache without a TTL.

A manager forms an impression of an employee during a difficult first month and carries it for years. A doctor inherits a diagnosis entered long ago and treats it as settled. A newspaper profile fixes a public figure at one moment and readers keep the portrait. A credit system remembers a missed payment after the circumstances around it have changed. A family assigns a role to the unreliable sibling, the responsible sibling, the difficult parent, the gifted child. The cache persists because refreshing the relationship is work.

The fact may have become a story.

Stories have especially long half-lives because they are compression devices. They organize many observations into one portable interpretation. "This neighborhood is dangerous." "That company is innovative." "This country is poor." "He is bad with money." "She hates change." "The agency is incompetent." Once a story becomes useful, new observations are filtered through it.

A story can survive the facts that created it.

This is not always irrational. Repeated behavior deserves memory. Reputation protects people from having to rediscover every risk from scratch. Institutions need precedent. Science accumulates rather than beginning from zero each morning. The problem appears when the update cost becomes so high that the story is effectively immortal.

Facts decay not only because the world changes but because our relationship to the fact changes.

A statistic can remain numerically correct while losing relevance. Suppose a report accurately describes household internet access five years ago. The number is still a true description of that year. It becomes less useful for deciding today's broadband policy as adoption, pricing, technologies, and work patterns change. Historical truth remains. Operational truth expires.

This distinction between historical and operational truth is essential.

Historical truth asks, "Was this accurate about the world then?" Operational truth asks, "Is this accurate enough about the world now to guide this decision?"

A statement can pass the first test and fail the second.

Archives optimize for historical truth. Operations require current truth. Many institutions force one artifact to serve both purposes.

The annual report is archived as a record and reused as a planning input. The census is preserved as history and used to allocate resources for years afterward. The scientific paper remains in the literature and continues influencing practice after newer evidence arrives. The legal ruling remains precedent even as technology changes the factual landscape around it. The résumé records a career and also competes for a current role.

Every reused historical artifact needs a bridge to the present.

Scientific knowledge complicates the picture because science is both durable and revisable. The popular caricature says science "keeps changing its mind," as if revision discredits knowledge. In reality, the ability to update is part of scientific strength. Conclusions differ in durability. Some are supported by enormous bodies of evidence. Others are early estimates, context-dependent findings, provisional associations, or results awaiting replication.

The problem for public understanding is that publication often erases these differences.

A headline can make a tentative study and a mature consensus look like the same kind of object: "Research shows." The phrase hides the age, replication history, method, sample, and relationship to prior evidence. It converts a living evidence process into a static fact-shaped sentence.

Then the next study arrives.

The apparent contradiction may reflect normal updating, but the audience experiences whiplash.

A freshness-aware culture would teach people not only to ask whether a claim is supported but what kind of support it has and how quickly that support is expected to change.

Medical evidence demonstrates this vividly. Some clinical principles remain stable. Others evolve as trials accumulate, drugs enter or leave use, diagnostic definitions change, population evidence improves, and harms become clearer. A clinician cannot reread the entire literature before every appointment. Guidelines compress evidence into usable recommendations. That compression is necessary. It also creates a new object with its own half-life: the guideline.

The same pattern appears everywhere expertise is packaged.

A building code compresses engineering knowledge. A security standard compresses threat experience. An investment policy compresses a risk philosophy. A textbook compresses a field. A model compresses data. A checklist compresses judgment. Each artifact allows people to act without recreating the original reasoning.

Compression increases scale and creates staleness risk.

The more widely an artifact is reused, the more important its refresh process becomes.

There is a temptation to solve this by attaching dates. Dates help. They are not enough.

A document "updated yesterday" may still rely on assumptions from ten years ago. A website can change its footer automatically. A policy team can revise formatting without revisiting the central premise. A model can be retrained on new data while keeping features that encode an obsolete worldview. Freshness cannot be inferred from modification time alone.

The right question is what changed in the world the artifact claims to represent.

This suggests a richer notion of age.

There is chronological age: how long since the artifact was created or reviewed.

There is observational age: how old the underlying data is.

There is conceptual age: how long since the categories and assumptions were reconsidered.

There is environmental distance: how much the world has changed since the artifact was validated.

A five-year-old model in a stable domain may have low environmental distance. A five-week-old model deployed into a market transformed by a new regulation may have high environmental distance.

Calendar time is only a proxy.

This is why "annual review" can be such a weak control. A review date says nothing about whether meaningful change happened six months earlier or whether nothing meaningful has changed for six years. Event-driven systems are often better where the important changes are observable.

A credit policy might need review after a major legal change. A disaster plan might need review after new construction alters evacuation routes. A curriculum might need review when a foundational tool changes the work students are preparing to do. A model might need review when input distributions shift. A professional credential might need continuing education because the field evolves.

Each trigger is a theory of what can make the old truth stop being sufficient.

The deepest challenge is that we often do not know the half-life in advance.

Novelty creates unknown decay rates. When smartphones appeared, many institutions could not know how quickly they would change navigation, retail, photography, journalism, dating, payments, transportation, and attention. When a new pathogen appears, the first estimates of transmission, severity, and treatment effectiveness are necessarily uncertain. When a new technology changes a labor process, we may know the old job description is aging before we know what should replace it.

Uncertain half-life requires monitoring rather than a fixed schedule.

The system must watch for evidence that its assumptions are breaking.

This is familiar in engineering. A machine can be maintained on a calendar or on condition. Calendar maintenance replaces parts after a certain period. Condition-based maintenance watches temperature, vibration, pressure, or other signals and acts when the machine's state changes. Neither approach is universally superior. The lesson is that age and condition are different dimensions.

Knowledge needs condition monitoring too.

What signals tell us a category is failing? Rising exceptions. More manual overrides. Declining predictive performance. Increased appeals. Customer behavior that stops matching segmentation. Experts creating workarounds. New terms appearing outside the official taxonomy. Decisions becoming harder to explain. People arguing over what a metric "really means."

These are signs of conceptual wear.

Organizations often respond by tightening compliance with the old system. More training. Better documentation. Stronger enforcement. Sometimes that is correct. Sometimes the people resisting the system are telling us the map has aged.

The half-life of a fact also depends on adversaries.

In cybersecurity, once a defense becomes known, attackers adapt. In markets, once an opportunity becomes widely known, behavior can erase it. In platform governance, once users understand moderation or recommendation rules, some will optimize around them. In taxation, regulation, and fraud detection, people respond to categories and thresholds.

The representation changes the environment, which shortens its own useful life.

Adversarial domains are therefore hostile to permanent models.

A fraud rule saying transactions above a certain amount deserve scrutiny may work until fraudsters adapt. A spam filter succeeds until senders learn what it blocks. A hiring screen becomes less informative when applicants learn which keywords it rewards. A school ranking changes behavior once schools optimize to the metric. A risk score changes the population that reaches later stages.

The more a rule matters, the more likely someone is to learn it.

This creates the Goodhart-shaped form of freshness debt: a measure can decay because people begin treating it as a target.

The original correlation may have been real. The environment changes in response to the measure's use.

Facts about social systems are often reflexive this way. They are not merely observed; they are heard.

This means a social fact's half-life can shorten as its influence grows.

A labor shortage estimate changes wages and recruiting. An election poll changes donations and turnout efforts. A product review changes demand. A crime map changes policing and residential decisions. A credit rating changes borrowing costs. The information participates in the world it describes.

There is no equivalent in the boiling point of water.

This is why importing the language of stable physical measurement into social systems can be misleading. The number may be precise while the underlying relationship is adaptive.

The half-life idea also reveals a problem with expertise. Experts accumulate knowledge, and accumulated knowledge is their advantage. But the asset can become a liability when the environment changes discontinuously. A novice may have less knowledge and fewer obsolete assumptions. This does not mean novices outperform experts generally. It means expertise needs an update mechanism just as databases do.

The most dangerous sentence in a changing environment can be "I have done this for thirty years" if the thirty years are being offered as proof that the next year will behave the same way.

Experience is evidence. It is not immunity from drift.

The same is true of institutions. Longevity can indicate robust design or merely successful adaptation in the past. A hundred-year-old institution is not necessarily stale. It may be extraordinarily good at renewal. The age of the organization and the age of its assumptions are different things.

Some old institutions survive because they know how to revise without losing identity.

This is an underappreciated form of resilience: the ability to keep a durable core while refreshing the operational map.

A constitution, a university, a newspaper, a scientific society, a company, or a family may preserve names, rituals, and commitments while changing practices. Stability at one layer can make adaptation at another layer possible.

Freshness is not a demand that everything become liquid.

It is a demand that we know what we are trying to preserve.

A fact can be durable because the world is stable. A principle can be durable because we choose to make it stable. A commitment can remain binding precisely when circumstances change. "I will care for you" is not a prediction. It is a promise. Treating promises as facts with expiration dates would misunderstand them.

This matters because institutions mix descriptive and normative statements.

"Employees work in the office five days a week" is descriptive until it becomes policy. "Citizens vote at these locations" describes an arrangement created by rules. "This profession requires this credential" may encode both a judgment about competence and a legal choice. When conditions change, we have to ask whether we are refreshing a description or renegotiating a commitment.

Not everything that ages should be updated automatically.

The half-life lens is most useful when it forces a conversation about the type of object we are dealing with.

Is this a measurement? A forecast? A memory? A model? A rule? A promise? A category? A reputation? A standard? A story?

What makes it valid?

What could make it stop being valid?

How would we know?

Who gets to decide?

Those questions turn freshness from a vague preference for newness into a discipline.

The world does not owe our facts permanence.

Some will last. Some will erode. Some will fail in a moment. Our responsibility is not to predict every expiration. It is to stop pretending that truth has no clock.
