# The Half-Life of Now

**Why Everything We Know Is Getting Old Faster**

Sven Hardy Benson

## Author's note

This book is about a peculiar kind of error: the error of being correct about a world that has already changed.

We know how to recognize false information. We have entire institutions organized around it. We correct mistakes, retract bad numbers, revise diagnoses, patch software, update laws, and replace maps. What we are worse at recognizing is information that remains true in the narrow sense while becoming unusable in the practical one. A restaurant really was open on Tuesdays. A neighborhood really did have that population. A clinical guideline really did summarize the best available evidence. A model really did fit its training data. A policy really did address the problem as lawmakers understood it. A résumé really did describe a person's experience. The trouble begins when the clock keeps moving and the artifact does not.

I call the accumulated gap between the world and the representations we continue to act on **freshness debt**. The term is deliberately plain. Debt is a useful analogy because staleness often feels harmless when it is first created. We postpone an update because the current version is good enough. We keep a process because changing it is expensive. We reuse a category because everyone already understands it. We delay revising a model because retraining takes time. We leave a law untouched because reopening it would create political risk. Each decision can be individually sensible. The liability appears later, when enough delayed updates compound.

Freshness debt is not the same thing as ignorance. It often hides inside expertise. The more carefully a system was built, the more confidence people may place in it after its assumptions have begun to expire. A spreadsheet with clean formatting can be more dangerous than a guess if nobody asks when its inputs were collected. A policy manual can outlive the organization it describes. An algorithm can give precise answers to a distribution that no longer exists. A person can spend years defending a self-description that once helped them survive and now prevents them from changing.

This is also not an argument for constant novelty. There is a reason civilization stores things. Memory is one of our greatest technologies. Laws should not change every morning. Scientific conclusions should not be discarded because a new preprint appears. Institutions need continuity, standards, archives, precedent, routines, and habits. Stability is not the enemy. The problem is unmarked age: when we preserve an answer without preserving the conditions under which it was an answer.

The central question of this book is therefore not, "How do we make everything faster?" Speed can make error travel faster too. The better question is, "How do we know what must be refreshed, how often, by whom, and at what cost?"

That question sounds technical until it reaches everyday life. It determines whether a doctor sees the newest evidence or an outdated contraindication. It shapes whether housing policy reflects the city people live in or the city planners measured years earlier. It decides whether an AI system knows that a company failed yesterday, whether a military command structure can absorb information without surrendering judgment, whether a classroom prepares students for a labor market that changed during the curriculum approval cycle, and whether a friendship can survive two people continuing to argue with old versions of each other.

The phrase "half-life" comes from domains where decay can be measured. I use it here as a metaphor, not as a claim that every fact loses value according to a neat exponential curve. Some knowledge lasts centuries. Some operational information expires in seconds. Some things become stale gradually; others cross a line because one event changes everything. The important move is to stop treating age as incidental. Information has a time dimension whether or not our systems record it.

The chapters that follow move from maps and measurements into organizations, medicine, markets, law, cities, education, artificial intelligence, media, conflict, climate, politics, and personal identity. The last part turns from diagnosis to design. It asks what expiry-aware institutions might look like and what rights people may eventually demand when stale data is used to make fresh decisions about them.

A book about staleness has to admit its own condition. Every concrete claim in these pages belongs to a date. The research trail records sources and counterevidence so later readers can tell where the manuscript's own knowledge may have aged. That is not a ceremonial appendix. It is part of the argument.

The book will become old. The question is whether it knows that.
