# The Company That Thinks Too Much

The first company to waste money on artificial intelligence will not know it is wasting money.

It will have dashboards.

The dashboards will show requests served, documents processed, hours estimated to have been saved, agents deployed, code generated, summaries created, and token costs declining. Employees will report that the tools are useful. Executives will have seen impressive demonstrations. The company will be doing more with AI every quarter.

The mistake can live inside the word more.

Organizations are good at measuring activity produced by a new system and bad at measuring the work the activity displaced, the errors it introduced, the attention it consumed, and the decisions that would have been made just as well without it.

Token maxing makes this accounting problem unusually severe because the marginal unit is tiny.

Nobody convenes a capital committee to approve another ten thousand tokens. An agent calls a model. A workflow adds a verification pass. A developer increases context. A vendor turns on a feature. The individual decision is too small to notice. The aggregate can become a line item measured in millions.

Cloud computing prepared companies for this experience.

Before the cloud, acquiring servers was visible. Hardware was purchased, installed, depreciated, and guarded by people whose jobs depended on knowing it existed. Cloud infrastructure turned capacity into an API. That flexibility helped companies move faster. It also produced bills whose complexity became its own industry. FinOps emerged because cheap, decentralized infrastructure consumption needed centralized economic discipline.

AI will need a similar discipline, but token accounting is more difficult than server accounting.

A server can be idle. A generated token looks like work.

That appearance makes cognitive waste politically harder to cut.

If a team says it needs more compute because the model is researching customer problems, the request sounds closer to asking for headcount than asking for disk space. Who wants to be the executive who saves twenty thousand dollars by making the system think less and then discovers a missed risk cost twenty million?

Fear favors overprovisioning.

The rational answer is not a universal token cap. It is value-based allocation.

A company should spend more machine cognition where the expected value of a better answer is high. Fraud detection, pricing, security, legal review, complex sales, scientific research, and major capital decisions can justify deep inference. Routine formatting may not.

This seems obvious until organizational incentives intervene.

Teams do not pay directly for every downstream cost. The AI platform team wants adoption. The business unit wants results. The cloud provider wants usage. The employee wants convenience. The compliance team wants more checks. The security team wants more monitoring. Each request is locally reasonable.

The company receives the sum.

This is the tragedy of the cognitive commons.

The resource is not necessarily scarce at the hardware level. The scarce resource is the organization's ability to distinguish useful machine work from machine work that exists because nobody has an incentive to remove it.

Legacy companies already contain enormous amounts of human process that fits this description.

A report is prepared because another report references it. A meeting exists because a committee requires the meeting. A presentation is built because the meeting needs slides. People copy data among systems because no one owns the integration problem. Bureaucracy is often work generated by work.

AI can automate the bureaucracy.

That sounds good.

It can also make bureaucracy cheap enough to expand.

A compliance department that once sampled one hundred transactions can inspect every transaction with AI. That may reduce risk. The same department can then create new categories of review because review is cheap. Each category produces alerts. Alerts require triage. Triage requires summaries. Summaries are generated by AI. Management receives more risk information than it can absorb.

The company becomes safer on paper and more confused in practice.

Information abundance does not eliminate managerial attention as a bottleneck.

A chief executive can read only so much, no matter how many agents prepare briefings. Summarization helps, but summarization is itself selection. The machine decides which facts survive compression. If managers respond by asking for more summaries from more agents, they can create an inverted pyramid of analysis above a narrow point of human decision.

The company that thinks too much is not one with too much intelligence.

It is one that fails to convert intelligence into commitment.

Strategy exposes this failure clearly.

AI can generate scenarios endlessly. What if the competitor cuts price? What if China slows? What if the regulator intervenes? What if rates fall? What if this supplier fails? What if adoption doubles? What if adoption disappoints?

Scenario analysis is valuable because the future is uncertain.

It becomes harmful when another scenario postpones a decision whose value depends on acting before certainty arrives.

Real strategy is partly the willingness to close branches.

A company chooses a market and therefore does not pursue another. It builds one factory and not five hypothetical factories. It hires a person. It signs a contract. It ships a product. Action is a reduction of possibility.

Token maxing increases the supply of possibility.

This can make anxious management teams worse.

Before AI, analysis paralysis encountered a natural cost. Consultants charged money. Employees had limited hours. Research took time. Eventually the organization had to decide because continued analysis was expensive.

An agent can keep producing alternatives at midnight.

The stopping rule disappears.

Some companies will solve this by formal governance: an inference budget for decisions, deadlines, confidence thresholds, escalation criteria. The best systems will know that a question has consumed enough research and force a decision state.

The interesting part is that the discipline resembles good management before AI.

Define the decision. Identify what information would change it. Set a deadline. Distinguish reversible from irreversible choices. Do not research facts whose outcomes cannot change the action.

AI does not make these principles obsolete.

It punishes organizations that never learned them.

A badly framed question consumes tokens without creating clarity.

This makes executive skill more important, not less.

The naive story says AI gives leaders better answers. The stronger story says AI increases the number of answers leaders can request, raising the value of knowing which answer matters.

This is also where consultants face both threat and opportunity.

If a consulting engagement consists mainly of collecting public information, making standard analyses, and producing polished slides, token maxing attacks the cost base. The client can perform more of the work internally.

If the consultant's value lies in framing, political navigation, proprietary benchmarks, implementation, or telling the chief executive something employees cannot safely say, the work is less easily commoditized.

The consulting firm itself becomes a token-maxed organization. It can produce more analyses per partner. That either raises margins, lowers prices, expands demand, or reduces junior hiring. Competition decides the split.

The same happens in law, accounting, investment banking, advertising, and software services.

Companies whose business model historically billed for cognitive hours face an uncomfortable arithmetic. If each worker can produce twice as much, charging twice as many hours becomes difficult. The firm must move toward value, outcome, risk, or scarce expertise.

Token maxing pressures the billable hour because it makes the fiction of hours increasingly visible.

Clients will ask why a task costs ten thousand dollars if an AI performed the first draft in ten minutes.

Professionals will answer, correctly in many cases, that the value lies in review, responsibility, expertise, and consequences.

That answer changes the profession's economics.

The company buying AI faces a parallel question internally. Do we reward employees for output volume or outcome quality?

If management measures generated artifacts, workers will generate artifacts. If performance systems reward quick response, employees will use AI to answer more quickly. If code contribution is measured by lines, agents will produce lines. If sales activity is measured by outreach, inboxes will fill with machine-crafted messages.

Token maxing magnifies every bad metric.

Goodhart's law becomes cheap.

This is why deployment quality depends on organizational maturity.

A well-run process with clear outcomes can benefit from AI because automation attacks real bottlenecks. A badly run process can use AI to accelerate confusion.

The technology industry often prefers to tell the first story because it is easier to sell a tool than a management reform.

The management reform is the larger prize.

When AI makes the cost of a task visible, companies can ask why the task exists at all.

Why summarize a meeting that should not have happened? Why automate a report nobody uses? Why build an agent to move data between two systems instead of removing one system? Why generate personalized outreach to people who never asked to hear from you?

The best AI project can be deletion.

This is a hard sell inside organizations because software vendors are paid to add capabilities, not remove needs.

The token-maxed company therefore risks accumulating a cognitive application stack the way it accumulated SaaS subscriptions. Every department buys an assistant. Every assistant stores context differently. Permissions diverge. Outputs are duplicated. Employees ask three systems the same question because each knows a different slice of the firm.

Convenience fragments memory.

Central IT then tries to consolidate. Business units complain that central systems move slowly. A new generation of shadow AI emerges. The cycle repeats.

Large companies have lived this story with every computing wave.

The difference is that AI systems can act across applications, so consolidation has a stronger case. A good agent layer can hide fragmented software behind a common interface.

That creates another concentration point.

Whoever owns the agent layer sees the user's intent, the company's context, and the downstream applications. It becomes the new operating system of knowledge work.

The strategic stakes are enormous.

Enterprise software vendors understand this. Cloud providers understand it. Model companies understand it. The company buying the system should understand it too.

A token-maxed firm that outsources its cognitive control plane can become dependent even if each individual model remains replaceable.

This is why architecture matters.

Can the company route among models? Can it export memory? Are tool permissions portable? Are evaluations tied to one vendor? Can critical workflows run if a provider changes terms? Is proprietary context separable from the model service?

These questions sound like IT procurement until AI becomes central to operations. Then they become corporate strategy.

The company that thinks too much can also become the company that remembers too much.

Agents generate logs. Prompts are stored. Outputs are stored. Reviews are stored. Employee interactions become data. The company accumulates a detailed record of how work was performed.

This is useful for improvement.

It is also discoverable in litigation, attractive to attackers, sensitive under privacy law, and potentially toxic to employee trust.

Data retention becomes another optimization problem with no natural maximum.

More history improves context until the history becomes liability.

The mature organization knows what to forget.

That is not a poetic luxury. Deletion is a security control. Data minimization reduces attack surface. Retention limits reduce legal exposure. Old context can mislead models. Some memories should expire.

Token maxing encourages accumulation because future usefulness is easy to imagine. The cost of storage is small. The cost of future misuse is uncertain.

Companies will default toward memory until regulation, incidents, or discipline pushes back.

The financial side becomes more visible as usage scales.

AI budgets will migrate from experimental innovation pools into operating expenses. At that point chief financial officers will demand unit economics.

How much inference does it take to close a support ticket? How much revenue does the sales agent influence? Does code-agent use reduce cycle time after review and rework? Does automated legal analysis reduce external counsel spending? Does research mode change decisions or merely add citations?

The strongest organizations will answer with experiments rather than anecdotes.

AI adoption will eventually become too ordinary for anecdotes.

Nobody proves email ROI by asking whether employees enjoy email. Nobody justifies databases by showing a demo. Mature technologies disappear into process metrics.

AI will reach that stage.

When it does, many fashionable uses will vanish because they never produced enough value. Other uses will become so normal that companies stop calling them AI.

This is healthy.

The label is currently doing too much economic work.

A company can announce an AI initiative and receive credit for modernity before proving the initiative improves anything. Token maxing can exploit that credit by making activity easy to display.

The reckoning comes when boards compare spending with outcomes.

Some organizations will discover extraordinary gains. Others will discover they purchased a very articulate form of overhead.

The difference will not be explained solely by which model they chose.

It will be explained by whether the company knew what it was trying to accomplish before the meter started running.
