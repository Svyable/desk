# The Centaur and the Cyborg

When researchers gave hundreds of consultants access to GPT-4, the interesting result was not simply that the consultants became faster.

Many did. On tasks that sat inside the model's capabilities, participants completed work more quickly and produced results that human evaluators rated more highly. The deeper finding was that people used the system in different ways.

The researchers borrowed two metaphors. Some participants behaved like centaurs. They divided labor: this part for the human, that part for the machine. Others behaved like cyborgs. They intertwined their work with the model, moving back and forth continuously.

The metaphors survived because they captured a question every knowledge worker now faces.

Where should the person end?

The centaur keeps a boundary. She asks the system to summarize sources, generate alternatives, or draft a section, then takes the artifact back into human work. The cyborg dissolves the boundary. He writes a sentence, asks for critique, modifies the prompt, pastes a result into code, runs it, returns the error, asks another question, edits again. The machine becomes less like a contractor and more like a cognitive prosthesis.

Neither pattern is universally better.

The choice depends on the job, the worker, the model, and how costly it is to be wrong.

Token maxing pushes toward the cyborg because interaction is cheap. When each turn costs little, the user can keep the machine inside the loop. The conversation itself becomes the workspace.

This can be extraordinarily productive.

A person can externalize half-formed thoughts without bothering another human. She can ask for five counterarguments before committing to one. She can translate between technical and ordinary language. She can generate a test case the moment she imagines a bug. She can paste a confusing paragraph and request an explanation at exactly the level she needs.

Human collaboration rarely offers this much patience at such low social cost.

You hesitate before asking a colleague the same question four times. You do not hesitate with a model.

That changes who benefits from expertise.

The customer-support evidence suggests that AI can raise the performance of novices by making high-performer practices available at the moment of need. The consultant experiments suggest large gains on tasks inside the technology's frontier. A 2026 BIS study of European firms found AI adoption associated with higher labor productivity in the short run, with gains concentrated more strongly among medium and large firms and supported by complementary investment in software, data, and training.

The phrase complementary investment deserves more attention than it usually receives.

A model is not a productivity statistic.

The productivity effect arrives through a system: worker skill, data quality, software integration, management, incentives, process redesign, and enough organizational slack to change the way work is done. Buying the tool is easy. Reorganizing around it is not.

This is one reason technology produces widening gaps between firms even when the technology itself becomes cheap.

Two accountants receive the same model. One firm has clean data, modern software, documented workflows, training, and permission to redesign tasks. The other has scanned PDFs, contradictory spreadsheets, old software, and a manager who requires every AI output to be copied manually into three systems. The model is identical. The productivity outcome is not.

The same is true between people.

A domain expert can ask better questions because she knows where uncertainty lives. She notices when an answer violates a constraint the model overlooked. She can reject a plausible hallucination quickly. A beginner may be more impressed by the fluency and less capable of detecting the mistake.

This creates a puzzle because some studies show the largest gains for less experienced workers.

Both can be true.

AI can compress routine experience while still rewarding deep expertise at the boundary. The novice receives a large boost on common cases. The expert retains an advantage on unusual cases, framing, judgment, and verification. The performance distribution can narrow in the middle while the value of the very top changes in either direction.

Think of navigation.

GPS dramatically reduces the advantage of knowing every street in a city. The experienced taxi driver's memorized route knowledge loses economic value. Yet expertise does not disappear. It moves toward dealing with traffic patterns, unusual passenger needs, unsafe streets, local events, and situations where the map is wrong.

AI can do this to professions.

The average case becomes easier. The tail becomes more important.

For workers this means the safest skill is not merely being better than the model at today's task. That is a temporary competition against a moving benchmark. The stronger position is owning something that complements the model when the task changes.

Domain knowledge is one such asset. Relationships are another. Responsibility is another. Physical access, trust, taste, capital, legal authority, and the ability to act in the world all matter.

The cyborg worker assembles these around machine cognition.

This is why the person who “uses AI” may not be the useful category. Almost everyone may use AI. The economic distinction becomes how deeply the tool is integrated and what complementary assets the person brings.

A mediocre salesperson with an excellent drafting assistant remains mediocre if the hard part of the job is reading the room. A brilliant salesperson can use the same system to prepare faster, remember more, and personalize follow-up. The model helps both. The returns are not equal.

In some domains the reverse occurs. If the expert's advantage was mostly memorized procedure, the tool can erode it. The novice may gain more because the machine supplies exactly what the expert used to sell.

This is where income distribution becomes complicated.

Automation is often imagined as a race between workers and machines. Complementarity turns it into a sorting process among workers.

Some workers become much more productive because AI amplifies what they already do well. Others become more replaceable because the machine commoditizes the scarce element of their work. Still others gain in performance but lose bargaining power because every competitor gains too.

The last case is easy to overlook.

Suppose AI doubles the productivity of freelance copywriters. Each writer can produce twice as much. If demand for copywriting also doubles, incomes may hold. If demand rises only modestly, the market receives excess supply. Prices fall. Clients capture much of the productivity gain.

The workers are more productive and can still earn less.

Productivity is not a wage guarantee.

It becomes especially dangerous in token-maxed markets because output supply can respond almost instantly. A worker does not need years of training to expand capacity. She turns up the inference budget.

This can produce winner-take-more dynamics around reputation and distribution.

If competent output is abundant, customers choose among providers using trust, brand, audience, relationships, and proven outcomes. The best-known consultant can use AI to serve more clients. The unknown consultant can also use AI, but the scarce input is no longer analysis. It is being chosen.

The centaur becomes a platform.

A prominent expert can multiply herself across newsletters, courses, advice products, software, and personalized interactions. Fans receive more access. The expert captures more of the market. AI democratizes production while concentration increases in attention.

The same pattern already appeared in media. Digital tools made publishing cheap. They did not distribute audience evenly.

Token maxing makes the leverage steeper.

One person with strong taste, an audience, and a network of agents can operate like a small studio. A founder can produce research, code, customer support, design variants, legal drafts, and sales material without hiring a department for each. This can create a renaissance for unusually broad individuals: people who know enough across several domains to direct machine specialists and integrate their output.

The old economy often punished breadth. Organizations rewarded specialization because acquiring deep competence in several fields was too slow. AI can reduce the cost of borrowing depth.

The generalist gains a new instrument.

This is one reason the token-maxed worker may look less like the narrow specialist imagined by twentieth-century management and more like an early modern workshop master. She does not personally perform every craft. She understands enough of several crafts to commission, combine, judge, and sell the result.

That is the attractive renaissance story.

It has an aristocratic shadow.

The workshop master needs capital, access, confidence, and enough prior knowledge to supervise the tools. People who already possess education and networks can compound them through AI. A well-connected founder can run more experiments. A top researcher can explore more papers. A wealthy investor can purchase better systems, private data, and expert verification.

Cheap cognition does not erase accumulated advantage.

It can increase the return to it.

The IMF has warned that AI could exacerbate inequality both within and across countries depending on exposure, complementarity, preparedness, and access. In advanced economies, a large share of jobs is exposed because cognitive work is common. Some of those jobs can be enhanced. Others face substitution. The direction depends partly on whether AI complements the worker's tasks or replaces them.

This is not an abstract distinction for individuals.

A nurse using an assistant to reduce documentation may gain time for patient care. The machine complements the job. A transcriptionist whose primary output is the document may face direct substitution. A teacher using AI to generate differentiated exercises can serve students better. A low-cost tutoring service built entirely around AI may compete with the teacher's supplemental income.

The same person can be complemented in one task and substituted in another.

Jobs are bundles.

Token maxing unbundles them.

Once the machine can perform many cognitive microtasks cheaply, companies can inspect a job task by task. Drafting: automate. Scheduling: automate. Analysis: assist. Client conversation: human. Approval: human. Documentation: automate. Judgment: human for now.

The resulting role may be better. It may be more humane. It may also contain fewer paid hours.

This unbundling is why employment effects can be slow at first.

Companies do not need to fire everyone to capture savings. They stop hiring. They let attrition reduce headcount. They raise output expectations. They consolidate roles. They ask one person to supervise work that required two. The labor market feels the technology through missing vacancies before it sees dramatic layoffs.

Young workers notice first.

At the individual level, the rational response is to become good at the new bundle. That sounds obvious and unfair because not everyone has equal ability to retrain, experiment, or absorb temporary income loss.

The cyborg style itself requires learning.

Good users develop a sense for decomposition. They know when to ask the model for alternatives rather than answers. They preserve context. They build reusable instructions. They recognize when a deterministic tool is better than a generative one. They notice failure patterns. They use AI to challenge themselves rather than merely agree.

These are not mystical prompt-engineering tricks. They are workflow skills.

As interfaces improve, some of the skill will be automated. Systems will ask clarifying questions, route tasks, and choose tools on the user's behalf. The visible technique matters less over time. The deeper skill remains: maintaining an accurate mental model of what the machine can and cannot reliably do.

That model is hard to keep current because capability changes quickly.

A task that failed six months ago may now work. A task that looked safe may fail under a slightly different condition. Users can become underconfident or overconfident based on stale experience.

The worker who learns to calibrate repeatedly has an advantage.

This is where experimentation becomes a form of human capital.

People should not only learn AI. They should measure it in their own work.

The METR developer study is valuable partly because it exposed a gap between perception and measured productivity. The developers believed the tools helped even when the experiment found a slowdown in that setting. Later tools may reverse the result. The durable lesson is that feeling assisted is not enough.

The cyborg needs a stopwatch.

Companies will eventually conduct internal experiments: AI allowed versus not, different workflows, different models, different review requirements. Some uses will look impressive and save nothing. Others will quietly transform a process.

Individuals can do smaller versions. How long does this task actually take? How many errors survive? Does the model improve the result or merely produce a more polished first draft? Do I understand the output afterward? Would I notice if it were wrong?

Token maxing without measurement becomes superstition.

Measurement without judgment becomes another form of superstition.

Some benefits do not fit easily into speed. A model may make work less unpleasant. It may reduce anxiety about a blank page. It may help a non-native speaker participate more confidently. It may let a disabled worker perform tasks that were previously inaccessible. It may allow a parent to finish work in time for dinner.

These are productivity effects in the broader human sense even when a narrow metric misses them.

Some costs are equally hard to measure. Constant AI interaction can fragment attention. Reviewing machine drafts can become tiring. Workers may feel less ownership over output. A job can become more efficient and less satisfying if the craft disappears and only supervision remains.

The centaur and cyborg metaphors make the future sound heroic.

The lived experience may be closer to office ergonomics.

How much of the work feels like yours? How often are you interrupted? Do you trust the assistant? Are you learning? Are you accountable for output you did not create? Does the tool reduce drudgery or convert the job into quality control?

These questions will determine adoption as much as benchmark scores.

People resist technologies that make them feel incompetent, surveilled, or responsible for opaque decisions. They embrace tools that preserve agency while removing pain.

The best cyborg systems will therefore support authorship, not merely throughput.

They will let the person see enough of the process to understand the result. They will expose uncertainty. They will make correction easy. They will hand back control at meaningful moments.

The worst will turn humans into rubber stamps placed at the end of an automated chain so the organization can say a person was involved.

Human in the loop can mean authority.

It can also mean liability sponge.

That distinction will matter enormously for workers.

If the machine proposes and the employee approves, who carries the blame? The employee may be expected to verify output produced faster than any person can genuinely inspect. Token maxing can generate work at machine speed and then demand human accountability at human speed.

A worker given one hundred AI-generated cases to review is not necessarily augmented. She may be overwhelmed more efficiently.

The centaur wins when the division of labor respects the strengths and limits of both sides.

The cyborg wins when integration increases capability without dissolving judgment.

Neither wins merely because more tokens were consumed.

The central individual advantage in the token-maxed world may be the ability to remain the author of a process that includes machines.

Not the person who does every task.

Not the person who accepts every answer.

The person who can still tell where she ends.
