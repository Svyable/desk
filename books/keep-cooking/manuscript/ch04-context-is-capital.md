# Context Is Capital

The first generation of language-model demos made intelligence look oddly amnesiac.

You asked a question, received an answer, closed the window, and much of the relationship disappeared. The model knew astonishing amounts about the public world and almost nothing about Tuesday morning. It could explain accounting standards yet did not know which supplier your company had just fired. It could draft a contract but did not know that the chief executive had promised the client an unusual indemnity on a phone call yesterday.

This was not a small weakness. It revealed the difference between general intelligence as a product and useful intelligence inside an institution.

Organizations run on context.

The official context lives in databases, contracts, policies, source code, invoices, customer records, research notebooks, design files, tickets, presentations, and email. The unofficial context lives in habits, reputations, grudges, exceptions, timing, tacit knowledge, and the sentence everybody remembers from the meeting even though nobody wrote it down. A new employee spends months learning what the company means when it says “urgent,” which customer is patient, which system is fragile, who can actually approve a change, and which rule is routinely ignored because the rule was written for a world that no longer exists.

A general-purpose model arrives without any of this.

Token maxing attacks the problem by feeding the machine more of the institution.

Long context windows are the bluntest method. If a model can read hundreds of pages, then instead of summarizing the relevant material in advance, a user can provide the whole contract, the whole report, the whole transcript, perhaps the whole repository. Context becomes a resource that can be purchased in larger quantities.

The psychological effect is enormous. The user no longer has to decide what matters before asking the model. She can delegate relevance itself.

This feels like freedom from a tedious constraint. It is also the beginning of a new capital structure.

A company with high-quality internal context can make the same underlying model much more valuable than a company without it.

This is the quiet reason the model layer may not capture all the economics of AI. Models can become widely available while context remains private. A law firm owns decades of briefs, matter histories, negotiated clauses, and outcomes. A manufacturer owns maintenance records and process data. A hospital owns longitudinal patient information. A software company owns code, incidents, support tickets, and product analytics. A logistics operator owns routing history and exception handling. These assets existed before generative AI. The model changes what can be extracted from them.

In the token-maxed world, proprietary context behaves like ore discovered after the invention of a better refinery.

The ore was always there. Its value changes because processing becomes cheaper.

This creates a seductive corporate idea: put everything in the context window.

The idea will be attempted with enthusiasm and then punished by reality.

More context is not automatically better context. Documents conflict. Old policies remain searchable after new policies replace them. Customer data contains errors. Meeting transcripts preserve speculation beside decisions. Duplicate files create the illusion of corroboration. Permissions matter. Trade secrets sit next to harmless material. Personal data cannot simply be poured into every external model. The larger the context, the more opportunities there are for irrelevant material to distract the system or for a malicious instruction hidden inside a document to redirect it.

A company can be information-rich and context-poor.

The distinction resembles the one between wealth and liquidity. Owning assets does not mean you can use them at the moment you need cash. Owning terabytes of documents does not mean an agent can retrieve the right fact at the moment it needs to act.

This is why retrieval became one of the most important supporting technologies around language models. Instead of stuffing the entire organization into every prompt, a system searches for relevant chunks and supplies them as needed. Retrieval-augmented generation is an inelegant name for a simple idea: let the model consult the filing cabinet.

The filing cabinet quickly becomes an institution of its own.

Someone has to decide what is indexed. Someone has to preserve access controls. Someone has to attach metadata. Someone has to deal with deleted files, superseded policies, version history, and records that are legally discoverable but operationally misleading. Someone has to know whether the assistant is permitted to retrieve payroll data while answering a manager's question about staffing.

The moment AI becomes useful, information governance stops being an administrative afterthought and becomes part of productive capital.

This will surprise companies that spent years treating their internal data as digital sediment.

A firm that kept careful records, standardized systems, and maintained clean permissions may suddenly discover that it can automate sophisticated workflows with less effort. A firm that grew through acquisitions and accumulated twelve incompatible customer databases may discover that its expensive AI cannot tell which address is current.

The gap is not model capability. It is organizational memory.

This is good news for boring competence.

Technological revolutions often reward assets that seemed unglamorous before the new technology arrived. Reliable electrical wiring became more valuable when factories electrified. Standardized shipping containers were not impressive pieces of engineering compared with ships, but they helped transform global trade because they made interfaces predictable. Clean data, consistent identifiers, documented processes, and explicit permissions may play a similar role in AI deployment.

The companies that win token maxing may be the ones whose organizations can be tokenized without becoming nonsense.

That is harder than it sounds.

Consider email. It contains context, but context contaminated by performance. People write for audiences. They hedge. They flatter. They omit. They forward. They change their minds. A model reading all corporate email would not obtain pure institutional truth. It would obtain a vast archive of human behavior, including the difference between what employees say and what they do.

Meeting transcripts are no cleaner. A meeting can contain brainstorming, jokes, tentative suggestions, rejected options, and a final decision. A transcript preserves them with equal textual dignity. The model has to infer which sentence became policy.

Humans make these distinctions using social knowledge.

Who spoke? How serious were they? Did the room agree? Was the issue resolved later? Was the meeting before or after the incident that changed everyone's mind? The words alone are only part of the state.

This means the best context systems will gradually collect structure around language. Decisions. Dates. authority. provenance. confidence. supersession. ownership. The machine needs not merely more text but a map of what the text means inside the organization.

Context engineering will therefore become a form of institutional design.

The phrase may sound like another temporary profession invented by an industry that enjoys naming obvious work. Yet the underlying function is real. Somebody must decide what an agent is allowed to know, what it should know, how current that knowledge is, and how the system can tell the difference between an instruction and a piece of content that merely contains imperative language.

The stakes rise sharply when agents can act.

A chat assistant that retrieves the wrong policy gives a bad answer. An agent with permissions can cancel an order, send a refund, modify code, schedule a transfer, or change a record. Context is no longer just evidence for text generation. It becomes part of the control plane.

This is where token maxing collides with security.

The more sources an agent reads, the larger its attack surface. A malicious instruction can be hidden in a web page, document, issue tracker, or message. If the system cannot reliably distinguish trusted instructions from untrusted content, additional context can make it more vulnerable rather than more capable. The very mechanism used to give the machine richer knowledge also gives adversaries more places to whisper to it.

The solution will not be “less context” in the abstract. It will be better boundaries.

Human organizations already understand this imperfectly. An employee can read an email from a stranger without treating the stranger as his boss. He can open a spreadsheet without executing the sentence in cell A14 as corporate policy. Software agents need analogous distinctions represented in machine-readable form.

This creates a hierarchy of context.

Some context describes the world. Some context describes the task. Some context grants authority. Some context sets policy. Some context is evidence. Some context is untrusted input. Treating all tokens as equal is as dangerous as treating every sentence in a courthouse as a judge's order.

The economics reward firms that encode these differences.

At first, companies will compete over model access. Then models become more interchangeable. The advantage shifts toward integration: who has connected the model to the right data and tools. After integration becomes common, the advantage shifts again toward the quality of the underlying institutional state.

This progression makes certain kinds of incumbent power stronger.

A company with millions of customers has interaction data a startup does not. A platform that mediates work has context about how work flows. An enterprise-software vendor already sits beside the databases where decisions are recorded. If AI value depends increasingly on context, incumbents can use installed systems as an advantage even when they do not have the best base model.

The startup answer is speed and focus.

A new firm can design its processes around machine-readable context from the beginning. It can avoid decades of legacy systems. It can choose one domain and capture structure incumbents ignored. It can build a product that creates better data as a by-product of use. The incumbent owns history. The startup can own cleanliness.

Both are forms of capital.

This also changes mergers and acquisitions. A company may be purchased not for its revenue but for its context: proprietary conversations, labeled outcomes, workflows, expert annotations, or access to a specialized environment. The old euphemism was data. Context is broader. It includes the relationships that make data interpretable.

Regulators will struggle with this because ordinary competition analysis often treats data as an asset that can be counted or described by category. The advantage may lie not in volume but in linkage. Ten million documents are less valuable than one million documents tied cleanly to outcomes, identities, permissions, and decisions.

The token-maxed firm learns from its own operation.

Every agent run produces traces. What did the system retrieve? Which draft was accepted? Where did a human intervene? Which recommendation led to a sale? Which code patch was rejected? These traces can improve routing, retrieval, evaluation, and future behavior. Usage produces context that improves usage.

This can create a flywheel.

It can also create surveillance.

The same instrumentation that teaches a company which AI workflow succeeds can reveal intimate details about employees. If every draft, revision, override, hesitation, and approval becomes training data for the system, work becomes measurable at a new resolution. A manager can see who accepts AI output, who edits heavily, who asks for help, who handles exceptions, who slows the agent down.

The company says it is building organizational memory.

The worker may experience it as organizational memory that never forgets her mistakes.

This is one of the individual costs hidden inside the phrase data advantage. Data is rarely found lying peacefully on the ground. It is generated by people, customers, communities, devices, and institutions. Turning it into context redistributes power toward whoever controls the system that records and interprets it.

The global version is even larger.

Languages with abundant digitized text, standardized terminology, and strong online representation become easier for models to serve. Countries with fragmented records, low digitization, or large informal sectors have less machine-readable context. Local knowledge may exist richly in people and poorly in databases.

A farmer can know exactly how rainfall behaves on a particular hillside and contribute nothing to the corpus used by an agricultural agent. A community health worker can understand local disease patterns that never enter a structured national system. A small manufacturer can operate through relationships and handwritten logs that are invisible to modern models.

The AI divide is therefore not only a compute divide.

It is a context divide.

The World Bank's work on AI foundations emphasizes connectivity, compute, data, and skills because the ingredients reinforce one another. A country can purchase model access and still fail to capture value if public records are unusable, local-language resources are thin, or institutions cannot integrate the outputs.

This is where simple stories about leapfrogging become dangerous.

A low-income country may indeed leap over missing layers of professional services by using AI. It may also discover that the services were missing partly because the underlying state capacity, records, infrastructure, and trust were weak. A model can explain how to register a business. It cannot make a contradictory land registry coherent by generating more tokens.

Tokens are good at consuming context. They are not magic substitutes for context that never existed.

The same is true inside a person's life.

Personal assistants become far more useful when they remember preferences, commitments, relationships, documents, health information, finances, and history. A model that knows nothing about you can answer questions. A model that knows your life can take responsibility for continuity.

The price is obvious.

The best personal context is also the most sensitive data a company can possess.

Calendar. Messages. location. purchases. medical records. family relationships. unfinished drafts. private doubts. The closer an assistant gets to being genuinely useful, the closer it gets to being a comprehensive behavioral archive.

This creates a profound bargain.

People will be offered more capable intelligence in exchange for more context. Many will accept because the convenience is real. The system can book the restaurant you actually like, notice the insurance form you forgot, prepare for the meeting, remind you what you promised, and filter the noise. The value comes from intimacy.

The risk comes from the same place.

A token-maxed society will therefore spend enormous effort deciding who may hold context, under what conditions, for how long, and with what ability to move it elsewhere.

Data portability becomes cognitive portability. If your assistant has learned you for five years, switching providers may feel less like changing software and more like moving a memory.

Companies will understand the lock-in value immediately.

Regulators will eventually understand it too.

The largest context windows in the world are impressive engineering achievements. They are not the end state. The end state is a world in which institutions learn to make their memory queryable by machines.

That world will produce astonishing competence.

It will also force a question older than computing.

Who gets to remember?
