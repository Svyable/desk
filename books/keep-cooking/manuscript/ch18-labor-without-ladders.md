# Labor Without Ladders

An economy can have plenty of work and still fail to produce careers.

That distinction matters more than the headline unemployment rate.

A job is a transaction: labor for pay.

A career is a sequence. The early job teaches something that makes the later job possible. Pay, responsibility, knowledge, reputation, and networks accumulate. The worker climbs.

Token maxing threatens some sequences before it threatens total work.

The mechanism is simple. AI performs or accelerates the standardized tasks usually assigned to beginners. Firms need fewer beginners relative to experienced workers. The remaining beginners become more productive, which can be good for them individually and bad for the number hired.

The ladder narrows at the bottom.

This is not unique to white-collar work. Automation has repeatedly changed entry routes in manufacturing, clerical work, and skilled trades. What is new is the breadth of cognitive exposure and the speed with which software can spread.

A factory robot must be installed in a physical plant.

A language model can reach millions of office workers through a software update.

The labor market experiences this as a faster diffusion of task change.

The ILO's estimate that one in four workers globally are in occupations with some generative-AI exposure is useful because it rejects both complacency and apocalypse. Most of those jobs will not disappear. Many will transform. The high-exposure share is much smaller than the broad exposure share.

Transformation sounds gentle.

It can be violent for the person whose transformed job no longer exists at her experience level.

Imagine an accounting firm that once hired twenty graduates. AI reduces the time spent on routine reconciliation, first-pass research, document extraction, and drafting. The firm can serve the same clients with twelve graduates and the same number of seniors.

Eight jobs vanish from the hiring plan.

No occupation disappeared. Productivity rose. Clients may pay less. The firm may earn more. Twelve graduates get better tools.

Eight people experience the revolution as rejection emails.

Multiply the pattern across sectors and the labor market can soften at the entry level while aggregate employment remains strong.

This is one reason young workers can feel a technological transition before older workers do.

Incumbents hold firm-specific knowledge, relationships, credentials, and senior responsibilities that are difficult to replace. New entrants compete against the machine for the standardized portion of work.

The first generation of AI displacement may therefore be less “robots fire fifty-year-olds” and more “companies hire fewer twenty-two-year-olds.”

That distribution has long-term consequences.

Early unemployment scars earnings. Delayed entry delays skill accumulation. Workers take jobs outside their intended field and may never return. Family formation, housing, and geographic mobility can shift.

A small change in the first rung can propagate through decades.

The macroeconomic policy system is not designed neatly for missing rungs.

Unemployment insurance supports people who had jobs. It does less for graduates who never get the first one. Retraining programs assume a worker is moving from an obsolete occupation into a growing one. The token-maxed problem may involve occupations that still grow while hiring fewer beginners.

The institution needed is not retraining after displacement.

It is formation before employment.

Apprenticeships, internships, subsidized first jobs, simulation-based training, public-service placements, and employer incentives can supply experience the market underprovides.

This sounds like education policy because the boundary between education and employment begins to blur.

If production no longer requires beginners to practice on real work, training must become explicit.

Professional schools may take on more of what firms used to teach. Firms may create internal academies. Governments may co-finance apprenticeship because human-capital formation has become a public good.

The countries that solve this problem gain a labor advantage.

They produce workers who can supervise and complement AI rather than merely use consumer tools.

The countries that do not may have educated populations with weak work histories.

This is especially relevant where youth unemployment is already high.

AI can create opportunities for self-employment and remote work, but it can also compress the outsourced service jobs that many developing economies hoped would absorb educated young workers.

The global labor ladder can narrow too.

For three decades, digital globalization allowed companies to move routine cognitive tasks across borders. Business-process outsourcing, call centers, software services, transcription, design, and back-office work created pathways into global service markets.

Generative AI competes with part of that model on cost and speed.

A company that once arbitraged wage differences between countries can now arbitrage labor against compute.

This does not end service globalization.

It changes what is worth trading.

Complex client work, domain expertise, cultural knowledge, relationship management, implementation, and AI supervision can remain valuable. Routine document production and standardized support face greater pressure.

Countries built around service exports need to climb the value chain faster.

That phrase is easy to say from a conference stage and hard to live at population scale.

Moving millions of workers from standardized service tasks into complex advisory work requires education, management quality, language skills, institutions, and time. AI itself can assist the transition, but it does not remove those complements.

This is how a technology can increase world productivity and widen inequality between workers.

The gains go first to people positioned near complementary assets.

High-skill workers with domain knowledge can use AI to expand output. Owners of firms and capital can capture profits. Consumers receive lower prices. Workers performing tasks that become abundant face wage pressure.

The distribution can look like a barbell.

At one end, highly leveraged experts. At the other, physical and interpersonal work that remains difficult to automate. In the middle, routine cognitive roles compressed.

This is not a certain forecast. Labor markets are inventive. New occupations appear. Demand changes. Technology creates tasks as well as removes them.

But the barbell mechanism is plausible enough to shape policy.

A routine office job has historically provided stable middle-class income in many economies. If those roles shrink, people do not all become AI architects or electricians.

Some move down the wage distribution.

Some exit the labor force.

Some become independent contractors using AI to compete in oversupplied markets.

Some build successful small firms.

The variance increases.

Token maxing can make labor more entrepreneurial by lowering the cost of business formation. Entrepreneurship is often celebrated as freedom. It also moves risk from firms to individuals.

The worker with a salary becomes a freelancer with an infinite colleague.

She gains autonomy and loses benefits, predictable income, and employer-sponsored training.

This can be a good trade for some people and a forced trade for others.

Policy should not confuse the existence of opportunity with the distribution of risk.

The same applies to wages.

If AI raises worker productivity, economic theory gives labor a reason to earn more only when workers retain bargaining power and the productivity is complementary to them. If the tool is widely available and workers are substitutable, competition can prevent them from capturing the gain.

The customer or capital owner captures it instead.

This is why firm-level evidence showing higher wages among AI-adopting firms, such as the 2026 BIS study of European firms, is encouraging but not a universal law.

Early adopters may be stronger firms. Gains can change as adoption diffuses. Different sectors have different labor markets.

The mature distribution depends on scarcity.

Which human capability remains scarce after the machine improves?

That capability earns the rent.

The answer can change quickly.

Prompt-writing skill looked scarce for a moment. Better interfaces reduced its scarcity. Model-specific expertise becomes less valuable when routing is automated. Basic coding skill can be commoditized while system architecture remains scarce.

Workers need portable complements.

Deep domain understanding. trust. leadership. sales. physical skill. regulatory accountability. creative judgment. the ability to learn new systems. These are broad categories, not guarantees.

The danger of lists like this is that they become advice to individuals for a structural problem.

“Become more adaptable” is useful and insufficient.

Not everyone can occupy the scarce complement. Scarcity means the position is limited.

A society needs enough good roles for ordinary competent people, not only strategies for exceptional survivors.

This is where demand creation matters.

If AI makes services cheaper, governments and markets can expand provision. More tutoring. more elder care administration. more preventive medicine. more small-business support. more scientific research. more public legal help. more infrastructure planning.

Productivity can be used to increase quantity rather than reduce labor.

Public policy can influence this through funding and service design.

Consider education.

If AI reduces the cost of lesson preparation and grading, a school can reduce staff or give teachers more time with students. The budget and institutional objective decide.

Healthcare is similar. Automating documentation can reduce administrative headcount, increase patient capacity, or return time to clinicians.

There is no technological imperative that chooses the distribution.

The same is true for working time.

Token maxing revives the old dream that productivity should buy leisure.

If a knowledge worker can produce in twenty hours what once required forty, why not work twenty?

Because output expectations are relative.

If competitors use the other twenty hours to produce more, firms feel pressure to do the same. Individuals competing for promotion do the same. Productivity becomes throughput rather than leisure.

Reducing hours requires coordination, norms, or bargaining.

The market does not automatically deliver the four-day week.

It can deliver the same week with twice the output.

This is not necessarily bad. More output can raise living standards. The question is whether the worker values the additional consumption more than the lost time.

Economic statistics cannot answer that for her.

The labor politics of token maxing will therefore revolve around how gains are divided among wages, profits, prices, and time.

Those are the four ledgers.

Higher wages reward workers.

Higher profits reward owners.

Lower prices reward consumers.

Shorter hours reward time.

A productivity gain can flow into all four in different proportions.

Societies will argue as if the technology chose the proportions.

It did not.

Institutions, competition, taxation, bargaining, and policy did.

The tax system becomes important because AI may increase the capital intensity of cognitive production. If a company replaces wage spending with compute spending, payroll-tax bases can weaken while profits or capital income rise.

Governments that finance social insurance heavily through labor taxes may need to reconsider the mix.

This does not imply a “robot tax” is automatically wise. Taxing automation can slow beneficial investment. The better question is whether the overall tax system captures enough of the gains from capital and high incomes to finance transition and public goods.

Token maxing can produce abundance and fiscal strain at the same time if the income shifts away from the tax base designed for the old economy.

Social insurance also has to fit more volatile careers.

If people move between employment, contracting, entrepreneurship, training, and caregiving more often, benefits tied tightly to one employer become less compatible with the labor market.

Portable benefits become more valuable.

So does lifelong education that is actually usable by adults with bills.

The phrase lifelong learning has been repeated for decades because it is easier than building institutions that let a forty-five-year-old retrain without losing income, housing, or health insurance.

AI makes the slogan less optional.

The technology itself can reduce training cost through tutoring and simulation. That is one of its strongest social uses.

A displaced worker can receive personalized instruction, practice, translation, and career guidance cheaply.

Training does not create demand for the new skill.

That final step still depends on the economy.

This is why labor-market forecasting is so difficult. We can estimate task exposure more easily than equilibrium outcomes.

If millions of workers retrain into the same supposedly safe occupation, the occupation is no longer safe in the same way. Wages respond. Employers automate more. New needs appear.

The labor market is a moving system reacting to the forecast itself.

The most resilient policy is therefore not predicting every winning job.

It is reducing the cost of movement.

Good education. portable benefits. housing supply in productive regions. competition. public infrastructure. credential portability. childcare. healthcare. support for entrepreneurship. income protection during transition.

These are not glamorous AI policies.

They are the policies that let people survive technological change.

The ladder metaphor eventually breaks because careers may become less vertical.

A person can move sideways among domains using AI to borrow expertise. A nurse becomes a healthcare-operations specialist. A teacher builds an education business. A technician manages robotic systems. A writer becomes a product operator. Generalists can recombine skills more easily.

The future career may be a lattice.

That is exciting for people with agency and terrifying for people who wanted a predictable sequence.

Institutions should preserve both possibility and stability.

The labor market does not need to freeze old jobs. It does need credible routes into new competence.

If token maxing gives every worker an extraordinary tool but removes the paid places where people learn to become valuable, society will have confused access with mobility.

A ladder is not merely something you climb.

It is something the next person can find.
