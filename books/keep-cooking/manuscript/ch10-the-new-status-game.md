# The New Status Game

Every abundant resource eventually finds a way to become a status symbol again.

Food became abundant in wealthy societies and people distinguished themselves by eating particular food. Clothing became cheap and people paid for scarcity, provenance, restraint, or the logo. Information became free and elite advantage moved toward filtering, networks, and credentials.

Machine intelligence will not escape status.

At first, the status object is access.

Who has the new model? Who received the research preview? Who can use the agent that works for an hour? Who has the larger context window? Who can afford the enterprise plan? The community screenshots benchmark scores and compares outputs. Capability itself is scarce enough to signal proximity to the frontier.

Then capability diffuses.

The status object becomes effort.

A short answer looks cheap. A long research trace looks serious. The premium interface spends minutes browsing. The model says it considered alternatives. The user tells friends that the agent ran overnight. A startup announces that every customer query is handled by a swarm of specialized agents. A research team reports how many samples it drew. A company describes an internal system that reads every document before making a recommendation.

Compute becomes conspicuous.

This is not entirely ridiculous. More effort can produce better results. A complex decision deserves more analysis than a trivial one. But social signals rarely remain perfectly attached to their original function.

Once “thought longer” becomes associated with “took the problem seriously,” institutions can overspend computation to demonstrate seriousness.

The same happens with human labor. A consultant can impress by sending a thick report. A lawyer can produce a long memo partly because the client expects visible work. Academic papers can become longer as methods and citations accumulate. Bureaucracies create documentation to show that procedures occurred.

Machine labor makes visible effort cheaper to manufacture.

A company can generate a hundred-page analysis at negligible marginal writing cost. The report's length no longer signals the hours once required to produce it.

This breaks an old heuristic.

Humans infer effort from artifacts. A hand-carved object reveals time. A dense financial model implies labor. A carefully written letter suggests attention. Generative AI weakens the connection between apparent effort and actual human cost.

The culture needs new signals.

One response is provenance: show the process, sources, evaluations, and approvals. Another is reputation: trust the person who selected the output. Another is scarcity: human-made becomes a premium label in some domains. Another is immediacy: a live performance proves something occurred in real time.

None is universal.

The interesting shift is that taste gains status when production loses it.

If everyone can generate a competent logo, the person who chooses the right logo matters more. If everyone can draft a polished essay, the value moves toward the idea, evidence, and editorial judgment. If every startup can produce a beautiful demo, investors care more about whether anyone wants the product.

This is not a romantic defense of human creativity. Machines can assist taste too. They can rank, critique, predict preferences, and search alternatives. But selection remains tied to an objective, and objectives are embedded in people and institutions.

Taste is compressed preference under uncertainty.

It says this one, not the other nine hundred.

Token maxing attacks taste by producing more candidates than a person can experience.

That creates another status divide: the ability to refuse abundance.

The wealthy have long paid for curation. A private banker filters investments. A gallery filters art. An editor filters manuscripts. A chef filters ingredients. A chief of staff filters information. Scarcity of attention makes delegation valuable.

AI can democratize curation by giving everyone a filter.

It can also create a new premium tier of human curation because machine filters themselves become abundant and correlated.

The person who can say “I read the whole thing” may acquire status in a world of summaries.

The student who solves without assistance may signal unusual ability. The programmer who understands the system without querying an agent may become valuable in emergencies. The executive who can write a lucid memo directly may appear refreshingly competent.

Old skills can become luxury signals after they stop being economically necessary.

Handwriting survived typing. Vinyl survived streaming. Film photography survived digital cameras. Chess remained popular after computers surpassed humans because the human contest retained meaning.

Some cognitive crafts will persist for the same reason.

People will write unaided not because unaided writing is the cheapest way to make text but because the act itself matters. People will memorize poetry, solve mathematics by hand, draw, code, translate, and navigate for pleasure, identity, education, and proof of skill.

This does not protect every job.

A craft can survive culturally while losing commercial volume. Horses did not vanish after automobiles. The economy around everyday transportation still changed completely.

The status game can hide that loss because elite practice remains visible.

A celebrated human writer continues to sell books while thousands of routine writing jobs shrink. A famous illustrator sells original work while commercial illustration becomes heavily automated. A top programmer becomes more leveraged while entry-level coding roles narrow.

The existence of prestigious survivors does not imply broad labor stability.

Token maxing creates another cultural hierarchy around model choice.

At first this looks like camera enthusiasts debating lenses. People compare reasoning models, context limits, benchmarks, tool use, and prices. Over time most users stop caring about the underlying model because applications route automatically.

Experts keep caring.

The ability to choose the right cognitive engine becomes analogous to knowing which database, compiler, or statistical method to use. It is invisible to outsiders and materially important inside the craft.

This is a familiar path in technology. Early automobile owners knew engines. Later drivers turned a key. Specialists still knew engines. AI will become easier at the surface and more complex underneath.

The status of technical fluency therefore bifurcates.

Basic prompt skill becomes ordinary literacy. Deep system understanding becomes specialized professional capital.

In the middle, a vast culture of folk expertise will flourish.

People will trade instructions for making models “think harder,” argue about whether politeness wastes tokens, share agent architectures, post screenshots of impossible tasks, and develop rituals whose empirical value ranges from substantial to imaginary.

This is inevitable because the system is probabilistic and changes quickly. When cause and effect are noisy, superstition grows.

Financial markets, sports, medicine, and farming have all produced rituals around uncertain outcomes. AI will produce its own.

The token-maxer becomes a social type.

He refuses the first answer on principle. He runs multiple models. He asks one to critique another. He keeps enormous context files. He talks about inference budgets the way an audiophile talks about signal chains. When a simple task fails, he adds agents.

Sometimes he gets astonishing results.

Sometimes he spends five dollars and twenty minutes to avoid thinking for thirty seconds.

The culture will parody him because cultures defend themselves against excess through humor.

“Keep cooking” is already a joke structure: the idea that more time, more compute, or another iteration may rescue the result. The joke works because everyone recognizes the impulse.

The danger arrives when the impulse becomes institutional prestige.

A chief executive can approve a large AI budget partly because sophisticated organizations are expected to have one. A government can announce a national model because national models signal technological sovereignty. A university can integrate AI into everything because appearing behind is reputationally costly.

The expenditure may have real strategic value. Status and strategy can coexist.

That coexistence makes waste hard to identify.

A data center can be both useful infrastructure and a monument. A large model can be both a research achievement and a prestige project. A corporate agent system can both improve work and reassure investors that management understands the future.

The economic historian has seen this before.

Railway stations, skyscrapers, corporate headquarters, airports, and factories are productive assets that also signal power. Infrastructure attracts symbolism because scale is visible.

AI infrastructure is less visible to ordinary people, but the numbers perform the symbolism instead.

Billions of parameters. Hundreds of thousands of accelerators. Gigawatts of planned power. Million-token contexts. Benchmarks with decimals. Capital expenditure in the tens or hundreds of billions.

Quantities become rhetoric.

This is especially potent in an industry where outsiders cannot directly inspect capability.

A person can look at a bridge. A model's intelligence is inferred through demos, benchmarks, anecdotes, and scale claims. Compute becomes a proxy for seriousness because more compute has historically correlated with capability.

The proxy can become a target.

Once investors reward scale, companies have incentives to emphasize scale. Once users associate longer reasoning with quality, interfaces emphasize reasoning. Once governments associate domestic compute with sovereignty, national plans emphasize compute capacity.

The relationship may remain real while becoming distorted.

This is Goodhart's law with expensive hardware.

The counterculture will prize smallness.

For every token-maxer there will be a token minimalist. Use the smallest model that works. Keep context tight. Cache results. Ask better questions. Prefer deterministic software. Run locally. Avoid agent loops. Measure before scaling.

The minimalist will have economics on his side whenever marginal compute adds little.

He will also have aesthetics.

Efficiency can be beautiful. A compact proof, an elegant algorithm, a short program, a clear sentence, a small model performing unexpectedly well—engineering culture has always admired doing more with less.

The conflict between maximalists and minimalists will be productive because both are partly right.

Maximalists discover capability by spending resources aggressively. Minimalists turn capability into infrastructure by making it affordable.

Frontier progress often follows this rhythm. A breakthrough is demonstrated at extravagant cost. Engineers compress it. The saved cost enables wider use. Wider use creates demand for more ambitious applications. The cycle begins again.

Token maxing is the expansion phase of the loop.

Optimization is the harvest.

The social question is who benefits during each phase.

Frontier scale favors capital-rich labs, hyperscalers, and states. Compression favors diffusion. A technique that requires enormous inference budgets belongs to a few. A technique that can be distilled into a small model spreads.

This makes efficiency research politically important even when it sounds technical.

A tenfold reduction in compute can change who is allowed into the market. It can move inference from cloud to device. It can reduce dependency on foreign providers. It can make a public service affordable. It can allow a small firm to run its own system.

Efficiency is distribution policy written in code.

The status culture can work against this by treating smaller systems as inferior even when they are adequate.

Consumers do this with many products. They buy specifications they do not use because higher numbers signal future-proofing or quality. AI vendors have every incentive to encourage the instinct.

A person may pay for the largest model to summarize a grocery list.

The waste is individually trivial.

At billions of users, trivial habits become infrastructure.

The cultural story also affects how humans judge one another.

If AI can make anyone sound polished, polish becomes less informative. Employers, readers, and audiences look for signals that are harder to generate. Specific experience. Original data. live reasoning. demonstrated execution. References from trusted people. A track record of being right.

This can improve culture by reducing the reward to superficial fluency.

It can also create harsher credentialism.

When written applications lose signal, employers may rely more on elite institutions, referrals, or verified work histories. The people who hoped AI would help them overcome a weak résumé may discover that everyone else's résumé improved too, causing gatekeepers to retreat toward harder-to-fake status markers.

Abundance can strengthen old hierarchies if new signals are not designed well.

The education system will confront this first.

A polished take-home essay no longer proves the same thing it once proved. Schools can respond with oral exams, supervised writing, project work, process logs, or assignments designed around AI use. Each choice privileges different skills and resources.

Students at wealthy schools can receive better preparation for the new assessments. Elite institutions can preserve costly human seminars. Mass institutions may rely more heavily on automated evaluation.

The same technology that personalizes learning can widen the prestige gap between human-rich and machine-rich education.

A private tutor becomes more valuable when everyone has an AI tutor if the private tutor's job is to provide motivation, accountability, and judgment rather than explanation.

Human attention becomes luxury education.

This does not make AI tutoring bad. It reveals the recurring pattern: make one input abundant and the complement becomes scarce.

The token-maxed society will discover scarce complements everywhere.

Original experience. verified identity. responsibility. physical presence. attention. silence.

Silence may be the strangest status symbol of all.

When every device can explain, recommend, summarize, and generate, the ability to work without machine commentary can become a premium experience. Retreats advertise no connectivity. Schools create AI-free sessions. Teams hold meetings where nobody consults an assistant. Writers rent rooms without screens.

People pay to restore the constraint technology removed.

This is not hypocrisy. Humans routinely build artificial scarcity to protect valued states. Gyms recreate physical exertion eliminated by machines. Meditation retreats recreate silence eliminated by communication. Camping recreates inconvenience eliminated by infrastructure.

We may create cognitive gyms for unaided thought.

The affluent will have more freedom to choose when to disconnect.

Workers measured by output may have less.

This is why token maxing cannot be understood only as a technology choice. It becomes a cultural expectation. If competitors use agents around the clock, declining to do so can look irresponsible. If colleagues generate extensive analysis, bringing a short memo can look lazy even when the memo is better. If students all use AI, unaided work can look inefficient.

Norms push people beyond the point their private cost-benefit calculation would choose.

That is the essence of a status game.

The individual is not merely deciding what works.

He is deciding what serious people are expected to do.

The healthiest culture will learn to separate visible compute from achieved value.

That sounds simple. It is one of the hardest things organizations do.

We still confuse hours worked with contribution, credentials with ability, meeting attendance with engagement, document length with rigor, and complexity with sophistication. Artificial intelligence gives us the chance to reproduce every one of those mistakes at machine scale.

The token count is measurable.

The judgment is not.

A civilization impressed by how long the machine thought may eventually rediscover the oldest mark of intelligence:

knowing what does not require another thought.
