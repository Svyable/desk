# The Grid Behind the Mind

A model does not know whether the electricity feeding it came from wind, gas, coal, uranium, falling water, or sunlight.

The grid knows.

The user asks for a poem in Chicago, a code review in Lagos, a molecular hypothesis in Boston, or a marketing plan in Singapore. The request travels through networks into a data center whose physical location may be invisible to the person. The answer returns as text.

Somewhere, generators moved.

This is the material fact token maxing keeps trying to hide through good interface design.

Intelligence arrives as a service. Energy arrives as infrastructure.

The International Energy Agency estimated in its 2025 *Energy and AI* report that global data-center electricity consumption could roughly double by 2030 in its base case, reaching around 945 terawatt-hours. In 2026 it reported that data-center electricity use had already surged during 2025 and that AI-focused facilities were growing faster still. Forecasts will change as hardware, models, and construction change. The direction is enough for our purposes.

Machine cognition has become a material energy demand.

In global percentage terms, the numbers can look manageable. Data centers remain a minority of total electricity consumption. In local grids, the same demand can be enormous.

Electricity systems are geographic.

A gigawatt consumed in one county cannot be supplied by a theoretical gigawatt on another continent. Transmission connects regions, but lines have capacity. Interconnection takes time. Local generation mixes differ. Water constraints differ. Permitting differs.

This is why national statistics can understate local conflict.

A data center that is modest compared with a country's electricity system can be one of the largest loads a regional utility has ever connected. The facility arrives with a timeline measured in a few years. Transmission and generation projects may require much longer.

The clocks collide.

Technology companies want capacity quickly because model competition moves quickly. Utilities plan assets that can operate for decades. Regulators must protect customers who did not ask to finance an AI race.

Token maxing therefore becomes a regulatory question at the substation.

Who pays for the new line?

If a hyperscaler requires a major grid upgrade, should the cost be recovered from that customer or spread across all ratepayers? What happens if the data center cancels after the utility has committed capital? What happens if it consumes more than forecast? How much generation should be built for a load whose technology and economics may change before the power plant is retired?

These are not questions model developers can answer with a benchmark.

They belong to public utility commissions, grid operators, engineers, local governments, and communities.

The distributional stakes are immediate.

A technology company may receive cheap power through a negotiated industrial rate while households face rising bills driven partly by infrastructure expansion. The company may create tax revenue and construction jobs. It may finance renewable generation that benefits the wider grid. It may compete with other industries for scarce connection capacity.

Every project carries a different balance.

This is why blanket claims that AI either “uses too much energy” or “will be solved by efficiency” are unsatisfying.

Efficiency and total demand are separate.

The IEA's 2026 analysis emphasized that energy use per AI task was falling rapidly while total AI-related electricity demand was rising. This is exactly what a successful token-maxing economy would produce.

The more efficient the task, the cheaper it becomes.

The cheaper it becomes, the more tasks become worth running.

The question is whether efficiency improves faster than demand expands.

No one knows the long-run answer because the set of future tasks is unknown.

If AI remains primarily a human-facing assistant, demand has one ceiling. If autonomous agents perform continuous research, monitoring, optimization, simulation, and machine-to-machine negotiation, the ceiling moves.

An agent does not need a person to be awake before using electricity.

This is why energy forecasts increasingly care about agentic use.

The rebound effect can be dramatic because computation is divisible. A company can increase reasoning effort on millions of transactions by a few percent without making a visible product change. The customer never sees the additional token demand.

The grid sees all of it.

Electricity therefore becomes part of AI product strategy.

Model providers can lower cost by using more efficient hardware, serving smaller models, batching requests, caching repeated computation, and shifting flexible workloads to times when power is cheap. Data-center operators can build near abundant generation. Workloads that do not require immediate answers can run when renewable output is high.

This gives AI an unusual potential role as flexible industrial demand.

Traditional factories often need to run continuously. Some inference can move across time and geography. A research agent asked to finish by morning can wait two hours for cheaper capacity. Training runs can sometimes be scheduled. Batch workloads can follow prices.

If designed well, token demand can help grids absorb variable renewable energy.

If designed badly, constant high utilization can add stress during peak periods.

The incentive depends on electricity pricing.

Flat rates hide scarcity. Dynamic prices reveal it.

This is a recurring theme of the book: good meters create better behavior when the meter tracks the real constraint.

A token price that ignores energy scarcity can encourage inference at exactly the wrong time and place. A system that includes time-of-use energy economics can route work intelligently.

The software architecture and the grid architecture begin to speak.

This could accelerate energy innovation.

Technology companies have strong incentives to secure large quantities of dependable power. They can sign long-term agreements that finance new renewable projects. They can support nuclear restarts, advanced geothermal, storage, or next-generation reactors. Their credit quality can make projects bankable.

AI demand can become an anchor customer for clean energy.

It can also become an anchor customer for fossil generation.

When demand arrives faster than clean capacity, natural gas is often an available dispatchable option. In some regions coal remains important. The IEA's analysis expects a mix of renewables, gas, coal, and nuclear to supply data-center growth globally, with regional differences.

There is no single carbon intensity of a token.

The number changes by data center, hour, grid, hardware, model, and workload.

This is why environmental claims built around one universal estimate are fragile.

A short request served on efficient hardware in a low-carbon grid differs radically from a large reasoning task in a fossil-heavy region. Training and inference differ. Cooling differs. Water use differs. Utilization matters.

The correct response to complexity is not to stop measuring.

It is to measure at the level where decisions can change.

Providers can report energy and carbon intensity more transparently. Customers can route workloads based on environmental constraints. Regulators can set standards. Researchers can compare efficiency.

Token maxing makes such transparency more important because users otherwise experience the resource as immaterial.

The water issue illustrates the same invisibility.

Data centers use water directly in some cooling systems and indirectly through electricity generation and semiconductor manufacturing. The amount varies widely by technology and climate. In water-stressed regions, a facility's demand can become politically salient even when global totals look small.

Again geography dominates averages.

One liter used where water is plentiful is not equivalent to one liter used during drought.

The token-maxed economy will learn to price local constraints or fight over them.

The physical footprint also creates winners that the software conversation ignores.

Electrical contractors. Construction firms. utilities. landowners. transformer manufacturers. cooling specialists. fiber providers. generator developers. grid engineers. power traders.

A boom in machine cognition can raise wages for workers who never interact with a language model.

This is an important correction to the claim that AI is simply a labor-substituting technology.

It substitutes for some cognitive work and creates demand for physical infrastructure work.

The geographic distribution differs.

High-paying AI research jobs cluster in a few cities. Data-center construction can occur in regions with land, power, and fiber. Energy projects can revive industrial corridors. Semiconductor fabs create specialized manufacturing ecosystems.

The gains spread unevenly and often to different political constituencies.

A city may lose entry-level office jobs and a rural county may gain data-center tax revenue.

A state may gain construction and see higher power demand.

A country may import chips and export electricity-intensive AI services.

Token maxing rearranges comparative advantage.

Historically, energy-rich regions exported molecules or electrons and technology-rich regions exported high-value services. AI can link the two more tightly. A region with abundant cheap clean power and good connectivity can export computation.

Machine cognition becomes an energy-intensive tradable service.

This creates opportunities for countries with renewable potential.

Hydropower-rich regions, sunny regions with cheap solar plus storage, geothermal resources, nuclear fleets, or abundant gas can attract data centers if they also have stable institutions and networks.

Power alone is not enough.

A data center requires political reliability, skilled labor, physical security, connectivity, and supply chains. Cheap electricity in a fragile state does not automatically become compute leadership.

Still, energy can change the map of the digital economy.

This matters for development because countries that missed earlier manufacturing waves may try to position themselves as compute hubs.

The risk is enclave development.

A large data center can consume enormous capital and power while creating relatively few permanent local jobs. If tax incentives are generous and local firms are not integrated into the value chain, the host region can supply infrastructure without capturing much of the value.

The situation resembles extractive industries in one uncomfortable respect: the resource leaves in a form whose highest-value use occurs elsewhere.

The “resource” may be electricity converted into computation rather than oil converted into exports.

Good policy tries to build complementary ecosystems: research, startups, training, cloud services, local applications, grid investment that benefits other users.

Bad policy counts megawatts as development.

The same distinction matters in wealthy countries.

A region can attract data centers and still regret the bargain if electricity rates rise, water conflicts intensify, and tax revenue disappoints. Another can use the investment to finance grid upgrades that enable broader industrial growth.

Local governance decides much of the outcome.

This is the real-world consequence of token maxing that can be missed by people staring at model leaderboards.

The next unit of reasoning may require a transformer whose delivery time is measured in years.

The transformer becomes part of intelligence.

That fact changes national security too.

Electric grids are critical infrastructure. Concentrating enormous AI capacity in a small number of facilities creates strategic targets. Resilience against cyberattack, sabotage, weather, and grid failures becomes part of AI reliability.

Distributed inference can reduce some concentration. On-device models can operate without data centers for many tasks. Edge computing can shift demand. Smaller models can preserve services during network disruptions.

Efficiency is resilience again.

A society that can perform useful AI locally on modest hardware is less vulnerable than one that requires a handful of giant clusters for everything.

The frontier may remain centralized while the baseline diffuses.

This two-layer architecture resembles electricity itself. Giant generation and transmission coexist with batteries, rooftop solar, and local backup. Centralization and decentralization serve different needs.

Token maxing will find its balance.

High-stakes research and frontier reasoning may consume large centralized resources. Routine personal tasks move onto devices as hardware improves. Companies route work according to privacy, latency, cost, and energy.

The result could reduce pressure on data centers per task even as total cognition expands.

The environmental question is ultimately not whether AI consumes energy.

Everything a technological civilization does consumes energy.

The question is what value the energy produces, what alternatives exist, who bears the costs, and how quickly the supply can decarbonize.

A token used to discover a drug candidate and a token used to generate the thousandth spam email both consume infrastructure. The grid cannot distinguish their social value.

Markets only partly can.

Policy sometimes must.

This raises uncomfortable questions about rationing during scarcity. If a grid is constrained, should an AI data center curtail before a factory? Should flexible inference receive lower rates in exchange for interruption? Should critical public services receive priority compute? How should environmental costs be reflected in token prices?

These debates will seem strange until the loads become large enough.

Then they will look like ordinary industrial policy.

The glamorous language of machine intelligence will fade at the utility hearing.

There will be spreadsheets, rate classes, depreciation schedules, transformer orders, and angry customers asking why their bill changed.

That may be healthy.

A civilization understands a technology more honestly when the technology reaches the boring institutions that must make it work.

The grid is one of those institutions.

It does not care whether the model is creative.

It asks how many megawatts, at what hour, for how long.
