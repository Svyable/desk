# Software Eats Headcount

Companies rarely announce the future of work by firing everyone at noon.

They change the requisition.

A team that expected to hire six people is approved for four. A vacancy remains open for another quarter. A manager is told to try the new tooling before requesting headcount. Attrition is not replaced. Contractors disappear first. A role is consolidated with another. The surviving employees receive higher output targets because they now have assistants.

This is how software can eat headcount without producing a dramatic layoff headline.

Token maxing makes the mechanism powerful because it attacks the marginal hire.

The relevant executive question is not “Can AI do this job completely?”

It is “Do I still need the next person?”

A company does not need an autonomous accountant to reduce accounting employment. It needs accountants with AI to handle more accounts. It does not need a model that replaces lawyers. It needs fewer associates per partner. It does not need a fully automated marketing department. It needs one marketer to produce the campaigns that once required three.

The threshold for labor-market impact is therefore much lower than full automation.

This matters because public debates often oscillate between two exaggerated positions. One says AI will replace most white-collar workers. The other notes that models still make errors and concludes jobs are safe.

Both miss the arithmetic of partial substitution.

A tool that performs thirty percent of a job does not eliminate thirty percent of the people mechanically. Tasks interact. Demand can expand. Workers can move into other activities. Firms can improve quality rather than reduce staffing. But if the tool consistently raises output per worker, competitive pressure eventually asks why staffing should remain unchanged.

The answer depends on elasticity.

When productivity increases, the price of a service can fall. Lower prices increase demand. If demand expands fast enough, total employment can rise even though each worker produces more.

This happened in many technologies.

ATMs reduced the need for tellers to dispense cash but helped banks operate more branches with changed teller roles for a period. Computers automated calculations while expanding the software industry. Cheaper transportation increased travel. Mechanized agriculture destroyed agricultural jobs as a share of employment while enabling labor to move into entirely different sectors.

There is no law that productivity gains reduce total jobs.

There is also no law that new demand arrives in time, in the same place, or for the same people.

The transition is the politics.

Generative AI is unusual because it reaches cognitive tasks across many occupations at once. The ILO's 2025 work estimated that roughly one in four jobs worldwide had some exposure, though high exposure was much smaller and concentrated in certain occupations. The IMF has estimated higher exposure in advanced economies because knowledge work is more prevalent.

Exposure is not unemployment.

It is a map of where task composition can change.

Companies will use that map selectively.

The first functions compressed are likely to share several traits: digital inputs, digital outputs, high labor costs, standardized work, measurable quality, and low need for physical presence. Customer support, software development, content operations, document processing, analytics, and administrative work fit many of these conditions.

The last functions are not necessarily the most intellectually sophisticated. They may simply be the most embedded in the physical or social world.

A plumber has lower direct exposure to language-model automation than a junior analyst because the pipe is in a house. A nurse's documentation may automate before bedside care. A salesperson's research may automate before the client dinner. An executive assistant's scheduling may automate before the emotional intelligence required to tell a powerful person that the day has become impossible.

The economy can therefore experience a strange inversion in the value of embodied work.

For decades, digitization rewarded jobs that could be performed on computers. Those jobs could scale globally and command high wages. AI arrives through the same digital channel and therefore reaches them first.

The screen becomes both ladder and target.

This does not mean electricians will all become richer than lawyers. Education, licensing, capital intensity, demand, and institutional power still matter. It means the relative scarcity of physical execution can rise when cognitive output becomes cheaper.

Software cannot unclog a drain by generating more tokens.

It can schedule the plumber, diagnose likely causes from photographs, quote the customer, optimize the route, prepare the invoice, translate the conversation, and teach a novice technician what to check next.

The worker at the end of that chain can become far more productive.

This is where labor substitution and augmentation meet.

One skilled technician supported by strong AI may handle jobs that once required a dispatcher, estimator, translator, and more experienced colleague on call. The physical worker survives and the support structure compresses.

Organizational charts get thinner around the edges of action.

Middle management faces a similar question.

Managers exist for many reasons, not all noble. They allocate work, summarize information, resolve conflicts, monitor performance, translate strategy, coach employees, approve exceptions, and represent layers of hierarchy to one another. Many of those functions are information processing.

Agents are good at information processing.

A company can use AI to generate status reports, assign tasks, flag delays, summarize meetings, compare performance, draft feedback, and answer policy questions. This reduces some managerial load.

What happens next depends on management philosophy.

One company expands each manager's span of control. Another removes a layer. Another keeps the managers and asks them to spend more time coaching. Another uses AI surveillance to centralize decisions.

The same technology can produce flatter organizations or more intense hierarchy.

The incentive for flattening is financial.

If AI makes coordination cheaper, some of the economic justification for layers disappears. A chief executive can receive synthesized information from farther down the company. Teams can access policy directly. Routine approvals can be automated.

The danger is that information summaries are not relationships.

Middle managers often carry tacit context upward and emotional reality downward. They notice when a high performer is about to quit, when a policy is producing resentment, when a customer situation does not fit the dashboard. Remove the layer and the formal information flow may improve while institutional sensing deteriorates.

Token maxing can make a company legible to senior management and less understood at the same time.

The labor savings look clean in a spreadsheet.

The cultural costs arrive later.

The biggest companies may nevertheless have strong incentives to try because labor is expensive and AI capital can scale. This shifts the capital-labor balance.

A company that substitutes purchased compute for wages moves spending from payroll toward cloud bills, software contracts, depreciation, and electricity embedded in vendor prices. The total cost may fall. The distribution changes even if it does not.

Workers receive wages and spend them broadly in local economies. Capital spending flows toward technology vendors, chip manufacturers, utilities, and investors. If AI raises the share of value captured by capital, income distribution can become more unequal even while total productivity rises.

This is one of the macroeconomic fears around AI.

It is not inevitable.

Workers can capture gains through higher wages if their complementary skills become scarce. Labor markets can remain tight. New industries can create jobs. Ownership can broaden through pensions and equities. Tax systems can redistribute. Cheaper goods and services create consumer surplus that benefits households regardless of employment category.

The distribution depends on institutions, not only technology.

Company strategy matters too.

An employer can use AI savings to reduce prices, expand, invest, raise wages, or increase profit. Competition often determines how much choice it really has.

If every rival can adopt the same tools, cost savings can become a requirement rather than a windfall. Prices fall until much of the productivity gain passes to customers.

This is good for consumers and harsh for workers if labor bears the adjustment.

The company may not feel like a villain. Management is preserving margins in a market where competitors also became more efficient.

Technology turns private rationality into collective pressure.

This is why “companies will choose to augment rather than replace workers” is not a complete policy strategy.

A company can prefer augmentation and still reduce hiring because augmented workers are more productive. Ethical intent does not repeal arithmetic.

The strongest offset comes from expanding demand.

If AI lowers the price of legal help enough, more people may purchase legal help. If personalized education becomes affordable, tutoring demand can explode. If software becomes cheaper to build, far more businesses may want custom software. If scientific analysis becomes faster, research programs can expand.

The future employment effect in each sector depends on how much latent demand exists.

This is why AI can simultaneously threaten incumbents and create access.

A routine legal document may become a low-margin automated service. That hurts some legal work. Millions of people who previously received no legal assistance may gain something useful. Society can be better served while a profession earns less per transaction.

The profession responds by moving toward difficult cases, relationships, litigation, negotiation, and liability.

The same happens to software.

If code generation lowers the cost of building applications, demand for software may expand dramatically. Businesses that never justified custom tools can now create them. Programmers can become more productive and remain numerous because the world wants much more software.

Or the market can mature and headcount can fall because much existing demand is already met.

The evidence will vary by period and domain.

The METR developer results are useful precisely because they warn against assuming automatic productivity gains. Experienced developers in mature codebases were slowed by early-2025 AI tools in that study, despite believing otherwise. By 2026 the research environment had changed enough that METR described suggestive signs of improvement but also severe measurement problems as developers became unwilling to work without AI.

The technology changes faster than labor statistics.

Companies will make staffing decisions under uncertainty.

They will overreact in both directions.

Some will cut too aggressively, lose expertise, and rehire. Some will preserve structures that become uncompetitive. Some will discover that AI makes their best people much more valuable and pay them more. Some will discover that average work quality can be produced by fewer people and compress wages.

This heterogeneity is the real future of work.

There will be no single AI labor market.

There will be thousands of task markets adjusting at different speeds.

The corporate hiring funnel changes before employment totals make the change obvious.

Entry roles become harder to justify. Senior specialists supported by AI can absorb more junior work. Companies hire fewer general analysts and more people with domain accountability. Job descriptions ask for AI fluency alongside existing expertise.

This can create an experience paradox.

Employers want experienced workers because AI makes experience more leveraged, while hiring fewer beginners who would become experienced.

Wages at the top can rise while the pipeline below weakens.

Consulting, banking, law, software, media, and other pyramid-shaped professions are especially exposed because their economics historically relied on many juniors beneath fewer seniors.

If AI compresses the pyramid, the business model changes.

Partners can serve more clients with fewer associates. Senior engineers can ship more with smaller teams. Editors can handle more material with fewer writers. The firm becomes flatter and potentially more profitable.

The profession becomes harder to enter.

This can increase inequality inside occupations.

The established expert owns reputation, clients, tacit knowledge, and credentials. AI multiplies that expert's output. The beginner owns potential.

Potential is difficult to bill.

Labor policy may need to care about training capacity the way industrial policy cares about production capacity.

Apprenticeships, subsidized training, professional education, and incentives for entry-level hiring can become infrastructure for human capital rather than private employee benefits.

Companies will resist paying alone for skills competitors can hire away.

This is a classic externality. Society benefits from a trained workforce; individual firms underinvest if they cannot capture the return.

AI increases the externality by making beginner production less necessary.

The other policy lever is bargaining power.

If workers have strong representation, they may capture more of the productivity gain through wages, shorter hours, or negotiated use of automation. If labor is weak and jobs are scarce, companies can capture more.

The technology does not choose between a four-day week and a four-person layoff.

Institutions do.

This point becomes almost embarrassingly important as productivity rises.

For more than a century, people have imagined machines creating enough abundance to reduce working time. In some periods hours did fall. In others productivity gains flowed into consumption and output rather than leisure.

AI renews the old promise.

If one worker can do the work of two, society can employ one worker half as much or employ one worker fully and produce twice as much.

The second option tends to win because firms compete and people like more goods, services, and income.

Token maxing intensifies that logic by making additional cognitive output almost frictionless.

The employee finishes early. The system suggests more work.

This is how abundance becomes expectation.

The company that once needed ten analysts can use five analysts and ten agents. The five may earn more. They may work less. They may supervise more. They may become exhausted by the volume of output. The other five may move into better jobs or spend months searching.

All of those futures can occur in the same economy.

The aggregate statistic arrives afterward and calls it productivity.
