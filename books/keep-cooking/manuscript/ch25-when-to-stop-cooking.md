# When to Stop Cooking

The problem with abundance is that it removes excuses.

When research was slow, you could stop because the library closed.

When design was expensive, you could stop because the budget was gone.

When analysis required a team, you could stop because the team had other work.

When writing another draft meant losing another evening, fatigue eventually settled the argument.

Machine cognition weakens all four stopping rules.

The library is always open. The designer is software. The team can be copied. The draft arrives in seconds. If another pass has even a small chance of improving the result, why not request it?

Keep cooking.

The phrase contains the promise and the pathology of the token-maxed age.

Sometimes the machine is one attempt away from finding the bug, locating the source, noticing the contradiction, or generating the alternative that changes the decision. Additional inference can buy real quality. Test-time scaling is valuable because hard problems sometimes deserve more effort.

Sometimes the additional tokens are steam escaping from a process that has forgotten what dinner is for.

The mature token-maxed society needs stopping rules at every level.

For a model, the rule asks whether another unit of inference is likely to improve the answer enough to justify cost and latency.

For a worker, it asks whether another generated alternative changes the choice.

For a company, it asks whether more analysis changes the decision or merely delays accountability.

For an investor, it asks whether more capital creates productive capacity or destroys returns through overbuilding.

For a government, it asks whether another national AI project adds capability or prestige.

For a society, it asks whether more machine output increases welfare after energy, attention, trust, labor transitions, and concentration are included in the ledger.

None of these questions has a fixed numerical answer.

That is irritating in an industry that loves metrics.

The temptation will be to invent one.

Tokens per task. agents per decision. percentage automated. model benchmark. AI adoption rate. compute capacity. productivity uplift. Every measure can illuminate something and become dangerous when it substitutes for the underlying objective.

The most useful measure is often boringly local.

Did the customer problem get resolved?

Did the student learn?

Did the code survive production?

Did the patient receive better care?

Did the worker save time?

Did the company make a better decision?

Did the public service become easier to use?

Did the investment earn an adequate return?

Token maxing deserves to survive only where it improves outcomes like these.

This principle sounds modest because technological culture tends to make the method feel like the objective. A company becomes proud of being AI-first. A government becomes proud of national compute. A worker becomes proud of an elaborate agent stack.

Tools become identities.

The corrective is to remember what tools are for.

The first policy of sensible token maxing is therefore measurement against a counterfactual.

Not “people liked the tool.”

Not “the model generated impressive work.”

Compared with what would have happened otherwise?

The customer-support study was valuable because it measured output with and without assistance across thousands of workers. The METR developer study was valuable because it found a result users did not expect. Both did something cultural anecdotes cannot do: they forced the tool to compete with reality.

Companies should reproduce this discipline internally.

Run trials. Measure cycle time after review. Measure error rates. Measure customer outcomes. Measure rework. Measure whether the employee actually saved time or spent the saved time supervising the system. Compare different models and different budgets.

Do not assume more inference is productive because more inference is visible.

This creates the foundation for adaptive compute.

Easy tasks deserve cheap treatment.

Hard tasks deserve escalation.

High-stakes tasks deserve independent verification.

Some tasks deserve humans before any machine attempt.

Some deserve machines first because withholding cheap assistance would be wasteful.

The system should treat cognition like medicine: enough for the case, not maximum dose because maximum exists.

The second policy is to preserve escalation.

A good AI service needs a route out of AI.

The customer can reach a person. The patient can reach a clinician. The employee can challenge the automated recommendation. The citizen can appeal the administrative decision. The junior professional can ask the senior why the machine is wrong.

Escalation is not a failure of automation.

It is part of safe automation.

This matters most for people with the least power because automated systems tend to handle the mass tier. Wealthy users can purchase exceptions. Public systems must design them.

The third policy is to preserve apprenticeship deliberately.

If AI removes routine beginner work, institutions need to reconstruct the learning function without reconstructing every pointless task.

This means simulation, supervised practice, shadow decisions, rotations, oral examination, controlled AI-free exercises, and paid roles whose purpose includes formation rather than immediate output.

The phrase paid matters.

Unpaid apprenticeship becomes a filter for wealth.

If professional experience moves out of entry-level employment and into private extracurricular practice, class advantage hardens.

Companies benefit from a skilled future workforce. Governments benefit. Professions benefit. The cost should not fall entirely on the twenty-two-year-old asked to somehow arrive experienced.

The fourth policy is portability.

Context becomes capital. Memory becomes switching cost. Agents become more useful as they learn users and organizations.

Without portability, the provider holding the memory gains extraordinary power.

Users should be able to move data, preferences, histories, and permissions in practical forms. Companies should be able to route among models and preserve critical workflows across providers. Public institutions should avoid architectures in which one vendor becomes permanently synonymous with institutional memory.

Perfect portability may be impossible because systems represent knowledge differently.

The goal is credible exit.

Markets behave differently when customers can leave.

The fifth policy is competition across the stack.

Model competition matters. So do chips, clouds, app stores, operating systems, agent platforms, identity systems, and energy markets. A competitive model layer does not protect users if one distribution platform decides which model every user receives.

Open standards can matter more than open models in some layers.

Interoperable tools, portable context, transparent pricing, and neutral interfaces can keep the token economy contestable even when infrastructure requires scale.

Competition policy should focus on bottlenecks as they move.

This requires humility from regulators because the bottleneck in 2028 may not be the one that looked obvious in 2026.

The sixth policy is to price physical constraints honestly.

Cheap tokens should not depend on hidden subsidies from households, water systems, or overloaded grids.

Large data centers should face rate structures that reflect incremental infrastructure costs while recognizing benefits they can provide through long-term contracts and flexible demand. Environmental measurement should be specific enough to distinguish locations, hardware, and workloads rather than relying on universal token myths.

Flexible inference should follow cheap clean power where latency allows.

Efficiency should be rewarded because it broadens access and reduces infrastructure pressure.

But efficiency gains should not be mistaken for guaranteed reductions in total consumption. The rebound effect belongs inside planning assumptions.

The seventh policy is public capability.

Markets will allocate the most compute toward uses with the greatest ability to pay. Some socially valuable uses will lose that auction.

Universities, public-interest researchers, schools, healthcare systems, small firms, and government agencies may need shared compute, procurement capacity, and technical expertise to participate effectively.

Public compute should not become a prestige monument.

Its success should be measured by work enabled: research performed, startups formed, services improved, languages supported, public institutions strengthened.

A cluster without users is hardware theater.

The eighth policy is to keep humans answerable for coercive and high-stakes decisions even when machines supply most of the analysis.

This is not because humans are magically unbiased.

They are not.

It is because accountability requires an institution that can be questioned, appealed to, disciplined, or changed. A model cannot carry democratic responsibility. A company can. A professional can. An agency can.

Human responsibility should be real rather than ceremonial.

Do not place a person's signature behind a process whose volume makes meaningful review impossible.

If the employee cannot inspect the work, change the workflow rather than pretending the employee did.

The ninth policy is to protect the right to cognitive privacy.

The most useful assistant wants to know everything.

That is exactly why limits matter.

People should be able to use intelligence without turning every private uncertainty into permanent corporate memory. Sensitive context should have retention controls. Local and on-device inference should be encouraged where practical. Data collection should be proportional to function.

A token-maxed society can become a surveillance society by convenience rather than decree.

The assistant asks for one harmless permission at a time.

The tenth policy is cultural rather than legal.

Reward concise success.

This sounds absurdly small beside semiconductor fabs and national strategies.

It may be one of the most important norms.

If organizations equate extensive machine effort with rigor, every incentive points toward excess. If they reward clear answers, measured outcomes, well-chosen evidence, and explicit stopping criteria, systems learn to spend compute where it matters.

A four-page analysis that changes the decision should outrank a hundred-page analysis that documents activity.

A small model that solves the problem should outrank a frontier model used for theater.

An employee who deletes an unnecessary workflow should receive as much credit as one who automates it elegantly.

Token minimalism is not the opposite of token maxing.

It is what token maxing becomes after adulthood.

The frontier needs extravagance.

Researchers discover capabilities by pushing systems past economically sensible limits. They run large experiments, sample many paths, and learn what additional compute can buy. Society benefits from this exploration because tomorrow's efficient method is often discovered through today's expensive demonstration.

Production needs discipline.

The company serving a billion routine tasks cannot behave like a laboratory proving what is possible. It must care about marginal value.

The two cultures should coexist without confusing their objectives.

This distinction applies to national strategy too.

A country needs some frontier experiments precisely because markets may underinvest in public scientific capability. It also needs broad diffusion because frontier prestige without economy-wide use produces weak returns.

The national goal should not be maximum tokens.

It should be maximum capability per unit of scarce social resources, plus enough frontier investment to move the capability boundary.

That is a less exciting slogan and a better strategy.

The individual version is more intimate.

AI can make a person enormously more capable and slightly less present in his own life.

He can answer every message, optimize every purchase, research every decision, summarize every conversation, track every metric, and generate every alternative. The assistant can eliminate idle gaps that once forced him to tolerate uncertainty.

The result can be a life with less friction and less silence.

There is no economic statistic for the value of not optimizing dinner.

People will need private stopping rules.

Some decisions do not deserve research because they are reversible.

Some messages deserve the person's own imperfect words.

Some skills deserve practice without assistance because the practice is the point.

Some questions deserve to remain unanswered for an afternoon.

The machine's availability does not create an obligation to use it.

That may become difficult to remember when AI is embedded everywhere and competitive norms reward constant augmentation.

The right to be unaided can become a form of autonomy.

Schools may protect it for learning. workplaces may protect it for craft or privacy. individuals may protect it for identity.

The future does not need to choose between unaided humanity and machine-maxed cognition.

A good civilization can use both deliberately.

That deliberateness is the larger theme beneath every policy in this chapter.

The technology makes more possible.

Possibility does not choose purpose.

Markets choose according to prices and property rights. Companies choose according to incentives and governance. Governments choose according to politics. Individuals choose according to values, habits, and social pressure.

The outcomes described in this book are therefore contingent.

Token maxing can democratize expertise or create two-tier services.

It can empower small companies or lock them into giant platforms.

It can raise worker productivity or remove entry ladders.

It can accelerate clean-energy investment or prolong fossil generation.

It can narrow capability gaps or widen global income gaps.

It can produce better decisions or merely longer reasoning traces.

The same technical tendency contains each pair because technology changes constraints before society decides how to use the new freedom.

This is why asking whether AI is good or bad is less useful than asking which constraint it removes and which constraint becomes binding next.

Remove the cost of drafting and verification becomes scarce.

Remove the cost of expertise and judgment becomes scarce.

Remove the cost of software production and maintenance becomes scarce.

Remove the cost of analysis and commitment becomes scarce.

Remove the cost of intelligence and energy, trust, context, attention, physical action, and responsibility become more visible.

The token-maxed economy is a sequence of revealed bottlenecks.

The best institutions keep looking down the sequence rather than celebrating the last constraint they defeated.

There is a deeper reason to remain optimistic.

Human beings are good at finding uses for abundance.

Cheap steel became bridges, buildings, cars, machines, and tools nobody could have justified when steel was dear. Cheap computing became spreadsheets, games, maps, communication, scientific instruments, and strange social rituals no central planner would have predicted. Cheap bandwidth became video calls, streaming, remote work, online education, and an ocean of nonsense.

We got all of it.

Machine cognition will be similar.

We will waste astonishing quantities of it.

We will use some of it to defend against the waste created by other quantities of it.

We will overbuild. We will underbuild. We will mistake polished output for competence. We will use tiny models for great tasks and giant models for grocery lists. We will create new professions devoted to cleaning up the consequences.

And we will also do things that were previously too expensive to think through.

A rare-language student will receive an explanation no local textbook contains. A scientist will search a space of hypotheses too large for one career. A small company will enter a market because administrative competence became rentable. A disabled person will gain an interface built around her needs. A doctor will notice something because the machine organized ten years of records. A public-interest lawyer will serve more people. An engineer will test more designs before pouring concrete.

These gains do not need to be utopian to be historic.

The danger is not abundance itself.

The danger is letting the existence of abundant cognition relieve us of judgment about where it belongs.

A cook tastes.

That is the part the slogan leaves out.

The cook does not keep the pan on the heat because heat is available. He uses heat to produce a state in the food. He watches, smells, listens, adjusts, and eventually turns the burner off.

The stopping point is part of the craft.

So it will be with machine thought.

We should give difficult problems more time when more time changes the answer. We should spend compute generously where errors are costly and discovery valuable. We should let machines explore where human attention is the true bottleneck. We should make cheap expertise available to people who have gone without it.

Then we should measure.

We should preserve the human capacities whose loss would make us fragile. We should keep markets open enough that abundance reaches users. We should make infrastructure costs visible. We should rebuild ladders where automation removes them. We should insist that responsibility remains attached to someone capable of answering for the decision.

And when the next thousand tokens are unlikely to change what we do, we should stop.

Serve dinner.

Keep cooking was never supposed to mean never serve.
