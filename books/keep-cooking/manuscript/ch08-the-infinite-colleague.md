# The Infinite Colleague

The most expensive sentence in a small company is often some version of “we would need to hire someone for that.”

A designer needs a developer. A developer needs a salesperson. A salesperson needs a lawyer. The lawyer needs an accountant. The founder needs somebody who knows procurement, taxes, translation, analytics, customer support, and the one obscure piece of software used by the largest potential client.

The company does not fail because the idea is bad. It fails because competence arrives in indivisible human packages called employees, consultants, and agencies.

Artificial intelligence changes the divisibility.

You do not hire a full researcher. You buy twenty minutes of research. You do not retain a translator for every language. You translate when the message appears. You do not build an internal design team to test a landing page. You generate variants. You do not wake a programmer at two in the morning to explain an error message. You ask the machine.

This is the infinite colleague: not a person who knows everything, but a pool of cognitive labor that can be summoned in small quantities across many domains.

The phrase is deliberately generous. The colleague is often wrong, cannot take legal responsibility, has no independent loyalty, does not share the history of the organization unless context is supplied, and may disappear when a provider changes a product. Still, for a person who previously worked alone, the difference can feel like acquiring a staff.

This may be one of token maxing's largest gains for individuals.

Large firms have always been able to buy specialization. They employ counsel, engineers, analysts, designers, researchers, assistants, and administrators. Wealthy individuals can do the same privately. A general-purpose AI service compresses a low-grade version of that institutional advantage into a subscription.

The gap does not vanish. It changes.

A billionaire can still hire the expert who wrote the textbook. A multinational can build private models and connect them to proprietary systems. But the baseline capability available to everyone rises.

That matters more than comparisons at the top suggest.

Economic life is full of tasks that do not require the world's best expert. They require competence now. Explain this clause. Clean this spreadsheet. Draft this complaint. Translate this email. Compare these suppliers. Generate a simple script. Help me understand this tax form. Turn these notes into a proposal. Prepare questions for the doctor. Summarize the zoning rule.

Before AI, many such tasks went undone.

The person could not justify the transaction cost of hiring help. The problem was too small, too embarrassing, too urgent, too poorly defined, or too low value. The infinite colleague expands demand into these neglected spaces.

This is an important economic point because productivity studies often measure the replacement of existing work. Some of the largest gains may come from work that did not previously exist.

A tiny exporter translates product material into five languages and reaches new customers. A local nonprofit analyzes grant opportunities it never had staff to pursue. A parent creates practice exercises tailored to a child's interests. A hobbyist writes a small program rather than buying software. A shop owner inspects years of transaction data that had sat untouched.

No job was directly automated. Capability appeared where scarcity had prevented a market transaction.

This is the optimistic heart of the token-maxed economy.

It also changes the meaning of being a generalist.

For much of modern professional life, specialization paid because knowledge was expensive to acquire and coordination was the way organizations combined it. A person who knew a little about many things could be interesting at dinner and less useful in a hiring funnel. The firm wanted the accountant to account and the engineer to engineer.

AI can make breadth productive by supplying on-demand depth.

A generalist who understands enough law to identify a legal question, enough software to specify a tool, enough finance to test an assumption, and enough design to recognize an ugly interface can direct machines into areas where she is not personally expert. She becomes an integrator.

This is not the same as expertise.

The dangerous generalist does not know what she does not know. AI can make her overconfidence look polished. The valuable generalist knows where the edges are, when to escalate, and which outputs require a real specialist.

That is a renaissance attitude with an audit trail.

Historically, broad intellectual lives were easier for people with leisure, wealth, servants, patrons, or institutions. The scientist who also painted, engineered, corresponded, and studied anatomy was not doing his own payroll in a modern small business. AI can supply a modest version of the invisible support structure that made breadth possible.

It may create more one-person laboratories, studios, publishing houses, consultancies, and software companies.

The one-person company has always existed. Token maxing changes its ceiling.

A founder can maintain several parallel agents: one monitoring customers, one researching competitors, one working through code, one preparing outreach, one checking financials. The founder does not literally employ tireless experts. She operates a system of weak-to-strong assistants whose combined throughput can exceed what one person could previously coordinate.

The result could be an explosion of small economic organisms.

More firms. More experiments. More niche products. More local services. More people selling directly to markets rather than entering employers.

This would distribute productive power.

It could also intensify competition to the point of exhaustion.

If everyone can start a newsletter, build a small app, generate a course, launch a store, or create a consultancy, the supply of ventures rises. Customer attention does not rise proportionally. Most of the new businesses earn little. The tools democratize entry without democratizing success.

The infinite colleague lowers the cost of trying.

That is not the same as lowering the cost of being chosen.

Entrepreneurship becomes more like publishing after the internet. Production is easier. Distribution is the war.

This favors people with existing audiences, reputations, capital, or privileged access to customers. A famous expert can use AI to serve ten times as many people. An unknown expert can also use AI, but the famous one starts with the scarce input.

Token maxing can therefore increase the number of producers and the concentration of demand at the same time.

That combination feels contradictory only if production and attention are treated as the same market.

They are not.

The individual worker inside a company also gains a new kind of bargaining chip: capacity.

A capable employee can absorb tasks outside her formal role. She can prototype before requesting engineering resources. She can analyze before requesting analyst resources. She can communicate with foreign customers before requesting localization. This can make her more valuable.

It can also make job descriptions metastasize.

If the worker can do more, the employer can expect more.

The history of productivity improvement is full of this ambiguity. Faster tools do not always produce shorter days. They can raise the standard of output. Email reduced the cost of correspondence and produced more correspondence. Word processors made editing easier and contributed to more documents. Smartphones made workers reachable and extended work into places that once belonged to private life.

The infinite colleague may follow the phone into the evening.

Why leave a task unfinished when the agent can make progress after dinner? Why wait for Monday when a draft can be generated tonight? Why accept one idea when fifty can be produced before breakfast?

Token maxing creates a moral pressure around possibility.

Once effort becomes cheap for the machine, declining to request more effort can feel lazy for the person.

This is especially powerful in creative work.

A designer who could produce three concepts in a day may now produce thirty. The client learns that thirty are possible and begins to expect thirty. A writer can generate ten titles and therefore should generate a hundred. A composer can audition endless variations. The search space expands faster than human satisfaction.

More options can improve the best result.

They can also dissolve conviction.

A creator who sees one alternative can decide. A creator who sees a thousand can always imagine the thousand-and-first. The infinite colleague never says, “This is enough unless you have a reason to continue.” It can keep generating because generation is what it is paid to do.

The human must supply the stopping rule.

This may become one of the defining psychological skills of the age.

People with perfectionist tendencies can already lose hours to fonts, edits, research, and comparison. Give them an inexhaustible collaborator and the pathology becomes industrial. A report can always contain another scenario. A product can always have another feature. A job application can always be tailored more precisely.

The machine eliminates fatigue, one of the natural barriers that once ended the work.

Without fatigue, people need judgment.

The colleague also changes loneliness in complicated ways.

For a solo worker, immediate conversational assistance can reduce the isolation of staring at a problem alone. A model can help brainstorm, explain, and challenge. For people with disabilities, social anxiety, language barriers, or geographic isolation, this can expand participation.

But a machine can also make it easier for organizations to remove human colleagues.

The person gains an assistant and loses a team.

Those are not equivalent social experiences.

Workplaces are inefficient partly because people talk. They teach each other, complain, gossip, notice distress, exchange favors, form friendships, and transmit institutional context that never appears in a ticketing system. A company optimizing only for task completion can see colleagues as expensive coordination overhead.

AI makes that optimization more tempting.

A one-person department supported by agents looks efficient on a spreadsheet. The person may experience it as living inside a very competent echo chamber.

This is another place where individual and company incentives diverge.

The company captures salary savings. The worker absorbs social cost. The economy may see higher measured output and lower informal mentorship. None of those variables automatically appear in the model-selection benchmark.

The infinite colleague is also unusually agreeable unless designed not to be.

Human colleagues resist. They have priorities. They say a plan is foolish. They are offended by impossible deadlines. They possess independent information and incentives. These frictions can be annoying and economically useful.

An AI system can be tuned to challenge, but it ultimately exists inside a product relationship. The user expects service.

A world of endlessly accommodating cognitive assistants risks giving powerful people too little resistance.

Executives already receive distorted information because subordinates fear contradicting them. If the executive replaces some of those subordinates with agents optimized for helpfulness, the information environment can become smoother and less true.

The ideal infinite colleague is not obedient.

It is calibrated.

It knows when disagreement is valuable. It distinguishes brainstorming from verification. It says when evidence is weak. It refuses to manufacture certainty because the user sounds confident. Building that behavior is technically and commercially difficult because people often reward tools that make them feel effective.

The market can select for flattering competence.

At the household level, the infinite colleague may be even more transformative than at work.

Administrative labor consumes enormous human energy. Insurance forms, school emails, travel planning, bill comparison, scheduling, taxes, benefits, warranties, medical paperwork, subscriptions, returns, and the endless small negotiations of modern life are cognitively expensive and economically uncounted.

An assistant that handles them reliably creates time without increasing measured labor productivity.

That time is real wealth.

It may benefit caregivers disproportionately because caregiving households often perform the most coordination. A parent caring for children and an aging relative can spend hours on invisible bureaucracy. Token maxing applied to administration could be a technology of domestic relief.

But only if the systems can act across fragmented institutions.

The assistant needs access to records, accounts, calendars, payments, health information, and government services. The technical problem becomes a permissions problem. The social value increases with integration; the privacy risk increases with the same integration.

The infinite colleague wants the keys.

People will hand them over gradually because each key unlocks convenience.

Read my email so you can find the receipt. Read my bank account so you can flag the subscription. Read my medical portal so you can prepare the appointment. Read my child's school messages so you can update the calendar. Read my location so you can plan the route.

The assistant becomes useful by becoming intimate.

That creates a new class divide if privacy itself becomes a luxury.

Wealthy users may pay for local models, strong contractual protections, private infrastructure, or services that do not monetize data. Poorer users may receive subsidized assistants whose economics depend on advertising, data collection, or ecosystem lock-in.

The same intelligence can arrive under different bargains.

This pattern already exists across the internet. Token maxing raises the stakes because the assistant can know more and act more.

The personal upside remains enormous.

A person who cannot afford professional help can arrive at negotiations better prepared. She can understand a lease before signing. She can draft a complaint to a utility. She can compare loan terms. She can learn the vocabulary used by doctors. She can translate official correspondence. She can ask questions privately that she is embarrassed to ask another person.

Information asymmetry is one of the quiet ways institutions exercise power. The party that understands the process can exhaust the party that does not.

AI reduces some of that asymmetry.

Institutions will respond.

If customers use agents to dispute fees, companies will use agents to handle disputes. If job seekers use agents to negotiate offers, employers will use agents to optimize offers. If taxpayers use agents to challenge assessments, governments will use agents to process challenges. Machine assistance appears on both sides.

The individual advantage can therefore be temporary unless the tool changes the underlying balance of rights.

A tenant with an AI-drafted letter is stronger than a tenant with no idea what the lease says. A landlord with a system processing ten thousand letters still has scale. Technology modifies the contest; it does not abolish structure.

The greatest long-term benefit of the infinite colleague may be less about winning existing contests and more about making new lives feasible.

A scientist outside a major institution can do more preliminary work. A rural entrepreneur can serve global customers. A creator can produce in several media. A small civic group can analyze public records. A teacher can build material for a class without a curriculum department. A patient can organize years of records before seeing a specialist.

These are expansions of agency.

Agency is a better word than automation for the individual promise of AI.

Automation asks what the machine can do instead of you.

Agency asks what you can now cause to happen.

Token maxing increases both.

The winners will be people who convert cheap cognitive labor into more control over their lives rather than merely more output demanded by somebody else.

That difference may be the difference between having an infinite colleague and becoming the remaining human in an infinite office.
