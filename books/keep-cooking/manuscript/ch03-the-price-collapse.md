# The Price Collapse

The easiest way to misunderstand a technological revolution is to watch the price of one unit fall and assume the bill will fall with it.

In November 2022, according to a comparison later assembled in Stanford's AI Index, obtaining a level of language-model performance around the old GPT-3.5 benchmark cost roughly twenty dollars per million tokens. By October 2024, a model reaching the same benchmark level could be queried for about seven cents per million tokens. The comparison was not a perfect constant-quality price index. Models differed in latency, context, output, and capability. Benchmarks age. Still, a decline of that scale does not need perfect measurement to be economically startling.

Imagine if a competent hour of legal research, graphic design, translation, or junior programming fell in price by a factor of hundreds in less than two years.

Nobody would ask only how much money lawyers, designers, translators, or programmers had saved. We would ask what people began doing now that the input was cheap enough to waste.

This is the important question about tokens.

A token does not have to become free to become behaviorally free. It merely has to become cheap enough that the person making the decision stops noticing the marginal cost.

Modern life is full of such thresholds. Most people do not calculate the electricity used by sending an email, the storage consumed by taking another photograph, or the marginal cellular data required to refresh a map. Those actions are not literally costless. Their price is bundled into a plan, hidden inside infrastructure, or too small relative to the value of attention to matter. Once a resource crosses that psychological line, demand can change shape.

The same transformation is happening to machine cognition.

When a user paid visibly for each generation, she conserved. When a subscription includes generous use, she experiments. When an enterprise contract turns inference into an allocated budget, teams build workflows that would have looked extravagant under a per-call mentality. When an agent can invoke a model without asking a person each time, the person is no longer even present at the purchasing moment.

This is how quantity escapes.

The nineteenth-century economist William Stanley Jevons is remembered for observing a related paradox in coal. Improvements in the efficiency of steam engines did not necessarily reduce coal consumption. Better engines made coal more economically useful, expanding the number of applications and increasing total demand. The modern version is usually called a rebound effect. Efficiency reduces the resource required per unit of service. The cheaper service then becomes more attractive, so society consumes more units.

Token maxing is built for rebound.

Make a model twice as efficient and a company can halve its inference bill. Or it can keep the bill constant and run twice as many checks. If quality improves when several answers are sampled, cheaper inference makes sampling more attractive. If agents can work on tasks too small to justify human attention, cheaper inference expands the addressable market. If personalized tutoring costs a fraction of a cent per exchange, students can ask questions nobody would have hired a tutor to answer.

The efficiency gain creates its own demand.

This is why arguments about AI's environmental footprint often talk past one another. Engineers correctly point out that hardware becomes more efficient, models become smaller, algorithms improve, and the energy required for a given capability can fall dramatically. Energy analysts correctly point out that total data-center demand can still rise because use expands faster than efficiency.

Both can be true.

The International Energy Agency reported in 2026 that electricity demand from data centers had risen sharply in 2025, while the power required for an individual AI task was falling rapidly. The report projected continued growth because usage, more capable systems, and agentic applications were expanding even as efficiency improved.

The paradox is not a bug in the analysis. It is the likely shape of success.

If AI remained inefficient, expensive, and rarely used, its total energy consumption would be easier to control. The environmental problem becomes interesting precisely because the technology is useful enough to spread.

The same paradox appears in corporate budgets.

A chief financial officer may watch the price per million tokens collapse and still see the AI line item grow. The company moves from occasional chat to automated support. Then to code review. Then to sales qualification, document processing, compliance monitoring, forecasting, and internal search. Each use case is cheaper than it would have been last year. There are simply many more use cases.

The price collapse also rearranges bargaining power.

When an input is expensive, the producer can capture much of the value. When competition drives the input toward commodity pricing, value migrates elsewhere. It moves into distribution, proprietary data, workflow integration, customer relationships, regulated trust, physical assets, or whatever remains scarce.

This possibility should terrify and delight investors in equal measure.

A technology can become civilization-changing and still be a terrible place for some of its producers to earn excess returns.

The internet did not become less important because bandwidth became cheaper. Quite the opposite. Cheap bandwidth destroyed certain tollbooth economics while enabling enormous new businesses built above the network. Storage followed the same pattern. Computing cycles followed it. Open-source software repeatedly moved value away from the thing being commoditized and toward services, hosting, integration, and complementary assets.

Foundation models may experience the same pressure.

There are reasons to resist the analogy. Frontier training remains capital-intensive. The best models can command a premium. Proprietary data, reinforcement-learning pipelines, product design, inference infrastructure, and safety systems create meaningful differentiation. Scale can generate advantages. The field is not a simple commodity market.

But the cost curve is hostile to complacency.

A model provider that spends billions to create a capability may discover that competitors reproduce much of the capability quickly, open-weight alternatives narrow the gap, and customers route routine work to cheaper models. The frontier can remain expensive while yesterday's frontier becomes abundant.

That pattern is especially important for token maxing because most tokens will not be frontier tokens.

The glamorous part of AI is the hardest benchmark problem. The volume business may be classification, summarization, translation, extraction, drafting, routing, monitoring, and millions of repetitive agent steps that do not require the world's best model. If smaller models become good enough, the average token can get much cheaper even while a premium tier remains costly.

The economy then begins to resemble transportation.

Most freight does not fly first class. It travels by the cheapest method consistent with the deadline and risk. Machine cognition will be routed the same way. A request enters the system. Cheap local model first. Stronger model if confidence is low. Search if uncertainty persists. Human if the consequence is high. The expensive intelligence becomes an escalation path rather than the default.

This makes the phrase artificial intelligence misleading in business budgets. Companies will not buy a single thing called intelligence. They will buy a stack of cognitive logistics.

Some reasoning will happen on devices. Some in private data centers. Some through public cloud APIs. Some through open models. Some through premium systems. A smart organization will treat tokens like packets on a network: route them according to cost, privacy, urgency, and quality requirements.

The company that can do this well gains twice.

It benefits from falling prices and avoids paying frontier rates for commodity work.

The company that cannot may turn token maxing into a leak.

This is already a familiar cloud-computing story. The cloud made servers easy to acquire. Ease encouraged sprawl. Teams created instances, databases, logs, storage buckets, and services. Bills grew. A whole discipline of financial operations emerged to help companies understand and control infrastructure spending that was individually cheap and collectively enormous.

AI will produce its own version.

Call it cognitive FinOps if you must. Somebody will.

Organizations will measure cost per resolved ticket, cost per generated lead, cost per accepted code change, cost per contract reviewed, cost per successful research task. They will cache answers. They will compress context. They will route requests. They will fine-tune smaller models. They will set token budgets. They will discover that engineers left an expensive model in a loop over the weekend.

The comedy writes itself because the underlying economics are ordinary.

Abundance produces waste before it produces discipline.

Consumers may enjoy the waste.

Stanford's 2026 AI Index estimated large and growing consumer surplus from generative AI in the United States. Any estimate of consumer surplus for a young technology rests on assumptions, but the mechanism is easy to understand. Many users receive real value from services priced at zero or a modest subscription. The gap between what they pay and what they would have been willing to pay becomes an economic benefit that may not appear as provider profit.

This is one of the central distributional possibilities of the token-maxed world: enormous social value, savage producer competition.

If model prices keep falling, a student in a rural town could receive explanations that approximate expensive tutoring. A sole proprietor could obtain market research, contract drafts, translations, and software help that once required several professionals. A migrant could navigate bureaucracy in her own language. A disabled user could transform text, speech, images, and interfaces at low cost.

None of those gains require the model company to have a beautiful margin.

The user can win while the supplier struggles.

Economists have seen this before. The firms that finance infrastructure are not guaranteed to capture the consumer surplus that infrastructure creates. Railroads made land useful far beyond the tracks. Electricity created productivity gains inside factories that utilities did not entirely capture. Open protocols created fortunes for companies that did not invent the protocol.

Token maxing could be a giant transfer from capital to cognition users.

It could also be the opposite.

If the market consolidates around a small number of providers with durable technical and infrastructure advantages, falling underlying costs may not fully pass through to customers. A few firms could capture rents from scarce accelerators, distribution, proprietary systems, or control over business workflows. Token budgets could become a new tax paid to a concentrated cognitive utility sector.

Both futures are plausible because cost decline and market power are separate variables.

The cost of producing a unit can fall while price remains high. The cost can fall and price can collapse faster. The competitive structure decides who receives the surplus.

This is where geopolitical strategy enters a conversation that initially looks like software pricing.

If AI becomes a general-purpose input to economic activity, countries care not only about access but about the terms of access. A nation dependent on foreign model APIs is purchasing more than software. It is purchasing a continuing flow of cognitive services priced by companies operating under another country's laws, export controls, currency, and strategic priorities.

Cheap tokens can still create dependency.

The same is true for firms. A startup can become astonishingly capable by renting intelligence instead of hiring a large staff. That lowers barriers to entry. It also creates exposure to providers that can change prices, models, rate limits, data policies, or product direction.

Abundance often arrives through somebody else's pipe.

The rational response is not necessarily to build your own model. Most companies do not generate their own electricity or manufacture their own CPUs. They accept dependence because specialization is efficient. But dependence becomes a strategic issue when the input is central enough.

Token maxing pushes AI toward that threshold.

The more cognition an organization outsources, the more a price change matters. The more workflows become agentic, the harder it becomes to switch systems if each is integrated into memory, tools, permissions, and internal data. Cheap tokens at the beginning can support expensive lock-in later.

This is one reason free or subsidized intelligence should be analyzed like any other platform strategy.

A provider may price aggressively to encourage developers to build around its APIs. A consumer service may offer generous usage to establish habit. An enterprise vendor may bundle AI into an existing software suite, making the marginal price appear low because the economic goal is to defend the larger subscription.

The sticker price of a token can therefore conceal the strategic value of owning the workflow that consumes it.

The most powerful companies in the token-maxed economy may not be the ones that charge the most per unit of intelligence. They may be the ones that decide when intelligence is invoked.

An operating system can call a model. A productivity suite can call a model. A browser can call a model. A search engine can call a model. A customer-service platform can call a model. A cloud provider can route among several models. The company closest to the user's intent can control enormous downstream token demand.

This creates a new layer of economics: demand aggregation.

If billions of small requests are routed through a few platforms, those platforms can negotiate lower inference prices, choose providers, favor their own models, or package intelligence as a feature. Individual model companies may become suppliers to distribution channels with stronger bargaining power.

Again the internet provides a warning. Producing content became cheap. Distribution remained scarce. Search engines, social networks, app stores, and marketplaces captured power because they controlled attention and discovery.

Producing acceptable cognitive output may follow the same trajectory.

If so, token maxing will not eliminate scarcity. It will move it.

First intelligence is scarce. Then intelligence becomes abundant. Trust becomes scarce. Distribution becomes scarce. Proprietary context becomes scarce. Good questions become scarce. Permission to act becomes scarce. Physical execution becomes scarce. Attention becomes scarce.

A person who understands this will not ask, “What happens when intelligence becomes free?”

Nothing important becomes free. The bottleneck simply relocates.

This is also why cheap machine output can increase inequality between individuals who use the same tools.

When the output itself was expensive, access mattered most. Once access broadens, complementary skill matters more. Two people receive the same model. One knows the domain, recognizes errors, supplies rich context, and turns the answer into action. The other accepts the first plausible result. The tool is equal. The leverage is not.

The paradox resembles calculators. Cheap calculators democratized arithmetic. They did not eliminate the advantage of understanding mathematics. In many settings they increased the payoff to knowing which calculation to perform.

Token maxing can do the same for knowledge work.

As drafting becomes cheap, framing rises in value. As research becomes cheap, source judgment rises. As coding becomes cheap, architecture rises. As analysis becomes cheap, deciding which variable matters rises. As persuasion becomes cheap, credibility rises.

The price collapse is therefore both liberating and cruel.

It liberates people from tasks that once consumed hours simply because cognition was expensive. It is cruel to occupations and businesses whose margins depended on those tasks remaining expensive.

A translator who charged for routine prose faces a different market when adequate translation costs pennies. A junior analyst who assembled comparable-company tables faces a different market when a system can do it in seconds. A software agency that billed by developer hour faces a different market when clients know that much of the implementation can be generated. A stock-photo business learned this lesson from image models earlier than many white-collar professions will.

The response is predictable. People move toward the parts that remain hard to commoditize.

Some move up the value chain. Some specialize. Some package trust. Some own customer relationships. Some become excellent at supervising machines. Some lose income because the market does not create an equally valuable adjacent role for everyone.

Technology histories become dishonest when they imply that displaced workers smoothly become owners of the new bottleneck.

They do not.

An economy can gain while a person loses. Consumers can gain while a profession contracts. A country can gain productivity while a region loses its tax base. A company can become more efficient while reducing the number of entry-level jobs that trained its future managers.

The price of a token falls cleanly on a chart.

The consequences do not.
