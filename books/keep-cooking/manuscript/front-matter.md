# Keep Cooking

*Who Wins When AI Never Stops Thinking*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

There is a new kind of extravagance in computing.

For most of the history of software, intelligence was expensive enough that engineers learned to be stingy with it. A database query should be efficient. A web request should return quickly. A program should not perform the same operation twice if it could remember the result. Faster was usually better, and doing unnecessary work was a defect.

Artificial intelligence has begun to invert the instinct.

A difficult question can now be answered once, then answered again. A model can draft a plan, criticize the plan, search for evidence, rewrite its answer, ask another model to attack the argument, run code, inspect the output, and return to the beginning. Several agents can attempt the same task in parallel and a judge can choose among them. A reasoning system can spend more compute on a problem simply because the problem appears difficult. Context windows can swallow documents that once would have been summarized by hand. The cost of generating another thousand tokens keeps falling, so the temptation to generate another thousand tokens keeps rising.

The phrase I use for the culture forming around this is token maxing.

It is not a technical term of art, and that is useful. The phenomenon is larger than tokens. It includes inference-time compute, long contexts, repeated sampling, search, tool use, self-critique, agent swarms, synthetic data, machine-to-machine conversation, and every other method that converts additional computation at the moment of use into a better chance of a useful answer. A token is simply the visible grain of a much larger industrial process.

Token maxing begins with an attractive observation: sometimes the machine does better when you let it think longer.

That observation is supported by real evidence. Research on test-time compute has shown that additional inference can improve performance on hard problems, and modern reasoning systems are deliberately built to spend more computation on some tasks than others. Agentic systems can benefit from searching, retrying, comparing answers, and using external tools. At the same time, the cost of inference has collapsed quickly enough that yesterday's waste can become tomorrow's default workflow.

The consequences do not stop at model quality.

If machine thought becomes cheaper, societies will buy more of it. Companies will reorganize around it. Workers will compete with it and through it. Schools will have to decide which mental work remains educational when a machine can complete it instantly. Utilities will discover that abstract reasoning has a power bill. Nations with chips, capital, electricity, data centers, and strong institutions will be able to manufacture more machine cognition than nations without them. The cost of producing an acceptable memo, translation, contract draft, advertisement, lesson plan, software prototype, market analysis, or scientific hypothesis may fall sharply. The cost of determining which of ten thousand acceptable outputs deserves to matter may not.

This book is about that second-order world.

It is not a forecast that tokens themselves become money, nor that every organization will run enormous language models forever, nor that intelligence is reducible to the length of a model's answer. It is a thought experiment grounded in an observable trend: as the marginal cost of machine reasoning falls and performance can often be improved by spending more compute at inference, the rational response in many settings is to spend more.

The first-order benefit is easy to love. A scientist can explore more hypotheses. A small business can afford analysis that once required a department. A student with weak local schools can obtain patient explanation. A programmer can ask for five implementations instead of one. A public agency can translate its services into languages it could not previously support. A patient can arrive at a medical appointment having already organized the questions she wants to ask.

The first-order harm is also easy to imagine. Spam becomes thoughtful. Fraud becomes personalized. Bureaucracies discover that generating paperwork is cheaper than reading it. Employers demand the output of three workers from one because the one has an AI subscription. A young professional loses the tedious assignments through which professionals used to become experienced. A company mistakes the appearance of effort for the presence of judgment and pays a fortune to make machines deliberate over problems that were badly posed in the first place.

The interesting part is that the same mechanism produces both outcomes.

Cheap machine thought can democratize expertise and centralize power. It can reduce the advantage of elite education while increasing the advantage of owning proprietary data. It can make small firms astonishingly capable while giving the largest firms an even larger edge through infrastructure, distribution, and integration. It can lower the energy required for a single unit of useful inference while raising total electricity consumption because billions more units are demanded. It can give individuals more leverage and employers more ability to measure, standardize, and replace individual work.

The distribution of gains will not follow a simple line from rich to poor or from capital to labor.

Some highly paid professionals may lose bargaining power because parts of their expertise become abundant. Some lower-paid workers may become more productive because the machine supplies knowledge they did not previously possess. Some developing countries may leap over missing layers of professional services. Others may fall further behind because the new productive input is not merely software but dependable power, broadband, chips, local-language data, institutional capacity, and the money to purchase all of them.

Consumers may be among the largest winners even when investors are disappointed. A brutal price war among model providers could destroy enormous amounts of capital while making machine intelligence nearly free to everyone else. The history of technology contains many versions of this trick: society becomes richer because producers discover how difficult it is to keep the surplus for themselves.

A book about token maxing therefore has to keep several ledgers at once.

There is the compute ledger: how much machine thought is consumed.

There is the financial ledger: who pays for chips, data centers, electricity, software, and models, and who earns a return.

There is the labor ledger: which tasks disappear, which workers become more valuable, and which paths into expertise narrow.

There is the cognitive ledger: what people still know how to do after years of delegating first drafts, memory, calculation, translation, research, and judgment.

There is the geopolitical ledger: where the physical machinery lives, who controls its supply chain, and whose languages and institutions are represented inside it.

And there is the human ledger, the least measurable and probably the most important: what we choose to do with a civilization that can manufacture more answers than it can possibly read.

The title is a joke borrowed from the behavior the technology encourages. When a model is close but not quite there, when an agent is still searching, when a benchmark improves with more inference, the instinct is to keep cooking.

Sometimes that is exactly right.

Sometimes dinner is burning.

The distinction is the book.
