# Nations at the Context Window

A century ago, national power could be counted in coal, steel, ships, factories, rail lines, oil, and men under arms.

Today the list has acquired a stranger item.

Compute.

The word sounds weightless enough to belong in an accounting spreadsheet. Governments increasingly treat it like industrial capacity. They track accelerators, data centers, semiconductor fabrication, cloud regions, research talent, model capability, electricity supply, and the ability to train or run advanced systems without asking another country for permission.

Token maxing makes this sensible.

If machine cognition becomes a general-purpose input to production, a nation with cheap and abundant access can do more of many kinds of work. A nation without access purchases that productive input from abroad or does without it.

This resembles earlier strategic commodities, but the analogy is incomplete.

Compute is manufactured rather than extracted. It depends on a supply chain distributed across countries. The leading chip may be designed in one place, fabricated in another, packaged elsewhere, installed in a data center powered by a fourth region, running a model trained on data drawn from the world.

National AI capacity is therefore built out of international dependence.

The dependence is concentrated in uncomfortable places.

Stanford's 2026 AI Index highlighted the extraordinary concentration of leading AI-chip fabrication at TSMC in Taiwan. The United States hosts an enormous number of data centers and many leading model companies. China has closed much of the capability gap on leading models while building its own hardware and software ecosystems. Europe possesses large markets, research institutions, and regulatory influence while depending heavily on foreign cloud and chip supply.

The map is not one hierarchy.

It is several overlapping ones.

Research leadership. chip design. fabrication. cloud infrastructure. electricity. data. capital. talent. adoption. regulation. local-language capability.

A country can lead one and depend on another.

Token maxing turns those dependencies into economic exposure because inference is recurring.

Buying a machine once is different from buying machine cognition every day.

If a country's firms route growing portions of work through foreign AI APIs, the country imports a stream of cognitive services. This may be efficient, just as importing software or oil can be efficient. But the dependency becomes strategic when the service affects finance, government, defense, health, research, and industrial production.

This is why “sovereign AI” emerged as a political phrase.

The phrase contains several different ambitions that should not be confused.

One is data sovereignty: sensitive information remains under domestic law.

Another is compute sovereignty: domestic institutions can run important workloads on infrastructure they control.

Another is model sovereignty: the country can train, adapt, or operate models without relying on a foreign provider.

Another is cultural sovereignty: local languages, norms, and institutions are represented well enough that the systems are useful and legitimate.

These goals require different investments.

A government can build a giant cluster and still depend on imported chips. It can train a national model and still struggle with local data quality. It can require data localization and make services more expensive without producing better domestic alternatives.

Sovereignty is not achieved by owning one impressive object.

It is an ecosystem.

This creates a difficult choice for smaller countries.

Frontier competition is expensive. Attempting to reproduce every layer domestically can waste capital. Renting from global providers can deliver better technology immediately.

The rational strategy is often selective sovereignty.

Control what must be controlled. import what is safely commoditized. build local capability where the country's language, law, industry, security, or comparative advantage demands it.

This is how nations already approach aviation, pharmaceuticals, energy, and telecommunications.

AI will mature into similar realism after a period of prestige projects.

The global inequality question is more serious.

The IMF has modeled a future in which AI benefits advanced economies disproportionately because they combine higher occupational exposure with stronger preparedness and better access to technology. A 2025 IMF working paper argued that gains in advanced economies could substantially exceed those in low-income countries under plausible scenarios, widening cross-country income gaps.

The World Bank has made a related warning through its framework of AI foundations: connectivity, compute, context or data, and competency determine whether countries can capture benefits.

This is the opposite of the simple leapfrog story.

The leapfrog story says AI makes expertise cheap, so poorer countries gain most because expertise was previously scarce.

There is truth in it.

A village clinic can access diagnostic guidance. A farmer can ask questions in local language. A student can receive tutoring. A small exporter can translate and research foreign markets. A government can automate document processing despite limited administrative staff.

The marginal value can be enormous where the baseline service is weak.

But receiving value and capturing value are different.

If the models, clouds, chips, payment systems, and application platforms are foreign, much of the revenue flows outward. If local workers lack skills to integrate AI into firms, adoption remains superficial. If electricity is unreliable, cloud access expensive, or broadband limited, the service is not practically cheap. If local-language data is poor, quality lags.

The country becomes a consumer of intelligence rather than a producer in the intelligence economy.

This can still improve welfare.

It may not close income gaps.

The analogy to smartphones is useful. Mobile technology brought enormous benefits across developing economies. It enabled payments, communication, commerce, and access to information. The highest-value platform and semiconductor layers remained concentrated in a few countries and firms.

Users gained. Local entrepreneurs gained. Global platform companies gained even more.

AI can repeat that pattern at a deeper cognitive layer.

Language is one of the decisive battlegrounds.

A model performs best when it has abundant high-quality material and evaluation in a language. English enjoys enormous digital representation. Major languages attract investment because the user base is large. Thousands of smaller languages do not.

Token maxing helps and hurts.

More inference can sometimes compensate for weaker performance. Translation can bridge languages. Synthetic data can expand resources. But all of those techniques cost compute and depend on evaluation by people who understand the language and culture.

A low-resource language can be technically supported while remaining economically neglected.

The consequence is not only inconvenience.

Language determines who can use public services, education, medicine, law, and commerce comfortably. If AI becomes a primary interface to institutions, poor language support becomes a new form of exclusion.

Countries and communities may need to treat language data as cultural infrastructure.

That raises ownership questions.

Who has the right to collect, digitize, and train on cultural material? Who benefits when indigenous language resources improve a commercial model? How should consent work for texts produced under conditions that never contemplated machine learning?

The token-maxed world wants context.

Cultures are context.

The extraction analogy becomes uncomfortable again.

Data can be taken from a community, processed by a foreign company, and sold back as a service. The community benefits from the service and may have little ownership of the value created.

New licensing, public datasets, cooperatives, and national data strategies may emerge to change the bargain.

The wrong approach can make local AI worse by restricting access to material without creating alternatives.

The right approach is difficult because cultural representation and economic rights do not align automatically.

Compute access itself has a geopolitical hierarchy.

Export controls can restrict advanced hardware. Sanctions can limit cloud services. War can disrupt supply chains. A company in one country can receive the latest accelerators while a researcher in another waits years or pays more for older equipment.

If test-time compute becomes a major source of capability, these differences influence not only who can train frontier models but who can run the best systems at scale.

Inference becomes strategic capacity.

This is especially important for science.

A wealthy university can give researchers agentic systems that search literature, write code, simulate, and analyze continuously. A poorer university may have intermittent access to slower services. The human intelligence can be equal. The machine multiplier differs.

Scientific inequality compounds.

Researchers in compute-rich institutions publish faster, attract funding, and train students with better tools. Their work enters future models, making the ecosystem even more centered around those institutions.

This is a Matthew effect written in inference.

Cloud services can reduce it by making hardware rentable rather than requiring ownership.

Public compute can reduce it further.

Governments and international institutions may eventually treat baseline compute access like research infrastructure, providing credits or shared clusters to universities, startups, and public agencies.

This is one of the stronger arguments for public investment.

The market allocates compute toward ability to pay, not necessarily social value. A hedge fund can outbid a tropical-disease researcher. That is normal market behavior. It may not be the allocation a society wants at the margin.

Public compute creates its own governance problems. Who receives access? Which projects qualify? How is misuse prevented? Does the infrastructure stay technologically current?

No policy removes scarcity.

It chooses a rationing mechanism.

The national competition can also produce waste through duplication.

Every country does not need a frontier model. Prestige can make leaders believe otherwise. A domestic model launch is visible. Investments in data cleaning, teacher training, digital public infrastructure, or grid reliability are less glamorous and may create more value.

Token maxing tempts states in the same way it tempts companies: visible compute looks like progress.

The better measure is capability delivered to the population.

Can firms adopt AI? Can citizens access useful services? Can researchers compute? Can critical systems operate during geopolitical disruption? Are local languages served? Are workers trained? Does the value remain in the domestic economy?

A small country can score well on these questions without winning a model benchmark.

Singapore and the Gulf states illustrate one route: high digital capacity, capital, aggressive adoption, and strategic infrastructure can create influence disproportionate to population. Stanford's 2026 AI Index reported very high generative-AI adoption in places such as Singapore and the United Arab Emirates.

These countries can import technology while building ecosystems around use.

Large emerging economies have another route.

India, Brazil, Indonesia, Nigeria, and others possess huge populations, growing digital systems, local-language diversity, and large service sectors. AI can expand service exports and domestic productivity. It can also threaten outsourcing models built on lower-cost cognitive labor.

This is a crucial distributional conflict.

For decades, globalization moved call centers, software services, back-office processing, and other knowledge work toward lower-cost labor markets. Generative AI attacks exactly some of these tradable tasks.

A company in a rich country no longer has to choose between an expensive local worker and a cheaper foreign worker.

It can choose a machine.

This could reduce one path by which developing countries moved into higher-value services.

At the same time, the same countries can use AI to make their workers more productive and move into more sophisticated work.

The race is between substitution and upgrading.

Education, infrastructure, and firm capability decide much of the outcome.

Countries that treat AI primarily as a threat to outsourcing may defend a declining cost advantage. Countries that use it to raise the complexity of services can climb.

The transition will not be painless because the workers displaced from routine service roles are not instantly transformed into AI-augmented consultants.

Again the aggregate opportunity and individual loss coexist.

Manufacturing economies face a different calculus.

AI can improve design, robotics, quality control, predictive maintenance, logistics, and energy efficiency. Countries with industrial bases can embed intelligence into physical production. This may favor places that combine manufacturing know-how with AI rather than those that specialize only in digital models.

China's strength in industrial deployment and robotics matters for this reason.

The token-maxed economy is not purely virtual.

The country that can turn machine reasoning into factories, batteries, drugs, infrastructure, and exports captures more than the country that produces text.

This is perhaps the central geopolitical lesson.

Cognition is an input.

Value is captured when cognition meets complementary assets.

The United States has frontier labs, capital markets, cloud firms, and software distribution. China has scale, manufacturing, research, and a large domestic market. Europe has industrial depth, institutions, talent, and regulatory power. Taiwan has semiconductor fabrication of extraordinary strategic importance. Energy-rich regions have another bargaining chip. Developing countries have demographics, markets, and opportunities to leap over missing service infrastructure.

No one wins because of tokens alone.

The losers are more identifiable.

Countries with weak grids, poor connectivity, low digital skills, fragmented institutions, little local-language data, and no strategic plan face the risk that AI raises the productivity of everyone they trade with faster than it raises their own.

They can become relatively poorer even while receiving useful AI services.

Relative decline is politically potent because people compare.

A doctor in a low-income country may become more capable with an AI assistant and still watch colleagues abroad gain access to better tools, records, diagnostics, and research systems. Absolute capability rises. The gap widens.

This is why access alone is not enough for development policy.

The goal is productive integration.

That requires the unglamorous foundations the World Bank emphasizes. Power. connectivity. skills. context. institutions.

The global token race will produce spectacular national projects.

The countries that benefit most may be the ones that spend less time performing sovereignty and more time building those foundations.

A context window can be a million tokens wide.

It is still useless if the country's knowledge never enters it.
