# Cheap Expertise, Expensive Judgment

There is a particular kind of inequality that does not appear cleanly in income statistics.

One person knows what question to ask.

Another does not.

The first person knows which professional to call, which form matters, which symptom is unusual, which clause is dangerous, which school rule can be appealed, which government office actually has authority, and which number in the statement deserves suspicion. The second person receives the same official information and cannot turn it into action.

Professional societies are full of these asymmetries because expertise is expensive, unevenly distributed, and often sold in inconvenient packages. A lawyer bills by the hour. A specialist physician may require a referral and a long wait. A tutor charges for a scheduled session. A financial adviser may not serve a household with little wealth. A consultant does not take a forty-dollar problem.

A language model will.

This may be the most socially important feature of cheap machine cognition.

The first useful service is not replacing the expert. It is giving people enough borrowed expertise to enter the expert system less helplessly.

A tenant can paste a lease clause into an assistant and learn the vocabulary needed to ask a lawyer a sharper question. A patient can organize symptoms and medications before an appointment. A parent can ask for an explanation of an individualized education plan. A small-business owner can understand the difference between revenue and cash flow before meeting an accountant. A citizen can translate a bureaucratic letter into ordinary language.

None of these acts requires the system to be trusted with the final decision.

They require it to reduce the cost of orientation.

Orientation is an underrated public good.

Institutions often publish enormous amounts of information while remaining difficult to navigate. The tax code is public. Court rules are public. Medical literature is public. School policies are public. Public availability does not make the material practically accessible to a person who lacks time, vocabulary, literacy, confidence, or context.

Search engines reduced one barrier by making documents easier to find.

Generative systems reduce another by making documents easier to interrogate.

The difference is profound.

A person no longer has to know the phrase that appears in the document before searching for it. She can describe the problem in her own language and ask the system to translate her experience into institutional categories.

That can redistribute practical power downward.

It can also create a new class of confident mistakes.

The lawyer knows that a rule may have exceptions. The physician knows that a symptom can mean different things in different patients. The accountant knows that a tax answer depends on facts the client forgot to mention. The model may produce a clean answer before it has discovered the missing fact.

Cheap expertise is most dangerous where the user cannot recognize the boundary between general information and case-specific judgment.

This is the high-stakes version of the verification tax.

A restaurant recommendation can be wrong without much consequence. A legal deadline cannot. A medication interaction can matter enormously. A financial mistake can consume years of savings. The expected harm of a plausible error changes the appropriate amount of machine autonomy.

The answer is not a universal ban on AI advice.

That would preserve the existing inequality in which millions of people receive little advice at all.

The relevant comparison is not always AI versus a perfect professional.

It can be AI versus no help.

This baseline is essential in medicine.

A wealthy patient in a major city may have access to specialists, testing, second opinions, and a primary-care physician who can interpret the results. A rural or poor patient may have a short appointment after a long wait. In countries with severe clinician shortages, the professional alternative may be geographically unavailable.

An AI system that improves triage, translation, record summarization, or patient preparation can create value even if it is not safe to diagnose autonomously.

The same is true in law.

The legal-services market contains a large access-to-justice gap because professional help is expensive relative to many disputes. A tenant facing a deposit dispute, a worker contesting a small wage claim, or a person trying to understand a benefits denial may never hire a lawyer.

A machine that helps organize facts, explain procedure, and draft a first letter can be better than the practical alternative.

The professional bar understandably worries about bad advice.

Society should also worry about no advice.

Token maxing changes the frontier by making layers of assistance economically possible.

There can be a cheap general model for orientation, a stronger system grounded in current authoritative sources, a specialized model for the domain, automated checks, and human escalation when uncertainty or stakes cross a threshold.

This is not one AI replacing one professional.

It is a service pyramid.

At the bottom, mass low-cost guidance.

Above it, increasingly expensive verification and responsibility.

The human expert moves toward the apex.

That can expand access while preserving professional judgment for the cases where it matters most.

It can also create a two-tier system if the bottom tier becomes an excuse to deny poor people human service.

This is the central distributional danger.

A wealthy patient receives a physician assisted by AI.

A poor patient receives AI instead of a physician.

A wealthy student receives a teacher assisted by AI.

A poor student receives an AI tutor instead of a teacher.

A wealthy defendant receives lawyers using powerful AI tools.

A poor defendant receives a chatbot link.

The technology is identical enough for policymakers to call both groups “served.” The human complement is not.

Cheap expertise can democratize professional knowledge while making human judgment a luxury good.

This possibility should shape public-service design from the beginning.

The strongest use of AI in under-resourced systems is not necessarily to remove people. It is to make scarce people available for the cases that need them.

A public benefits agency can let an assistant explain forms in many languages, check completeness, and answer routine questions so staff spend more time on exceptions. A school can use AI for practice and feedback while teachers concentrate on motivation, diagnosis, social development, and difficult misconceptions. A clinic can automate documentation and record review while preserving clinician time for patients.

This is the augmentation story at institutional scale.

It requires budgets to preserve the human complement.

If the efficiency savings are harvested immediately through headcount cuts, the service can become more automated without becoming more humane.

The economics will push hard in that direction because human labor is expensive and machine guidance becomes cheaper every year.

Public institutions face additional pressure because their demand is often effectively unlimited. A tax office can always answer more questions. A school can always provide more feedback. A court can always offer more procedural help. A healthcare system can always monitor more patients.

Token maxing can turn rationed professional attention into abundant preliminary service.

The value can be enormous.

So can the temptation to define preliminary service as sufficient.

This is why accountability cannot be an afterthought.

If an AI system used by a public agency gives wrong guidance, who bears the cost? The citizen who relied on it? The agency? The vendor? The employee who approved the system? If the answer is unclear, risk tends to fall on the least powerful party.

The same occurs in private services.

A financial platform can offer personalized guidance at scale. If the guidance is framed as education rather than advice, legal responsibility may be limited even when users experience it as advice. A medical assistant can tell a patient to seek care without formally diagnosing. A legal product can generate documents while disclaiming representation.

The boundary between information and professional service becomes economically valuable.

Regulators will spend years drawing it.

The traditional professional model tied judgment, liability, and licensure together. The person who advised you had qualifications and could be disciplined. AI unbundles those components.

Knowledge can be generated by a model.

Verification can be performed by another system.

Responsibility can sit with a company.

Licensure can remain with a human reviewer.

Insurance can absorb residual risk.

The resulting service may be cheaper and safer than traditional delivery.

It can also become a shell game in which responsibility is always somewhere else.

The quality of institutions determines which version emerges.

Professional licensing itself may change.

Licensing often protects the public from incompetence and protects incumbents from competition at the same time. Cheap AI will make this tension more visible. If a machine can perform routine legal, accounting, medical, or financial tasks reliably, prohibiting all low-cost assistance preserves professional rents along with safety.

If regulators open the market too quickly, vulnerable users can become test subjects for systems they do not understand.

A sensible middle path will be task-specific.

Some tasks can be automated with clear rules and auditing. Others require licensed review. Some require disclosure that a machine was used. Some require the provider to maintain evidence, testing, and incident reporting. Some should remain human decisions because the social objective includes dignity or democratic accountability, not merely error rates.

That last category is easy to forget.

A parole decision can be statistically informed and still demand human responsibility because society wants a person or accountable institution to own the coercive judgment. A teacher can use AI to assess writing and still need to understand the student's work. A government can automate eligibility checks and still owe citizens a comprehensible appeal process.

Efficiency is not the only value institutions serve.

Legitimacy matters.

People accept decisions partly because they recognize the authority and process that produced them. A machine can be more accurate on average and still be unacceptable if nobody can explain how a citizen contests its error.

Token maxing can improve the evidence available to decision-makers without earning the right to become the decision-maker.

This distinction becomes especially important in finance.

Cheap machine analysis can improve household decisions. People can compare mortgage terms, identify fees, model retirement scenarios, and understand insurance products. The financial industry has long profited from complexity and consumer inattention. An agent working for the customer can reverse some of that advantage.

Banks and insurers will deploy agents too.

The consumer's agent negotiates with the company's agent. Each reads the contract. Each optimizes. The transaction becomes a contest among systems operating under different objectives and resources.

At first this can empower consumers.

Eventually the market may become machine-native. Products are priced dynamically for agents. Disclosures are written to be parsed. Negotiations happen too quickly for humans to follow.

Human comprehension ceases to be the operating layer.

That is dangerous if legal consent remains attached to a click.

A person can formally authorize an agent to make choices whose complexity exceeds her ability to audit. The agent can save time while weakening meaningful consent.

Society has dealt with delegated financial decisions before through fiduciary duties.

The idea may return in software form.

Whose interest must an agent serve?

Can a shopping assistant accept commissions from sellers? Can a financial assistant recommend a product from an affiliate? Can a healthcare assistant steer a patient toward a provider owned by the same company? The economics of advice become inseparable from conflicts of interest.

Human professionals have conflicts too. Laws and norms evolved around them.

Machine advisers will need equivalent rules because scale makes small conflicts powerful.

A one-percent steering bias applied across millions of recommendations is an industry.

This is another sense in which judgment remains expensive. Trustworthy judgment requires not only capability but aligned incentives.

The model can know the answer and still be paid to recommend something else.

Education offers a different form of the same problem.

An AI tutor can adapt infinitely, but what is it optimizing?

Test scores? completion? student happiness? time in product? subscription retention? conceptual mastery? curiosity?

Those objectives conflict.

A patient tutor sometimes makes a student struggle. A commercial product may discover that immediate assistance retains users better. The most helpful interface can reduce the very effort education is meant to cultivate.

Teachers carry goals that are partly social and moral. They decide not only what answer is correct but what kind of learner the student should become.

AI can support that judgment. It does not remove the need to choose the goal.

This is the broad pattern across expert services.

Machine cognition reduces the cost of means.

The scarcity moves toward ends.

What outcome matters? Which risk is acceptable? Which tradeoff is fair? Who deserves exception? When should a rule bend? When should a person be heard even if hearing them is inefficient?

These questions are not beyond computation because they are mystical.

They are expensive because they encode values, responsibility, and context that institutions cannot outsource without changing themselves.

The most successful professionals in the token-maxed economy may therefore perform less routine analysis and more judgment under accountability.

That can raise the value of top expertise.

A renowned surgeon becomes more leveraged when AI handles documentation, imaging support, literature review, and follow-up. A great teacher can reach more students with machine support. A strong lawyer can supervise more routine work. A financial adviser can serve smaller clients using automated analysis.

The expert becomes a scarce signer of reality.

This creates another inequality risk.

The best experts can scale their judgment through AI and capture larger markets. The middle tier of routine professional service can be squeezed. The very bottom gains access to automated guidance.

The distribution becomes hourglass-shaped.

Mass cheap machine service.

A smaller layer of human professionals.

A highly paid apex of trusted experts with reputations, liability, and scarce experience.

Whether this improves welfare depends on how well the mass service performs and how easily users can escalate.

Escalation is the key design variable.

A good system knows when it is no longer the right system.

It can say: this is unusual; this deadline matters; this symptom requires a clinician; this legal question varies by jurisdiction; this financial choice could create irreversible tax consequences; bring in a person.

That humility can be engineered through rules, confidence estimates, evaluation, and domain design.

It can also be undermined by business incentives that reward keeping the user inside the automated product.

The professional future is therefore partly a market-structure question.

A company paid per automated resolution has an incentive to avoid escalation.

A company liable for harmful errors has an incentive to escalate.

A public service judged by access may automate aggressively. One judged by successful outcomes may preserve human capacity.

Metrics decide human presence.

This is why the public debate should not ask whether AI is better than doctors, teachers, lawyers, advisers, or civil servants in the abstract.

The answer depends on task, system, population, stakes, and comparison baseline.

The better question is what service architecture gives more people useful expertise while preserving accountable judgment where it matters.

That architecture will consume a great many tokens.

A low-cost layer will answer routine questions. Stronger models will inspect difficult cases. Retrieval systems will ground advice. Verifiers will check. Human professionals will supervise exceptions. Audit systems will record decisions. The amount of machine cognition surrounding each scarce human hour may rise dramatically.

Token maxing can therefore increase the demand for expert judgment even while reducing the demand for expert routine labor.

This apparent contradiction is the central professional opportunity.

The machine makes the expert's knowledge cheap.

It makes the expert's responsibility more valuable.

For the individual seeking help, the best future is not one in which everyone receives a robot instead of a professional.

It is one in which nobody has to arrive ignorant at the door of a scarce professional, and the professional's time is saved for the moment when judgment actually changes the outcome.

Knowledge can become abundant.

Being answerable for the answer should remain expensive.
