# Three Futures for Cheap Thought

The easiest way to misunderstand a technological future is to give it only one ending.

A trend becomes visible, the line is extended, and the imagination quietly stops doing work. If inference becomes cheaper, there will be more inference. If models become more capable, people will use them for more tasks. If firms can substitute software for some labor, they will. If nations regard compute as strategic, they will build more of it.

All of that may be true and still leave the most important question unanswered.

What kind of society emerges after those reactions collide?

Cheap machine thought does not arrive on an empty planet. It arrives inside legal systems, power grids, labor markets, schools, firms, families, capital markets, telecom networks, tax systems, cultural habits, and geopolitical rivalries. The same technical capability can produce very different outcomes depending on who owns the infrastructure, whether markets remain contestable, how quickly institutions adapt, and whether people retain credible ways to exit systems that have become too important to refuse.

The future of token maxing therefore cannot be forecast from token prices alone.

It has to be imagined as an equilibrium.

An equilibrium is not a prediction that nothing changes. It is a description of the pattern that persists after everyone has had time to react to everyone else. The employer changes wages because workers have agents. Workers change skills because employers have agents. Schools change teaching because students have agents. Regulators change rules because companies have agents. Companies change architecture because regulators changed rules. Energy producers build because data centers bid for power. Data centers move because power prices changed. Countries subsidize capacity because other countries moved first.

The interesting future is the one left after that chain of adjustment.

There are many possible equilibria. Three are especially useful because they expose the variables that matter most.

The first is the broad-abundance equilibrium.

In this world, model quality continues to improve while inference costs fall quickly. Competition survives at the model layer. Open-weight and locally deployable systems become good enough for a large share of useful work. Hardware efficiency rises. Specialized accelerators proliferate. Routing systems learn to send routine work to cheap models and reserve expensive reasoning for difficult cases. Context becomes more portable. Tool standards improve. Companies can switch vendors without rebuilding the nervous system of the firm.

The user experience is not a single giant oracle. It is a market of cognition.

Most people do not know which model answered a request any more than they know which data center served a web page. They own or control an agent layer that can route work across many providers. Their personal context is encrypted, permissioned, and sufficiently portable that changing providers is inconvenient rather than catastrophic. Some inference happens locally. Some goes to regional clouds. Some difficult tasks escalate to frontier systems.

The important price is not zero.

It is low enough that ordinary people stop rationing many forms of analysis.

A shop owner can ask for demand scenarios before ordering inventory. A tenant can have a lease explained. A student can receive patient tutoring in a language rarely served by commercial education. A nurse can organize a complicated history before a clinical conversation. A farmer can combine weather, soil, equipment records, commodity prices, and financing options without hiring a consultancy. A programmer in a small city can work with a virtual team of specialists previously available only at large technology companies.

Large firms still have advantages. They own distribution, data, trust, capital, and physical assets. But the gap created by administrative competence shrinks.

This matters more than it first appears.

A significant fraction of organizational scale has always been purchased because coordination is expensive. Large firms can afford legal departments, procurement teams, security staff, analysts, finance operations, market researchers, translators, compliance functions, and layers of management. These capabilities are not decorative. They allow the firm to participate in complicated markets.

If software makes a respectable fraction of that competence rentable in small increments, a five-person firm can look less like a five-person firm.

The result is not the disappearance of corporations. It is a change in the minimum efficient size of an organization.

More people can become viable producers.

The labor market in the broad-abundance equilibrium is turbulent but not hollowed out. Routine cognitive tasks contract. Some entry-level roles disappear. But new work appears around integration, physical service, customer relationships, validation, domain-specific deployment, training, auditing, toolmaking, local adaptation, and the enormous volume of projects that become economical once the cost of competent planning falls.

Crucially, institutions rebuild ladders.

Professions discover that removing junior work without replacing its training function is self-defeating. Firms create supervised simulation, rotating apprenticeships, shadow decisions, and paid practice. Schools stop pretending that a take-home essay proves what it once proved. Assessment moves toward oral defense, live problem solving, project history, and demonstrated judgment. Workers learn with AI and also practice without it when unaided skill is part of professional resilience.

The distributional result is imperfect but surprisingly broad.

The largest gain goes to people who previously faced high prices for expertise. Not because the machine replaces every professional, but because the first ninety percent of a question often becomes cheap enough to ask.

A person who could not afford a lawyer can organize facts before a legal-aid appointment. A patient can arrive with better questions. A small exporter can understand a foreign procurement process. A student can receive unlimited practice. The professional becomes more productive because the client arrives prepared, and scarce human time shifts toward judgment rather than explanation of basics.

This is the optimistic version of token maxing because it turns machine cognition into something like a general-purpose utility.

It still has costs.

Energy demand rises. Networks fill with synthetic output. Fraud becomes cheaper. Attention becomes more contested. Some white-collar wages fall. Intellectual property regimes strain. Privacy becomes harder to defend because useful agents want intimate context. The society spends more on authentication, provenance, cybersecurity, identity, and verification simply because more competent action is possible at low marginal cost.

Abundance does not remove scarcity.

It changes the shopping list.

The broad-abundance equilibrium survives because the new scarcities remain contestable. Trust can be earned by many firms. Energy can be produced by many technologies. Context can move. Models can be substituted. Workers can form new businesses. Governments enforce enough interoperability that network effects do not harden into permanent captivity.

This world produces substantial consumer surplus.

It may produce less spectacular returns for some AI infrastructure investors than the scale of social change would suggest. Competition drives inference prices down. Models improve rapidly enough that expensive assets depreciate faster than investors hoped. Open systems narrow margins. Customers capture much of the value.

That is not failure.

It is one of capitalism's strangest successes: a technology can change civilization while commoditizing the people who financed parts of it.

The second equilibrium is the cognitive landlord economy.

The technical progress is similar at first.

Models improve. Agents become useful. Context becomes essential. Firms discover that machine cognition can perform a growing share of administrative and analytical work.

But competition does not remain broad.

A handful of integrated platforms control the key bottlenecks: chips, cloud capacity, frontier models, operating systems, identity, enterprise distribution, payments, and the agent interfaces through which users reach the digital world. The most valuable context accumulates inside those platforms because moving it is technically possible but practically painful.

The product is no longer merely intelligence.

It is continuity.

Your agent knows your history, files, habits, relationships, preferred tradeoffs, past decisions, medical questions, work style, and thousands of small corrections you made over years. A competing model may be cheaper or better on a benchmark. Leaving still feels like losing part of your memory.

Companies face the same trap at larger scale.

The platform has indexed the firm's documents, workflows, permissions, code, procurement history, customer service patterns, and internal exceptions. It has built tools around all of them. The nominal switching fee may be zero. The organizational switching cost is enormous.

The landlord does not need to charge monopoly prices immediately.

It can subsidize adoption while dependence forms.

This is an old strategy wearing a new interface.

Once the agent layer becomes the place where work begins, the platform gains the power to steer. It can prefer its own cloud, marketplace, advertising system, payments product, model, search results, or enterprise applications. It can tax complements. It can decide which third-party agents receive privileged context. It can bundle until stand-alone competitors find that the market has vanished around them.

Token prices may continue falling throughout this process.

Users can therefore feel that competition is healthy because the visible unit becomes cheaper.

The rent is collected elsewhere.

It appears in cloud commitments, distribution fees, advertising spreads, data advantage, financing, enterprise bundles, preferred placement, and the value of context that cannot credibly leave.

In the cognitive landlord economy, large firms become even larger without necessarily employing proportionally more people.

The corporation owns a dense stack of automated capability. Revenue can rise while headcount grows slowly. A smaller number of senior employees supervises machine labor, handles exceptions, builds relationships, and makes high-consequence decisions. Junior hiring declines because much of the work that justified broad analyst classes can be performed by agents.

Productivity statistics improve.

The social mood may not.

The workers who remain inside high-productivity firms can be paid well because they control large amounts of capital and machine capacity. People outside those firms encounter weaker bargaining power. Some become successful independent operators using the same platforms. Others find themselves competing against extremely capable software in markets where output is abundant and attention scarce.

The result is a barbell.

At one end are people with ownership, distribution, trusted identities, elite judgment, scarce physical skills, or positions close to decision rights. At the other is a large population able to produce respectable cognitive work but struggling to make that output economically scarce.

Education becomes more valuable and less reliable as a signal at the same time.

Everyone can submit polished work. Employers respond by emphasizing pedigree, networks, live tests, portfolios with verifiable histories, and prior access to high-responsibility environments. The very technology that can democratize instruction can therefore increase the value of scarce social proof.

This is one of the uncomfortable patterns of abundance.

When production becomes cheap, selection can become elitist.

The global distribution of the landlord equilibrium is also uneven.

Countries without frontier infrastructure can still access powerful systems, but they pay rents to foreign platforms and expose more of their economic context to companies governed elsewhere. Local software industries grow at the application layer yet struggle to capture the deepest margins. Governments discover that administrative modernization has created dependence on a small number of providers whose failure or policy changes now have national consequences.

Some states respond with sovereignty programs.

They subsidize domestic clouds, local models, national identity layers, and data localization. A few build useful alternatives. Others spend heavily on prestige systems that remain technologically behind and economically underused.

The cognitive landlord economy does not require conspiracy.

It can emerge from ordinary returns to scale.

Large models require capital. Data centers require capital. Security and compliance reward scale. Enterprise buyers prefer integrated vendors. Consumers prefer convenience. Agents improve with context, context accumulates with use, and accumulated context increases switching costs.

Each step has a business rationale.

The equilibrium is simply what happens if policy and technical standards fail to preserve credible exit.

This future may still be prosperous in aggregate.

That is what makes it politically difficult.

People receive extraordinary services. Firms become productive. Scientific research accelerates. The price of many cognitive tasks falls. Yet bargaining power concentrates because the machinery behind abundance is owned by fewer institutions.

A society can become richer and less free in the same transaction.

The third equilibrium is the token arms race.

In this world, no one trusts abundance enough to enjoy it calmly.

Companies believe competitors will use more inference, so they use more inference. Job applicants believe other applicants will use agents, so they use agents. Investors believe other funds will analyze every signal, so they analyze every signal. Students believe other students will submit polished machine-assisted work, so they do the same. Governments believe rivals are building national compute, so they build national compute.

The strategic logic is individually coherent.

The aggregate result is exhausting.

Think about hiring.

One company once received five hundred applications. Cheap agents make it possible for candidates to tailor thousands of credible applications with little effort. The company responds with automated screening. Candidates respond by optimizing against the screening systems. Employers add work samples. Agents complete the work samples. Employers add live interviews. Candidates receive real-time assistance. Employers add identity controls, monitored environments, and deeper background checks.

Nobody intended to build a more expensive hiring system.

Everyone was responding to everyone else.

The same pattern appears in security.

Attackers use agents to find vulnerabilities and generate convincing social engineering. Defenders use agents to monitor, patch, simulate attacks, and inspect code. The cost of both offense and defense falls, but the total amount of activity rises because neither side can safely abstain.

It appears in finance.

Analysts generate more scenarios because competitors can. Companies answer more investor questions because investors can ask them. Markets ingest more alternative data, transcripts, satellite images, supply-chain clues, and synthetic forecasts. Some inefficiencies disappear. New ones appear at machine speed. The advantage shifts toward proprietary data, latency, infrastructure, and the ability to act on a signal before it becomes common knowledge.

It appears in law.

Discovery becomes cheaper, so more material becomes discoverable and more arguments become economical to produce. Courts and counterparties receive longer submissions. Software then summarizes them. The cost per page collapses while the number of pages explodes.

It appears in management.

Every strategic decision can summon market research, competitive simulations, risk matrices, customer transcripts, legal analysis, and five independent model critiques. The existence of these capabilities changes the standard of diligence. A manager who chooses without using them may later be asked why.

Analysis expands partly because it is valuable and partly because not performing it becomes professionally dangerous.

The token arms race is the society in which cognition becomes defensive expenditure.

A large share of machine thinking is used not to create new welfare but to remain competitive against other machine thinking.

This does not mean it is useless.

Locks are useful because thieves exist. Audits are useful because organizations make mistakes. Defensive analysis can prevent real harm. The problem is that private value and social value can diverge sharply when activities are strategic complements.

If one law firm writes a fifty-page brief, the other may need fifty pages. If all firms could credibly agree to ten, nobody would necessarily be worse represented.

If one company screens every job application with an elaborate agent pipeline, others may feel compelled to match it. If every candidate generates hundreds of tailored applications, each individual applicant gains less than the additional noise imposed on the system.

Cheap cognition can create cognitive congestion.

In the arms-race equilibrium, energy demand is especially high because efficiency improvements are swallowed by strategic escalation. Better models do not simply reduce cost. They allow more passes, more agents, more monitoring, more simulations, and more always-on systems.

Compute becomes a factor of national power in the same way industrial capacity once was.

States treat leading accelerators, semiconductor equipment, grids, and data centers as strategic assets. Export controls deepen. Alliances form around supply chains. Countries with large energy surpluses and stable institutions attract data-center investment. Countries without those advantages fear permanent dependence.

The economic geography of AI becomes the geography of electricity, fiber, capital, cooling, and political trust.

The labor market is productive but anxious.

Workers are not simply replaced. Many are accelerated into higher throughput. The standard for a competent employee rises because the employee is assumed to have access to strong tools. A weekly memo becomes a daily one. A small analysis becomes a comprehensive one. Response time shortens. Availability expands.

The worker receives a machine that saves two hours and a culture that finds three new hours of work to fill the gap.

This has happened before.

Email reduced the cost of written workplace communication and produced more written workplace communication. Smartphones reduced the cost of being reachable and expanded the expectation of reachability. Spreadsheets reduced the cost of modeling and produced more modeling.

A civilization often spends efficiency gains on higher standards.

That can produce genuine quality. It can also make life feel mysteriously no easier.

The token arms race therefore generates high measured capability without proportional increases in felt abundance.

People can do far more and still experience scarcity because the reference point moved with them.

Which of these three worlds is most likely?

Probably none in pure form.

Different sectors can settle into different equilibria.

Consumer creative tools may look like broad abundance while enterprise software looks like the landlord economy. Cybersecurity may become an arms race by necessity. Healthcare may combine cheap expertise with severe human bottlenecks. Education may democratize tutoring while making credentials more positional. Some countries may preserve competitive model ecosystems while others prefer tightly controlled national platforms.

The value of the scenarios is not that one must win.

It is that each reveals a hinge.

Broad abundance depends on falling inference cost, yes, but more importantly on portability, interoperability, competition, cheap energy, and the reconstruction of human learning systems.

The cognitive landlord economy becomes likely when context is captive, distribution is concentrated, infrastructure returns to scale overwhelm application-layer competition, and users cannot credibly exit without abandoning accumulated memory.

The token arms race becomes likely when machine cognition is strategically complementary: when one actor's use increases the pressure on every other actor to use more, regardless of whether the additional activity creates equivalent social value.

These hinges are not speculative abstractions.

They are design choices already being made.

Does an agent's memory export cleanly?

Can a firm route a workflow across providers?

Can a worker prove competence without revealing every private interaction with a model?

Can schools measure learning rather than merely submitted output?

Do utilities charge large new loads in ways that protect ordinary customers from infrastructure costs while still enabling useful investment?

Can governments buy AI capability without locking themselves into one vendor for a generation?

Can small countries access competitive compute markets without surrendering all economic context abroad?

Can companies distinguish productive inference from defensive inference?

Can people decline augmentation without becoming unemployable?

These questions sound smaller than artificial general intelligence.

They may determine more of daily life.

The science-fiction version of AI asks whether a machine becomes smarter than a human.

The political-economy version asks what happens when millions of machines become cheap enough to participate in ordinary institutions every minute of the day.

The second transition does not require a godlike machine.

It requires useful systems, falling costs, integration, and time.

That is why token maxing matters even if the frontier advances more slowly than enthusiasts expect. A merely very capable model, repeated cheaply and embedded everywhere, can change organizational economics before it solves every benchmark. The consequence comes from multiplication as much as intelligence.

And multiplication is precisely where equilibrium thinking becomes necessary.

The first-order effect of a cheap analyst is cheaper analysis.

The second-order effect is that everyone expects more analysis.

The first-order effect of a cheap tutor is better access to instruction.

The second-order effect is that schools must redesign what counts as evidence of learning.

The first-order effect of a capable personal agent is convenience.

The second-order effect is that context becomes a valuable asset somebody will try to own.

The first-order effect of cheap code is more software.

The second-order effect is more attack surface, more maintenance, and more competition among software producers.

The first-order effect of better scientific agents is faster research.

The second-order effect is that laboratories, regulators, journals, and funding institutions must absorb a larger flow of plausible claims and experiments.

The future is made of second-order effects arriving after people have already celebrated the first.

A good society will not stop the first-order gains in order to avoid adaptation.

It will adapt on purpose.

The strongest reason for optimism is that none of the darker equilibria is technologically mandatory.

Concentration can be countered by interoperability and competition. Apprenticeship can be rebuilt. Grids can be expanded. Inference can become more efficient. Public institutions can develop technical capacity. Authentication can improve. Workers can share productivity gains through bargaining, ownership, tax policy, or competitive labor markets. Standards can make context portable. Liability can keep responsibility attached to institutions capable of bearing it.

The strongest reason for caution is that none of those outcomes arrives automatically either.

Efficiency does not guarantee equality.

Abundance does not guarantee competition.

Capability does not guarantee agency.

Cheap thought does not guarantee wise institutions.

The token-maxed age will reward societies that understand this distinction early.

They will treat machine cognition neither as magic nor as ordinary software. They will recognize it as a new input whose falling price changes the relative value of nearly everything around it. They will look for the next bottleneck before it becomes a monopoly, a shortage, or a social crisis. They will preserve the human formation systems that markets are tempted to strip for short-term efficiency. They will measure welfare rather than throughput.

Most of all, they will avoid confusing quantity of cognition with quality of civilization.

A society can think constantly and decide badly.

It can generate infinite advice and distribute little power.

It can automate every memo and still fail to build houses, transmission lines, laboratories, schools, clinics, and companies.

It can spend fortunes on machine deliberation while refusing to make the political choices the analysis keeps recommending.

Cheap thought is leverage.

Leverage magnifies whatever grips the other end.

If that is competitive institutions, broad ownership, strong public capacity, portable identity, abundant energy, and human judgment, token maxing can distribute capabilities once reserved for the rich and the large.

If it is concentrated infrastructure, captive context, brittle labor ladders, weak institutions, and positional competition, the same technology can intensify hierarchy while appearing to democratize intelligence on every screen.

If it is fear of falling behind, the world may simply cook because everybody else is cooking.

The future will contain all three impulses.

The work is to decide which one becomes normal.
