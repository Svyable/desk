# The Abundance Trap

The internet solved publishing before it solved reading.

By the time almost anyone could place words, photographs, music, software, opinions, and advertisements in front of the world, another problem had become obvious. The world did not have enough attention for what it could publish.

Search engines, feeds, rankings, recommendations, editors, influencers, brands, and communities grew powerful because abundance required filters.

Generative AI repeats the sequence at a higher speed.

It does not merely make publication cheap. It makes competent-looking production cheap.

The distinction matters.

Spam was once betrayed by bad grammar, generic wording, implausible formatting, or the sender's inability to personalize at scale. A machine can fix all four. A mediocre advertisement can become polished. A phishing message can use the language of the company, the role of the recipient, and recent public events. A fake review can describe the product naturally. A lobbying letter can be varied enough to look less templated. A job application can be tailored to every listing.

The cost of seeming attentive collapses.

Attention itself does not.

This is the abundance trap: when production becomes so cheap that the receiving side must spend increasing resources determining what deserves response.

Token maxing appears on both sides.

The sender uses more tokens to produce personalized output.

The receiver uses more tokens to filter it.

This can create an arms race in which total computation rises while the underlying human objective barely improves.

Job applications provide an almost comic example.

A candidate once read ten listings, adjusted a résumé for a few, and submitted applications. Generative tools make it possible to identify hundreds of jobs, tailor the résumé and cover letter, answer screening questions, and submit at scale. The candidate is rational. More applications can increase the chance of employment.

Employers receive the volume and automate screening.

Candidates learn the screening rules and optimize against them.

Employers add assessments.

Candidates use AI to prepare or complete the assessments.

Employers move toward live interviews, identity verification, work trials, or trusted referrals.

The market spends much more computation to reconstruct a signal that once came from the fact that applying took effort.

Effort had been a crude filter.

AI removed it.

This pattern appears anywhere submission cost once rationed participation.

Academic journals can receive more manuscripts. Grant programs can receive more applications. Government consultations can receive more comments. Venture investors can receive more polished decks. Freelance marketplaces can receive more bids. Customer-service departments can receive more elaborate disputes.

Lower submission cost is often democratic.

People excluded by time, language, or writing skill can participate.

The institution then confronts volume.

If it responds with automated filtering, a new question appears: whose participation survives the filter?

Abundance does not eliminate gatekeeping.

It creates demand for more gatekeeping.

The gatekeeper may be a model.

This is one reason token maxing can concentrate power even while it democratizes creation. The number of producers explodes, increasing the value of whoever controls ranking, recommendation, and access to scarce attention.

The dynamic is already familiar in media.

Millions can publish. A small number of platforms determine what is seen.

Generative AI extends the logic to professional and institutional markets.

A procurement platform can decide which supplier proposals deserve human review. A recruiting system can decide which candidate reaches a manager. A scientific search system can decide which papers enter the synthesis. A personal assistant can decide which messages interrupt its user.

The filter becomes an economic actor.

Its objective matters.

Does it optimize relevance, profit, engagement, diversity, safety, novelty, price, or loyalty to the user? Small differences in ranking criteria can redistribute enormous amounts of opportunity because the supply side is effectively infinite.

This gives the owner of the filter leverage over creators who thought AI had liberated them from intermediaries.

The new intermediary can be more powerful than the old one because it sits inside the user's delegated attention.

Search results could be inspected.

An agent may simply act.

The abundance trap has a security version.

Cybersecurity is already an adversarial market in which each side responds to the other's tools. AI can help defenders inspect code, summarize alerts, detect suspicious behavior, write patches, and analyze threats. It can help attackers research targets, generate social-engineering messages, discover vulnerabilities, adapt malware, and operate at larger scale.

The net effect is not determined by whether AI is “good for cyber” or “bad for cyber.”

It depends on which tasks improve more, which resources remain scarce, how quickly patches spread, and whether defenders can exploit asymmetries such as centralized visibility.

Token maxing increases the tempo.

A defender can ask agents to inspect more code. An attacker can ask agents to try more paths. A system can generate hypotheses continuously. Another system can test them.

Security becomes a computational contest.

The same applies to fraud.

Personalized scams become cheaper, but fraud detection becomes more sophisticated. Banks can use behavioral data attackers do not possess. Identity systems can improve. Transaction monitoring can incorporate richer context.

The equilibrium may be safer in some domains and noisier in others.

The individual experiences the arms race through friction.

More identity checks. more captchas. more delayed transactions. more warnings. more verification. more suspicion of unexpected messages.

AI can create convenience and provoke countermeasures that remove convenience.

This is a classic externality. The scammer's cheap output imposes verification costs on everyone else.

Spam email already did this. Generative AI improves the spam enough that old filters lose signal.

The social cost is not the tokens the scammer spent.

It is the attention and infrastructure everybody else spends defending against them.

This suggests that the true economics of token maxing cannot be measured only through compute prices and productivity gains.

We need transaction costs.

A technology can make each side more capable while increasing the cost of trusting the interaction.

That can reduce trade.

Imagine an online marketplace flooded with generated product listings, generated reviews, generated seller identities, generated customer-service conversations, and generated disputes. The platform responds with verification, deposits, identity checks, reputation systems, and surveillance.

The market remains functional because the platform spends heavily recreating trust.

The final system may be better than the pre-AI market, worse, or simply more computationally elaborate.

The same logic enters culture.

Music, images, video, fiction, games, and advertising can be produced in unprecedented quantities. The optimistic view is obvious: more people create, niche tastes are served, and the cost of experimentation falls.

The pessimistic view is not that the art is fake.

It is that abundance can make discovery impossible without stronger platforms and brands.

A musician competes not only with other musicians but with an effectively unlimited supply of tailored background music. An illustrator competes with generated images created at the exact moment of demand. A stock-photo catalog becomes less valuable when the customer can describe the image instead of searching for it.

Some markets collapse toward the cost of generation.

Others move toward authenticity, live experience, fandom, reputation, physical objects, or scarce rights.

The economic value of art separates from the cost of making artifacts.

This can liberate artists from technical constraints and devastate business models built around routine production.

Both are real.

The abundance of software may be even more consequential.

If code becomes cheap, organizations can build custom tools instead of purchasing generalized products. That challenges software vendors whose moat was the cost of implementation.

But cheap code also produces maintenance abundance.

Every custom application must be secured, updated, integrated, and understood. Agents can help with maintenance, generating more code to manage the code they generated.

Software entropy accelerates.

A company can create an internal application in an afternoon and discover three years later that nobody knows why it controls a critical workflow.

The token-maxed world can drown in its own prototypes.

This is why deletion, standardization, and architecture gain value.

The ability to build anything makes deciding what not to build more important.

Science faces a particularly interesting abundance trap because hypothesis generation has always been cheaper than experimental confirmation.

AI widens the gap.

A system can read literature, propose mechanisms, generate molecules, identify correlations, and suggest experiments at enormous speed. This can accelerate discovery if laboratories become better at selecting which hypotheses to test.

The physical experiment remains scarce.

Wet labs, telescopes, particle accelerators, clinical trials, field studies, and patient populations do not scale like tokens.

Machine reasoning can therefore pile up behind physical verification.

The productive response is active learning: choose the experiment that reduces uncertainty most, use automation in labs, improve robotics, and connect models tightly to empirical feedback.

The unproductive response is literature inflation.

If researchers use AI primarily to produce more papers, proposals, analyses, and speculative hypotheses, the scientific system spends more peer-review attention without increasing contact with nature.

Publication count becomes even less meaningful.

This may force science toward new signals.

Reproducible datasets. verified experiments. registered reports. code and materials. independent replication. strong prediction before observation. evidence that survives machine-generated critique.

In other words, abundance can improve scientific norms by making cheap rhetoric less impressive.

It can also overwhelm institutions before the norms adapt.

News and politics confront a related problem.

Generative AI lowers the cost of creating plausible narratives about events. It can summarize genuine documents and fabricate nonexistent ones. It can translate propaganda. It can generate targeted political messages for small groups whose concerns differ.

The democratic fear is not merely a single spectacular deepfake.

It is a lower background level of certainty.

If every embarrassing recording can be dismissed as synthetic, genuine evidence loses some force. Scholars have called versions of this the liar's dividend: the existence of convincing fakes creates plausible deniability for real material.

Abundance of fabrication taxes truth even when the fabrication fails.

Verification technologies can help. Content credentials, signatures, secure capture, trusted publication systems, and forensic tools can establish provenance in narrow cases.

The broader defense is institutional credibility.

A society with trusted election officials, courts, journalists, researchers, and public records is more resilient because citizens have sources they are willing to believe when individual artifacts become uncertain.

A society with low institutional trust enters the synthetic era with less ballast.

This makes social trust a form of infrastructure for token maxing.

It was always valuable.

It becomes more valuable when generating plausible counterstories is cheap.

The abundance trap also changes interpersonal communication in small ways that accumulate.

An employee receives a thoughtful three-paragraph email from a colleague and wonders whether the colleague wrote it. A friend sends an unusually polished birthday message. A dating profile is optimized. A condolence note is generated. None of these acts is necessarily deceptive. The sender may have used a tool to express something genuinely felt.

The recipient still cares about effort.

Human relationships use effort as evidence of attention.

If effort can be simulated cheaply, people seek other signals.

A phone call. a handwritten note. a specific memory. showing up.

Physical presence appreciates because digital care becomes easy to manufacture.

This is not a tragedy. It is another bottleneck migration.

The danger is that people begin distrusting polished language itself.

A non-native speaker who uses AI to express herself more clearly can be unfairly treated as inauthentic. A disabled person who relies on assistive generation can have her authorship questioned. A thoughtful user can collaborate deeply with a model and produce work that is genuinely hers even if the sentences were generated.

Society will need a more sophisticated idea of authorship than “typed every word personally.”

The printing press did not make the printer the author. A photographer uses a machine. A film is collaborative. Software developers use libraries written by strangers.

AI expands the collaboration so far that old intuitions wobble.

Provenance can tell us how something was made.

It cannot tell us whether the maker cared.

This is why the abundance trap is ultimately not a problem of volume alone.

It is a problem of meaning under cheap production.

Markets previously used production cost as one signal of commitment. It took time to write the letter, money to produce the advertisement, skill to make the image, labor to apply for the job, expertise to generate the analysis.

Token maxing removes pieces of those costs.

The receiver must infer commitment through other evidence.

Reputation. identity. history. stakes. reciprocity. verified action.

These are harder to generate from nothing.

That makes them economically valuable.

Companies that can authenticate people, transactions, records, and provenance may become major winners. So may communities with strong reputational norms. Small trusted networks can outperform giant open channels for important communication.

The open internet can become more useful for discovery and less useful for trust.

Agents themselves may mediate this transition.

A personal agent can filter inboxes, verify senders, summarize claims, inspect links, compare prices, check citations, and decide which notifications deserve attention. The user delegates the abundance problem.

This is a genuine benefit.

It also gives enormous power to whoever controls the agent.

The filter can decide which merchant is visible, which article is summarized, which message interrupts, which political claim is presented, which job is worth applying to.

The agent becomes the editor of the user's world.

A token-maxed society can therefore move from mass platforms to personalized gatekeepers without eliminating gatekeeping.

The gatekeeper simply becomes intimate.

Competition and transparency matter profoundly at this layer.

Can users choose the objectives? Can they inspect why something was filtered? Can they switch providers without losing context? Are commercial incentives disclosed? Can independent agents access the same underlying markets?

These questions determine whether personal AI increases agency or becomes a new form of enclosure.

The abundance trap is not solved by making generation expensive again.

That would sacrifice real gains.

The better response is to lower the cost of trustworthy filtering while preserving openness and contestability.

Machine verification. provenance. reputation. rate limits in adversarial systems. better identity infrastructure. stronger penalties for fraud. interfaces that reveal uncertainty. institutions that can be appealed.

Most of these are boring compared with generative demos.

They are the plumbing of abundance.

There is one final trap.

People can become addicted to the volume itself.

More alternatives feel like more possibility. More research feels like more rigor. More scenarios feel like more preparation. More messages feel like more opportunity. The machine can always produce another option, so stopping can feel like leaving value unused.

This is the individual form of the same social problem.

The output exceeds the capacity to choose.

Psychologists have studied choice overload in human contexts for decades with mixed and context-dependent findings. The sensible conclusion is not that more options are always bad. It is that options impose selection cost, and the cost rises when differences are hard to evaluate.

AI creates exactly that environment.

A hundred competent logos. fifty vacation itineraries. thirty business strategies. twenty possible diagnoses. ten versions of the email.

At some point another candidate does not increase freedom.

It postpones commitment.

The cure is not fewer capabilities.

It is better objectives.

A person who knows what matters can use abundance as search. A person without criteria experiences abundance as noise.

The same is true for companies and societies.

Token maxing makes the supply side of cognition astonishingly elastic.

It does not make purpose elastic.

The trap closes when we mistake the number of things we can generate for the number of things worth having.
