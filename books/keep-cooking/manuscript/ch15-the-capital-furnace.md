# The Capital Furnace

Tokens look weightless until somebody builds the building.

The interface is a box on a screen. The physical system beneath it is land, concrete, copper, steel, fiber, transformers, cooling equipment, generators, substations, chips, memory, networking, and power purchased every hour the system runs.

Token maxing turns an abstract appetite into capital expenditure.

In April 2026, the International Energy Agency reported that capital spending by five large technology companies had exceeded four hundred billion dollars in 2025 and was set, by its tally, to rise sharply again in 2026. The exact corporate budgets will change. Projects will be delayed, repriced, or canceled. What matters is the scale.

The machine-intelligence boom has become an industrial buildout.

That should make anyone who studies capital cycles pay attention.

A capital cycle begins with scarcity.

Demand rises. Existing capacity becomes valuable. Prices and profits improve. Investors see the returns and finance more capacity. New entrants arrive. Suppliers expand. The scarcity attracts its own cure.

If demand keeps growing faster than supply, the boom can continue for a long time.

If supply catches up first, pricing weakens, returns compress, and yesterday's shortage becomes tomorrow's overcapacity.

This pattern is not evidence of irrationality. It is how competitive investment works under uncertain demand.

The AI buildout contains every ingredient.

Demand for training and inference has risen. Frontier models require large compute clusters. Agents increase usage. Companies fear being capacity constrained. Nations care about strategic compute. Model providers compete on capability and latency. Cloud companies want enough infrastructure to serve customers and enough scarcity to justify the infrastructure.

The rational response is to build.

When many rational actors build simultaneously, the collective result can still overshoot.

This is the ancient joke inside modern capitalism.

No company wants to be the one without capacity if demand explodes. Capacity takes time to construct. Chips must be ordered. Power must be secured. Data-center shells, substations, and transmission cannot appear instantly. Management must commit before demand is fully known.

That creates the option value of excess.

A company can justify building more than current demand because the cost of missing the market is enormous. If AI becomes foundational, having spare capacity may be better than being rationed.

The same reasoning appears across the industry.

The aggregate spare capacity can become enormous.

Whether this is a bubble depends on definitions that are usually applied too casually.

A technology can be transformative and overbuilt. Investors can lose money while users gain. Infrastructure can become socially valuable after the original owners earn poor returns. Railways, fiber networks, and other capital-intensive systems provide variations on this history.

The correct question is not whether AI is real.

It is whether the cash flows generated by the buildout justify the capital committed at the prices paid.

Token maxing makes the answer harder because demand is endogenous to price.

Build more capacity, and inference becomes cheaper. Cheaper inference encourages more use. More use supports more capacity. The industry can create the demand that absorbs the supply.

This is the optimistic loop.

The pessimistic loop differs by one variable.

Prices fall faster than volume rises.

The same physical infrastructure becomes heavily utilized while owners earn disappointing returns.

Consumers receive abundant machine intelligence. Investors receive a lesson in price elasticity.

This is why revenue growth alone cannot settle the investment case.

Suppose token consumption grows tenfold while price per token falls by ninety percent. Revenue stays roughly flat. Compute utilization explodes. Social usage explodes. The technology looks ubiquitous. The infrastructure provider can still struggle.

Alternatively, price falls by half while volume rises tenfold. Revenue grows fivefold and the buildout looks brilliant.

The elasticity decides the business.

The industry cannot know it perfectly in advance because lowering price changes behavior in ways no historical dataset fully captures.

Agentic demand makes the uncertainty larger.

Humans have limited hours. Agents do not. If an agent can profitably spend more compute to improve an outcome, usage can rise far beyond human query volume.

This is the strongest argument for aggressive capacity investment.

The market for machine cognition may be much larger than the market for chat.

The counterargument is efficiency.

Models become better at using compute. Hardware improves. Smaller systems achieve capabilities that once required larger systems. Distillation, quantization, caching, sparse architectures, specialized chips, and better software reduce the resources required per task.

The same industry that creates demand is furiously reducing unit cost.

This creates an investment race against your own suppliers' progress.

A data center can be physically durable while its economically valuable equipment becomes obsolete quickly. Accelerators purchased at enormous cost can be superseded by hardware with better performance per watt. Networking standards improve. Cooling requirements change. Model architectures shift.

Depreciation becomes a strategic variable.

The accounting schedule may say the asset lasts several years. The competitive schedule may say the asset is second-tier much sooner.

This matters because AI infrastructure is not only concrete and power. A large share of value sits in equipment whose economic life is linked to a fast research frontier.

Investors therefore face a layered asset.

The building can last decades.

The substation can last decades.

The servers may not.

The chip may remain useful while earning far less because newer chips process the same work more cheaply.

A token-maxed economy can consume every old accelerator and still destroy its original owner's expected return.

Again social value and financial return diverge.

This is one reason secondary markets matter. Older hardware can migrate from frontier training into inference, research, education, smaller companies, and lower-cost regions. What is obsolete for the frontier can remain transformative elsewhere.

The hand-me-down effect can spread compute globally.

It can also create energy problems because older hardware is less efficient.

Capital cascades down the performance hierarchy.

The same may happen with models. Yesterday's frontier becomes today's commodity. Yesterday's expensive accelerator becomes today's budget inference server.

Abundance arrives through depreciation.

The geographic investment decisions create local winners and losers before the national economics settle.

A data center brings construction, tax revenue, demand for land, electrical work, and infrastructure. It may bring fewer permanent jobs than residents expect relative to its physical scale. It consumes power and sometimes water. It can require new transmission or generation whose costs are allocated through local utility systems.

Communities therefore negotiate over who pays for the buildout.

A giant technology company can sign long-term power contracts. A household receives the electricity bill. Regulators have to decide how grid upgrades are allocated. The answer determines whether local residents experience AI investment as development or as a subsidy to remote users.

This is not a moral side issue.

Political backlash can become a physical bottleneck.

Data centers need permits, land, grid connections, and community acceptance. A token-maxed company can buy chips faster than a region can build transmission.

The IEA has emphasized this mismatch in lead times. Data centers can be built relatively quickly compared with parts of the energy system. Transformers, turbines, transmission, and interconnection queues impose slower clocks.

The capital furnace therefore cannot be fed by money alone.

Some constraints are industrial.

This creates scarcity rents.

Transformer manufacturers can charge more. Land near strong grid connections becomes valuable. Power developers gain bargaining power. Regions with surplus generation attract projects. Engineers and electricians become bottlenecks.

The AI boom distributes income into the physical economy in ways the software narrative obscures.

It may revive regions built around energy and industry.

It may also create speculative behavior around them.

Land options near proposed substations. power contracts. data-center shell developments. gas generation plans. small modular reactor announcements. Infrastructure booms attract promoters because future scarcity is easy to narrate.

Some projects will never be built.

Announced capacity is not installed capacity.

Installed capacity is not utilized capacity.

Utilized capacity is not profitable capacity.

These distinctions are essential in a boom and routinely blurred.

The token-maxing thesis can itself become a financing story.

Investors hear that reasoning improves with more test-time compute, agents multiply inference calls, adoption is accelerating, and costs are falling. The conclusion seems obvious: demand for compute will be insatiable.

Insatiable demand is one of the most dangerous phrases in capital markets.

Demand can be enormous and price-sensitive. It can shift among regions and providers. Efficiency can reduce the amount needed for a given task. Customers can cache, optimize, or move workloads to devices. Regulators can restrict use. Model architectures can change.

The best industrial forecast is usually less certain than the investment spreadsheet needs it to be.

This does not mean the buildout should stop.

Underbuilding a transformative technology has costs too.

If compute becomes a central productive input, shortages can constrain research, business formation, and national competitiveness. Capacity investment creates option value for applications not yet invented. Excess infrastructure can lower prices and enable experimentation.

From society's perspective, some overbuilding can be useful.

From the investor's perspective, useful overbuilding can be painful.

This is another reason public policy and private returns diverge.

A government may rationally want abundant domestic compute even if abundance compresses provider margins. Cheap capacity can support startups, universities, public services, and industrial resilience. The public objective is not to maximize data-center return on invested capital.

Industrial policy can therefore finance capacity that private markets would build more cautiously.

That can create strategic resilience.

It can also socialize bad bets.

Governments are not immune to prestige investment. National compute projects can become monuments. Subsidies can chase yesterday's architecture. Political leaders can confuse owning hardware with creating an innovation ecosystem.

Compute without skills, software, data, and institutions can become expensive metal.

Private firms make analogous mistakes.

A chief executive can believe that owning dedicated capacity is strategic when renting would be cheaper. Another can rent everything and discover the provider has bargaining power during scarcity. The correct capital structure depends on usage stability, strategic sensitivity, financing cost, and how rapidly the hardware changes.

There is no universal answer.

The token-maxed economy will contain every arrangement: owned data centers, leased capacity, cloud APIs, sovereign clusters, on-device inference, edge systems, and spot markets for compute.

Markets will learn to price time itself.

A low-priority agent can wait for cheap capacity. A real-time service pays for latency. Batch research runs when renewable power is abundant. Critical workloads reserve hardware. Compute becomes dispatchable demand in an energy system trying to balance supply.

This is where AI and electricity begin to co-design each other.

Data centers can locate near generation. Workloads can shift in time and geography. AI can help optimize grids while AI demand changes the grid. Long-term contracts can finance new generation. Technology companies can accelerate nuclear, geothermal, storage, and renewable investment because they need dependable power.

The capital furnace can drive energy innovation.

It can also prolong fossil generation where gas or coal is the fastest available answer.

The outcome varies by region.

This makes the environmental argument inseparable from the capital-cycle argument. A data center built today can influence generation decisions that last decades. If the expected AI demand fails to materialize, the infrastructure remains. If demand exceeds forecasts, emergency generation may be dirtier than planned.

Forecast error becomes physical.

The scale of capital involved means the consequences spread into interest rates, credit markets, construction, utility regulation, and public finance.

A technology industry that once appeared asset-light becomes one of the largest industrial investors in the world.

That reversal should change how we think about AI companies.

They are not merely software firms selling intelligence.

At scale they are infrastructure companies financing factories for computation.

Factories have utilization risk.

Factories have power risk.

Factories have supply chains.

Factories become obsolete.

Factories can overbuild.

The clean interface hides all of this because the user sees words appear on glass.

The capital furnace glows somewhere else.

If token maxing plays out, it will glow brighter.

The largest financial question will not be whether society wants more machine intelligence.

It probably will.

The question will be how much investors can charge for producing a resource whose cost keeps falling precisely because they spent so much capital making it abundant.
