# The Moat Moves Downstack

A technology company loves a moat because a moat makes competition someone else's problem.

The ideal business owns something difficult to copy: a network, a brand, a distribution channel, proprietary data, regulation, capital intensity, switching costs, a manufacturing process, or simply enough scale that the next entrant cannot afford the race.

Artificial intelligence initially appeared to offer the cleanest moat imaginable.

Train the smartest model in the world.

Everybody else rents it.

The problem with that story is that intelligence is a moving target and research diffuses.

A model can be the best on Monday and merely excellent months later. Competitors learn. Techniques spread through papers, employees, open-source code, and observable product behavior. Hardware improves. Smaller models become stronger. Distillation transfers capability. Customers learn which tasks do not need the frontier.

The moat does not necessarily disappear.

It moves.

This is one of the most important consequences of token maxing for companies because more inference increases the value of the infrastructure beneath inference.

If models become more interchangeable while total token consumption explodes, the economic prize can migrate toward whoever supplies chips, cloud capacity, power, networking, distribution, and the software that routes demand.

A commodity can create fortunes upstream.

Oil is broadly standardized; owning low-cost reserves matters enormously. Internet packets are standardized; network and platform businesses still became powerful. Electricity is fungible at the socket; generation, transmission, and regulated distribution remain strategic assets.

Machine cognition can become cheaper without becoming economically unimportant to supply.

The first moat sits in frontier capability.

Training leading models requires large research teams, capital, data pipelines, infrastructure, and access to accelerators. The barrier is real. Stanford's 2026 AI Index described a world in which industry produced the overwhelming majority of notable frontier models and global AI compute capacity had expanded rapidly. Frontier work is not a garage hobby at full scale.

The second moat sits in serving the models.

Inference at enormous volume requires data centers, optimized hardware, memory, networking, power, cooling, compilers, scheduling, and operational expertise. Token maxing strengthens this moat because the value of efficient serving grows with usage.

Saving one microcost on a rare request is trivial.

Saving it across trillions of requests is a business.

The third moat sits in distribution.

The model that is already inside the operating system, browser, phone, productivity suite, customer-service platform, cloud, or search engine does not need to win every benchmark to receive enormous demand. Convenience routes tokens.

This is how technology markets often mature. The best component does not automatically own the customer. The interface closest to intent can choose the component.

A consumer asks for help writing an email. She may not know which model handled the request. A company asks an enterprise platform to analyze contracts. The platform can route among several models. The underlying providers compete for a position in someone else's product.

This commoditizes the model layer selectively.

The strongest frontier systems can still charge premiums for difficult tasks. But routine inference becomes a procurement category. The customer asks which model meets the quality threshold at the lowest total cost.

Total cost includes more than tokens.

Latency. privacy. reliability. data residency. tool integration. fine-tuning. observability. safety. contractual terms. support. switching effort.

This is why simple token-price comparisons can mislead. A cheap model that requires more retries or produces more errors can be expensive. A premium model that resolves a task in one pass can be cheap.

The mature market prices outcomes.

That outcome pressure encourages routing.

An enterprise may use small models for classification, a mid-tier model for routine generation, a reasoning model for difficult analysis, and a specialized system for coding. The application layer becomes a portfolio manager of cognition.

The firm that controls routing sees the economics of every model.

That is valuable information.

It knows which provider performs well on which task, how prices change, when customers tolerate latency, and where failures occur. It can arbitrage model providers against one another.

This makes the orchestration layer a potential moat.

It also makes it a target for acquisition by model companies that do not want to become commodity suppliers.

Vertical integration follows naturally.

Chip companies build systems. Cloud companies build models. Model companies build applications. Application companies train specialized models. Everyone moves toward the layer where the margin appears safer.

The token-maxed stack becomes a field of firms trying to escape commoditization by owning the adjacent bottleneck.

This is ordinary capitalism with unusually fast-moving boundaries.

Open-weight models accelerate the movement.

When capable models can be run, modified, or hosted outside a single provider, customers gain options. Developers can bring inference closer to private data, control costs, and reduce vendor dependence. Countries can build domestic services without reproducing the entire frontier research effort.

Open models do not make compute free.

They can increase demand for compute by lowering software barriers.

This is another rebound effect. The easier it becomes to deploy a model, the more organizations deploy one. Hardware and cloud providers can benefit from openness even when proprietary model vendors face pricing pressure.

The history of open-source software offers a close analogue.

Linux did not eliminate technology profits. It changed where they were earned. Companies built businesses around hardware, cloud hosting, support, managed services, databases, security, and enterprise integration. An open component can expand the market around itself.

If foundation models follow this path, the companies that own complementary assets can win enormously.

Data is the obvious complement, but the word is too broad.

Raw data is not automatically a moat. Public information can be copied. Customer data can be noisy. Large datasets can be legally constrained or strategically useless.

Useful proprietary context is the stronger asset.

A model connected to the history of every repair a manufacturer performed can deliver value competitors cannot reproduce from the public internet. A medical system with high-quality longitudinal records can build workflows around context. A financial platform sees transaction patterns. A software company sees code and incident history.

The moat lies in the relationship between data and action.

This is why companies that mediate workflows are so well positioned.

A system of record knows what happened. A system of action can learn what happened after a recommendation. That feedback loop turns usage into better context.

Token maxing intensifies the flywheel because agents interact with the workflow constantly.

Every recommendation, acceptance, rejection, correction, and outcome becomes potential training or evaluation data.

The company operating the loop improves faster.

The competitive concern is obvious.

A dominant platform can use customer activity to improve a service that makes the platform more dominant. New entrants lack both distribution and feedback.

Competition policy has dealt with versions of this problem in search, social networks, e-commerce, and operating systems. AI adds a cognitive layer that can make the feedback more encompassing.

The provider does not merely know what the customer clicked.

It may know what the customer asked, considered, rejected, and intended.

Intent data is an extraordinary asset.

A shopping assistant knows the product a person wants before the purchase. A business agent knows which suppliers a company is evaluating. A coding assistant knows what software a firm is building. A productivity assistant sees drafts before publication.

This information can improve service.

It can also create conflicts of interest if the platform operates adjacent businesses.

The agent that advises you which product to buy may belong to a company that sells products. The agent that helps a business choose cloud resources may belong to the cloud provider. The assistant that recommends software may operate an app marketplace.

Token maxing puts more decisions through intermediaries with potential incentives of their own.

The moat becomes advisory position.

This is more powerful than ordinary search because the agent can act.

A search engine presents links. An agent can choose, purchase, schedule, or negotiate. The difference between ranking and delegation is enormous.

Companies will compete to become trusted delegates.

Trust creates switching costs because users do not want to retrain a system that knows their preferences and workflows. Context memory deepens the moat.

This is why portability matters again.

If users can export their agent's memory, preferences, permissions, and history in a useful format, competition remains possible. If memory is proprietary, a long relationship becomes a cage.

The technical standard for exporting “what my AI knows about me” does not exist cleanly because knowledge is not a file. It is spread across conversations, embeddings, summaries, retrieval indexes, tool connections, and learned preferences.

Solving that problem may become as important as number portability in telecommunications.

The economic reason is the same: switching costs shape market power.

The moat also moves beneath software into electricity.

An AI company can optimize models brilliantly and still fail to deploy capacity if it cannot obtain power. Data centers require grid connections, transformers, substations, generation, land, cooling, and construction.

The IEA's 2026 work described tightening bottlenecks across several of these systems even as investment surged.

This gives temporary strategic value to assets that technology investors once considered boring.

Utilities. turbine manufacturers. transformer suppliers. electrical contractors. land near transmission. fiber routes. cooling systems. power developers.

The intelligence boom becomes an infrastructure boom.

The gains spread into sectors that never train a model.

This is how general-purpose technologies propagate economically. The visible product creates demand through its supply chain.

Some of the largest winners in a gold rush sell shovels.

Others own the land under the shovel factory.

The semiconductor supply chain makes the point dramatically.

Leading AI systems depend on advanced chips whose manufacturing is highly concentrated. Stanford's 2026 AI Index highlighted the extraordinary role of TSMC in fabricating leading AI chips. This concentration turns a company, an island, and a regional security environment into inputs to global machine cognition.

Model competition can be fierce while physical fabrication remains concentrated.

The moat moves all the way down to lithography, packaging, memory, and manufacturing know-how.

Countries notice.

Industrial policy returns.

Governments subsidize fabs, restrict exports, fund power infrastructure, and treat compute capacity as strategic. The market for tokens becomes entangled with national security.

This makes corporate planning more difficult because the cheapest provider today may not be available tomorrow under the same rules.

Redundancy gains value.

A company may deliberately pay more to avoid dependence on one region, one chip, one cloud, one model, or one energy market. The optimization objective expands from cost to resilience.

Token maxing therefore creates two contradictory pressures.

Scale pushes toward concentration because giant infrastructure is efficient.

Resilience pushes toward diversification because giant concentration is dangerous.

The equilibrium will vary by layer.

Some components become utility-like. Others remain oligopolistic. Some open-source ecosystems flourish. Some regulated national stacks emerge. Some companies vertically integrate to secure supply.

The phrase “AI industry” becomes less useful because the industry stretches from uranium and natural gas to transformer steel, silicon wafers, network switches, cloud software, foundation models, workflow applications, auditors, and training providers.

Who wins depends on where scarcity settles.

If models remain difficult to reproduce, model labs win.

If models commoditize but inference volume explodes, infrastructure wins.

If infrastructure competition drives prices down, distribution and proprietary context win.

If agents become interchangeable, trusted brands and rights to act may win.

If regulation constrains everything, institutions capable of compliance win.

The moat moves because progress attacks the current moat.

This is why investors should be cautious about treating technical leadership as permanent economic leadership.

The company with the best model has achieved something remarkable.

It has not solved capitalism.

Competitors will attack the margin from above, below, and beside it.

Customers will seek alternatives. Open systems will improve. Suppliers will raise prices if they own a bottleneck. Platforms will bundle. Governments will intervene.

The technology can become more important as the original business model becomes less attractive.

Consumers often benefit from this violence.

The moat that disappears becomes the price that falls.

Token maxing accelerates the process because customers consume so much cognition that small differences in unit economics matter. Volume creates pressure for substitution.

The most durable companies will understand which layer of scarcity they actually own.

Not which layer is fashionable.

Not which benchmark they lead this quarter.

Which thing cannot be cheaply replaced when the tokens become cheap.
