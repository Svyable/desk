# THE MOON KEPT BLINKING

## What Happened When NASA Built a Machine for an Anecdote

Sven Hardy Benson

This book begins with an embarrassing scientific object: a report that something on the Moon changed color for a little while.

The embarrassment is useful. In the 1960s NASA did not merely collect such reports. It funded instrumentation intended to detect them. A later NASA catalog assembled 1,468 descriptions of reported lunar transient phenomena from the literature. Apollo-era measurements then supplied genuinely physical lunar activity—moonquakes, charged particles, radiogenic gases—while modern monitoring turned one class of brief lunar light into a routine measurable phenomenon: meteoroid impact flashes.

None of that means the old reports were all real, all lunar, or all caused by one thing. It means the history gives us an unusually clean way to watch an anomaly cross several epistemic regimes: eyewitness report, catalog, targeted instrument, landed sensor, orbital measurement, automated telescope. Each regime changes what the word *phenomenon* is allowed to mean.

The evidence is kept in four classes throughout. **Class 1: verified/documented** includes NASA technical reports, instrument programs, published measurements, archived catalogs, and modern confirmed impact flashes. **Class 2: credible but incomplete** includes well-recorded observations whose physical cause or observational denominator remains uncertain. **Class 3: disputed/unconfirmed** includes claims of specific unexplained surface changes, extraordinary artificial activity, or causal interpretations not independently established. **Class 4: speculative synthesis** includes this book's own ideas—`instrumental translation`, `anomaly migration`, `observer tax`, and the `survival test`. They are arguments, not discoveries.

The strongest competing explanation stays alive: most historical transient lunar phenomena may be heterogeneous observational error—seeing, glare, telescope optics, expectation, reporting bias, impacts, and occasional ordinary lunar processes—with no residual exotic category required. A catalog of reports is not a catalog of physical events. The book fails if it quietly turns one into the other.

The reader promise is narrower and stranger. We will ask what happens when science takes an anecdote seriously enough to build a machine for it—and whether the anomaly survives when the machine changes what can be seen.
