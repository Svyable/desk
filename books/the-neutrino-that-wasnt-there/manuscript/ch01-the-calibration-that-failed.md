# 1. The Calibration That Failed

The gallium anomaly has the best origin story in particle physics because it was not supposed to be a discovery experiment. It was a distrust experiment.

GALLEX and SAGE had been built to count solar electron neutrinos through the reaction 71Ga(νe,e−)71Ge. The attraction of gallium was threshold. It could see the low-energy pp neutrinos that dominate the solar flux. The price was absurd delicacy: turn a tiny number of gallium atoms into radioactive germanium atoms, chemically extract them from tens of tonnes of target, and count their decays.

A sensible person asks whether the chemistry works. A more sensible person puts an artificial neutrino source inside the apparatus.

GALLEX used intense 51Cr sources in the 1990s. SAGE used 51Cr and later 37Ar. The ratios of observed to expected 71Ge production tended to come out below one. Not one spectacular failure. Several low answers.

This is the first epistemic hinge of the book. The denominator was theoretical and experimental at once. “Expected” depended on source activity, geometry, neutrino lines, target mass and the capture cross section. A deficit could mean disappearing neutrinos. It could mean an overestimated cross section. It could mean a source or extraction bias. It could mean several smaller effects aligned.

Years later the collection was named the gallium anomaly. Names are useful and dangerous. Once a residual has a proper noun it begins to behave socially like an object.

The sterile-neutrino interpretation supplied an elegant geometry: electron neutrinos oscillate into a state invisible to ordinary weak interactions over meter-scale baselines. The mass-squared splitting would be around the eV² scale, far beyond the established solar and atmospheric splittings. If true, the calibration problem would be a crack in the three-neutrino framework.

But calibration anomalies deserve a different habit of mind from ordinary discoveries. The apparatus is interrogating itself. If it answers strangely, the strangeness may live in nature, but it may also live in the assumptions that convert a source decay into an expected count. This book follows every link in that conversion.
