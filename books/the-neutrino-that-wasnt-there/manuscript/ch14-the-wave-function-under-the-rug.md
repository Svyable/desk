# 14. The Wave Function Under the Rug

In 2025 and 2026, the gallium anomaly moved into a place where particle physicists do not instinctively look for missing neutrinos: the electron wave function.

Cadeddu and collaborators recalculated the capture process using self-consistent relativistic electron wave functions, solving the Dirac-Coulomb problem rather than relying on the conventional leading-order treatment. Their February 2026 Physical Review D paper reassessed the capture cross section and the anomaly's significance.

This was not yet the final turn. The deeper issue concerned factorization—whether the leptonic wave-function contribution can be separated from the nuclear transition density using approximations inherited from detailed-balance reasoning.

At low energy, the outgoing electron samples the nuclear charge distribution. Nuclear transition densities are spatial objects. If the electron wave function varies enough over the relevant volume, pulling one factor outside an integral may produce a bias that looks small in formalism and large in a precision anomaly.

There is a delicious inversion here. Sterile-neutrino phenomenology had treated the cross section as nuisance normalization. Nuclear theory began treating the normalization as the discovery surface.
