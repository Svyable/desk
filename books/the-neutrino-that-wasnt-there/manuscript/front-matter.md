# The Neutrino That Wasn't There

## Gallium, BEST, and the Calibration Anomaly That Will Not Go Away

By Sven Hardy Benson

### Author's note

There is a particular kind of embarrassment available only to experimental physics. You build a calibration source because you do not trust yourself. The source is supposed to be the easy thing. Its activity is measured. Its neutrinos are born from known decays. Its distance from the target is centimeters, not one astronomical unit. You put it inside the apparatus so the Sun cannot be blamed.

Then the calibration fails.

Not catastrophically. That would be merciful. It fails by roughly a fifth.

This book is about that fifth.

The gallium anomaly began in source tests of GALLEX and SAGE and returned, with uncomfortable clarity, in the Baksan Experiment on Sterile Transitions. BEST did not merely repeat an old number. It built two concentric gallium targets precisely to look for distance-dependent disappearance. Both zones came up low. The deficit became harder to dismiss and, simultaneously, harder to explain as the simple sterile-neutrino story many of us wanted.

The rules here are severe. A deficit is not a particle. A significance is not a mechanism. A cross section is not a constant handed down from Sinai. And a new calculation that makes an anomaly smaller does not retroactively make the experiments mistaken. We will keep measured extraction yields, source activities, nuclear matrix elements, electron wave functions, oscillation fits, reactor exclusions and cosmological assumptions in separate boxes until the data earn the right to connect them.
