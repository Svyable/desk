# 2. Gallium in the Dark

Gallium is not a glamorous detector medium. That is part of its charm. It is a metal that melts in a warm hand and, in radiochemical neutrino work, behaves like an ocean in which the signal consists of a few changed atoms.

The solar-neutrino problem trained physicists to distrust both stars and detectors. Chlorine experiments had measured too few solar neutrinos. Kamiokande saw a deficit too. Gallium experiments were built to reach lower energies and therefore a different part of the solar spectrum. Eventually neutrino oscillations solved the central solar puzzle: electron neutrinos were not vanishing from existence, they were changing flavor.

That triumph matters because it set the emotional conditions for the next anomaly. Neutrino deficits had already rewritten physics once.

The gallium reaction is sensitive to low-energy electron neutrinos because 71Ga can capture one and become 71Ge. The daughter decays by electron capture with a half-life of roughly 11.47 days. Counting it requires chemistry, proportional counters or equivalent low-background techniques, and a careful efficiency model.

This is not a calorimeter where every interaction leaves a visible flash. It is bookkeeping over atoms.

The unusual feature makes gallium both fragile and powerful. A radioactive source test can probe the complete chain: neutrino production, propagation, capture, chemical extraction, and decay counting. If the answer is low, one cannot blame the solar model. The source is in the room.

Yet the nuclear part remains. The 71Ga capture probability depends on transitions to the 71Ge ground and excited states. The ground-state strength is constrained by the inverse electron-capture decay, but excited-state contributions require nuclear-structure information. Charge-exchange measurements can help, but converting hadronic probes into weak transition strengths carries model assumptions.

That fact would spend thirty years waiting to become central.
