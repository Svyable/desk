# Stealing Time

## How Defense Science Learned to Treat Biology's Clock as an Engineering Variable

### Sven Hardy Benson

## 1. The Golden Hour Is an Engineering Specification

A wounded body has a clock in it.

Not the useful clock on the medic's wrist. A worse one. Bleeding becomes shock. Oxygen debt becomes cell injury. Proteins lose their shape. Membranes stop behaving like borders. The difference between a patient and a body can be measured in minutes, which is why emergency medicine inherited that brutal phrase: the golden hour.

The ordinary engineering response is transportation. Move the casualty faster. Put blood closer to the fight. Improve evacuation. Shrink geography.

DARPA asked a more impolite question.

What if you slowed the casualty instead?

Its Biostasis program was not a suspended-animation program in the science-fiction sense, and calling it one would ruin the interesting part. The stated objective was narrower and stranger: use chemical biology to reversibly slow biological processes, without relying on cooling, so that damaged cells and tissues take longer to become irreversibly damaged. Do not make the ambulance infinitely fast. Make pathology temporarily late.

That move belongs beside another DARPA program, Biochronicity, which treated biological timing itself as a discoverable control architecture: cell cycles, metabolism, growth, aging, death. And beside ADAPTER, which proposed an internal bioelectronic carrier able to release therapies on demand, including interventions intended to regulate disrupted circadian rhythms. And beside newer work trying to make sleep transitions and alertness more controllable under operational constraint.

None of these programs demonstrates command over biological time in the grand sense. Program ambition is not experimental success. A solicitation is not a capability. A benchtop result is not a medic carrying a pause button.

But the recurrence matters.

Defense research has spent years approaching time not merely as the dimension in which physiology happens, but as a variable physiology might be persuaded to surrender.

NASA reaches the same problem from the opposite direction. Mars is not bleeding out. It is simply far away. A crew must remain alive, psychologically functional, fed, protected, housed and metabolically expensive while physics takes months to deliver them. NASA-supported torpor studies have therefore examined whether hibernation biology might eventually reduce some of the human cost of transit. Current NASA material is explicit about the gap: mammalian hibernation has not yet been studied in microgravity, and synthetic human torpor for Mars remains a long-term possibility rather than an achieved technology.

This book is about that gap.

Not between reality and science fiction. Between several real biological clocks that medicine and evolution already know how to move, and the much harder proposition that an engineer can move them deliberately, reversibly, together, and safely.

A hibernating mammal does not merely sleep harder. Metabolism changes. Temperature changes. Cardiovascular behavior changes. Gene expression changes. Muscle and bone somehow survive conditions that injure an immobilized human. The organism executes a coordinated state transition that medicine cannot reproduce by simply turning down one knob.

That is the trap waiting for every story about suspended animation. The spectacle is unconsciousness. The technology is coordination.

If biological time is plural—circadian time, metabolic time, inflammatory time, proteostatic time, cell-cycle time, neural time—then 'slowing the body' may be the wrong abstraction. There may be no master clock to slow. There may only be a treaty among clocks.

And treaties fail when one party keeps moving.

The central question here is therefore narrower than immortality and more consequential than sleep optimization: **how much control over biological time is physically real, how much is engineering rhetoric, and what happens when institutions begin designing missions around the difference?**

We will keep four piles on the table throughout.

**Documented facts.** Public program records, peer-reviewed experiments, mission studies, clinical physiology, measured animal states.

**Credible but incomplete evidence.** Performer results or translational claims whose scope, replication, or operational relevance remains bounded.

**Disputed claims.** Assertions of broad human stasis, dramatic performance enhancement, or mature operational capability without adequate independent evidence.

**Open conjecture.** The synthesis that biological timing may become an engineering substrate comparable, in limited domains, to temperature, pressure or voltage.

The last pile is why the book exists. It is also the pile most likely to be wrong.

A useful thesis must be allowed to die.

If the timing programs reduce, after close inspection, to ordinary pharmacology and ordinary preservation with no transferable control principles; if torpor cannot be induced in humans without costs that erase mission advantage; if slowing one injury cascade merely allows another to dominate; if sleep architecture resists compression without cumulative cognitive or physiological debt; if biological clocks are too coupled, individual and context-dependent to become reliable control surfaces—then 'engineering biological time' is a metaphor, not a field.

That would still be worth knowing.

Because we are already spending money as though the clock has handles.
