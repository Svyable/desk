# The Handoff Test

The test begins with an absence.

The builder is unavailable.

Not busy.

Not on vacation.

Unavailable.

The phone number is dead.

The company is gone.

The principal investigator retired.

The maintainer moved on.

The official who negotiated the agreement is no longer in office.

The donor died.

The engineer who knew the workaround cannot be found.

A competent stranger arrives.

Now what?

This is the simplest way to expose whether a long-lived system is genuinely inheritable.

The stranger is not ignorant.

That distinction matters.

The stranger may be an excellent engineer, archivist, administrator, scientist, lawyer, maintainer, curator, operator, or public official.

The stranger has general competence.

What the stranger lacks is informal context.

The stranger was not in the room.

The stranger does not know which sentence in the manual exists because of an incident ten years ago.

The stranger does not know which supplier relationship depends on a personal friendship.

The stranger does not know why the founder always vetoed one kind of change.

The stranger cannot distinguish a deliberate constraint from an old workaround unless the system itself makes the difference legible.

That is the Handoff Test.

Could a competent stranger continue this without calling the builder?

The question sounds operational.

It is really six questions braided together.

What must persist?

What must remain knowable?

Who may act?

What resources arrive with the obligation?

Can the system be inspected and changed?

Can the successor decide not to continue it?

A system can pass five and fail the sixth.

That is why the stack matters.

## What is the inheritance?

Start with the artifact.

This is less obvious than it sounds.

What exactly are we trying to preserve?

The physical object?

The function?

The evidence?

The relationship?

The route?

The legal right?

The identity?

The option?

The institution?

A bridge may need to preserve transportation function and historical character while replacing most components over time.

A scientific study may need to preserve comparability and provenance while changing instruments.

A software system may need to preserve customer data and business rules while the original code deserves retirement.

A seed bank may need to preserve genetic diversity and metadata rather than the specific freezer or institution holding the samples today.

A constitution may need to preserve legitimate self-government while amendments transform parts of the original order.

A dam may deserve removal while the river becomes the deeper inheritance.

If the builder cannot answer what level matters, the successor will infer it from whatever is easiest to see.

That is how objects become sacred accidentally.

The artifact is visible.

The function may not be.

The first task of a handoff is therefore definition.

What must cross?

What may change?

What may disappear?

What would count as continuity even if the original material is gone?

What would count as failure even if the original object remains?

The answer should be concrete enough to guide a stranger.

“Preserve the mission” is too vague.

“Never change anything” is too rigid.

The useful middle says which properties carry value and which mechanisms are replaceable.

## What must the stranger know?

Now remove the founder’s memory.

What does the system stop knowing?

This is the memory layer.

A complete archive is not the goal.

The successor does not need every message the team ever sent.

The successor needs the state of the system and the reasons that constrain the next decision.

What is canonical?

What changed recently?

What is known to be fragile?

Which assumptions are active?

Which decisions were controversial?

Which repair is temporary?

Which dependency is near end of life?

Which record explains a strange rule?

Which measurements are comparable and which cross a methodology break?

Which obligations survive shutdown?

Where is the source of truth?

The most useful handoff packet is not necessarily large.

It is discoverable.

A stranger should be able to begin from a clear entry point and follow references outward as consequence increases.

A building might have an asset record, current condition assessment, drawings, major intervention history, and critical inspection schedule.

A codebase might have a README, architecture record, build instructions, release process, secrets succession plan, dependency inventory, tests, and known-risk list.

A research project might have the protocol history, data dictionary, consent constraints, specimen inventory, analysis provenance, and governance rules.

A trust might have the governing instrument, current assets, restrictions, appointment process, reporting obligations, and unresolved legal questions.

Different systems require different memory.

The test is the same.

Can the stranger reconstruct enough context to make the next consequential decision without inventing history?

If not, the system has context debt.

The debt may be survivable now.

It will become more expensive with every succession.

## Who gets to decide?

Knowledge does not create authority.

The stranger may understand the system perfectly and still be unable to act.

This is one of the most common handoff failures because founders confuse access with legitimacy.

The password is transferred.

The signing key is transferred.

The bank account is transferred.

The title is transferred.

The successor still lacks a recognized mandate.

Or the opposite happens.

The successor has the formal title and everyone continues treating the founder as the real authority.

A passing Handoff Test needs a source of power, a scope, a succession rule, and a revision rule.

Why may this person or institution act?

What decisions are inside that authority?

How does authority move again?

How can the governance structure itself change?

Then test behavior.

Give the successor a real decision before the founder leaves.

Let the incoming maintainer ship a release.

Let the future operator run the shift.

Let the deputy sign the agreement within delegated authority.

Let the new board handle a disputed decision.

Let the future scientist own a protocol change.

Watch who everyone waits for.

The hidden veto is the true authority map.

If the founder still has to rescue every consequential decision, the handoff has not occurred.

Authority needs rehearsal.

## What pays for the next obligation?

The system may be well designed, well documented, and legitimately governed.

Then the roof needs replacement.

The archive needs migration.

The spare part disappears.

The pension payment arrives.

The contaminated site needs remediation.

The decommissioning project begins.

Who pays?

This is the resources layer.

The mistake here is to ask whether money exists today.

The correct question is whether a credible financing path exists when the obligation matures.

What revenue supports stewardship?

What reserve is dedicated?

What assumptions determine adequacy?

How often are those assumptions revisited?

What happens if returns disappoint, costs rise, or the original revenue source disappears?

Who is responsible for increasing contributions?

What part of the obligation is unfunded?

What happens at retirement, not just operation?

The stranger should inherit a model, not merely a balance.

A large fund based on unrealistic assumptions is weak.

A smaller fund with transparent assumptions and a legitimate adjustment mechanism can be stronger.

The test is not whether the founder predicted the future price of every repair.

It is whether the successor can see the financial state and has authority to respond when reality differs from the prediction.

Resources are agency stored for later.

An unfunded obligation narrows the future’s choices.

## Can the stranger inspect and change it?

The next failure surface is repairability.

The system works today.

What happens when one component does not?

Can the stranger see the failure?

Can the stranger reach it?

Can the stranger obtain a replacement?

Can the stranger reproduce the interface?

Can the stranger rebuild the software?

Can the stranger migrate the data?

Can the stranger rotate credentials?

Can the stranger test the repaired system?

Which part depends on one vendor, one person, one obsolete tool, one proprietary file format, one authorization server, or one country’s supply chain?

This is where long-lived systems reveal their short-lived dependencies.

Every dependency has its own expiration clock.

The useful exercise is to list the dependencies expected to die sooner than the system.

Supplier.

Software version.

Cryptographic scheme.

Storage medium.

Battery chemistry.

Staff expertise.

Legal authority.

Communication protocol.

Replacement part.

Revenue source.

File format.

Institution.

Then ask what the receiving state is when each one ends.

Standard interface.

Alternative supplier.

Migration path.

Published specification.

Spare capacity.

Escrowed source.

Successor institution.

Safe shutdown.

There does not have to be a perfect replacement plan for every dependency.

There should be enough visibility that expiration does not arrive as a surprise embedded inside a larger system.

The strongest test is rehearsal.

Break something non-catastrophic on purpose.

Have the stranger repair it.

Restore the backup.

Rebuild from source.

Replace the sensor.

Use the spare part.

Fail over the authority.

Move the archive copy.

Run without the vendor.

The exercise reveals the hidden knowledge and hidden permissions no inventory can see.

## Can the successor say no?

This is the question that keeps the Handoff Test from becoming preservation ideology.

Suppose the stranger understands the system.

Has legitimate authority.

Has money.

Can repair it.

Must the stranger continue it?

No.

A passing handoff includes a way to make an informed decision about replacement or retirement.

What obligations survive shutdown?

What records must remain?

What users need migration?

What physical effects require remediation?

Which rights have to be transferred?

Which dependencies should be unwound?

What purpose might be preserved through a different mechanism?

What evidence would justify ending the system?

A successor who cannot retire an obsolete system has inherited captivity, not stewardship.

The right to disagree is therefore part of the handoff stack even though it is not a physical layer.

Agency crosses all the layers.

The future can reinterpret the artifact.

Correct the memory.

Revise the governance.

Reallocate resources.

Replace the mechanism.

End the mission.

Founders will find some of these choices painful.

That is a sign the future is real.

## Run the test at ordinary scale

The phrase “hundred-year handoff” can make the framework sound grander than it is.

Do not start with a century.

Start with Tuesday.

One person takes leave.

Can another run the system?

A maintainer rotates off the project.

Can the next one release safely?

A finance lead changes roles.

Can someone else explain the reserve assumptions?

A lab technician retires.

Can another reproduce the procedure?

A city engineer leaves.

Can the next engineer find the condition history of the asset?

A founder stops answering messages for a month.

Does the organization keep making decisions?

The century is built out of ordinary transfers.

Every failed small handoff is a warning about the long one.

Every successful small handoff increases institutional memory and successor confidence.

The best time to run the Handoff Test is while the builder is still available to observe failure.

The founder can watch the stranger struggle.

Not to rescue the stranger immediately.

To discover what the system forgot to carry.

The manual assumes a tool nobody listed.

The access process depends on an undocumented email alias.

The financial model assumes a subsidy nobody described.

The archive index points to a server being retired.

The backup restoration requires a key stored on one laptop.

The authority document says the deputy can approve, but the bank does not recognize the deputy’s signature.

The test turns hidden context into visible work.

Then repair the handoff.

Not by making the stranger memorize the founder.

By changing the system so the next stranger has less to reconstruct.

That is the governing metric.

The goal is not zero dependence on people.

People are the system.

The goal is to reduce dependence on irreplaceable people.

Not zero tacit knowledge.

Some skills require practice.

The goal is to create a path for practice to transfer.

Not infinite documentation.

The goal is enough memory to make important state discoverable.

Not permanent funding certainty.

The goal is visible assumptions and a mechanism for adaptation.

Not total repairability.

The goal is a level of repair and migration appropriate to the expected life and consequence.

Not immortality.

The goal is an intelligent next decision.

The Handoff Test ends where it began.

The builder is unavailable.

The stranger arrives.

The system has to answer for itself.

What am I?

What happened to me?

Who may decide?

What resources remain?

How can I be changed?

Do I still deserve to continue?

If the system can answer those questions well enough for a competent stranger to act, the handoff is not complete forever.

Nothing is.

It is complete enough for the next successor.

That is the only century-scale continuity civilization ever actually gets.
