# Back Matter

## Notes on Sources and Method

*The Hundred-Year Handoff* is a cross-domain argument about succession. It uses cases from infrastructure, archives, constitutional systems, long-running science, standards, biological collections, hazardous-site stewardship, software, and institutional governance to test one narrower claim: systems expected to outlive their builders need more than durable artifacts. They need ways to carry forward usable memory, legitimate authority, resources for stewardship, practical repairability, and successor agency.

The cases are not offered as proof that longevity is inherently good. Several chapters deliberately test the opposite proposition. Long-lived systems can survive by accident, lock-in, political power, wealth, path dependence, or repeated rescue. Documentation can become noise. Repairability can increase cost or risk. Governance constraints can become dead-hand control. A successor can rationally replace or retire an inherited system.

The research standard for the manuscript is therefore comparative rather than celebratory. Wherever possible, factual claims are tied to primary, official, regulatory, judicial, institutional, or other authoritative sources. Current programs, laws, standards, and institutional policies should be refreshed before a released edition is snapshotted.

The working research trail in Sven Hardy Benson’s Desk contains the source ledger, chapter evidence notes, research spine, and falsification boundaries used during drafting. Those working materials remain separate from the reading text so the manuscript can stay readable while its claims remain auditable.

## The Handoff Stack

The book’s core model has six interacting elements:

**Artifact.** What must physically, technically, legally, or functionally persist—and what may be replaced?

**Memory.** What state, provenance, rationale, and history must remain discoverable so a successor can understand what was inherited?

**Authority.** Who is legitimately allowed to operate, alter, transfer, or retire the inheritance, and how does that authority itself pass on?

**Resources.** What pays for stewardship after launch incentives and original revenue sources fade?

**Repairability.** Can a successor inspect, maintain, reproduce, migrate, or replace critical elements without dependence on an irreplaceable builder or supplier?

**Agency.** Can the successor disagree intelligently, including changing the mechanism, revising the mission, or retiring the inheritance?

The stack is diagnostic, not a checklist that rewards maximal durability. Different systems deserve different horizons and different levels of handoff architecture.

## The Competent-Stranger Test

The practical question behind the book is:

**Could a competent stranger continue this without calling the builder?**

The stranger may possess the relevant professional skill but none of the founder’s informal context. A system passes only provisionally: well enough for the next steward to understand its state, exercise legitimate judgment, perform necessary work, and leave another workable handoff behind.

For systems that may deserve retirement, the stronger form is:

**Could a competent stranger end this well?**

That means preserving necessary records, migrating users or beneficiaries, transferring rights and obligations, remediating physical consequences, and distinguishing what should survive from what should not.

## Falsification Boundaries

The book should be revised if evidence shows that its framework systematically rewards longevity for its own sake; treats documentation volume as equivalent to usable memory; confuses formal ownership with legitimate authority; assumes dedicated funds guarantee future adequacy; treats openness as automatically safe or maintainable; or labels adaptation and retirement as failures of stewardship.

The framework is useful only if it helps distinguish:

- preservation from lock-in;
- useful context from record hoarding;
- legitimate continuity from dead-hand control;
- resourced stewardship from nominal funding;
- repairability from unsafe modification;
- adaptive succession from careless drift;
- deliberate retirement from abandonment.

## A Note on Time Horizons

The title uses a century because a hundred years is long enough to make founder absence unavoidable while still being relevant to bridges, institutions, archives, standards, scientific programs, forests, utilities, legal arrangements, and public infrastructure. The framework is intentionally scale-independent. Many of its most useful tests happen at the next staff change, vendor transition, retirement, software migration, board succession, or major maintenance cycle.

A century is crossed through ordinary handoffs.
