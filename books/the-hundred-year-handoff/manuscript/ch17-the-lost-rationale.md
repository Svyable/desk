# The Lost Rationale

The rule survives.

The reason does not.

This creates two opposite mistakes.

The successor removes a safeguard because it looks pointless.

Or the successor preserves an obsolete rule because nobody can prove it is safe to change.

Both are failures of memory.

The first destroys useful inheritance.

The second traps the future inside an inheritance that no longer deserves to govern it.

This is the problem of lost rationale.

Organizations are full of decisions whose visible surface outlives their explanation.

A valve is installed in an awkward location.

A report requires two signatures.

A software service refuses a particular kind of input.

A road has a strange curve.

A machine has a maintenance interval that seems too conservative.

A contract contains a clause nobody likes.

A laboratory protocol requires a step that appears redundant.

A building keeps an apparently useless access space.

A database stores a field that no current screen displays.

The successor sees friction.

The founder saw a reason.

Sometimes the founder was right.

Sometimes the founder was wrong.

Sometimes the founder was right under conditions that no longer exist.

The future cannot tell which from the artifact alone.

That is context debt reaching maturity.

The present accumulates context debt whenever it records what was decided without recording enough of why.

At first, the debt feels harmless.

Everyone remembers.

The engineer who moved the valve is still down the hall.

The lawyer who insisted on the clause is still on the call.

The manager who added the second signature remembers the fraud incident that prompted it.

The programmer who rejected that input remembers the data corruption bug.

The team can ask.

Then the people leave.

The decision remains embedded in the system.

The successor inherits the cost without inheriting the evidence.

This is when the rule begins to look irrational.

The classic warning is often summarized through the story of a fence whose purpose is unknown: do not remove it until you understand why it was built.

The intuition is useful.

The slogan is incomplete.

A future organization can spend decades worshiping fences whose purposes expired long ago.

The correct principle is not “never remove what you do not understand.”

It is “recover enough rationale to distinguish a safeguard from residue.”

That is a harder discipline.

Rationale is not the same as explanation.

People can explain almost anything after the fact.

A durable rationale record should connect a decision to evidence, constraints, alternatives, and assumptions that can later be tested.

We chose material A because material B failed under this temperature range.

We require a second signature because one person previously had the ability to initiate and approve the same payment.

We keep this clearance because maintenance access requires a human body and tool to reach the joint.

We preserve this field because it is part of the legal record used during appeals.

We reject this input because downstream equipment cannot safely handle a value above this threshold.

We use this sampling interval because faster collection changed participant behavior and slower collection missed the effect.

Each explanation gives the successor something to interrogate.

Is the temperature range still relevant?

Has the payment system changed?

Can the joint now be reached another way?

Did the law change?

Was the downstream equipment replaced?

Does new evidence support a different sampling interval?

A good rationale record does not freeze the decision.

It gives the successor the ability to change it intelligently.

This is why decision logs can be more valuable than procedure manuals.

A procedure manual says what to do.

A decision record says what problem the procedure was solving.

The difference becomes decisive when the environment changes.

If the successor knows only the procedure, the successor has two weak options.

Obey it literally.

Discard it intuitively.

If the successor knows the rationale, a third option appears.

Preserve the function while changing the mechanism.

That is living continuity.

The same distinction appears in safety engineering.

A safety system often contains conservative limits, interlocks, separation rules, inspection requirements, and redundancies whose value is difficult to observe during normal operation.

When they work, nothing dramatic happens.

That makes them vulnerable to efficiency campaigns.

A new team sees a delay.

A redundant check.

An idle component.

A margin that appears unused.

A process that has not caught a problem in years.

The absence of failure can be misread as evidence the protection was unnecessary.

Sometimes it is evidence the protection worked.

This is one of the most dangerous epistemic traps in long-lived systems.

Success erases evidence of the avoided failure.

The successor needs some way to see the counterfactual.

Why is the interlock here?

What accident or hazard analysis justified it?

Which failure mode does the redundant pump cover?

Why is this inspection interval shorter than ordinary wear would suggest?

Why do two roles have to approve the action?

What assumption would make this requirement obsolete?

Without that information, the future is tempted to optimize away invisible protection.

The opposite failure is more common than organizations admit.

A rule remains because nobody knows why it exists.

People assume someone once had a good reason.

The uncertainty becomes a reason not to change.

Years pass.

The procedure accumulates around the old decision.

New systems are built to accommodate it.

Training incorporates it.

Audits begin checking for it.

The original rationale disappears completely.

Now the rule has acquired institutional mass.

It cannot be removed because too many other processes expect it.

This is rationale fossilization.

The organization is no longer preserving a known purpose.

It is preserving the cost of not knowing.

That can become enormous.

A legacy software field remains because nobody knows which downstream report depends on it.

A form asks for information because an old database required the column.

A committee approves a decision because the workflow was designed before automated controls existed.

A building standard preserves a dimensional assumption tied to machinery no longer used.

A procurement rule protects against a market structure that disappeared twenty years ago.

The rule is not defended by evidence.

It is defended by uncertainty.

This is context debt charging interest.

The longer the rationale is lost, the more expensive recovery becomes.

A successor has to reconstruct history from old emails, code, drawings, meeting minutes, regulatory records, incident reports, and the memories of people who may barely remember.

Sometimes the reconstruction succeeds.

Sometimes the organization gives up and keeps the rule.

Sometimes it removes the rule and discovers too late what it was doing.

The obvious response is to document every decision.

That creates another problem.

Infinite rationale is unusable rationale.

A team that records every conversation, every alternative, every assumption, every chat message, every ticket, every meeting, and every intermediate thought can produce a perfect haystack with no discoverable needle.

The successor needs compression.

What was the decision?

What problem was it solving?

What evidence mattered?

What alternatives were rejected?

What assumptions would cause us to revisit it?

Who had authority?

When should the decision expire or be reviewed?

That is enough for many ordinary decisions.

High-consequence systems need more.

The principle is proportionality.

Preserve rationale in proportion to the cost of future misunderstanding.

A trivial interface color does not need a ten-page decision record.

A hidden safety boundary probably does.

A one-week marketing experiment does not need archival permanence.

A permanent restriction on a public asset may.

A reversible software choice can tolerate thin rationale.

An irreversible environmental intervention deserves much more.

The artifact itself can help carry rationale.

Good design makes constraints visible.

A warning label explains a hazard.

A code comment explains why the strange branch exists.

A drawing note identifies a no-drill zone.

A maintenance panel gives access where future work is expected.

A schema marks a field as legally required rather than merely legacy.

A physical barrier shows that access is intentionally limited.

These embedded signals reduce the distance between rule and reason.

They are not enough by themselves.

Labels disappear.

Comments become stale.

Warnings become ritual.

But they give the successor a clue that the visible inconvenience has history.

Rationale should also have an expiration mechanism.

This sounds paradoxical.

Why preserve a reason and then plan to challenge it?

Because the purpose of memory is not obedience.

It is informed agency.

A decision record that says “review if supplier X is no longer the only certified source” gives the future a trigger.

A policy that says “this control exists because the current payment platform lacks role separation” tells the future when redesign could remove the control.

A maintenance interval tied to a known material property can be revisited if the material changes.

A legal restriction tied to a statutory requirement can be revisited if the law changes.

The rationale contains its own possible ending.

That is mature stewardship.

The founder is saying, “Here is why we did this. If the world becomes different in this specific way, do not confuse our mechanism with our purpose.”

This is one of the best gifts a builder can leave.

It reduces both reckless change and superstitious continuity.

There is still a social problem.

Organizations tell stories about themselves.

A procedure created because of an embarrassing failure may later be described as best practice.

A founder preference may be retold as a customer requirement.

A political compromise may be retold as an engineering necessity.

A temporary workaround may become part of institutional identity.

The surviving rationale can itself be false.

Memory is editable.

That is why evidence matters.

When the consequence is large, the successor should be able to trace the rationale beyond legend.

Incident report.

Test result.

Court decision.

Regulatory requirement.

Failure analysis.

Customer contract.

Scientific measurement.

Design calculation.

The record does not have to preserve every detail.

It should preserve enough provenance to distinguish “we have always done this” from “this control responds to a documented hazard.”

The distinction can change a decision.

This is also where disagreement becomes productive.

A successor who questions an inherited rule is not necessarily disrespecting the past.

The question may be evidence that the handoff worked.

The successor has enough agency to interrogate the inheritance.

A founder who leaves rationale is inviting that interrogation.

Here is what we knew.

Here is what we feared.

Here is what we tried.

Here is what failed.

Here is what we could not know.

Now you decide.

That is very different from leaving a command.

The hundred-year handoff cannot preserve all reasons forever.

Language changes.

Records are lost.

Assumptions become so alien that explanation itself becomes difficult.

Some rationale will disappear.

The practical goal is to slow the loss around decisions whose future misunderstanding would be expensive.

The competent stranger test becomes specific.

A stranger finds a strange rule.

Can the stranger discover why it exists?

Can the stranger find evidence supporting that explanation?

Can the stranger identify which assumptions still hold?

Can the stranger tell whether the mechanism is essential or merely historical?

Can the stranger change the mechanism without silently discarding the purpose?

Can the stranger remove the rule when the purpose no longer deserves protection?

If yes, the rationale crossed the handoff.

If no, the future is left to choose between superstition and amnesia.

Neither is stewardship.
