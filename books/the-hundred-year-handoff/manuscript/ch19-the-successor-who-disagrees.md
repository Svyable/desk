# The Successor Who Disagrees

The successful handoff ended with two dams disappearing.

That sounds like a contradiction.

For most of the twentieth century, the Elwha and Glines Canyon dams stood on the Elwha River in Washington’s Olympic Peninsula. They generated electricity and became part of the region’s inherited infrastructure. They also blocked salmon from most of the river’s habitat and transformed an ecosystem central to the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe.

Later generations did not merely inherit the dams.

They inherited the question of whether the dams should remain.

Congress authorized restoration of the Elwha River ecosystem in 1992. Removal began in 2011. By 2014, both dams were gone.

The successor disagreed with the inheritance.

And acted legitimately.

This is the chapter the handoff framework needs in order to remain honest.

If every successful handoff ends in preservation, the framework becomes a philosophy of dead-hand control.

The future is reduced to maintenance staff for the past.

That is not stewardship.

Stewardship includes the authority to conclude that an inherited artifact no longer deserves continuity.

The difficult part is distinguishing disagreement from neglect.

A successor can destroy value carelessly.

Can remove a safeguard without understanding it.

Can sell a public asset for short-term revenue.

Can erase records that become inconvenient.

Can dismantle a scientific program because its benefits are politically invisible.

Can abandon a structure whose maintenance costs were exaggerated.

Can reject a founder’s mission out of vanity.

“Future agency” cannot mean every later preference is wise.

The point is narrower.

The successor must have a legitimate path to reconsider the inheritance using evidence the founder could not possess.

The Elwha case makes the distinction unusually clear because the dams’ effects were not hidden.

The dams provided benefits.

They also imposed costs.

The political and ecological valuation of those costs changed over time.

Legal rights and obligations changed.

Scientific understanding expanded.

The restoration project required years of study, legislation, acquisition, planning, engineering, sediment analysis, infrastructure adaptation, and ecological work.

This was not a future generation casually deleting the past.

It was a successor institution making a different decision after the inheritance had been made legible enough to contest.

The result was not preservation of the artifact.

It was preservation and restoration of a different function: a connected river ecosystem and fish passage.

This teaches a powerful handoff principle.

The thing that should cross time may not be the thing the founder built.

The founder built a dam to produce power.

A successor may decide the more valuable inheritance is the river.

A founder builds a factory.

A successor may preserve the industrial building and change the use.

A founder creates a trust.

A successor court may modify an administrative restriction to protect the charitable purpose.

A founder creates a software system.

A successor may migrate the data and retire the code.

A city builds a highway.

A future city may remove or narrow it because the surrounding urban priorities changed.

A museum receives an object.

A successor may return it because the original acquisition was unjust.

A scientific classification survives for decades.

A later community may replace it because new evidence makes the old categories misleading.

Continuity can occur at a deeper layer than the artifact.

This is why the handoff test begins by asking what must persist rather than assuming the object must persist.

Sometimes the function matters.

Sometimes the evidence matters.

Sometimes the rights matter.

Sometimes the ecological system matters.

Sometimes the option to reverse matters.

Sometimes nothing about the original mechanism deserves continuation except the record of what happened.

The founder should not get to answer that question forever.

This is hard for builders because building creates attachment.

A person who creates an institution naturally imagines its continued existence as success.

A person who writes software imagines future users running the software.

A person who constructs infrastructure imagines future generations using the infrastructure.

A donor imagines the gift serving the donor’s chosen purpose.

A policymaker imagines the policy being vindicated by duration.

The builder’s perspective equates survival with proof.

But some systems survive because removing them is expensive.

Some survive because authority is fragmented.

Some survive because nobody remembers the alternative.

Some survive because maintenance creates a constituency.

Some survive because the cost is imposed on people with less political power.

Some survive because the founder made change legally difficult.

Longevity can be evidence of value.

It can also be evidence of lock-in.

The handoff framework has to distinguish them.

One useful test is to ask whether the successor can say no without destroying unrelated value.

A tightly coupled system makes disagreement expensive.

A city may know an old software platform should be replaced but discover that every department shares the same undocumented database.

A company may want to leave a vendor but find that data export loses critical history.

A region may want to remove obsolete infrastructure but find that water, roads, utilities, and property expectations were built around it.

A family trust may contain a restriction whose legal removal requires years of litigation.

A successor technically has agency.

The inheritance has made exercising agency extraordinarily costly.

This is another form of dead-hand control.

It does not require a legal prohibition.

Architecture can bind the future through switching cost.

A good handoff therefore creates reversibility where reversibility is plausible.

Data can be exported.

Components can be separated.

Rights can be transferred.

A mission can be distinguished from the mechanism chosen to pursue it.

A decommissioning path exists.

A standard has versioning and migration.

A building can be adapted.

A governance system has amendment.

Not every system can be reversible.

A dam changes a river even after removal.

A mine changes geology.

A city street pattern can shape development for centuries.

A species extinction cannot be reversed by a later board vote.

A child’s lost year cannot be restored.

A contaminated aquifer may require generations of remediation.

Irreversibility is precisely why successor agency should be considered before the irreversible decision is made.

The unmet user has no seat at the founding meeting.

The current generation can at least avoid taking away more of that user’s future choices than the project requires.

This does not mean optimizing for maximum optionality.

Commitment creates value too.

A bridge needs a location.

A constitution needs rules that cannot change every afternoon.

A scientific study needs a stable enough protocol to produce a time series.

A standards ecosystem needs compatibility promises.

A city needs infrastructure people can plan around.

A person building for the future cannot leave every decision open.

The goal is not refusal to commit.

It is clarity about what the commitment binds.

This is where the distinction between mission and mechanism becomes useful.

A founder may want to preserve education.

The founder does not know what classroom technology will exist in seventy years.

A public institution may be charged with providing reliable water.

It need not preserve every original pipe.

A scientific archive may exist to preserve evidence.

It need not preserve every software platform used to store the evidence.

A conservation program may seek ecological health.

It need not preserve every intervention forever.

A company may promise customers access to their records.

It need not keep the same application running if a better migration preserves the promise.

Mission can survive mechanism disagreement.

Sometimes the mission itself should be rejected.

That is harder.

A founder can create an institution around values later generations conclude were unjust.

A law can encode exclusion.

A collection can embody dispossession.

A public monument can celebrate a political order the present community no longer wants to honor.

A scientific program can rely on practices later understood as unethical.

A future generation should not be forced to preserve the mission simply because the mission was once formalized.

This is the deepest limit on founder intent.

The future is morally real.

Its people are not just beneficiaries of the founder’s foresight.

They are political and ethical agents with claims of their own.

This is why legitimate procedure matters so much.

If disagreement is inevitable, the handoff should leave a way to disagree that is better than neglect, sabotage, or rupture.

Amendment.

Judicial review.

Public process.

Technical deprecation.

Asset retirement plans.

Restitution procedures.

Sunset clauses.

Review dates.

Elections.

Forks.

Appeals.

These are not weaknesses in continuity.

They are pressure-release valves.

A system that cannot change peacefully raises the cost of every future disagreement.

Eventually the only way to change it may be to break it.

A good handoff makes disagreement ordinary enough that the inheritance does not have to become sacred or disposable.

The Elwha dams also show that retirement has its own maintenance burden.

Removing a large inherited system is not the opposite of stewardship.

It can require more stewardship than leaving it alone.

Sediment has to be understood.

Water systems may need protection or replacement.

Infrastructure downstream has to be considered.

Ecological restoration continues after the concrete is gone.

Monitoring continues.

The successor who says no still inherits responsibility for the transition.

This matters because “just remove it” can be as shallow as “preserve it forever.”

The old artifact is embedded in a present system.

People depend on it.

Habitats adapted around it.

Contracts assume it.

Jobs depend on it.

Records refer to it.

Removal changes distribution of benefits and costs.

A legitimate retirement acknowledges those dependencies instead of pretending the old system can vanish without consequence.

The same is true in organizations.

Retiring a legacy software system requires migration.

Closing a program requires handling beneficiaries.

Ending a scientific study requires archiving data and honoring participant obligations.

Dissolving a nonprofit requires transferring restricted assets.

Replacing an old standard requires compatibility planning.

Changing a constitution requires a legitimate transition of authority.

Retirement is a handoff to a world without the old mechanism.

That world has to be built too.

This suggests a useful addition to the competent stranger test.

Could a competent stranger not only continue this, but end it well?

Can the stranger identify which obligations survive shutdown?

Can the stranger preserve important records?

Can the stranger move users or beneficiaries safely?

Can the stranger remediate physical effects?

Can the stranger transfer authority?

Can the stranger tell what should remain after the artifact is gone?

A system without an answer is more permanent than its builders may realize.

The successful hundred-year handoff is therefore not a promise of hundred-year survival.

It is a promise of hundred-year intelligibility and agency.

The successor can understand what was built.

Can maintain it if it remains useful.

Can change it if the environment changes.

Can reject it if the mission no longer deserves continuation.

Can retire it without abandoning the consequences.

That is the opposite of legacy worship.

It is trust.

The builder admits that the future may know something the builder does not.

May value something differently.

May possess better technology.

May see a harm the builder discounted.

May inherit a constraint the builder never experienced.

May simply disagree.

A durable handoff leaves room for that disagreement to become stewardship rather than rebellion.

The Elwha River did not receive the dams forever merely because one generation built them.

Later generations received something harder.

The responsibility to decide again.
