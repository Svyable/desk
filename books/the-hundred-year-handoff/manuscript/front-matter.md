# The Hundred-Year Handoff

*How Civilization Builds for People It Will Never Meet*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

© 2026 Sven Hardy Benson. All Rights Reserved.

---

There is a peculiar kind of customer who cannot attend the design meeting.

They cannot approve the budget, complain about the interface, or tell you what language they speak. You do not know which tools they will possess, which institutions they will trust, which laws they will live under, or whether the organization building the thing will still exist when they arrive.

They may not be born for a hundred years. Or a thousand. Or ten thousand.

Civilization builds for these people anyway.

We bury radioactive waste beneath the desert and try to leave warnings for societies whose symbols we cannot know. We preserve seed collections for crop failures nobody can schedule. We pour concrete for bridges expected to carry the grandchildren of today's drivers. We keep court files, land records, scientific observations, maps, source code, standards, constitutions, museum collections, forests, dams, tunnels, pension promises, and public institutions because somebody after us is supposed to receive more than rubble.

The ordinary word for this is durability. It is not enough.

A thing can last and still fail its future user. Stone can survive after an inscription becomes meaningless. An archive can survive after its index is lost. Software can survive after nobody knows how to build it. A bridge can remain standing while deferred maintenance turns the next repair into an emergency. A rule can remain perfectly legible while the world around it changes enough to make literal obedience absurd.

Longevity is partly a material property. Continuity is a social achievement.

That distinction is the subject of this book.

A long-lived system is not one object moving untouched through time. It is a sequence of handoffs. One generation builds; another maintains; another interprets; another repairs; another inherits the bill; another decides whether the thing is still worth keeping.

Sometimes the chain is obvious. A construction crew hands a building to an operator. A retiring scientist trains a younger researcher. A public official transfers records to an archive. A software maintainer writes a release note and leaves a repository behind.

Sometimes the chain disappears inside the language of permanence.

We say a constitution has lasted two centuries, but paper did not govern for two centuries. People kept teaching the text, arguing about it, staffing courts, conducting elections, preserving interpretations, amending parts of the legal order, and accepting enough continuity for the next transfer of authority to occur.

We say a bridge lasted a hundred years, but steel and concrete did not perform a century of inspection, painting, drainage, resurfacing, load management, repair, financing, and record keeping by themselves.

We say an archive preserved the past, but shelves did not decide what to keep, describe what survived, migrate formats, stabilize paper, document provenance, control access, or explain why a future reader should trust the record.

Every durable thing has invisible successors inside it. The builders are only the first shift.

That creates a design problem because the successors are absent from the room. Present users vote with money, complaints, elections, lawsuits, usage, prestige, and attention. Future users send no signal. They cannot reject an unrepairable detail, negotiate a larger maintenance reserve, object to a data format that will become unreadable, or tell a scientist which metadata will matter to a question nobody has thought to ask.

The unborn are the ultimate non-participants in their own procurement process. They inherit the result anyway.

This book calls that transfer the hundred-year handoff. The phrase does not mean every case lasts exactly a century. Some handoffs happen in five years; others must cross millennia. A century is useful because it destroys the fantasy that the builder will still be around to explain.

At one hundred years, the informal Slack thread is gone. The executive who said, “Everyone knows why we did it this way,” is gone. The engineer who could hear a machine and know which bearing was unhappy is gone. The lawyer who remembered the political compromise behind an awkward sentence is gone. The scientist who knew why one anomalous run was kept and another discarded is gone. The maintainer who carried half the build system in her head is gone.

What remains has to work without access to the people who made it obvious.

The first chapter begins in southeastern New Mexico, where the United States has had to think about this problem at an almost absurd scale. The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant disposes of defense-related transuranic radioactive waste in deep salt formations, inside a regulatory horizon of ten thousand years after closure.

Ten thousand years is not a planning cycle. It is an assault on ordinary assumptions. Most modern languages, states, borders, currencies, technologies, and institutions do not survive even a fraction of it in recognizable form. Beneath the nuclear engineering sits a practical question:

How do you tell people in the far future not to dig here?

The Department of Energy has studied passive institutional controls for the site: physical markers, records, archives, maps, and other ways of carrying knowledge beyond the period in which guards, agencies, and active management can be treated as the whole solution. The work asks what materials might remain, how warnings could survive language change, whether valuable components would be stolen or recycled, and how several layers of information might outlast different forms of social disruption.

This is often told as one of the strangest design exercises in modern history. It is stranger when you notice that WIPP is an exaggerated version of an ordinary problem.

Every serious handoff faces familiar families of failure. The carrier can decay. The language can drift. The institution can disappear. The incentive can reverse. The money can vanish. The original rationale can become folklore. The archive can become so large that the important record is harder to find than if less had been saved. The artifact can be preserved so rigidly that future people cannot adapt it to changed conditions.

The chain can break even when each individual link once looked reasonable.

The answer is not permanence. Permanence can be its own failure. People who inherit a bridge should be allowed to replace it. People who inherit software should be able to change it. People who inherit an archive should be able to add context. People who inherit an institution should be able to reform or end it.

The future needs continuity and agency at the same time.

Preservation asks whether we can keep something. Handoff asks whether successors can understand what they received, operate it, question it, repair it, pay for it, and change it without first rediscovering everything we already learned.

That question crosses departments that rarely speak to one another. Nuclear waste looks like environmental engineering. A cathedral looks like architecture and religion. An archive looks like librarianship. A constitution looks like law. A seed vault looks like agriculture. A bridge looks like civil engineering. A codebase looks like software. A pension looks like finance.

They are different in almost every important way. Each still has to solve some version of succession.

What exactly is being handed over? Where is the memory stored? Who has authority after the original authority disappears? What resources accompany the obligation? Can a successor inspect and repair the thing? Which parts should remain stable, and which must remain revisable? How does a future steward distinguish an intentional feature from an accident nobody cleaned up? What happens when the successor wants a different future?

The middle of this book develops those questions into a handoff stack.

The **artifact** is what physically or digitally persists. The **memory** is what lets successors understand state, provenance, rationale, and risk. The **authority** is the legitimate power to operate, interpret, modify, or retire what has been inherited. The **money** is the stream of resources that makes stewardship possible after the excitement and revenue of construction have faded. **Repairability** is the practical ability to inspect, maintain, reproduce, replace, or adapt critical parts.

A weak layer can break the transfer. A beautifully documented bridge with no maintenance budget still deteriorates. A well-funded archive with no provenance can preserve misinformation very efficiently. A technically repairable system with no legitimate authority to alter it can freeze. An institution with clear authority but no memory of why its safeguards exist can confidently remove the wrong thing.

The hardest layer is not listed separately because it belongs to all the others: the successor is not your employee.

They do not owe you obedience merely because you arrived first. The goal of a handoff cannot be perfect replication of the builder's intent. If it were, every inheritance would become a command from the dead. The goal is to transfer enough context and capability that future people can make an informed decision of their own.

That may include deciding that your work is finished.

A bridge may be replaced. A standard may be superseded. A scientific protocol may improve. A codebase may be rewritten. A warning may be translated into a language the designers never imagined. An institution may complete its mission and close.

The most successful century-scale systems may be the ones that keep handing forward the ability to change.

That is the wager of this book. The future does not need us to be immortal. It needs work that can survive the disappearance of our explanation.
