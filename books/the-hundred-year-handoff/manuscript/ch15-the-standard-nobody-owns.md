# The Standard Nobody Owns

The letter A is not a product.

It does not belong to the company that made your phone.

It does not belong to the company that made your operating system.

It does not belong to the browser, the database, the font, or the messaging service.

Yet all of them need to agree on what A is.

That agreement has to survive product generations.

It has to survive companies.

It has to survive hardware architectures.

It has to survive programmers who were not alive when the earlier encoding choices were made.

This is what makes a standard an extraordinary handoff device.

A good standard carries meaning across organizational succession without requiring the original participants to remain in the room.

Unicode makes the mechanism unusually visible.

The Unicode Standard is continuously extended as new characters and scripts are encoded. At the same time, the Unicode Consortium maintains explicit stability policies designed to protect existing data and implementations. Once a character is encoded, its code point is not casually moved or removed. Published versions remain fixed, while later versions expand under rules intended to preserve compatibility.

The standard changes.

The meaning has to remain stable enough that old text survives the change.

This is not immobility.

It is disciplined evolution.

The distinction matters because standards are often imagined as frozen agreements.

A committee meets.

A specification is published.

The world follows it.

The end.

Real standards live more like institutions.

Bugs are found.

Ambiguities appear.

New use cases emerge.

Old assumptions collide with new systems.

Implementers disagree.

Security concerns change.

New languages, technologies, or domains need representation.

The standard has to grow without destroying the installed base that made the standard valuable.

That is a handoff problem.

The successor inherits coordination created by people who may no longer participate.

The successor also inherits constraints.

A strange historical decision may now be embedded in billions of files.

A cleaner design is imaginable.

Changing it would break the world.

The standard has acquired path dependence.

This is where backward compatibility becomes a form of memory.

The old choice is not preserved because the old committee was infallible.

It is preserved because other systems built expectations around it.

The meaning escaped the document and entered the ecosystem.

That is what long-lived standards do.

They become infrastructure.

A standard is therefore larger than its text.

It includes the specification.

Reference data.

Test suites.

Implementations.

Governance.

Version histories.

Errata.

Conformance expectations.

Communities of practice.

Documents explaining edge cases.

Compatibility promises.

The companies that choose to keep supporting it.

The users whose stored data depend on it.

The standard is the relationship among these things.

This is why an abandoned specification can remain technically public and practically dead.

The PDF still exists.

Nobody tests new implementations.

No one resolves ambiguity.

The reference software no longer builds.

The certification program disappears.

The domain moves on.

The artifact survived.

The institution did not.

A living standard needs succession of authority.

Who may change it?

Who decides what counts as a correction rather than a breaking change?

Who can add new capability?

Who arbitrates when two implementers interpret a clause differently?

Who publishes the version everyone agrees is canonical?

Who can promise that a decision will remain stable enough for others to depend on it?

These questions sound procedural.

They are the reason standards can become durable.

A vendor specification can be excellent.

If one company controls it completely, every user inherits the company as a dependency.

The company may remain healthy for decades.

It may also be acquired, change strategy, raise licensing costs, end the product, or decide that backward compatibility is inconvenient.

A multi-stakeholder standard does not eliminate power.

It redistributes the succession problem.

The relevant institution has to continue representing enough implementers and users that its decisions remain meaningful.

This is why “nobody owns it” is not quite accurate.

Standards need custodians.

They need editors.

They need rights structures.

They need organizations.

They need authority.

The important feature is that practical meaning is not trapped inside one product lineage.

The successor can implement the agreement without buying the original machine.

That is temporal portability.

It resembles repairability.

An open, stable interface allows one component to disappear while the surrounding system continues.

A file can outlive the program that created it if other programs understand the format.

A network protocol can outlive a router model if other devices implement the protocol.

A character encoding can outlive a word processor if later systems preserve the mapping.

A measurement standard can outlive an instrument maker if the unit remains reproducible and agreed.

Standards compress handoff information into shared rules.

Instead of every successor learning every bilateral relationship, the successor learns the standard.

This is why standards can create extraordinary handoff bandwidth.

Imagine a city in which every electrical device used a unique plug.

Every repair would require knowledge of a product lineage.

Every building renovation would be archaeology.

Every new manufacturer would negotiate interfaces one by one.

A shared standard turns thousands of private histories into one public expectation.

The successor does not need to know who made the original socket.

The geometry tells the next device what relationship to expect.

That is coordination carried through time.

The power of a standard also creates a danger.

The more widely adopted the standard, the more expensive change becomes.

A bad decision can become durable precisely because everyone coordinated around it.

Backward compatibility protects users.

It can also preserve complexity.

Legacy encodings remain supported.

Old protocol behaviors remain because too much software depends on them.

Security weaknesses can be difficult to remove when interoperability requires contact with older systems.

A standard can become the technical version of constitutional dead-hand control.

The original choice constrains future designers who would never choose it fresh.

Good standards therefore need explicit rules about what is stable and what is allowed to evolve.

Unicode’s stability policies are interesting because they make this boundary legible.

Some properties receive strong guarantees because changing them would damage stored text or implementations.

Other aspects can evolve under specified procedures.

The institution does not promise that nothing will change.

It promises limits on change.

That is a more honest kind of durability.

The same principle can help any long-lived system.

Do not promise permanence for everything.

Identify the invariants successors need in order to depend on you.

Make those invariants unusually expensive to break.

Leave the rest adaptable.

This reduces future ambiguity.

A maintainer knows which behavior is contractual.

A user knows what stored data can rely on.

A later committee knows which changes require migration rather than ordinary revision.

The handoff gains a map of sacred and ordinary ground.

There is another reason standards survive.

They are reproduced by use.

A protocol nobody implements can disappear even if every document is preserved.

A language nobody speaks can be archived and still lose living fluency.

A craft specification nobody practices becomes historical evidence rather than an operating standard.

Use is a form of maintenance.

Every compatible implementation tests the agreement against reality.

Every conformance suite exposes ambiguity.

Every bug report adds memory.

Every new version forces the custodian to decide whether the past and future can still meet.

The standard stays alive because successors keep exercising it.

This is the same reason authority rehearsal matters.

A succession rule that is never used can hide a flaw.

A disaster recovery plan that is never tested can be fictional.

A standard implemented by many independent parties is continuously rehearsed.

Interoperability is a live audit.

If two independently built systems can still exchange meaning, the handoff is working.

Not perfectly.

Specifications leave room for interpretation.

Implementations contain bugs.

Dominant vendors can shape de facto behavior more strongly than committees.

Patent claims can complicate supposedly open standards.

Certification can become expensive enough to exclude smaller actors.

A standard can be formally open while practical compatibility depends on one proprietary reference implementation.

Governance can be captured.

Committee participation can favor organizations wealthy enough to send experts for years.

The handoff framework should not romanticize standards any more than it romanticizes constitutions.

Shared rules can carry shared power.

The relevant question is whether successors have a legitimate and practical way to understand, implement, challenge, and revise the rules.

The standard also has to survive collision with replacement.

Not every successful standard deserves eternal support.

Some should be deprecated.

Some should be replaced because their assumptions no longer fit.

The difficulty is creating a migration path that respects the installed base.

A clean break may be technically elegant and socially impossible.

A permanent compatibility layer may become a security or complexity burden.

The successor has to decide how much old coordination is worth preserving.

This is where versioning becomes governance.

Versioning says the old state existed.

The new state is different.

The relationship between them is documented.

Users can decide when to cross.

A good migration allows overlap long enough for the ecosystem to move without pretending everyone moves at once.

That is a handoff across versions.

The concept scales beyond technical standards.

Building codes.

Accounting rules.

Scientific nomenclature.

Clinical terminology.

Mapping coordinate systems.

Rail gauges.

Container dimensions.

Emergency radio procedures.

Date formats.

All carry coordination across time.

Their value is not that any specific rule is beautiful.

Their value is that strangers can meet inside the same expectation.

A standard is one of the ways civilization allows competent strangers to continue each other’s work.

The builder does not have to explain the interface personally.

The successor does not have to rediscover the agreement.

The shared rule stands between them.

That is why the most important property of a long-lived standard may not be technical elegance.

It may be the quality of its promises.

What will not change?

What may change?

Who decides?

How will we know?

What happens to old implementations?

Can independent successors still participate?

Can the ecosystem leave if the standard stops serving it?

A standard that answers those questions well becomes more than a document.

It becomes an intergenerational interface.

The original committee can disappear.

The products can disappear.

The companies can disappear.

If the agreement remains legible, implementable, governable, and revisable, the next stranger can still connect.

That is what crossed the handoff.
