# Proposal — The Hundred-Year Handoff

## Working title

**The Hundred-Year Handoff: How Civilization Builds for People It Will Never Meet**

## Author

Sven Hardy Benson

## One-sentence promise

A narrative nonfiction book about the hidden succession problem inside every long-lived system: how builders transfer artifacts, memory, authority, money, repairability, and freedom to adapt to successors they may never meet.

## The central inversion

We usually describe century-scale projects as problems of durability.

Durability is only one layer.

A bridge can survive while its inspection knowledge disappears. An archive can survive while its provenance becomes unreadable. A constitution can survive as text while the institutions that reproduce legitimate authority decay. A codebase can survive while its build chain vanishes. A nuclear marker can survive while its symbols lose meaning.

The stronger model is **handoff**.

A long-lived system is not one object persisting through time. It is a chain of successors repeatedly receiving, interpreting, maintaining, funding, modifying, and sometimes retiring what earlier people built.

## The handoff stack

The book develops five recurring layers:

1. **Artifact** — what physically or digitally persists.
2. **Memory** — state, rationale, provenance, records, measurements, and history.
3. **Authority** — who is legitimately allowed to operate, interpret, change, repair, transfer, or retire the system.
4. **Resources** — money, skills, tools, supply chains, and institutional capacity.
5. **Repairability** — practical access to inspect, reproduce, replace, migrate, and adapt critical parts.

A sixth principle runs across all five: **successor agency**. A good handoff does not bind future people to the founder's preferences. It transfers enough context and capability for informed change.

## Opening

Chapter 1 begins with the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant in southeastern New Mexico.

DOE's passive institutional-control program attempts to warn future generations about buried radioactive waste. Its conceptual marker design is meant to communicate the site's location, design, contents, and danger over a regulatory horizon of 10,000 years. Sandia's marker work brought together disciplines including linguistics, anthropology, archaeology, architecture, materials science, and engineering because physical persistence alone could not solve the problem. The message had to remain interpretable across cultural and linguistic change.

The case creates a near-perfect extreme version of the book's question: what must a builder transmit when the future user cannot be assumed to share the builder's language, institution, tools, incentives, or worldview?

## Reader promise

After the book, a reader should be able to look at any project intended to outlive its current team and ask:

- What exactly is being handed forward?
- Which knowledge exists only in people's heads?
- Which dependencies are shorter-lived than the system?
- Who inherits legitimate authority?
- What money travels with the obligation?
- Can successors inspect and repair the critical parts?
- What rationale must survive so later people can distinguish principle from accident?
- Which decisions should remain stable, and which must remain revisable?
- What happens after the founder, vendor, agency, language, file format, or institutional culture disappears?
- Could a competent stranger continue this without calling us?

## Structure

### Part I — The Unmet User

The opening section introduces the succession problem. WIPP supplies the deep-time case. Cathedrals show construction distributed across generations. The missing-builder chapter examines tacit knowledge. The half-life of instructions explores how language, format, dependency, and context decay at different speeds.

### Part II — The Handoff Stack

Five chapters isolate the layers: artifact, memory, authority, resources, and repairability. Each chapter pairs at least two domains so the mechanism is earned rather than asserted.

### Part III — Institutions Across Generations

The book moves into constitutions, archives, century-scale infrastructure, seed preservation, long-running science, and technical standards. The point is not that these institutions should be permanent. It is to see how continuity is reproduced without continuous access to founders.

### Part IV — Where Handoffs Break

The system can become orphaned, lose its rationale, accumulate deferred maintenance, or encounter a successor who legitimately rejects the original design. This section carries the strongest counterevidence to naive preservation.

### Part V — Building for the Unmet

The final chapters develop a practical Handoff Test and return to the central ethical constraint: the future is not the builder's employee. The objective is not permanent obedience. It is informed succession.

## Why this is different from adjacent Desk books

### The Delete Key

*The Delete Key* asks why civilization is structurally better at adding than removing and how retirement, revocation, archiving, and safe destruction can become first-class capabilities.

*The Hundred-Year Handoff* asks the opposite operational question: when something is intended to continue beyond its builders, how is stewardship transferred without losing meaning, authority, resources, or capacity to change?

Deletion may appear in one boundary case, but the book is about succession rather than removal.

### The Reliability Tax

*The Reliability Tax* studies redundancy, slack, reversibility, and alternative pathways as underpriced resilience capacity.

This book is about temporal transfer. Redundancy may help a handoff, but the central failure is not insufficient spare capacity. It is the loss of context and capability between generations of stewards.

### The Never Ending Intelligence

*The Never Ending Intelligence* concerns persistent intelligence as durable causal identity. This book concerns human and institutional succession around long-lived artifacts and obligations. It does not require persistent machine identity.

### Gatekeepers / The Delegation Machine

Authority appears here as one layer of succession. The book is not principally about access control or delegation. Its question is how authority remains legitimate and legible after the original grantor and original operator are gone.

## Tone

Narrative, concrete, non-sentimental. Avoid reverence for permanence. Avoid "think seven generations ahead" as a moral slogan. The interesting work is operational: what exactly crosses the handoff, how does it fail, what makes it repairable, and what rights belong to the successor?

The book should be comfortable showing that some inheritances should be ended. A handoff that cannot permit informed refusal is not stewardship; it is rule by the dead.

## Novelty discipline

There are extensive literatures on future generations, long-term stewardship, nuclear semiotics, archives, infrastructure maintenance, constitutional continuity, legacy planning, maintainable software, and intergenerational justice.

The project therefore must not claim that nobody has studied long-lived systems.

The proposed contribution is the synthesis of these domains as one **handoff architecture**: artifact, memory, authority, resources, repairability, and successor agency.

A September 2026 title/topic scan found no obvious general-audience trade nonfiction book organized around this complete cross-domain handoff stack under the title *The Hundred-Year Handoff*. That is a collision check, not proof of absolute historical novelty.
