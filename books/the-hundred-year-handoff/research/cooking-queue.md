# Cooking queue — The Hundred-Year Handoff

Part I is drafted. This queue now advances into the Handoff Stack.

## Next cluster: Chapters 5–9

### Chapter 5 — The Artifact

Research systems that remain continuous by replacing parts. Use at least one infrastructure case and one scientific/technical artifact. Distinguish original material, functional continuity, identity, and planned replacement. The chapter should make clear that "lasting" often means preserving interfaces while individual components are renewed.

### Chapter 6 — The Memory

Build from archives, configuration management, maintenance histories, scientific provenance, and decision logs. Find one case where excellent records made a later repair or investigation possible and one where record abundance created unusable noise. Develop the idea that the successor needs discoverable signal, not total capture.

### Chapter 7 — The Authority

Research succession of legitimate control in a standards body, trust/foundation, public institution, and constitutional setting. The central question is not simply who can act, but why later people recognize that actor's right to interpret, modify, or retire the inheritance.

### Chapter 8 — The Money

Research endowments, decommissioning trusts, infrastructure reserves, pensions, dedicated fees, and lifecycle budgeting. Find one apparently well-funded long obligation where assumptions later proved inadequate. The chapter should show that financing is a transfer mechanism, not a guarantee.

### Chapter 9 — The Right to Repair

Research inspection access, spare parts, open specifications, parts obsolescence, military sustainment, infrastructure maintenance, and software reproducibility. Carry the strongest safety/security/certification counterarguments. The chapter's claim should be about temporal sovereignty: whether the successor can act without a vanished original supplier.

## Institutional cluster: Chapters 10–15

### Chapter 10 — The Constitution You Inherit

Choose a constitutional mechanism with visible succession and adaptation. Avoid triumphalist longevity stories. Include amendment, interpretation, conflict, and the problem of dead-hand control.

### Chapter 11 — The Archive That Waits

Use NARA, Library of Congress, or equivalent primary sources. Focus on provenance, description, migration, access, privacy, and future questions that cannot be predicted at the moment of preservation.

### Chapter 12 — The Hundred-Year Bridge

Choose a century-scale bridge with good inspection and repair records. Treat design life, service life, component replacement, ownership, and maintenance funding as separate variables.

### Chapter 13 — The Seed Vault

Use Crop Trust, NordGen, Norwegian government, and FAO sources. Explain Svalbard as a backup node in a living distributed genebank system rather than a self-sufficient apocalypse freezer.

### Chapter 14 — Science After the Scientist

Select one longitudinal study or scientific time series that crossed multiple principal investigators. Trace methodology, calibration, data governance, funding, and succession.

### Chapter 15 — The Standard Nobody Owns

Use one protocol or standard whose meaning survived generations of products because specifications, governance, reference implementations, and compatibility remained active. Keep the temporal-continuity focus narrow enough not to duplicate *The Compatibility Dividend*.

## Failure cluster: Chapters 16–19

Find one strong case for each:

- useful system becomes ownerless;
- rationale disappears and a successor makes the wrong inference;
- maintenance is postponed across political or organizational generations;
- successor improves the outcome by rejecting or replacing the inherited design.

The last case is mandatory. Without it the book drifts into preservation ideology.

## Final synthesis: Chapters 20–21

Do not draft the final Handoff Test until at least fifteen evidence chapters exist.

Candidate test:

1. What must persist?
2. What must a competent stranger know?
3. Who inherits legitimate authority?
4. What resources cross the transfer?
5. Can the successor inspect and repair critical elements?
6. Which dependencies expire sooner than the system?
7. Can the successor change or retire the inheritance intelligently?

The final chapter returns to the WIPP problem, then shortens the horizon: the practical hundred-year handoff begins with the next maintainer and compounds through every successor after them.
