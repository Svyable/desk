## Premise

Some of civilization's hardest projects have a peculiar customer: a person who is not alive yet.

A nuclear repository must warn strangers ten thousand years from now. A bridge may be inspected by engineers whose grandparents have not been born. A seed vault preserves options for farmers facing disasters its founders will never see. Archives keep evidence for governments, courts, scientists, and families that do not yet know what questions they will ask. Constitutions, scientific observatories, standards, codebases, forests, pension systems, and public infrastructure all depend on chains of successors who inherit obligations they did not originate.

We usually describe these as problems of durability. That misses the harder part. **A long-lived system is a sequence of handoffs.** The object can survive while the meaning disappears. Instructions can survive while the reason for them is forgotten. Money can survive while authority fragments. Records can survive while nobody knows which ones matter. A project can remain physically intact and still fail because the next generation cannot understand, repair, govern, finance, or legitimately change what it inherited.

*The Hundred-Year Handoff* is about designing work for successors you will never meet. It follows the mechanics of intergenerational continuity across deep-time nuclear warnings, cathedrals, infrastructure, archives, constitutions, seed banks, long-running science, standards, software, and stewardship. The central claim is not that everything should last forever. It is that anything expected to outlive its builders should be designed for succession as deliberately as it is designed for use.
