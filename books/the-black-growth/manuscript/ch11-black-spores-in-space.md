# Black Spores in Space

The International Space Station has an ecology whether mission planners want one or not.

*Aspergillus niger* is among its recurring fungal occupants. Its dark spores are built for endurance: thick walls, low metabolic activity, pigment, dormancy. In a 2020 study, wild-type spores tolerated striking doses of X-rays and heavy ions. The comparison with a Mars mission is instructive. The experimental doses that drove major mortality were orders of magnitude above the roughly sub-gray cumulative dose expected for a human-scale round trip in the study’s cited scenario.

The important lesson is not that a fungus is immortal. It is that spacecraft sterilization assumptions borrowed from actively growing bacteria can fail against a spore.

Pigment is only part of that package. Dormancy reduces the number of active biochemical processes available to disrupt. Thick walls alter deposition and diffusion. DNA repair can wait for germination. Desiccation changes radical chemistry. An inert-looking particle may carry an ecosystem’s future through a radiation field that would devastate active tissue.

NASA and other space agencies now study melanized fungi not merely as contaminants but as models for radiation survival and possible biomaterials. Experiments exposed melanin-containing fungi outside the ISS for extended periods and recovered viable material. NASA summaries have noted that thin fungal layers produced small but measurable reductions in radiation, though the exact shielding value depends strongly on geometry, spectrum, thickness and how the comparison is made.

This brings an old Chernobyl question into engineering.

A living shield has possible advantages over lead or polyethylene that have nothing to do with exotic metabolism. It can grow from shipped inoculum and local feedstock. It can repair itself. It can conform to surfaces. It can produce pigment continuously. On Mars, where launched mass is expensive, a material that is biologically manufactured may outperform a denser passive shield economically even if its attenuation per centimeter is mediocre.

But living material also breathes, mutates, sheds spores and contaminates habitats. A shield that self-repairs is also a thing that escapes containment.

The black fungus in space therefore occupies the opposite end of the Chernobyl story. At the reactor, humans left and biology came in. In a spacecraft, humans may deliberately invite the biology inside.

We should know exactly what it is doing before we build the wall out of it.
