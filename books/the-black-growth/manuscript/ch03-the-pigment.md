# The Pigment That Refuses a Formula

“Melanin” sounds like a chemical in the way glucose is a chemical. It is not so obliging.

Melanins are heterogeneous, polymeric pigments formed through different biochemical routes and assembled into structures whose exact composition depends on organism, substrate and environment. Fungi can produce eumelanin-like pigments, DHN-melanin, DOPA-derived melanin and other dark polymers. The useful properties arise partly from this messiness: broad absorption, stable radical populations, redox activity, metal binding and physical integration into cell walls.

That is also why mechanistic claims are difficult. A pigment that absorbs across a wide electromagnetic spectrum can protect by simple attenuation. A polymer rich in stable radicals may quench newly generated reactive species. A redox-active material can accept or donate electrons. Change its oxidation state and you may change several of these functions at once.

In 2008, work comparing melanized and non-melanized fungal cells under ionizing radiation argued that radioprotection depended on chemical composition, stable-radical presence and the spatial arrangement of melanin. The geometry mattered. Melanin is not merely dissolved sunscreen. In fungal cells it can form a shell-like architecture in the wall, placing a chemically active material exactly where incoming radiation and secondary electrons first encounter the organism.

This gives us at least three ways for the same black layer to create a biological advantage.

It can be armor. It can be chemistry. It can be circuitry.

The third word is intentionally provocative, but not mystical. In the 2007 experiments, ionizing radiation altered the electron spin resonance signal of melanin and irradiated melanin showed an increased capacity to reduce NADH in the assay used. Those are changes in electronic and redox behavior. If they are causally connected to cellular metabolism, the pigment is not merely blocking injury; it is participating in electron handling.

The gap between those sentences is where radiotrophy lives or dies.

Melanin is particularly dangerous to reason about by analogy. Because it is dark, people reach for chlorophyll. Because radiation is electromagnetic, they reach for sunlight. Because cells grow more under one irradiated condition, the analogy appears complete. But chlorophyll operates in a molecular machine whose excitation energies, charge separation, reaction centers and downstream chemistry have been worked out in exquisite detail. Gamma rays deposit energy through ionization tracks and secondary electrons. They do not politely excite one pigment molecule and wait for an electron transport chain.

A useful theory of radiotrophy must therefore explain why an apparently destructive form of energy deposition can be rectified into chemistry rather than merely survived.

Melanin is a plausible place to look precisely because it is chemically untidy. Its disorder supplies electronic states, radicals and redox flexibility. The feature that makes it annoying to draw may be the feature that makes it useful.

But disorder is not an explanation. It is an invitation to measure.
