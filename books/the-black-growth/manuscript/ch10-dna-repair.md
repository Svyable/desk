# The DNA Repair Problem

Radiation resistance is often narrated as armor. Much of it is housekeeping.

Ionizing radiation breaks DNA directly and indirectly, damages proteins, oxidizes membranes and generates lesions whose biological importance depends on whether the cell can repair or route around them. An organism can appear extraordinarily resistant because it prevents damage, because it repairs damage, or because it tolerates broken components until replacement is possible.

Adaptive-evolution experiments with the melanized fungus *Exophiala dermatitidis* produced an especially strange result. Repeated irradiation and outgrowth generated lines with greatly increased gamma resistance, and several evolutionary paths converged on disruption of non-homologous end joining. A DNA repair pathway was, in that context, part of the problem rather than simply the solution.

The obvious moral—“fungi have amazing DNA repair”—is therefore insufficient. Repair pathways can compete. A fast but error-prone or structurally inappropriate repair route can reduce survival under some lesion patterns. Evolution under repeated radiation may reweight the repair portfolio.

This matters to radiotrophy because growth under irradiation is net growth. If pigment reduces a modest fraction of damage while repair consumes less energy or becomes more efficient, biomass can rise without radiation contributing a single useful electron to metabolism. Protection changes the denominator.

Imagine two identical cultures supplied the same chemical food. One spends twenty percent of its resources repairing oxidative injury; the other spends ten percent because melanin quenches radicals. The second produces more biomass under radiation. It has profited from melanin. It has not eaten radiation.

The experimental way out is to measure the cost side and the income side separately.

Track ATP, NADH/NAD+, repair activity, oxidative lesions, carbon flux and absorbed dose. Compare pigment mutants and repair mutants. Ask whether enhanced biomass remains after the energetic savings from reduced damage are accounted for.

A metabolism is not defined by thriving near an energy source. It is defined by a pathway through which that source changes free energy available to the organism.

DNA repair is the accountant standing at the door, reminding us that spending less can look exactly like earning more if all we measure is the balance at the end.
