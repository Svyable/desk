# When the Replication Refuses the Story

The cleanest way to ruin the phrase *radiation-eating fungus* is not to argue with it.

Change the fungus. Hold the absorbed energy still. Measure the growth.

In 2022 a Sandia-led team published [a deliberately unromantic experiment](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-16063-z) on *Cladosporium cladosporioides* and *Paecilomyces variotii*. One was melanized and had been described in the radiotrophy literature. The other was non-melanized. Both had been reported from Chernobyl environments. The investigators exposed them to ultraviolet light and gamma radiation while trying to normalize a variable that earlier comparisons often left loose: how much energy the fungal target actually absorbed.

They used a cesium-137 gamma source dominated by 662-keV photons and aimed for a total dose of about 50 rad over a week. They photographed the expanding colonies every six hours, estimated radial growth rates, and quantified pigmentation from grayscale images at the end.

The result was not the one the mythology ordered.

*P. variotii* grew at about 5.6 millimeters per day across conditions. The gamma-exposed colonies were numerically a little faster, but the difference was not statistically significant: *P* = 0.255.

*C. cladosporioides* grew at about 3.4 millimeters per day. Again, no significant radiation-associated growth difference: *P* = 0.615.

Pigment did respond.

That response was stranger than the simple story. Ultraviolet exposure darkened both fungi. Gamma exposure pushed pigmentation the other way. In *C. cladosporioides*, the pigmentation differences among conditions were highly significant, and the gamma-treated culture was lighter than the control.

If the proposed rule had been *more radiation → more melanin → more growth*, this experiment did not merely fail to reproduce it. It broke the arrows apart.

That is useful.

The paper was not a perfect replication of the 2007 experiment that helped launch the modern radiotrophy story. It did not use the same strains, radiation source, nutrient regime, dose rate, or endpoint. That is exactly why the comparison becomes interesting rather than dispositive.

The 2007 study by Dadachova and colleagues reported several positive effects under its own conditions. Melanized *Wangiella dermatitidis* and *Cryptococcus neoformans* exposed to ionizing radiation roughly five hundred times background grew faster by colony counts and dry biomass than comparable non-irradiated melanized cells or irradiated albino mutants. The irradiated melanized cells also showed roughly threefold greater incorporation of carbon-14 acetate. In a separate experiment, radiation enhanced growth of melanized *Cladosporium sphaerospermum* under limited nutrients.

Those observations are real measurements. They are also a long way from a general metabolic law.

Put the two papers side by side and the field stops looking like a yes-or-no argument.

The positive result used particular organisms and, in the *Cladosporium* case, nutrient limitation. The later null used different species, carefully modeled dose deposition, and growth on media that did not recreate the same scarcity. One study asks whether radiation can change growth under conditions where conventional energy may be limiting. The other asks whether two fungi grow faster when given matched ultraviolet or gamma energy under its chosen culture conditions.

A mechanism worth keeping should tell us why those differences matter before the next experiment is run.

There is more counterpressure.

In 2020 Zachary Schultzhaus and colleagues examined the melanized yeast *Exophiala dermatitidis* after acute gamma exposure. The result complicates an even more basic part of the story: melanin itself was not the major determinant of survival in that system. Nutrient availability, culture age, and cell density mattered more. The fungus mounted a large transcriptomic response involving DNA repair, protein degradation, and morphological pathways, and that response was largely unaffected by whether melanin was present.

That does not make melanin unimportant. It makes *which function* of melanin important.

A pigment can absorb radiation without feeding the cell.

It can quench radicals without increasing biomass.

It can alter redox chemistry without being the dominant survival mechanism.

It can change optical or electronic properties while DNA repair does the life-saving work.

And it can matter in one species and barely move the outcome in another.

This is where the literature becomes more interesting than the popular summary.

The 2022 experiment was designed partly because previous fungal-radiation studies occupied an absurd range of dose regimes. The authors' review found experiments ranging from very large acute doses delivered in hours to tiny chronic exposures spread over much longer periods. Two papers can both say “gamma irradiated” while describing biological situations that have almost nothing in common.

Dose is not enough either.

Dose rate changes how fast radicals and damage accumulate. Nutrient state changes whether a tiny energetic or metabolic advantage is visible. Colony geometry changes self-shielding. Pigment localization matters. Acute survival and chronic growth are different phenotypes. A filamentous fungus spreading across agar is not doing the same engineering as a yeast recovering from a radiation pulse.

The phrase *failed replication* can therefore be too crude.

But so can the phrase *different conditions*.

“Different conditions” is scientifically useful only when the conditions are converted into predictions.

Suppose the original growth advantage requires carbon limitation. Then the next experiment should cross radiation dose with carbon availability prospectively. The predicted effect should grow as ordinary energy becomes scarce, disappear in rich media, and track a measurable metabolic pathway rather than merely a darker colony.

Suppose the advantage comes from melanin-mediated electron transfer. Then a melanin-deficient strain and a genetically rescued strain should separate in the same direction under the same radiation history. Redox measurements should move with growth. Blocking the downstream electron-transfer pathway should erase the advantage even if the pigment remains physically present.

Suppose radiation is merely triggering a generalized stress program that improves scavenging or repair. Then additional biomass need not balance against absorbed radiation energy in any meaningful way. Transcriptomic and metabolomic changes should point to stress adaptation, not an extra energy source.

Suppose the reported growth effect is actually repair-cost savings: melanized cells spend less conventional energy repairing damage and therefore end with more biomass. That is biologically valuable. It is also not radiosynthesis.

These are not semantic distinctions. They predict different experiments.

The same applies to pigmentation.

The 2022 result is especially hard on lazy storytelling because gamma exposure decreased pigmentation while ultraviolet increased it. If melanin were simply a radiation dosimeter built by the fungus, this is the wrong shape. The cell is responding to radiation quality, damage chemistry, signaling, or metabolic cost in a more complicated way.

That complexity should make us suspicious of one-variable explanations and more interested in mechanism.

There is a recurring failure mode in frontier biology: one striking experiment establishes a verb, and later literature quietly changes what the verb means.

A fungus *survives* radiation.

Then it *prefers* radiation.

Then it *uses* radiation.

Then it *eats* radiation.

Each sentence demands more evidence than the one before it.

The 2022 null does not erase the 2007 positive. The 2020 *Exophiala* work does not prove melanin never contributes to radioresistance. The positive studies do not become false because later systems behave differently.

What disappears is the right to speak as though all black fungi share one radiation metabolism.

That is progress.

A useful next experiment would stop treating “radiation” and “melanin” as binary switches. It would run a matrix: several fungal genotypes; melanized, pigment-deficient, and rescued strains; multiple nutrient states; acute and chronic exposures; matched absorbed doses across gamma, beta, and perhaps particle radiation; direct biomass and calorimetry; isotope tracing; redox-state measurements; DNA-damage and repair costs.

Then the field could ask a harder question.

Not: does the fungus look happier near radiation?

How many joules entered the organism, where did they go, and did any of them become chemical free energy the cell could spend?

The black growth becomes scientifically dangerous to the old story only when the accounting closes.

Until then, the contradictory experiments are not an embarrassment. They are the map.
