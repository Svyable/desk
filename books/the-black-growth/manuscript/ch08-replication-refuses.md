# When the Null Refuses the Story

In 2022, a Sandia-led team put two fungi into a carefully modeled radiation experiment and got a result the popular story has little use for.

They watched *Cladosporium cladosporioides*, a melanized fungus, and *Paecilomyces variotii*, a pigmented but non-melanized comparison organism, grow for seven days under ultraviolet light, gamma radiation from cesium-137, or control conditions. The apparatus was designed to normalize absorbed energy across the two radiation treatments. Photographs were taken every ten minutes. Colony diameter supplied the growth rate; grayscale image analysis supplied pigmentation.

The colonies changed color. They did not grow significantly faster.

For *C. cladosporioides*, mean growth across treatments was about 3.4 millimeters per day, with no significant irradiation effect. *P. variotii* averaged about 5.6 millimeters per day; its somewhat higher gamma-exposed growth rate also failed the significance test. Both organisms became darker under ultraviolet exposure and lighter under gamma exposure. The last result is awkward for any account in which ionizing radiation simply turns up fungal melanin because melanin is useful around ionizing radiation.

It is tempting to call this a failed replication of the famous 2007 experiment. That is too generous to the word *replication*.

Dadachova and colleagues had tested *Cryptococcus neoformans*, *Wangiella dermatitidis*, and *Cladosporium sphaerospermum*. Their growth experiments emphasized limited nutrients. The 2022 team used *C. cladosporioides* and *P. variotii* on nutrient-rich potato dextrose agar. Dadachova’s *C. sphaerospermum* colonies sat for fifteen days in a 0.05 mGy-per-hour field. In the newer apparatus, dose varied across a radially expanding colony: the authors estimated that different parts of *C. cladosporioides* received roughly 6.1 to 85.2 rad over the experiment, with the actively growing edge continually moving away from the source.

Those are not bookkeeping differences. Species, carbon availability, dose rate, accumulated dose, radiation spectrum, geometry and the age of the hypha being measured can all change the biological question.

The 2022 result therefore cannot tell us that the 2007 result was an artifact. It tells us something narrower and, in a way, more troublesome: the reported growth advantage does not travel automatically with a black colony into another plausible radiation experiment.

The authors themselves offer two obvious escape routes. Their isolate had not spent years in a gamma-rich Chernobyl environment, and their nutrient-rich medium may have concealed an advantage that appears under starvation. Both explanations are biologically plausible. Neither is free.

If prior radiation history is necessary, adaptation or physiological memory becomes part of the hypothesis. If carbon limitation is necessary, nutrient state becomes part of it. If only certain species respond, melanin cannot be treated as a sufficient mechanism. Each rescue makes the claim smaller and more testable.

That is what contrary data are for.

The strongest case for the original result also deserves its full weight. In 2007, irradiation of melanized *C. neoformans* was associated with about 2.5 times as many colony-forming units and nearly three times the incorporation of carbon-14 acetate as the same melanized cells without irradiation, while the laccase-deficient non-melanized mutant changed only slightly. Dry biomass rose by a much smaller but statistically significant 6.5 percent. In *C. sphaerospermum*, irradiated melanized colonies outgrew both non-irradiated melanized colonies and melanin-deficient controls under the tested low-nutrient conditions. Those are not observations that a later experiment with different organisms and food can simply erase.

But the scale mismatch inside the 2007 measurements is itself useful. A 2.5-fold increase in colony-forming units, a nearly threefold increase in acetate incorporation and a 6.5 percent increase in dry mass are not three interchangeable descriptions of “more growth.” Cell number, substrate uptake and accumulated biomass report different pieces of physiology. The authors noted that many irradiated melanized cells were smaller, which helps explain why the dry-weight effect was modest beside the colony count.

That detail makes the story better because it makes it harder. Radiation may have changed division, carbon handling, cell size or allocation in ways that produced several correlated readouts without yet identifying where usable energy entered metabolism.

So the useful experiment is not 2007 versus 2022 as a referendum. It is 2007 crossed with 2022.

Run the same species, isogenic pigment manipulations, nutrient-poor and nutrient-rich media, several dose rates and matched accumulated doses in one apparatus. Measure the advancing hyphal edge separately from old mycelium. Keep colony area, viable cell count, dry biomass and isotope flux as different outcomes rather than collapsing them into “growth.” Add a preregistered interaction test: does irradiation help specifically when melanin is present and conventional energy is scarce?

If that interaction survives, the 2022 null result becomes a boundary condition rather than an embarrassment. If it does not, one of the most seductive biological stories of Chernobyl gets much smaller.

Either way, the disagreement has done its job. It has replaced a slogan with coordinates.
