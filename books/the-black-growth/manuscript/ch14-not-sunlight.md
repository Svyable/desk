# Radiation Is Not Sunlight

Sunlight arrives as a spectrum biology learned to parse. Ionizing radiation arrives as damage with bookkeeping.

A visible photon absorbed by chlorophyll creates an excitation that can be funneled through a reaction center before thermalization. A gamma photon is vastly more energetic and typically interacts through processes that generate energetic electrons and ionization tracks. The initial energy is not neatly localized to a pigment designed to catch it.

That difference makes the phrase “radiosynthesis” dangerous.

It does not make energy harvesting impossible in principle. Life routinely turns messy gradients into ordered work. Chemolithotrophs exploit redox differences in rocks. Radiolysis of water can generate molecular hydrogen and oxidants that microbes may subsequently use. In deep geological settings, radiation can therefore support life indirectly by producing chemical fuel in the environment.

This mechanism is already conceptually distinct from melanin-mediated radiotrophy.

If uranium decay splits water and a microbe consumes the resulting hydrogen, the organism is ultimately supported by radioactive decay without needing a pigment that transduces ionizing photons. If melanin changes redox chemistry inside a fungal wall and that change directly increases electron flow into metabolism, the pathway is different.

Astrobiology should care about both, but it should not confuse them.

A subsurface world rich in radionuclides might support radiolysis-fed chemistry even in complete darkness. A melanized organism on a spacecraft hull might gain only protection. A Chernobyl fungus could exhibit radiotropism because the field predicts reduced competition. One word cannot carry all these ecologies.

The broader lesson is that “energy source” has levels. Radiation can be a primary physical source, an environmental chemical generator, a selective pressure or a cue. Life can exploit each level differently.

The job is to identify the coupling, not admire the coincidence.
