# The Black Growth

Chernobyl Fungi, Melanin, and the Question of Whether Life Can Learn to Use Radiation

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a version of this story in which black fungus crawls toward a broken nuclear reactor and eats gamma rays.

It is a terrific story. It is also too tidy.

What was actually found inside and around the damaged Chernobyl reactor was stranger in the useful way: fungi growing where radiation, desiccation, metals, darkness, heat history, scarce nutrients and human abandonment had produced an ecosystem nobody designed. Some of those fungi were heavily melanized. Some experiments later found enhanced growth under ionizing radiation. Melanin exposed to radiation changed measurable electronic behavior. Other experiments failed to reproduce the neat growth story. Still others showed that DNA repair, culture age and metabolic state mattered more than pigmentation in particular organisms.

That is better than a miracle. A miracle is finished the moment you believe it. A contradiction can still teach you something.

This book asks whether radiotrophy is a metabolism, a protective phenotype, a stress response, a sensory behavior, an experimental artifact, or an untidy mixture of all five. It also asks why the distinction matters. A fungus that merely survives radiation is interesting. A fungus whose pigment reduces oxidative injury is technologically useful. A fungus that changes electron-transfer chemistry under radiation is stranger. A fungus that turns a measurable fraction of ionizing-radiation energy into additional chemical work would force biology to admit a new kind of environmental energy capture.

The evidence does not presently justify that last sentence as a fact.

Throughout the book I keep four bins open. Class 1 is documented observation and reproducible established physics or biology. Class 2 is credible but incomplete evidence that deserves replication. Class 3 is a disputed claim that has not earned promotion. Class 4 is synthesis: the ideas that become interesting only after the first three are kept separate.

I am especially suspicious of the word *radiotrophic*. It does too much labor. A sunflower is phototrophic because the energetic bookkeeping closes. For fungi under gamma rays, that ledger has not yet been closed in the same way. The question is not whether radiation can alter melanin or fungal physiology. It can. The question is whether any organism obtains a net, mechanistically attributable energetic advantage from ionizing radiation in a manner that deserves comparison to photosynthesis or chemotrophy.

Chernobyl gave us the organisms. Spaceflight is giving us another reason to care. Between those two places lies a surprisingly unfinished piece of biology.
