# The Geopolitical Stack

Globalization encouraged companies to draw supply chains as arrows. Inputs entered from the left, finished goods exited on the right, and managers optimized the flow for cost, quality, inventory, and speed. The diagram was useful because politics often appeared as background.

Then the background moved.

A supply chain is not merely a commercial arrangement. It is a stack of jurisdictions, currencies, ports, energy systems, standards, military assumptions, diplomatic relationships, and legal permissions. Efficiency can hide these layers during calm periods because they are shared infrastructure. Shocks make them visible.

Semiconductors are a particularly vivid example. A leading chip can involve architecture, electronic-design automation, intellectual property, specialized manufacturing equipment, ultrapure chemicals, wafers, fabrication, packaging, testing, firmware, and global distribution. No single country necessarily controls every layer. That interdependence can promote trade and create extraordinary specialization. It can also create chokepoints.

A chokepoint is a layer whose replaceability is lower than its apparent share of cost suggests.

This is why strategic importance cannot be inferred from revenue alone. A small supplier of a specialized material can matter more to system continuity than a much larger consumer brand. A port, strait, cable landing station, transformer, machine tool, or standard can be economically modest and systemically central.

Complex systems fail according to topology, not prestige.

The geopolitical value stack therefore asks different questions from ordinary procurement. How concentrated is production? How long would substitution take? Which inputs require tacit knowledge rather than blueprints? Which facilities depend on stable electricity, water, or logistics? Which legal regimes can block transfer? Which alliances make continuity credible? Which inventories are buffers and which are illusions because every competitor needs the same substitute during a crisis?

Resilience costs money before it creates visible value. Redundant suppliers, spare capacity, inventories, domestic capability, and diversified routes often look inefficient in normal times. Their return appears only in states of the world that managers hope never occur.

This creates a behavioral problem. Organizations systematically underinvest in capabilities whose success is measured by the absence of disaster. The unused backup generator looks wasteful until the grid fails. The second supplier looks expensive until the first cannot ship. The diplomatic relationship looks ceremonial until a permit, evacuation, or emergency corridor depends on it.

Risk lives in counterfactuals, and counterfactuals do not appear on quarterly income statements.

Governments respond by trying to move value downward in the stack. Industrial policy, subsidies, procurement rules, stockpiles, export controls, and research funding are attempts to preserve or recreate foundational capabilities. These policies can be justified by externalities and security; they can also become vehicles for lobbying, nationalism, and expensive duplication. The existence of a strategic layer does not make every intervention wise.

The analytical challenge is to distinguish true bottlenecks from fashionable anxieties.

A bottleneck has three properties: the layer is necessary, substitution is difficult within the relevant time horizon, and disruption has consequences large enough to justify the cost of mitigation. All three matter. A scarce input with easy redesign is inconvenient, not strategic. A necessary input with many independent suppliers is important but not necessarily vulnerable. A dramatic dependency whose interruption causes only minor delay may not deserve national policy.

Time horizon is decisive. Markets are often excellent at substitution given enough time. Geopolitical crises are often about not having enough time.

This is where business strategy and state strategy diverge. A firm can sometimes exit a market, sell an asset, or allow a product line to fail. A state inherits obligations to territory, citizens, and institutions that cannot be liquidated so easily. National resilience therefore values options that a narrow corporate model may discard.

But states also depend on firms. Advanced capability is embodied in engineers, supplier relationships, production routines, intellectual property, capital equipment, and managerial know-how. A government cannot summon an industrial ecosystem by declaration. Capacity is cumulative.

That cumulative character explains why losing a layer can matter even when imports are cheaper. Once production disappears, the adjacent knowledge, training pipelines, maintenance skills, and supplier networks can decay with it. Rebuilding later may cost far more than the earlier savings implied.

This does not mean self-sufficiency is optimal. Autarky throws away the gains from specialization and can make systems poorer and less innovative. The strategic objective is not to own every layer. It is to understand which dependencies are acceptable.

Interdependence can itself be a source of stability when the costs of rupture are mutual. But asymmetric interdependence creates leverage. If one side can tolerate separation and the other cannot, the commercial stack contains political power.

Finance adds another layer. Reserve currencies, correspondent banking, payment messaging, sanctions compliance, sovereign debt markets, and access to deep capital pools can extend geopolitical influence far beyond physical trade. Technology standards do the same. A standard adopted globally can shape markets without a border checkpoint.

The result is a world in which power is increasingly exercised through stacks rather than territories alone.

The strategic map therefore looks less like colored countries and more like dependency graphs. Ports connect to factories, factories to machines, machines to software, software to standards, standards to institutions, institutions to alliances, alliances to credibility. The graph is recursive because every node contains smaller dependencies of its own.

A wise company asks where it sits in that graph before a crisis answers for it.

A wise country does the same.
