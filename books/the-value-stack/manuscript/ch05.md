# The Tollbooth

The most profitable square foot in a grocery store is not necessarily where the best food sits. It may be the place every customer has to pass.

Checkout lanes taught generations of retailers the economics of forced proximity. Candy, gum, batteries, magazines, and small impulse goods occupied a strip of attention created by architecture. The customer had already decided to buy something, had already entered the store, had already filled a basket, and now had to wait. Distribution had produced a captive moment.

The lesson is bigger than retail: a layer that controls passage can charge even when it does not create the thing passing through.

Tollbooths are not inherently parasitic. Roads need to be built and maintained. Markets need rules, fraud controls, discovery, dispute resolution, and infrastructure. A distributor may create enormous value by gathering fragmented supply, carrying inventory, financing sellers, making quality legible, or placing goods near demand. The danger begins when the layer's contribution and its power drift apart.

A useful intermediary can become an obligatory intermediary.

The distinction between those states explains much of modern business strategy.

Consider the old wholesaler. A small manufacturer could not economically visit every shop in a country. A wholesaler aggregated products from many manufacturers and delivered them to retailers. The wholesaler reduced transaction costs on both sides. Retailers dealt with fewer suppliers. Manufacturers reached more stores. Inventory moved in useful quantities. Credit could be extended. Returns could be managed. Local market knowledge was concentrated.

The margin paid to the wholesaler was not simply a tax. It purchased coordination.

But if a wholesaler became the only practical route to a region, the relationship changed. A producer that could not reach customers directly had weak bargaining power. The wholesaler could demand better terms because its scarce asset was not a truck or warehouse alone. It was access.

Access is one of the most misunderstood layers of value because it can look like nothing.

A factory is visible. A warehouse is visible. A software product is visible. A customer relationship is harder to see on a balance sheet, yet it can dominate the economics of everything beneath it. The company that owns habitual access to demand can invite suppliers to compete for the right to serve that demand.

This is the quiet logic behind shelf space, search ranking, app distribution, hotel booking, food delivery, advertising markets, brokerage, ticketing, and much of media.

The structure repeats. First, the intermediary solves fragmentation. Then participants invest around the intermediary. Then those investments make leaving costly. Then the intermediary discovers that it can charge for the dependency it helped create.

The process is not inevitable. Competition between intermediaries can keep fees low. Sellers can build direct channels. Regulation can limit practices. Standards can preserve portability. Customers can multi-home across platforms. But the gravitational pull is real because aggregation creates its own scarcity.

When supply is fragmented, buyers value a place that brings it together. When buyers gather in one place, sellers value access to the buyers. Each side attracts the other. The platform's value grows not because it made the underlying goods but because it reduced the difficulty of finding and transacting with them.

Two-sided markets formalize part of this dynamic. A platform may subsidize one group to attract another. A credit-card network can make cards attractive to consumers by encouraging broad merchant acceptance and attractive to merchants by delivering customers who want to use the cards. A marketplace can charge sellers while keeping access free for buyers. A media platform can subsidize users with content and monetize advertisers seeking attention.

The pricing of each side reflects system design, not an isolated cost-plus calculation.

That is why asking, “Why does this intermediary deserve five percent?” can be less informative than asking, “What would happen to the transaction if the intermediary disappeared tomorrow?”

If buyers and sellers could reconnect with minimal friction, the fee is vulnerable. If discovery collapses, trust disappears, payment becomes harder, fraud rises, logistics fragment, or coordination becomes expensive, the intermediary is doing more than sitting in the middle.

The second question is equally important: “What happens if the intermediary raises the fee to fifteen percent?”

That reveals the bypass pressure.

A healthy distribution layer lives in tension between convenience and extraction. Charge too little and the system may not fund itself. Charge too much and participants search for direct routes, competitors, regulation, or technical workarounds.

The most powerful distribution businesses manage this tension partly by making themselves more useful over time. They add logistics, payments, analytics, financing, identity, returns, security, marketing tools, and software. Each added service can create customer value. It can also make the bundle harder to leave.

The Value Stack treats bundling as a form of vertical movement.

A distributor that adds payments has entered the trust and financial layers. A marketplace that provides logistics has entered physical infrastructure. A device maker that operates an app store has entered distribution. A bank that adds advisory services moves from balance-sheet capacity toward judgment and relationship. A retailer that launches a private label moves downward into product ownership.

Companies rarely remain in one layer once they find a profitable border.

They expand because adjacent layers contain either risk or opportunity. If a supplier has too much power, integrate downward. If a distributor has too much power, go direct. If customers need financing, add credit. If quality uncertainty limits adoption, add certification. If competitors can copy the product, build a brand. If the brand depends on another platform for customer access, build an owned channel.

This can create extraordinary systems. It can also create empires whose internal logic is simply, “Never let another layer control our oxygen.”

The impulse is understandable. Dependency is strategically uncomfortable.

A manufacturer whose largest retailer accounts for half its sales may look successful and feel trapped. A media company whose audience arrives through one search engine or social platform may have millions of readers and little control over demand. A software developer whose users discover products through one app store can have a flourishing business and still face a sovereign gatekeeper. A small restaurant that relies on delivery apps can gain incremental orders while wondering whether the customer belongs to the restaurant or the platform.

The answer is often both.

Customer ownership is not a metaphysical category. It is a set of capabilities. Who has the contact information? Who can initiate communication? Who controls identity? Who records purchase history? Who decides ranking? Who handles payment credentials? Who can change terms? Who holds the reputation data? Who can offer a substitute at the moment of choice?

Distribution power is the accumulation of these small rights.

This is why email lists, loyalty programs, direct accounts, and saved payment methods matter even when they seem mundane. They preserve a route to the customer that does not require renting attention anew each time.

The word “renting” is apt. Much digital advertising is a market for temporary access to attention. A business that lacks an owned relationship pays repeatedly to reacquire customers it has already served. That can be rational if the advertising platform is efficient. It can also leave the business structurally exposed to changes in auction prices, targeting rules, privacy regulation, or platform policy.

The strongest consumer businesses often combine rented distribution with owned habit.

Habit is a tollbooth inside the mind.

The phrase sounds sinister, but habits can be useful. A customer does not want to reevaluate every toothbrush, bank, grocery store, note-taking app, or coffee shop from first principles each day. Habit lowers cognitive transaction costs. A familiar route through the value stack becomes default.

This makes consumer psychology part of distribution.

Physical placement, app icons, subscriptions, notifications, default settings, search rankings, recommendations, and social routines all influence what enters the choice set. The scarce resource is not only money. It is the moment in which alternatives are considered.

Brands understand this intuitively. One reason to invest in mental availability is to be present before comparison begins. A brand that is remembered first has effectively shortened its route to purchase.

The same logic governs B2B markets. A vendor already approved by procurement, integrated into systems, trained into employees, and recognized by legal and security teams has a distribution advantage that a technically superior newcomer may underestimate. The incumbent is not merely “selling software.” It occupies a procedural path through the customer's organization.

Enterprise sales is partly the craft of constructing that path.

The salesperson maps stakeholders. Security reviews the product. Legal negotiates terms. Finance approves the budget. Procurement creates a vendor record. IT integrates identity. Users are trained. Managers revise workflows. The final contract is the visible surface of a much longer journey through institutional gates.

Once embedded, the vendor can become difficult to dislodge even if alternatives improve.

This is sometimes called switching cost. The phrase can sound mechanical, as though the cost were merely a migration fee. In practice it includes uncertainty, career risk, retraining, data cleanup, political capital, downtime, contract timing, and the possibility that the replacement will fail. A manager deciding whether to switch is not comparing product features alone. He is comparing two stacks of consequences.

Distribution inside institutions is therefore also a layer of trust.

This helps explain why young companies can struggle to sell objectively better products. The incumbent's advantage may be less about product quality than about being legible to the buyer's system. The newcomer must not only create superior utility; it must finance the cost of crossing every gate.

Sometimes a new distribution model wins by removing those gates rather than persuading them.

Software sold through a credit card to an individual employee can enter an organization from below. Open-source tools can spread through technical communities before procurement is involved. Consumer apps can establish habits that later become enterprise demand. Direct-to-consumer brands can bypass wholesale distribution. Crowdfunding can bypass parts of traditional financing. Online publishing can bypass editors and printing presses.

Disintermediation is the dream of every layer paying a toll.

Reintermediation is what often happens next.

Remove the record store and streaming platforms emerge. Remove the travel agent and online booking platforms emerge. Remove the newspaper bundle and social feeds, search engines, newsletters, podcasts, and aggregators compete to organize attention. Remove the traditional bank branch and app stores, identity providers, payment processors, fraud systems, and cloud infrastructure become part of the new financial route.

Middlemen do not disappear. Their jobs are decomposed and recombined.

This is why technological narratives that celebrate “cutting out the middleman” age poorly. Coordination costs do not vanish because a website exists. They move into software, standards, networks, trust systems, and new organizations. The relevant question is whether the new intermediary performs the coordination more cheaply or usefully and how the resulting power is governed.

The political economy of distribution becomes obvious when private gateways influence public life.

A payment network can affect which merchants can transact. A cloud provider can host critical services. A social platform can shape political communication. A search engine can influence what information is discovered. A dominant mobile ecosystem can decide what applications reach users. These firms may be private, but their distribution layers begin to resemble infrastructure.

Infrastructure invites a different social bargain than ordinary products.

Societies tolerate substantial private discretion over a shoe brand. They become more uneasy when a small number of firms can effectively determine access to speech, commerce, identity, or essential digital services. The reason is not merely size. It is layer position.

A company deep in a stack can be large without governing anyone else's route to the market. A smaller company at a narrow gateway can exercise disproportionate power.

Antitrust debates often become arguments about this architecture. Is the firm simply succeeding because customers prefer it? Is it using one layer's dominance to privilege another? Are participants free to leave in practice? Does the platform compete with the businesses that depend on it? Are fees disciplined by alternatives? Does scale improve the service enough to justify concentration? Would interoperability preserve benefits while reducing gatekeeper power?

These questions resist slogans because distribution creates real efficiencies and real dependencies at the same time.

The same ambivalence appears inside a career.

A person who becomes the sole route to a client, system, or executive can gain job security. She can also become a bottleneck. Organizations sometimes reward people for hoarding access because the hoarding looks like indispensability. The short-term value capture is obvious; the long-term contribution is not.

A healthier form of personal distribution power comes from being trusted across a network without making the network dependent on secrecy. The valuable colleague introduces people, creates shared context, documents what matters, and improves the interface between groups. Her influence grows because others choose to route through her, not because she blocks alternate paths.

The distinction between bridge and tollbooth is character.

At organizational scale it is governance.

A layer with gatekeeping power must decide what kind of institution it wants to become. It can maximize the fee that dependence allows. It can restrain itself to preserve ecosystem health. It can create transparent rules, portability, appeals, and interoperability. It can separate its role as referee from its role as competitor. It can treat participants as tenants or partners.

The economics will push toward capture. Trust may require limits.

Long-lived systems often discover that restraint is not charity. It is maintenance of the layer beneath their own prosperity. A marketplace that impoverishes all its sellers eventually has fewer good sellers. A franchisor that extracts every dollar from franchisees degrades local execution. A platform that surprises developers with arbitrary rule changes teaches developers to invest elsewhere. A procurement organization that squeezes suppliers beyond sustainability may save money this quarter and lose resilience next year.

The tollbooth depends on traffic.

This is the recursive constraint on distribution power. Every fee changes the route-selection behavior of the people paying it. Every rule changes what participants build. Every ranking system changes supply. Every subsidy changes entry. Every act of extraction becomes information for potential bypasses.

The best gatekeepers understand that they are cultivating a road, not merely charging at a booth.

The worst discover the difference when traffic learns another way around.
