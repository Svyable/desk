# The Ladder Under the Price

A price is an answer to a question that nobody asked in full.

How much will you pay for this, here, now, under these alternatives, with this information, through these institutions, from this seller, given your expectations about what happens next?

The number on the screen looks compact because markets are compression machines. They take millions of private circumstances and force them into bids, asks, salaries, rents, interest rates, and checkout totals. That compression is one of civilization's great conveniences. It is also why price can be mistaken for a complete theory of value.

The mistake is easiest to see in a hospital. A nurse may spend forty minutes noticing that a patient's breathing has changed, escalating the concern, calming the family, and preventing a dangerous deterioration. The bill will not necessarily contain a line item called “noticed the thing that mattered.” It will contain codes, procedures, facility charges, medications, tests, and professional services. The value of attention sits inside a larger institutional architecture. Some of it is compensated. Some is bundled. Some is invisible. Some may be captured elsewhere in the system as shorter length of stay, lower liability, or a hospital's reputation for competent care.

The market price of a contribution is therefore not the same thing as the total value that contribution helps create. Nor is the profit of a firm equal to its usefulness to society. These distinctions are familiar in economics, yet everyday language constantly erases them. We say a person is “worth” a salary, a company is “worth” a market capitalization, a house is “worth” its sale price, and a technology is “valuable” because investors have assigned it a large number. The words move effortlessly between price, contribution, bargaining power, expected cash flow, and moral significance.

The Value Stack begins by separating them.

Imagine a simple transaction between a producer and a customer. The customer would have paid up to one hundred dollars. The product costs the producer sixty dollars to make and deliver. If they agree at eighty dollars, twenty dollars of potential benefit remains with the customer as consumer surplus and twenty dollars remains with the producer before accounting for other claims. The transaction creates room for mutual gain because the buyer values the product more than the resources required to provide it.

Real economies add complications immediately. The producer does not act alone. Suppliers, workers, lenders, landlords, governments, distributors, software vendors, insurers, and shareholders have claims. The customer's willingness to pay is shaped by substitutes, information, habit, wealth, culture, status, and fear. The cost of production depends on prior investments in roads, education, science, law, public health, and standards that may never appear on the company's income statement. Negative effects can be pushed onto people who never entered the transaction at all.

Still, the simple example gives us an important distinction: value can be created without being captured by the creator, and value can be captured without having been created by the capturer in the same proportion.

The first condition is common and often desirable. A public library creates benefits it does not charge each reader for. A scientific paper may enable decades of downstream innovation. A good manager can make an organization calmer and more effective in ways that show up in many other people's performance. A well-designed open standard can save countless hours across an industry while the committee that developed it earns no royalty.

The second condition is where arguments begin. A monopolist can raise prices after competitors disappear. A landowner can receive higher rent because a city builds a subway station nearby. A platform can increase its take rate after sellers have become dependent on its traffic. A patent holder can extract a large share of the surplus created by a product whose success also depends on manufacturing, complementary technologies, marketing, and public research. None of these examples can be judged by a single rule. Property rights, incentives, risk, investment, and market power all matter. But they reveal why “who gets paid?” is not the same question as “who made this useful?”

One way to see the difference is to imagine value as water moving through a terraced hillside. The water is not created by the terrace that collects it, yet without terraces much of it may run away. A layer can help generate the flow, channel the flow, prevent leakage, or position itself where the flow must pass. Those are different economic functions even when the accounting statement labels the result “revenue.”

This is where bargaining power enters the stack.

Suppose a manufacturer depends on a component available from twenty qualified suppliers. Each supplier may perform difficult technical work, but if their outputs are close substitutes, no single supplier can demand much of the final product's surplus. Now suppose one component requires a proprietary process available from only one firm, qualification takes two years, redesign would be expensive, and the component represents less than one percent of the product's bill of materials. That tiny component can command disproportionate bargaining power. Its value does not come from mass or visible labor. It comes from being necessary and difficult to route around.

The same logic applies to human organizations. A person can become unusually influential not by doing the most work but by holding context that others lack, maintaining trust across rival groups, controlling access to a decision maker, understanding an obsolete system nobody else can safely modify, or being the one person whose signature is legally required. Some of these positions reflect earned expertise. Others reflect institutional accident. All of them show how the stack turns scarcity into power.

Substitutability is the quiet variable beneath much of economic life.

We often call something a commodity when its units have become interchangeable enough that buyers can choose largely on price. The word can sound dismissive, as if the product no longer requires skill. That is wrong. Commodity production can be technically demanding, capital intensive, dangerous, and socially essential. Commoditization does not mean easy. It means the buyer can switch without losing too much of what matters.

Commercial aviation illustrates the distinction. Operating an airline safely is a staggering organizational achievement involving aircraft, maintenance, training, scheduling, regulation, fuel, weather, software, airports, crews, and constant operational discipline. Yet on many routes passengers compare fares and schedules with little loyalty. The underlying capability is extraordinary while the product can still face commodity-like price pressure. A difficult activity can have weak pricing power when several competitors can perform it adequately.

Luxury goods can show the reverse. The physical manufacturing cost of an item may be a modest fraction of its retail price, but the customer is not treating one logo as interchangeable with another. History, scarcity, design language, celebrity association, retail theater, resale expectations, and group recognition form a layer of meaning that resists substitution. The bag holds objects. The brand holds a social position.

To say this is not to say the customer is irrational. Human beings live inside symbolic worlds. We pay for wedding rings whose material could be replaced more cheaply because the ring is participating in a promise. We keep a parent's watch after a technically superior watch arrives because memory has attached itself to metal. We prefer a crowded neighborhood restaurant to an efficient chain because familiarity is part of the product. Value includes function, but human life includes more than function.

Behavioral economics complicates willingness to pay further. We do not evaluate every purchase from scratch. Reference points matter. Losses can loom larger than equivalent gains. Ownership can change perceived value. Defaults shape behavior. Framing matters. Social proof matters. The architecture around a choice can affect the price a buyer accepts even when the underlying object is unchanged.

This means higher layers in the value stack often work by shaping comparison.

A commodity is exposed to direct comparison. A differentiated product changes the comparison set. A strong brand may persuade the customer that the relevant alternative is not another product in the same category but another expression of identity. A subscription can shift attention from the price of each use to the comfort of access. A bundle can make individual prices harder to isolate. A financing offer can convert a large total cost into a monthly number. A platform can make its fee disappear inside a transaction that feels simpler than assembling the pieces separately.

The ethics of these moves depend on what they do to the customer. Simplification can be genuinely valuable. Confusion can be profitable too.

The difference between value creation and value capture becomes especially important when information is uneven. George Akerlof's famous market for “lemons” showed how quality uncertainty can damage markets because buyers, unable to distinguish good from bad, lower what they are willing to pay. That can drive high-quality sellers away, worsening the pool. One response is a trust layer: warranties, certification, reputation, inspection, branding, escrow, return policies, professional licensing. These mechanisms cost money, but they can create value by making transactions possible that uncertainty would otherwise prevent.

Once a trust layer succeeds, however, it can become a powerful position of capture. Certification bodies can become gatekeepers. Rating agencies can shape access to capital. Marketplaces can own reputation data that sellers cannot easily take elsewhere. Platforms can require participants to transact inside the system to preserve the trust signals accumulated there. The layer that solved an information problem acquires an asset: dependency.

Dependency is not automatically abuse. It is often the residue of a job well done. If you build a payment system that merchants and consumers trust, more people will prefer to use it. If you create a software tool around which companies reorganize their operations, switching becomes painful because the tool is now embedded in routines. If you run a marketplace that reliably attracts buyers, sellers will invest in learning its rules.

The strategic temptation is to treat this dependence as permission to extract indefinitely.

That is where the stack develops memory. Customers remember price increases. Suppliers invest in alternatives. Competitors attack profitable layers. Regulators notice chokepoints. Employees leave with tacit knowledge. An apparently dominant layer can provoke the forces that make it replaceable.

The process is recursive: capture changes behavior, behavior changes structure, structure changes future capture.

This feedback loop is one reason business positions are less permanent than they look from inside a successful quarter. High margins advertise opportunity. Tight control encourages customers to multi-source. Expensive software creates a market for migration tools. A dominant retailer encourages suppliers to build direct channels. A powerful nation weaponizing an economic dependency can motivate other nations to reduce exposure. The more aggressively a layer converts indispensability into extraction, the more incentive the rest of the stack has to route around it.

Value therefore has a dynamic dimension. What a layer can capture today affects whether it remains scarce tomorrow.

This is easy to miss in finance because discounted cash-flow models, by necessity, turn a changing competitive world into a sequence of projected numbers. The analyst must estimate revenue, margins, investment, risk, and duration. The spreadsheet can be meticulous while the most important question remains outside a cell: why should this company still have bargaining power ten years from now?

A good answer usually points to some combination of scarce assets and reinforcing behavior. Customers stay because switching costs are real. Developers build because users are present, and users come because developers build. Manufacturing know-how accumulates through repeated practice. A distribution network becomes denser and therefore cheaper or faster. A brand grows more recognizable through use. A standard attracts complementary products. Data improves a service, which attracts more usage, which produces more data. Trust, once earned, lowers the friction of future transactions.

But each of these mechanisms has limits. Switching costs can become resentment. Networks can fragment. Manufacturing leadership can be lost through underinvestment. Distribution can be leapfrogged. Brands can become culturally stale. Standards can ossify. Data can become less exclusive. Trust can disappear in a single scandal if the trust was concentrated in a promise the organization breaks.

The durability of value capture depends on whether the layer continues to solve a problem while remaining difficult to replace.

That sentence contains a quiet warning for investors and executives. A company can preserve its pricing power for years after its underlying contribution begins to weaken, particularly when customers are locked in or alternatives are immature. Financial performance can therefore lag strategic decay. The numbers look healthy because the stack has inertia. Then a threshold is crossed: a substitute becomes good enough, regulation changes, a standard opens, a key employee base revolts, or a new distribution channel lowers switching costs. What looked like a durable rent turns out to have been borrowed time.

The reverse can happen too. A company can create enormous customer value before it learns how to capture enough of it to survive. Early internet businesses often attracted users with services whose economics were unclear. Open-source projects can become essential infrastructure while maintainers remain underfunded. News organizations can produce journalism that influences public life while struggling to capture the value of attention once advertising and distribution move to other layers. Hospitals, schools, and public agencies can create diffuse benefits that conventional profit measures barely register.

This is why debates about “value” become confused when participants are talking about different ledgers.

A shareholder asks what cash flows belong to the firm. A customer asks whether the purchase improves her situation. An employee asks whether the compensation and work justify the time. A supplier asks whether the relationship is sustainable. A citizen asks about spillovers, fairness, resilience, and public goods. A strategist asks which layer can defend its share. A moral philosopher may ask who deserves what. These questions overlap, but none can answer the others by itself.

The Value Stack is most useful when it prevents one ledger from pretending to be the whole economy.

Take a city block whose property values rise after a public park, transit line, school improvement, and crime decline make the neighborhood more desirable. Homeowners may experience large capital gains. Their properties are genuinely more valuable to buyers. Yet the gain was co-produced by taxpayers, public employees, nearby businesses, demographic change, construction workers, community groups, and countless private decisions. The title deed determines who captures the appreciation. It does not identify all who created it.

Or take a social network whose users produce the posts, jokes, photographs, recommendations, arguments, and relationships that make the network compelling. The company contributes engineering, moderation, distribution, storage, ranking, identity systems, advertising infrastructure, and coordination. Users contribute content and attention. Advertisers contribute money. Developers may contribute complements. The value is jointly produced, while the platform's ownership structure determines how much of the monetary return accrues to shareholders.

Joint production is the normal case, not the exception.

The myth of the solitary value creator survives because stories prefer protagonists. We like the founder, inventor, star employee, visionary designer, or brilliant investor. They give causality a face. The stack restores the supporting cast: institutions, standards, suppliers, customers, predecessors, public infrastructure, accidents, timing, and competitors who forced improvement.

This does not diminish individual agency. It locates it.

A person can still make an extraordinary contribution. But the question becomes sharper: which layer did the contribution change? Did it lower the cost of a substrate? Create a new capability? Open distribution? Establish trust? Create meaning? Change rules? Alter the interfaces between layers? Make a previously scarce resource abundant? Turn a commodity into a differentiated experience? Turn a closed system into a standard others could build on?

Those are more useful questions than asking whether somebody “created value” in the abstract.

They also make strategy less mystical. A business does not need to be “innovative” everywhere. It needs to know which layer is decisive for the customer and which layer is decisive for capture. Those may not be the same.

A restaurant can win because of food and fail because of rent. A software company can delight users and fail because a distributor controls discovery. A manufacturer can produce the best component and lose because the system architect specifies a rival. A bank can have capital and lose trust. A brilliant scientist can discover something and watch another firm commercialize it. A media company can own content while another layer owns the audience relationship.

The ladder under the price is made of these mismatches.

Once you see them, the most interesting business question changes. Instead of asking, “What is this worth?” you begin asking, “What makes it worth that, who can change those conditions, and which layer gets to keep the difference?”

The price is still useful. It is just no longer the end of the explanation.
