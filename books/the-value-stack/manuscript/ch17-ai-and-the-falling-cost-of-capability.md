# Artificial Intelligence and the Falling Cost of Capability

Every important general-purpose technology changes the value stack twice. First it creates a new capability. Then, if it succeeds, it makes that capability ordinary.

Artificial intelligence is interesting not only because machines can perform tasks that once required human cognition, but because the price of certain cognitive outputs can fall faster than institutions know how to reorganize around them.

When a capability becomes cheaper, value does not vanish. It migrates.

If drafting competent prose becomes abundant, the scarce layer may become judgment about what should be written, access to proprietary facts, responsibility for accuracy, distinctive taste, distribution, or trust. If code generation becomes cheaper, the bottleneck may move toward problem definition, architecture, integration, security, data quality, organizational adoption, and the willingness to own the result in production. If image generation becomes abundant, provenance, brand, curation, rights, and cultural legitimacy may gain relative importance.

This is the same pattern seen in earlier technologies. Cheap printing increased the supply of text and raised the importance of editing, distribution, reputation, and attention. Cheap photography altered the economics of representation while creating new value in selection, journalism, advertising, celebrity, and visual culture. Cheap computation made some calculations trivial and created enormous industries around what abundant calculation enabled.

The error is to assume that automation removes a layer and leaves the rest unchanged.

Organizations are systems of tasks, incentives, identities, permissions, and accountability. A tool can automate a task while leaving the workflow bottleneck untouched. A lawyer who drafts faster may simply spend more time reviewing, negotiating, or handling a larger caseload. A scientist who generates hypotheses faster may become constrained by experiments. A manager who receives instant analysis may become constrained by decision rights and organizational politics.

The value stack asks: after this layer gets cheaper, what becomes scarce next?

That question is more useful than asking whether a profession “will be automated.” Professions bundle layers. A physician diagnoses, communicates, reassures, documents, coordinates, accepts liability, performs procedures, and participates in regulated institutions. A teacher explains, motivates, evaluates, supervises, models behavior, and maintains a social environment. A software engineer writes code but also interprets requirements, navigates legacy systems, makes tradeoffs, and bears responsibility for failures.

AI can move through these layers unevenly.

The first economic effect may be compression of routine production. The second may be expansion of demand as lower prices make previously uneconomic work possible. The third may be a redistribution of bargaining power toward whoever controls complementary assets: data, customers, compute, trusted brands, regulated access, or workflow integration.

This is why technological capability does not map cleanly onto financial value. A model can be remarkable while the profit accrues to application companies. An application can be beloved while infrastructure suppliers capture the margin. Infrastructure can become commoditized while a distribution gateway gains power. The winning layer depends on scarcity, substitution, and control.

Forecasts about this should be labeled as forecasts because the stack is adaptive. Firms respond to prices. Open-source alternatives emerge. Customers learn. Regulators intervene. New hardware changes cost curves. Competitors imitate interfaces. Workers redesign jobs. The future is not a static deployment of today's capability.

One robust prediction is narrower: verification becomes more valuable when generation becomes cheap.

Abundant synthetic content increases the relative importance of knowing where something came from, whether it corresponds to reality, who accepts responsibility, and which outputs have survived contact with the world. This does not imply a single technical solution. Provenance can be cryptographic, institutional, social, contractual, or embodied in reputation. The form will differ by domain.

Another robust prediction is that taste rises when possibility explodes. When a tool can produce a thousand plausible options, the ability to choose among them matters more. Taste is not mystical. It is compressed judgment built from exposure, feedback, standards, and a sense of context. It can be trained, though not perfectly formalized.

A third is that organizational adoption becomes a layer of its own. The best model in a benchmark does not automatically become the most valuable system in a company. Security reviews, procurement, integration, employee trust, data access, process redesign, and management incentives determine whether capability turns into productivity.

Technology history is full of delays between invention and institutional reorganization.

The human question is what happens to motivation when competence is partially externalized. People derive meaning from being able to do difficult things. If a machine makes the output easy, the person may feel liberated or diminished depending on whether identity was attached to the task. Organizations that ignore this psychological layer may encounter resistance that looks irrational from a productivity spreadsheet.

Resistance can be self-protection, status defense, legitimate safety concern, or grief for a craft. Often it is several at once.

The wise response is neither romantic refusal nor compulsory enthusiasm. It is to redesign the stack so that humans retain meaningful agency where agency matters. Automation should remove avoidable drudgery without accidentally removing the feedback loops through which novices become experts. If junior workers no longer perform routine tasks, organizations must invent new ways for them to acquire the tacit patterns those tasks once taught.

This is a classic second-order effect: eliminating low-level work can weaken the pipeline that produces high-level judgment.

AI therefore makes the value stack visible inside cognition itself. Perception feeds memory; memory shapes prediction; prediction guides attention; attention determines what becomes new memory. External tools enter this loop. A calculator changes what we practice. Search changes what we remember. Navigation changes how we learn space. Generative systems will change what we rehearse, notice, and consider worth mastering.

The question is not whether tools make us smarter or dumber in the abstract. It is which cognitive layers we outsource and which become more valuable because of the outsourcing.

The answer will differ by person, institution, and era.

But the migration has already begun.
