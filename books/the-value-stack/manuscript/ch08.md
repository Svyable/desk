# The Foundry Split

Morris Chang's great business insight did not require inventing a new transistor.

It required separating two activities that the semiconductor industry had long treated as one.

For much of the industry's early history, leading chip companies designed and manufactured their own products. The model made intuitive sense. Semiconductor design and fabrication were tightly coupled. Process knowledge mattered to design. Manufacturing facilities were extraordinarily technical. Owning the factory seemed inseparable from being a serious semiconductor company.

In 1987, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company was established with a different proposition: manufacture chips for customers without competing with them by selling TSMC-designed chips. The “pure-play foundry” model turned fabrication into a specialized layer that many designers could access.

The move reorganized the value stack.

A startup that wanted to design a chip no longer necessarily needed to finance a leading fabrication plant. That mattered because fabs were becoming more expensive and process development more demanding. By serving many customers, a foundry could concentrate manufacturing investment and expertise. Designers could concentrate on architectures, markets, and intellectual property.

Specialization lowered the barrier to entry above the manufacturing layer.

It also made the manufacturing layer more strategically important.

This is the paradox of modularization. Splitting a stack can make each layer more competitive while simultaneously allowing the best specialist in one layer to become more powerful than any integrated participant had been.

Before the split, manufacturing capacity was buried inside many firms. After specialization, enormous demand could converge on a smaller set of dedicated manufacturers. The layer became accessible to more designers but more concentrated as capability advanced.

TSMC's history is therefore not a story about manufacturing becoming a commodity. It is almost the opposite. It is a story about specialization revealing just how scarce elite manufacturing could become.

The distinction matters because business language often treats outsourcing as evidence that the outsourced activity is low value. In reality, a company may outsource something because an external specialist is better at it, because sharing the specialist's scale lowers cost, or because the capital required is too large to justify internally. None of those conditions make the activity unimportant.

Sometimes outsourcing is a vote of confidence in the supplier's layer.

The foundry model helped enable the rise of fabless semiconductor companies. Designers could pursue markets with less capital than an integrated manufacturer required. The ecosystem around chip design expanded: electronic-design automation, reusable intellectual-property blocks, packaging, testing, equipment, materials, and specialized engineering all became parts of a more modular system.

The stack gained more interfaces.

More interfaces meant more innovation and more dependency.

A chip design could be brilliant and useless if it could not be manufactured with acceptable yield. A foundry process could be extraordinary and unprofitable without enough customer designs. Equipment makers depended on fab investment. Designers depended on software tools. Advanced packaging became increasingly important as system performance depended on how multiple components were combined.

The industry's economics became a lesson in complementarity at extreme scale.

Nobody owned the entire stack, yet no layer could succeed alone.

This interdependence created a different kind of moat from the consumer-brand stories business schools often prefer. TSMC did not need to become famous to smartphone buyers. Its customer relationship sat one or more layers below the consumer. Its value came from technical execution, trust, neutrality, scale, accumulated process knowledge, capital investment, and the ability to deliver designs into silicon reliably.

The trust component is easy to underestimate.

A foundry customer exposes sensitive product designs and future plans. If the manufacturer also sold competing chips, every interaction could raise strategic anxiety. A pure-play foundry reduces one kind of conflict by promising not to compete at the design layer.

Neutrality becomes a product feature.

This appears in other markets too. Exchanges, marketplaces, cloud platforms, payment processors, distributors, and contract manufacturers all face a version of the same question: will the infrastructure provider use privileged information or control to compete with the customers who depend on it?

When the answer becomes uncertain, participants begin calculating the value of alternatives.

Trust in layer boundaries can therefore determine whether specialization works.

A modular stack is held together not only by technical standards but by expectations about behavior.

This is one reason vertical integration and specialization cycle. If a supplier becomes too powerful, customers invest in second sources or internal capability. If an integrated firm becomes too slow or expensive, specialized entrants appear. If interfaces stabilize, markets between layers improve. If a new technical frontier makes interfaces unstable, integration can return.

The semiconductor industry contains all of these motions at once.

At mature process nodes, manufacturing can be available from multiple suppliers and differentiation may lie elsewhere. At leading-edge nodes, the number of firms capable of competitive production shrinks. Packaging that once looked like a final assembly step becomes a frontier for system performance. Chip designers create custom accelerators. Cloud companies commission their own silicon. Equipment manufacturers occupy irreplaceable niches. Governments treat fabrication capacity as national strategy.

The stack refuses to stay in its assigned boxes.

This is a useful corrective to the popular phrase “move up the value chain.” The foundry business shows that moving deeper into a technically difficult lower layer can create extraordinary value if that layer becomes scarce enough.

There is no universal premium for proximity to the consumer.

Consumer businesses can have powerful brands and terrible economics. Industrial suppliers can be obscure and extraordinarily profitable. The relevant questions are substitutability, learning curves, scale, capital barriers, switching costs, qualification, and whether the layer's output is required for the rest of the system to advance.

Qualification is particularly important in high-stakes industries.

A supplier cannot always be replaced by finding another catalog item. Customers may spend months or years validating processes, redesigning products, testing reliability, obtaining regulatory approvals, and tuning operations around a supplier. Once qualified, the supplier acquires a form of embedded trust.

This is not the same as a consumer switching cost. Nobody feels emotional loyalty to a particular process recipe. The cost is institutional and technical.

It can be stronger because it is real.

Procurement departments often seek multiple suppliers precisely to prevent such dependence. The desire for second sources reflects a recognition that efficient specialization creates strategic risk. A company may knowingly pay more to maintain an alternate supplier, share volume, or preserve compatibility.

Redundancy is an option premium.

Finance struggles with this because the benefit is counterfactual. In most periods, the second source looks less efficient than consolidating volume with the cheapest provider. The savings from resilience appear only when the preferred supplier fails, demand spikes, geopolitics changes, or quality problems emerge.

Organizations optimized for quarterly metrics can therefore delete options that matter over longer horizons.

The semiconductor shortages that accompanied the pandemic era made this visible across industries. Automakers, consumer-electronics companies, equipment makers, and governments rediscovered that tiny components could halt expensive products. The monetary value of a component was not proportional to the economic value of having it at the right moment.

A one-dollar part can stop a fifty-thousand-dollar machine.

The stack is full of such asymmetries.

This helps explain why inventory policy cannot be evaluated only by carrying cost. Just-in-time systems can be extraordinarily effective when supply is stable and processes are disciplined. They expose problems rather than hiding them behind stock. But a company imitating “lean” by simply removing buffers while leaving suppliers, forecasts, and operational variability unchanged has not learned the philosophy. It has merely removed slack.

Slack has value when uncertainty is real.

The same is true of manufacturing capacity. Idle capacity looks wasteful until demand surges. Geographic diversification looks expensive until trade routes close. Extra tooling looks redundant until the primary line fails.

There is no fixed correct amount of resilience. The point is that resilience belongs in the value calculation rather than being treated as a sentimental preference.

The foundry split also illuminates the role of learning curves.

Some capabilities improve through accumulated volume. Producing more units creates opportunities to refine process control, discover defects, improve yield, train people, justify better equipment, and spread fixed costs. A specialist serving many customers can therefore become better faster than an internal supplier serving one company.

Scale creates knowledge, not just purchasing power.

This can make a lower layer increasingly difficult to re-create once it has been outsourced for years. The buyer's former capability decays while the supplier's compounds. Eventually “bring it back in-house” is not a procurement decision. It is an industrial-development program.

Governments are learning the same thing.

A manufacturing ecosystem is not a building plus machines. It includes suppliers, technicians, engineers, universities, utilities, construction expertise, chemical handling, maintenance, logistics, regulatory knowledge, and a labor market thick enough that specialized people can move among firms without leaving the region. These are network effects in physical industry.

Clusters are value stacks with geography.

Silicon Valley is often described through venture capital and software entrepreneurship, but its name came from semiconductor history. Taiwan's semiconductor ecosystem developed through institutions, talent, supplier relationships, industrial policy, and decades of cumulative experience. Similar cluster dynamics appear in aerospace, automobiles, finance, film, fashion, and biotechnology.

A region becomes valuable because the layers are close enough to learn from one another.

This is another reason the language of “labor cost” can mislead. A higher-wage region may remain competitive because it reduces other costs: faster iteration, deeper suppliers, better infrastructure, more reliable quality, specialized knowledge, financing, or access to customers. The stack price is not the wage line.

Companies that chase the cheapest visible input can increase the cost of the invisible interfaces around it.

The reverse is also true. Romantic attachment to domestic production can ignore real advantages of specialized global suppliers. The goal is not to assign virtue to one geography. It is to price the whole stack honestly.

That includes geopolitical risk.

Semiconductors became a strategic concern because advanced chips sit under communications, defense, transportation, finance, artificial intelligence, industrial control, and consumer electronics. The layer's importance exceeds its direct share of final product prices. Policymakers began treating fabrication capacity, equipment, design tools, and supply chains as instruments of national power.

Economic stacks can become security stacks when enough other systems depend on them.

This creates difficult trade-offs. Concentrating production can be economically efficient and strategically dangerous. Replicating advanced capacity across countries can improve resilience while raising cost. Export controls can slow adversaries while encouraging them to develop substitutes. Subsidies can build capacity but cannot guarantee competitiveness. National-security arguments can protect genuinely strategic layers or become excuses for ordinary industrial lobbying.

The stack framework does not resolve these disputes. It clarifies what is being argued about.

What layer is scarce? Who controls it? How quickly can it be replaced? What other layers fail if it disappears? How much redundancy is worth purchasing? What behavior will today's restriction induce tomorrow?

That last question is essential.

Power changes the incentives of everyone beneath it.

A foundry with strong pricing power encourages customers to design more efficiently or seek alternatives. An equipment choke point encourages competing countries to fund indigenous equipment. A dominant processor architecture encourages rival architectures. A proprietary interface encourages open substitutes. Scarcity attracts capital and political attention.

This is why the most valuable layer is rarely safe simply because it is valuable.

Its value is an invitation.

The defensive advantage of technically deep layers is that invitations can take years to answer. Capital can be raised quickly compared with the time required to accumulate yield learning, supplier trust, process integration, and operational discipline. A rival can announce a factory long before it can reproduce an ecosystem.

Time is part of the moat.

So is credibility.

Customers placing critical designs with a manufacturer are not purchasing wafers as anonymous units. They are purchasing a probability distribution: the probability that the process works, that schedules hold, that data remains confidential, that capacity is available, that quality is consistent, that future process generations arrive, and that the supplier survives long enough to support the roadmap.

This is value at the trust layer embedded deep inside manufacturing.

The foundry story overturns a simplistic hierarchy of business. Physical production can be a high-value layer. Neutrality can be differentiation. Specialization can create concentration. Outsourcing can increase dependence. Scale can produce knowledge. A company can sit far from the consumer and still govern the pace of entire industries.

Most of all, the story shows that when a stack is split apart, value does not simply rise to the top.

It flows toward the piece everyone else discovers they cannot do without.
