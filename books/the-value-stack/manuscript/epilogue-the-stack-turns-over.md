# The Stack Turns Over

Every stack contains the conditions of its own rearrangement.

A successful layer attracts capital and imitation. Capital expands supply. Imitation standardizes features. Standards reduce differentiation. What was scarce becomes common, and the margin begins to move elsewhere.

This is commoditization, but the word is too passive. Commoditization is an active transfer of power.

When components become interchangeable, buyers gain leverage. When information becomes abundant, attention gains leverage. When production becomes cheap, distribution gains leverage. When distribution becomes open, trust or brand may gain leverage. When brands proliferate, authenticity or community may gain leverage. When digital services become abundant, physical constraints—energy, chips, land, skilled labor—can reassert themselves.

The stack turns over rather than simply climbing.

This is why strategy built on a permanent hierarchy fails. There is no eternal rule that software beats hardware, platforms beat products, brands beat manufacturers, or intellectual property beats commodities. Each claim is conditionally true under a particular scarcity structure.

The task of strategy is to detect migration.

One signal is falling willingness to pay for features that once differentiated the category. Another is rising customer concern about something previously ignored. Another is supplier consolidation. Another is a new standard that makes switching easier. Another is regulation that converts a proprietary advantage into common infrastructure. Another is a shock that exposes a hidden dependency.

Margins are lagging clues. By the time the income statement clearly reveals migration, competitors may already occupy the new layer.

This is where disruption becomes more precise. A disruptive technology does not merely make an old product worse or obsolete. It can change the architecture through which value is created and captured. A new entrant may begin in a low-margin layer incumbents are happy to abandon, then improve until that layer becomes the gateway to the customer. Or a technology may modularize what an integrated incumbent once controlled, allowing specialists to attack pieces of the stack.

Incumbents struggle because their existing economics teach them what to ignore.

A low-margin market looks unattractive to a company optimized for high margins. A simpler product looks inferior when evaluated by the incumbent's best customers. A new distribution model looks small beside established channels. Rational resource allocation can therefore produce strategic blindness.

But disruption is not destiny. Incumbents can adapt when they recognize that protecting the current layer is different from protecting the customer problem. They can create internal businesses with different economics, acquire capabilities, open interfaces, or deliberately cannibalize themselves.

The hardest barrier is often identity.

A company that thinks it is a newspaper may defend paper. A company that thinks it organizes verified information for a community has more options. A company that thinks it sells cars may miss mobility services; a company that thinks it sells mobility may underestimate how much customers still value ownership. Abstraction helps only if it remains grounded.

Too broad an identity becomes meaningless.

The value stack therefore offers a disciplined form of strategic imagination. Start at the current source of customer value. Map the necessary layers beneath it. Mark which are scarce, concentrated, regulated, trusted, or difficult to replace. Then ask how technology, capital, policy, and culture could change each condition.

Do not forecast one future. Build several.

Probability matters because strategy is a portfolio of commitments under uncertainty. Some moves should be reversible experiments. Others require early, expensive commitment precisely because waiting destroys the option. The art is to know which is which.

A semiconductor fab cannot be conjured after demand arrives. A software feature often can. A reputation takes years. An advertising campaign can launch in weeks. A pipeline, mine, trained workforce, scientific field, or diplomatic alliance has a different clock from an app.

Layer maps are therefore also time maps.

This is where finance, technology, and geopolitics meet. Capital markets may reward asset-light models while national strategy values physical capacity. Consumers may reward low prices while resilience requires redundancy. Managers may prefer outsourcing because it improves return on invested capital while the firm quietly loses the tacit knowledge needed to evaluate suppliers.

No single metric can optimize the whole stack.

That is not an excuse for vagueness. It is a reason to make tradeoffs explicit.

The strategist's job is not to predict every turn. It is to preserve enough capability, information, trust, and optionality to survive being wrong.
