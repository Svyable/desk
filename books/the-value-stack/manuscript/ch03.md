# When the Bottom Becomes Cheap

For most of human history, cloth was expensive enough to be inherited.

A shirt represented land that grew fiber, hands that harvested it, labor that cleaned and spun it, skill that wove it, dyes that might have crossed oceans, and hours of sewing. Clothing was repaired because the material itself had weight in the household economy. Inventories of estates listed garments with the seriousness later generations would reserve for furniture or jewelry. A fine piece of fabric could outlive its owner and change hands several times.

Then production changed.

Mechanized spinning and weaving, steam power, factory organization, global cotton networks, chemical dyes, railroads, and later electricity and modern logistics drove down the real cost of making many forms of cloth. The transition was neither clean nor humane. It was entangled with slavery, colonial extraction, dangerous mills, child labor, pollution, labor conflict, and violent changes to older craft economies. But the economic direction was unmistakable: once a scarce transformation became reproducible at scale, the place where value accumulated began to move.

People did not stop caring about clothing when cloth became cheaper. They cared about different things.

Fit mattered. Fashion accelerated. Retail mattered. Distribution mattered. Brand mattered. A garment could become less valuable as fabric and more valuable as a symbol. The same physical category that had once stored household wealth became, at one end of the market, almost disposable and, at another, a canvas for identity and status.

This is one of the recurring motions of the value stack: when a lower layer becomes abundant enough, competition migrates upward.

The phrase “moves up the stack” is tempting because it sounds like progress. It is not always progress. Sometimes abundance below enables astonishing creativity above. Cheap computing makes millions of software experiments possible. Standardized shipping makes global commerce more accessible. Reliable electricity makes modern hospitals and communication possible. But cheapness can also conceal costs that were pushed outside the transaction: depleted soils, carbon emissions, labor exploitation, discarded textiles, fragile supply chains, or communities made dependent on distant production.

The stack tells us where the market's scarcity moved. It does not tell us whether the underlying abundance was socially well designed.

To understand the migration, consider what happens to a layer when its output becomes standardized. Buyers become more confident that one supplier can replace another. Procurement departments gain leverage. Prices become easier to compare. Improvements continue, often rapidly, but suppliers have difficulty charging for each improvement because competitors follow. The value of the capability spreads to customers and to firms in adjacent layers.

This is the paradox of successful infrastructure. The better it works, the less visible its excellence can become.

Electricity is the clearest example. A factory manager in an industrialized economy does not normally choose a city because one utility's electrons have superior personality. What matters is price, reliability, capacity, regulation, and access. The standardization of electric power allowed thousands of higher-level businesses to design around the assumption that a wall outlet would provide compatible energy. Enormous value was created by making electricity boring.

Boring, in this sense, is a compliment. It means a layer has become dependable enough that the next layer can forget about it.

Software engineers perform the same act of forgetting constantly. A programmer using a database does not inspect the magnetic domains or charge states in the storage hardware beneath each query. A designer publishing a website does not negotiate a new transmission protocol with every network operator. The ability to ignore lower layers is a form of economic leverage. It lets attention move upward toward a new problem.

The history of computing can be read as a sequence of such forgettings.

Early computer users had to think about the machine itself. Memory was scarce. Processing was scarce. Storage was scarce. Programs were tightly coupled to particular hardware. Over decades, improvements in semiconductor manufacturing and computer architecture made raw computation dramatically cheaper. Standard operating systems and programming environments insulated developers from hardware details. Networks insulated users from physical distance. Cloud services later insulated companies from many details of owning servers.

Every layer of abstraction opened a new market while placing pressure on the layer below to become more reliable, more standardized, and less demanding of human attention.

The economic rewards did not simply follow technical difficulty. They followed scarcity and control.

A hard drive manufacturer could perform extraordinary engineering and still face brutal price competition if buyers viewed drives of similar specifications as substitutes. An operating system could capture more value if applications and users became dependent on its interfaces. A cloud service could charge for convenience, reliability, global reach, managed operations, and the option to scale without buying hardware months in advance. An application could capture value by solving a specific workflow. A brand built on top of all of these could capture attention and trust.

The stack did not make the lower engineering less important. It made the lower engineering less differentiated from the buyer's perspective.

This distinction is essential because businesses often misread commoditization as insult. Executives hear that a product is becoming a commodity and respond by insisting on its technical superiority. The claim may be true and strategically irrelevant. If customers cannot perceive the difference, do not care about it, or can obtain enough quality from several vendors, the company has lost scarcity even if its engineers are brilliant.

When that happens, the company has several choices.

It can become the lowest-cost producer, turning scale and operational excellence into the scarce capability. It can specialize in a segment where performance differences still matter. It can integrate upward into services, distribution, or a system whose value is easier for customers to perceive. It can build complementary assets that make its product harder to replace. It can exit. Or it can continue telling itself that the market will eventually appreciate craftsmanship that the market has already decided to treat as interchangeable.

The last strategy has funded many sad investor letters.

There is a cultural cost to moving up the stack because identities become attached to layers. A company that thinks of itself as a manufacturer may experience services as undignified or soft. A newspaper that thinks its essence is the printed object may initially treat digital distribution as a lower-quality imitation rather than a change in where readers, advertisers, and attention are assembling. A retailer that takes pride in physical stores may underestimate a channel that begins with worse aesthetics but superior convenience. A university may define itself around lectures even as recorded explanations become abundant, because the deeper layers of its value—credentialing, community, selection, research, network, identity—are harder to name.

Organizations defend the layer that made them successful long after value has begun migrating elsewhere.

This is not mere stupidity. Existing layers contain expertise, jobs, status, assets, routines, measurement systems, and memories of victory. A steel company knows how to measure tons. A software firm knows how to measure licenses. A retailer knows same-store sales. A bank knows net interest margin. When the basis of value changes, the old metrics can continue improving while the strategic position deteriorates.

Clayton Christensen's work on disruption became influential partly because it described one version of this trap: established firms can rationally serve their best customers and improve the dimensions those customers historically valued, while entrants grow in a different performance space. The broader stack perspective adds another question. Is the contest still happening in the same layer?

Sometimes a challenger does not beat the incumbent's product. It makes the product less central.

Digital cameras did not initially need to surpass every property of film to change the value of instant review, storage, duplication, editing, and distribution. Streaming did not need to reproduce the ritual of a video store; it changed the distribution layer so thoroughly that many prior advantages became burdens. Smartphones did not merely make better telephones. They absorbed cameras, maps, music players, boarding passes, wallets, flashlights, clocks, and pieces of dozens of other product categories into an interface that could be continuously extended by software.

When one layer absorbs functions from several adjacent markets, value can collapse around it with surprising speed.

The winners of these transitions often appear visionary in retrospect. In the moment, they are making bets under ambiguity. Moving upward requires investment before the new layer's economics are proven. It can anger existing partners. It can demand different talent. It can weaken the simplicity of the old operating model. It can place a company in competition with its former customers or suppliers.

This is why value migration is easier to describe than to exploit.

A commodity producer may know perfectly well that margins are moving to branded products but lack the distribution, design, marketing, working capital, and cultural instincts to build a brand. A chip manufacturer may see value accumulating in design tools or system architecture but be unable to abandon the capital discipline and neutrality that made its foundry attractive. A consulting firm may see software automating routine analysis but struggle to productize its expertise because custom work and software require different economics.

Capabilities are sticky. Value is not.

The mismatch creates strategic openings for intermediaries. When lower-layer producers cannot easily reach customers, somebody else can aggregate, package, certify, finance, or distribute their output. Commodity coffee beans become roasted blends. Generic hotel rooms become inventory inside a booking platform. Independent software tools become products inside an app store. Small merchants become catalogs inside a marketplace. The intermediary may initially create enormous value by reducing search and transaction costs.

Then, if the intermediary becomes the customer's default route, bargaining power shifts.

The seller discovers that the buyer belongs to the platform. The supplier discovers that its quality is visible only through a ranking system it does not control. The creator discovers that distribution has become a tax. The layer that once solved commoditization for suppliers can eventually commoditize the suppliers themselves.

This is one reason abundance and concentration can grow together.

The internet made publishing, software distribution, communication, and commerce dramatically cheaper. One might have expected the resulting abundance to disperse power automatically. In some areas it did. In others, abundance increased the value of filters, search, reputation, and aggregation. When there are ten websites, finding one is easy. When there are a billion, discovery becomes a scarce service. Cheap creation can make attention more valuable.

This is the second motion of the stack: abundance below produces scarcity above.

It appears in music. Recording technology and digital distribution made it easier to reproduce and deliver songs. That reduced the scarcity of access to recordings. The scarce layers shifted toward discovery, live experience, cultural attention, artist identity, catalog rights, and the ability to gather an audience. A listener can access more music than a record-store owner of the past could have stocked, yet the listener still has only twenty-four hours in a day.

It appears in photography. Billions of images can be produced and stored at negligible marginal cost compared with film. The economic scarcity shifts toward curation, authenticity, reputation, rights, and the image that commands attention in a flood.

It appears in information. Once documents become cheap to duplicate, authority becomes more valuable. Once opinions become abundant, trusted judgment becomes scarce. Once AI can generate competent text at low cost, the value of knowing whether the text is correct, relevant, authorized, original enough, and worth acting on can rise.

Abundance does not end economics. It relocates economics.

The relocation can be uncomfortable for people whose identities were built around producing what has become abundant. A skilled copywriter may watch generic marketing prose become cheap. A junior programmer may see code generation automate tasks that once signaled competence. A financial analyst may see routine modeling accelerate. A teacher may see explanations available everywhere. The instinctive response is to defend the old scarcity by asserting that machines or low-cost competitors cannot replicate the work “properly.” Sometimes that is true. Often it is true only at the high end.

A more useful response is to ask what the newly abundant layer makes possible and what remains scarce around it.

If code is cheaper, understanding requirements may become more important. If prose is cheaper, editorial judgment and distribution may matter more. If information is abundant, trusted synthesis matters. If analysis is abundant, accountability for decisions matters. If content is abundant, community matters. If manufacturing capacity becomes abundant in one generation of technology, design or demand may become the constraint.

People can climb stacks too, but the phrase should be handled carefully. “Higher” does not mean more humanly worthy. A master machinist is not beneath a product manager in any moral sense. A nurse is not beneath a hospital administrator. The layers describe dependency and scarcity, not dignity.

For an individual, moving in the stack means adding forms of value that are less easily substituted around the current skill. A reliable craftsperson learns diagnosis, not only execution. An engineer learns to translate between technical and commercial constraints. A salesperson develops enough domain knowledge to become trusted before a transaction exists. A manager becomes good at creating conditions in which other people can exercise judgment. An investor learns not just valuation technique but when the technique's assumptions are breaking.

The danger is to abandon the substrate too early.

Upper layers are seductive because they often pay better and feel more strategic. Organizations become full of people who want to coordinate, advise, brand, allocate, and supervise, while fewer people understand the work being coordinated. The stack becomes top-heavy. Language floats free of reality. Decisions improve on slides while products worsen in use.

Healthy systems preserve feedback from the layers that touch the physical or operational world.

Toyota's production system became famous not because it treated manufacturing as a low-status substrate to be escaped, but because it made frontline process knowledge central to learning. Great software organizations keep senior engineers close enough to code and users to recognize when abstractions leak. Effective hospitals respect information from nurses and technicians who see patients continuously. Military organizations that ignore logistics eventually rediscover it under the least forgiving conditions imaginable.

The bottom becoming cheap is therefore not permission to stop understanding the bottom.

Cheapness can be temporary. Standardization can hide concentration. Outsourcing can reduce direct cost while increasing dependency. A company can spend years optimizing away “non-core” capabilities and discover during a shock that the discarded layer was also a source of resilience, learning, or negotiating power.

The semiconductor industry offers a particularly vivid example. Specialization allowed firms to separate chip design from manufacturing, unlocking remarkable innovation. Designers could build businesses without funding their own leading-edge fabrication plants. Foundries could concentrate process expertise and capital investment. The modular architecture created value on both sides.

But the more the world built on advanced chips, the more strategic attention returned to fabrication capacity, equipment, materials, packaging, and geography. The layer that specialization had made invisible became visible again because its scarcity mattered at national scale.

This is the third motion of the stack: what becomes boring can become strategic again.

The pattern should make us skeptical of permanent declarations. “Hardware is a commodity.” “Content is king.” “Distribution is everything.” “Data is the new oil.” “Brands are dead.” “Platforms always win.” Each slogan captures a moment in a moving system. The stack asks what conditions made the statement temporarily true.

A layer is cheap until demand outruns supply. It is generic until a performance frontier matters. It is invisible until failure. It is outsourced until dependency becomes dangerous. It is standardized until a new architecture breaks the standard. It is regulated lightly until private power becomes politically salient. It is a cost center until the rest of the business cannot function without it.

The strategic task is not to find the permanently highest layer.

It is to notice where scarcity is going next.
