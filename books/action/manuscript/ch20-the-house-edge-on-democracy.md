# The House Edge on Democracy

Everything this book has examined so far involves a game with a final score. A football game ends, a basketball game ends, and the bet resolves against a fact no one seriously disputes once the clock runs out. Political and election betting removes that anchor. The event a customer is trading — who wins a congressional majority, who becomes the next Federal Reserve chair, whether a specific piece of legislation passes before a specific date — resolves against a fact that is itself often contested, politically charged, and, in the case of an actual election, subject to recounts, certification fights, and occasionally litigation over what the "true" result even was. Betting on democracy's own outcomes is not a hypothetical extension of the sports betting infrastructure this book has spent nineteen chapters describing; it is already a live, regulated, multi-billion-dollar market, built by some of the same companies chapter 18 examined in the sports context, and it has already been tested against a presidential election, two midterm cycles, and at least one state-level attempt to shut it down entirely. This chapter treats that market as its own case rather than a footnote to chapter 18's sports-contract fight, because the stakes and the specific legal history are different enough to deserve separate treatment.

Even PredictIt was not the true starting point, and the deeper history is worth tracing because it shows this exact regulatory argument has now repeated itself three separate times across three decades, each round ending the same way — a novel platform, a CFTC accommodation granted for a narrow purpose, and an eventual crackdown once the platform's real-money volume outgrew whatever narrow purpose had justified the original exemption. The earliest version was the Iowa Electronic Markets, launched in 1988 as the Iowa Political Stock Market by faculty at the University of Iowa's business school, built to forecast that year's presidential race using real-money trading among a deliberately small, tightly capped pool of participants. The CFTC granted the university no-action relief in letters dated February 1992 and June 1993, permitting the market to continue operating specifically because it capped individual risk at amounts as small as five to five hundred dollars, limited each submarket to between one and two thousand traders, and — critically, a condition future platforms would struggle to satisfy — generated no profit of any kind for the university running it. That academic, not-for-profit model held for two decades. It broke down once a for-profit successor tried to scale the same basic idea into something resembling a real market: Intrade, an Irish-registered platform that had become popular enough among American political junkies by 2012 to correctly call forty-nine of fifty states in that year's presidential election, drew a CFTC lawsuit that November alleging it had unlawfully solicited and permitted U.S. customers to trade options products outside the CFTC's approved exchange system since at least 2007. Intrade shut its doors to American customers within weeks, and with them went the majority of its revenue base; the company folded entirely within a year. PredictIt inherited Intrade's former user base almost by default when it launched in 2014, operating under the same kind of narrow academic no-action letter the Iowa markets had pioneered two decades earlier — a structure that would hold for eight years before the CFTC moved against it too, in 2022, in the fight this chapter has already traced through to its 2025 resolution. Kalshi and Polymarket's current approach — full, permanent commercial registration rather than a revocable academic accommodation — is best understood as this pattern's third iteration trying, for the first time, to build something the CFTC cannot simply decide to unwind by letter: a fully licensed exchange rather than a research project the agency was only ever tolerating.

The most immediate chapter in that longer history belongs to PredictIt, whose own near-decade-long fight with its federal regulator is worth understanding in full because it previews almost every legal maneuver that would later appear in the Kalshi sports-contract fight chapter 18 described. PredictIt launched in 2014 as a research project affiliated with Victoria University of Wellington in New Zealand, operating in the United States under a no-action letter from the Commodity Futures Trading Commission — a formal statement that CFTC staff would not recommend enforcement action against the platform, issued specifically to support the academic research use case Victoria University had proposed, with the market capped at small trade sizes explicitly to keep it below the scale of a genuine commercial derivatives exchange. PredictIt operated under that arrangement for eight years, becoming, in the process, one of the most widely cited sources for real-time political-probability data in American journalism, routinely quoted by outlets covering close elections and legislative fights. In August 2022, the CFTC abruptly withdrew the no-action letter, giving PredictIt six months to wind down operations, and offered little public explanation for exactly which terms the agency believed the platform had violated. A coalition of PredictIt traders, academic researchers, and the Texas-based nonprofit Aristotle sued in federal court in Texas, arguing the withdrawal was arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act — the same basic legal framework federal agencies must satisfy any time they reverse a prior position without adequate justification. In July 2023, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals sided with PredictIt's supporters, reversing the district court and directing it to enter a preliminary injunction against the CFTC, reasoning that a no-action letter, once granted and relied upon for years, functioned enough like a license that withdrawing it counted as a reviewable final agency action rather than an internal staff decision the agency could reverse without explanation. The case, formally Clarke v. CFTC, finally reached a full resolution on July 22, 2025 — nearly three years after the original withdrawal notice — when the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Texas vacated both the original 2022 withdrawal notice and a follow-up 2023 notice, finding each arbitrary, capricious, and contrary to law. PredictIt had, by then, survived a regulatory shutdown attempt that lasted longer than some presidential terms.

The regulatory picture in the months before Judge Cobb's ruling was, briefly, almost the exact opposite of where it ended up, which is worth pausing on because it shows how contingent the eventual outcome actually was. In May 2024, four months before Kalshi's court victory, the CFTC itself voted 3-2, strictly along the party lines of its Democratic and Republican appointees, to advance a proposed rule that would have gone considerably further than simply denying Kalshi's specific congressional-control contracts — it would have banned election, sports, and award-contest event contracts across the board, formally classifying them as "gaming" contrary to the public interest and placing them, as a matter of federal derivatives law, in the same prohibited category as contracts wagering on war, terrorism, or assassination. The commission opened a public comment period on the proposal, later extended to August 8, 2024, drawing formal opposition from advocacy groups including Better Markets and Public Citizen, who argued that letting large financial positions accumulate around election outcomes created exactly the manipulation and interference risk this chapter has already described, and formal support from prediction-market advocates who argued the same markets served a genuine, well-documented forecasting function. The proposed rule was still pending, unfinished, when Judge Cobb ruled against the CFTC's narrower congressional-control decision that September — a court loss on one specific product line landing in the middle of the same agency's attempt to ban the entire product category by regulation. The broader ban was never finalized. Kalshi's court victory and the D.C. Circuit's refusal to stay it effectively mooted the more sweeping rulemaking before the CFTC could complete it, a sequence of events that turned on which of two parallel processes — a federal court's docket and a federal agency's rulemaking calendar — happened to move faster, rather than on any settled judgment about whether election contracts should exist at all.

Kalshi entered the same regulatory space PredictIt had spent nearly a decade defending, but took a fundamentally different route to get there — not a research exemption from enforcement, but full registration as a designated contract market, the same formal CFTC status that governs conventional commodity and financial-derivatives exchanges. That distinction mattered enormously once Kalshi tried to extend its registered product line into election outcomes specifically. In 2023, Kalshi asked the CFTC to approve a new category of contracts it called Congressional Control Contracts, letting customers trade on which party would control the House and Senate after the November 2024 elections. The CFTC refused, arguing the contracts amounted to a form of gaming or election gambling — a category the agency's own governing statute bars it from approving, on the theory that betting on the outcome of an election implicates the same public-interest concerns that make ordinary political wagering illegal in most states. Kalshi sued, and on September 12, 2024, U.S. District Judge Jia Cobb of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia ruled decisively in Kalshi's favor, granting summary judgment and vacating the CFTC's rejection. Judge Cobb's reasoning was narrower than it might sound: the ruling did not hold that election contracts were categorically legal or that the CFTC lacked any authority to regulate them, but found specifically that the CFTC had erred in classifying congressional-control contracts as "gaming" under the statutory definition the agency had invoked, since the contracts did not involve an activity independently illegal under state law in the way the statute's gaming exclusion contemplated. The CFTC filed an immediate notice of appeal to the D.C. Circuit and sought an emergency stay of Judge Cobb's order pending that appeal — a request that, if granted, would have kept election contracts off the market through the November 2024 vote. The D.C. Circuit denied the stay. Kalshi's congressional-control markets went live on October 4, 2024, just over four weeks before Election Day, the first time in modern American history a federally regulated exchange had offered a public, real-money market on the partisan outcome of a sitting Congress's control.

The volume that moved through political prediction markets in the following weeks gives some sense of how quickly a market can scale once the legal question clears, even provisionally and even with an appeal still technically pending. Kalshi's own presidential-race market, launched later than its congressional-control contracts, still passed roughly $30 million in trading volume within its first three weeks and had reached nearly $85 million by late October 2024 — a fraction of the total political betting activity that month, because Polymarket, unconstrained by the CFTC registration fight since it was still operating offshore at that point and not soliciting U.S. customers through its own compliant channel, had built by far the larger market. Polymarket's total trading volume across all markets rose from roughly $73 million in all of 2023 to approximately $9 billion in 2024, and the single largest contributor to that growth was the 2024 presidential race itself, whose winner-take-all market alone accounted for more than $3.3 billion in trading volume — making it, by trading volume, the largest single prediction-market event ever recorded up to that point, on a platform most American bettors reached, at the time, through workarounds rather than a fully domestic, CFTC-compliant product. Polymarket's own path back to full, direct U.S. legality followed the route chapter 18 already described in the sports context: a $112 million acquisition of the CFTC-licensed exchange QCX in July 2025, a CFTC no-action letter that September, and a full amended order of designation that November, positioning the platform to compete for the next major election cycle as a domestic, registered operator rather than an offshore one American customers had to route around.

Before turning to how the market has grown since 2024, it is worth crediting the strongest argument in these platforms' favor, because a fair account of political betting cannot only be a chronicle of legal fights and structural risk. Prediction-market advocates have long argued that a market where real money is on the line aggregates information more honestly than a poll ever can, since a trader with a financial stake has a direct incentive to be right rather than to signal a preference — and the 2024 presidential race gave that argument real, if imperfect, supporting evidence. Both Kalshi and Polymarket priced a Trump win with more confidence in the campaign's final days than most public polling averages, which continued showing a race within a percentage point or two of a toss-up almost to Election Day; Polymarket's own contract settled around 58 to 42 in Trump's favor shortly before the vote, a wider and, as it turned out, more accurate margin than the polling consensus captured. But the fuller record, once researchers examined the platforms' performance across thousands of down-ballot contracts rather than just the headline presidential race, is considerably less flattering to the "markets beat polls" narrative that took hold in the race's immediate aftermath. One independent analysis examining more than 2,500 individual election markets across the three major platforms found PredictIt's smaller, more tightly curated market correctly predicted outcomes roughly 93 percent of the time, meaningfully outperforming Kalshi's 78 percent and Polymarket's 67 percent — with accuracy on all three platforms falling sharply in thinner, lower-liquidity markets covering down-ballot races, or in genuinely niche proposition contracts, like whether a candidate would use a specific word in a debate, that behave less like efficient forecasting tools and more like the same kind of novelty prop bet chapter 8 of this book examined in the sports context. The honest conclusion is neither "prediction markets are a truth machine" nor "prediction markets are worthless," but something closer to what this book has argued about the sports-betting line itself in earlier chapters: a market price reflects the balance of money placed on it, which correlates with genuine information some of the time and with liquidity, attention, and a handful of large traders' private research the rest of the time — Théo's $80 million position being the clearest documented case of the latter.

That next cycle arrived quickly, and it confirmed political prediction markets were not a one-time novelty tied to a single presidential race. The November 2025 midterm elections drew a combined total of roughly $197 to $200 million in trading volume across Kalshi and Polymarket's election markets — smaller than the 2024 presidential figure by an order of magnitude, as expected for an off-cycle midterm with lower national attention, but still a sum considerably larger than the total handle many individual states' entire legal sports betting markets recorded in their own first full year of operation after 2018, a comparison worth sitting with for what it says about how quickly this specific product category has scaled relative to the sports-betting buildout the rest of this book has documented taking years, state by state, to reach.

The legal fight over whether any of this should be happening at all has not settled down since 2024; if anything, it has widened past the presidential and congressional contracts Judge Cobb ruled on, into state-by-state skirmishes that echo the sports-contract cease-and-desist campaign chapter 18 detailed in granular, state-by-state form. In August 2026, a Washington state judge barred Kalshi from continuing to offer election contracts within the state, a ruling that applied the same core theory Nevada's Ninth Circuit victory over sports contracts had established weeks earlier — that a state's own gambling law can reach a CFTC-registered product when the product functions, in the customer's actual experience, as a bet on an outcome rather than a hedge against a genuine commercial risk. That Washington ruling did not arise from the same Third Circuit versus Ninth Circuit sports-contract litigation chapter 18 traced in detail, but it drew on the identical underlying legal question — state gaming law versus federal derivatives preemption — now being litigated separately in the specific context of election markets, a second, parallel front to the sports-contract fight rather than a subset of it. Whether the Supreme Court, if it eventually grants New Jersey's pending certiorari petition from the sports-contract fight, resolves the preemption question broadly enough to settle the election-contract cases too, or whether election contracts end up requiring their own separate appellate resolution, is a genuinely open question this book's research, conducted in September 2026, cannot answer with confidence.

What makes political and election betting a distinct case from the sports-contract fight, rather than merely a variation on the same jurisdictional dispute, is the nature of the thing being priced. A sportsbook's same-game parlay chapter 8 examined in detail creates financial incentive around an outcome — who wins a football game — that no bettor, however large their position, has any realistic ability to influence. A market on which party controls the Senate, or whether a specific candidate wins a primary, prices an outcome that a sufficiently resourced participant could, at least in principle, attempt to influence through means entirely unrelated to the honest expression of a political preference: coordinated disinformation timed to move a specific contract's price before a market-moving news cycle breaks, manipulative trading in a thinly traded state-level contract to create a false signal that outside political operatives, journalists, or campaign donors might mistake for genuine predictive information, or — in the starkest version of the concern election-security researchers have raised in testimony to state legislatures — an incentive structure in which someone holding a large position on an election's outcome has a direct financial stake in that outcome actually occurring, layered on top of whatever ordinary political stake they might already hold. None of this book's research turned up a documented, proven case of election-outcome market manipulation actually swinging an American election result or its public perception as of this book's writing; this is a risk this chapter is naming as a structural concern raised by researchers and by state officials in litigation and legislative testimony, not a proven harm chapter 14's evidentiary standard for the sports-betting numbers would require before stating it as settled fact. But the concern is different in kind, not merely in degree, from anything the sports-contract fight raises, because a sportsbook customer betting on a football game has no lever to pull that affects the score, while a sufficiently large political-contract position is, in a narrow but real sense, itself a form of political speech and political stake-taking dressed in the same "just a trade" language Kalshi and Polymarket use for their sports products.

The clearest documented illustration of just how large and information-driven a single position in this market can get involves no proven manipulation at all, which is what makes it useful: it shows the structural concern operating in plain sight, entirely within the rules. In the weeks before the 2024 election, a French trader who identified himself only as Théo built a position on a Trump victory that grew, across four separate Polymarket accounts, to more than $30 million and eventually, according to his own later account, closer to $80 million by Election Day. Théo's stated edge was not inside information about the election's outcome but a bet on the polling methodology itself: he personally commissioned battleground-state surveys from YouGov, the same firm that also conducts polling for CBS News, and found those commissioned polls showing meaningfully more support for Trump in Pennsylvania, Michigan, and Wisconsin than the public polling average reflected — a discrepancy he read as evidence the public polls were underestimating Trump's true support, and he sized his position accordingly. Polymarket's own investigation into the unusually large, concentrated trading confirmed Théo's identity and found no evidence of rule-breaking; when Trump won, Théo's position resolved for a profit exceeding $80 million, making him, by a wide margin, the single largest documented winner of the 2024 election-contract cycle. Nothing about Théo's trade was manipulation in any legal sense — commissioning your own polling and trading on your own analysis is exactly the kind of research-driven position-taking a derivatives market is supposed to reward. But it is also a vivid illustration of the structural asymmetry this chapter has been describing in the abstract: a single, sufficiently resourced trader was able to generate proprietary information about a presidential election's likely outcome, unavailable to the ordinary voter or the ordinary small trader on the other side of his contracts, and turn that information into a nine-figure financial stake in one candidate winning — a stake that, unlike an ordinary campaign donation subject to federal disclosure limits and public reporting, appeared nowhere in any campaign-finance filing, because it was not, in the law's eyes, a campaign contribution at all. It was a trade.

Nevada's regulators, the same body chapter 17 credited with decades of comparatively boring, scandal-light sports betting oversight, have been explicit that this distinction is exactly why they have fought Kalshi as hard on election contracts as on sports ones, treating both as gambling requiring the same licensing and consumer-protection scrutiny regardless of the underlying event's civic significance — a position that, if it prevails at the Supreme Court, would fold election betting back into the same fifty-state licensing and taxation architecture chapters 15 and 16 spent this book's fourth part describing, taxed, monitored, and subject to the same self-exclusion and advertising rules that, however imperfect, at least give a regulator formal standing to investigate a specific complaint. If Kalshi's Third Circuit reasoning prevails instead, or if the Supreme Court declines to resolve the split at all, betting on the outcome of American elections will continue operating, as it has since September 2024, under a federal derivatives framework built for hedging commodity price risk, overseen by an agency with no statutory mandate to ask the question a state election-integrity office or a state gaming commission would ask by default: not merely whether the market is functioning efficiently, but whether the fact of the market's existence, and the size of the positions accumulating inside it, is itself doing something to the elections it prices.
