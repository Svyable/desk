# The Counter-Argument

This chapter's placement here, after ten chapters spent documenting how thoroughly the legal sports betting industry has engineered its product to maximize handle, is deliberate rather than incidental, and it is worth explaining why before making the case itself. Scattering this chapter's arguments across the book — a rebuttal after chapter 8's account of in-play betting, another after chapter 9's whale hunters, another after chapter 14's numbers on financial harm — would have let each individual counterpoint blunt its corresponding chapter's force in the moment, diluting the cumulative weight this book has spent its middle chapters building case by case. Gathering the strongest version of the opposing argument here instead lets that evidence stand fully on its own first, and then subjects it to the most serious challenge available, in one place, so that both the accumulated case for harm and its genuine limits can be weighed together rather than piecemeal. What follows is not a strawman built to be knocked down. It is the argument a serious, well-informed skeptic of this book's thesis would actually make, using real numbers, and this book has tried to give it the same rigor it has demanded of every other party in this story.

Start with the number every account of pre-2018 sports betting eventually cites, because it is worth examining rather than repeating uncritically. For years the American Gaming Association put the size of the illegal, pre-legalization American sports betting market at roughly $150 billion wagered annually — a figure that appeared in AGA press materials, in congressional testimony, and in hundreds of news articles covering the run-up to and aftermath of Murphy v. NCAA. The number's actual origin, as CNBC's reporting traced in the days after the 2018 ruling, was a 1999 federal government estimate of roughly $80 billion in illegal sports wagering, which the AGA then adjusted forward to 2017 dollars using GDP growth rather than any new underlying survey of actual betting activity — a methodology economists interviewed at the time considered shaky at best, since betting volume has no obvious reason to track GDP growth at a constant rate over eighteen years, and no comparable large-scale survey of illegal betting volume exists for any year between 1999 and 2017 to check the extrapolation against. The honest position, and the one this book takes, is that the pre-2018 illegal market was large — probably tens of billions of dollars annually, likely more — and that its exact size is genuinely unknowable, because illegal markets by definition do not file the kind of returns a regulator could audit. What is not in serious dispute is the qualitative picture underneath that contested number: before 2018, a person who wanted to bet on a football game outside Nevada had two realistic options, an office pool or a bookie, and a bookie's business model has nothing built into it resembling a consumer protection.

That absence is where the strongest version of the pro-legalization case actually lives, and it deserves to be stated plainly rather than assumed. An illegal bookmaker offers no self-exclusion program, because a self-exclusion program requires a regulator to enforce it and a paper trail the operator has every incentive to avoid creating. An illegal bookmaker offers no dispute resolution process if a bettor believes a line was manipulated or a payout was shorted, because the entire relationship exists outside any court a bettor could credibly threaten to use. An illegal bookmaker extends credit routinely — the debt-and-collection relationship at the center of decades of organized-crime prosecutions, from the Rothstein-linked fixing of the 1919 World Series through the mid-century Meyer Lansky and Frank Costello operations that ran alongside Nevada's own early legal casinos, down through the mob-linked bookmaking rings federal prosecutors were still unwinding in New York as recently as 2018 — and a bettor who cannot pay that debt has no bankruptcy court to petition, only a debt collector with a very different set of tools than a bank's. Sportico's 2025 reporting on ongoing mob-linked sports betting prosecutions found organized crime had not actually vanished from the illegal market even after legalization gave most bettors a legal alternative; it had simply lost the share of customers who moved to regulated apps, while continuing to operate for the customers a regulated operator would refuse — bettors who wanted more credit than a legal sportsbook's responsible-gaming rules allow, or who had already been excluded from the legal market and needed somewhere else to go. That last category matters enormously for how this book's own argument should be read: every consumer protection this book has criticized as inadequate in the legal market — chapter 17's own account of Nevada's self-exclusion gap below is a case in point — is still a protection that exists nowhere at all in the market legalization was built to replace.

Nor is the illegal market simply a historical relic this book can safely treat in the past tense. The AGA's own August 2025 research, produced by the same trade association whose $150 billion pre-2018 figure this chapter just treated skeptically, estimated that Americans still wager roughly $673.6 billion annually with illegal and unregulated operators — offshore sportsbooks, unlicensed skill-game machines, and traditional bookies alike — generating an estimated $53.9 billion in revenue for those operators and costing state governments an estimated $15.3 billion in taxes that would otherwise have gone through a licensed, monitored book. By the AGA's own accounting, illegal and unregulated gambling still accounts for nearly a third of the total American gaming market, a share the association says grew by roughly 22 percent between its 2022 and 2025 estimates even as legal sports betting expanded into a dozen additional states over the same period — a genuinely uncomfortable data point for a simple "legalization solved the problem" narrative, since it suggests the legal and illegal markets have been growing in parallel rather than the legal one steadily crowding the illegal one out. This book takes AGA-sourced market-sizing estimates with real caution, for reasons this chapter has already laid out. But the qualitative pattern behind the number is corroborated by evidence with no comparable industry interest in the outcome: federal and state prosecutors are still, in 2025 and 2026, actively unwinding organized-crime bookmaking operations that have simply adapted to a world where a legal alternative exists. In November 2025, the New Jersey Attorney General's office charged fourteen people, including Joseph "Little Joe" Perna, identified in the indictment as a soldier in the Lucchese crime family, with running an illegal bookmaking ring that used offshore gambling websites and recruited operators — among them, prosecutors allege, multiple college athletes — to run local sports books across several states, tracking roughly $2 million in transactions between 2022 and 2024. The specific customer base prosecutors described was telling: college students, a population the legal market is barred by law from serving at all in most states, and precisely the demographic this book's own chapter 13 identified as carrying the heaviest concentration of new problem-gambling cases. Where the legal market refuses to serve a customer — because they are underage, because they have hit a deposit limit, because they have self-excluded — the illegal market this chapter has been describing is still there, still recruiting, still extending the kind of informal credit a regulated operator legally cannot.

Nevada is the steelman's best piece of long-run evidence, and it deserves more credit than this book's earlier chapters, focused on the post-2018 national market, have had occasion to give it. Nevada legalized sports betting in 1949 and ran it, essentially alone, for nearly seven decades — through the 1992 federal ban that outlawed the same activity everywhere else, through the entire arc of the internet's arrival, through decades in which a skeptic might reasonably have expected a legal, regulated, taxed sportsbook industry to produce some major scandal on the scale of the point-shaving cases or mob-infiltration stories that had defined the pre-regulation era. It largely didn't. Nevada's sportsbooks operated, year after year, as a genuinely minor, well-understood piece of a larger casino-resort economy, subject to a licensing and auditing regime the state had forty extra years to refine before any other state had to build one from scratch. The Nevada Gaming Control Board's own historical handle data, tracked continuously since 1984, shows a market that grew steadily but never became a locus of the kind of periodic crisis that recurred, for instance, in Nevada's casino-credit and loan-sharking scandals of the 1970s and 1980s. Whatever else this book has argued about the post-2018 national market's design, Nevada's own decades of experience is real evidence that a regulated, legal sportsbook industry can operate for a very long time without becoming an engine of the kind of acute social harm its critics, in 1949 and in 2018, both predicted. The scale itself is worth noting: Nevada's sportsbooks handled roughly $7.1 billion in wagers in 2025 alone, a figure that would have been unimaginable to the state's own regulators in the industry's early decades and that now represents just one part of a national handle running into the hundreds of billions — yet Nevada's own licensing and enforcement apparatus, built gradually since 1949 rather than assembled in the eighteen-month sprint most other states underwent after 2018, has continued functioning through that growth with the same basic structure of background-checked operators, audited books, and a state gaming control board with the statutory authority to suspend or revoke a license outright, a power every other legal state has since copied in some form. That licensing threat is not merely theoretical, and it points to the steelman's other genuinely strong, underappreciated argument: a bettor with a real grievance against a licensed operator — a disputed payout, a line that moved suspiciously, an account frozen without explanation — has a state regulatory body to complain to, one with subpoena power and the ability to fine or delicense the operator, a remedy that simply does not exist in any form against an offshore book or a bookie's ledger. It is a slower, more bureaucratic form of justice than a bettor might want in the moment, and this book has no illustration of how often it actually works well or badly at scale — state gaming regulators do not publish comprehensive national data on bettor complaints resolved in the bettor's favor, which is itself a real limitation this book flags rather than papers over. But its mere existence, as an avenue that can be invoked at all, is not nothing, and the illegal market this book has spent this chapter describing offers no equivalent whatsoever.

The academic literature on problem-gambling prevalence, the ground this book's own thesis depends on most directly, is genuinely more contested than chapter 14's numbers alone convey, and a fair chapter has to say so plainly. The most widely cited recent findings on the harmful side are real and well constructed: an NBER working paper by economists including Brett Baker and Jonathan Meer, examining credit-bureau records across states that legalized online betting, found bankruptcy filings rising by something in the range of ten to thirty percent in the years following legalization, alongside measurable increases in debt collections, auto-loan delinquency, and debt-consolidation borrowing; a separate study by Brett Hollenbeck and coauthors at UCLA and USC, published in Management Science and drawing on credit-bureau data covering more than four million consumers, found legal online sports betting access associated with lower credit scores and higher bankruptcy rates, concluding in the authors' own words that gambling legalization measurably harms consumer financial health. Those are two independently constructed, peer-reviewed or peer-review-track studies reaching compatible conclusions using different data sources, which is a meaningfully strong form of evidence. But they are not the only recent academic word on the subject. A 2025 longitudinal study published in a leading gambling-studies journal tracked 112 American sports bettors over two years using a natural-experiment design built around staggered state legalization timing, and found no statistically significant interaction between time and legalization status in problem-gambling severity scores — a result the study's own authors were careful to describe as inconclusive rather than exonerating, since their own equivalence testing failed to establish that legalization's effect was small enough to dismiss as practically negligible. That is a genuinely different kind of finding than "no harm exists"; it is closer to "this particular small panel could not detect the harm the larger credit-bureau studies found," which is itself informative about how hard the underlying question is to answer cleanly, and how much depends on whether a study is powered, as the credit-bureau research is, to detect effects distributed thinly across millions of accounts rather than concentrated dramatically in a panel of a few hundred people.

The National Council on Problem Gambling's own long-running national survey data, the best available continuous measure of self-reported risky gambling behavior in the country, tells a story more complicated than either "legalization caused a crisis" or "legalization changed nothing." The council's 2021 survey, fielded when legal sports betting was live in twenty-one states, found the share of adults reporting potentially harmful gambling behavior "many times" in the past year had risen from 7 percent in 2018 to 11 percent in 2021 — a jump that coincided with both expanding legal access and the isolating, screen-heavy conditions of the pandemic, two forces the survey design cannot cleanly separate from each other. By the council's 2024 survey, with legal betting live in thirty-eight states, that figure had come back down to roughly 8 percent, essentially back near its 2018 starting point, even as overall sports betting participation held flat at about 23 percent of adults — a pattern the council's own researchers read as evidence that legalization did not durably expand the population engaging in risky behavior so much as it concentrated betting activity, and its risks, more intensely among people who were already betting. The same survey found the share of sports bettors making parlay wagers nearly doubled between 2018 and 2024, from 17 to 30 percent — a data point this book's own chapter 8 would read as evidence of successful behavioral engineering toward the highest-margin product, and a skeptic reading the same survey could just as reasonably read as evidence that overall population-level harm, the actual public-health metric that should matter most, has not moved nearly as much as the intensity of engineering directed at the existing betting population might have predicted.

The academic disagreement runs one layer deeper still, and a fair chapter has to include the layer that argues against this book's own thesis most directly rather than stopping at the two credit-bureau studies already discussed. The Progressive Policy Institute, a center-left think tank without the peer-review standing of a journal like Management Science or an NBER working paper series, published its own analysis of state-level bankruptcy filings between 2019 and 2024 and reached a strikingly different headline conclusion: bankruptcies fell by roughly 40 percent over that period in the earliest states to legalize online sports betting, compared with a 34 percent decline nationally — the opposite direction from the household-level credit-bureau findings, using a coarser, state-aggregate unit of analysis rather than individual account records. This book does not weight the Progressive Policy Institute's aggregate state-level comparison as heavily as the Baker-Meer and Hollenbeck teams' household-level, difference-in-differences designs, which control for far more of the noise a simple before-and-after state comparison cannot — national bankruptcy rates were falling across nearly every state through most of this period for reasons entirely unrelated to sports betting, from post-pandemic stimulus effects to a historically tight labor market, and a study that does not isolate betting-state households from otherwise-similar non-betting-state households risks crediting sports betting access with a decline that was happening everywhere regardless. But the disagreement itself is real, it comes from researchers making a genuine empirical claim rather than an industry talking point, and a reader deserves to know that the finding this book leans on in chapter 14 is contested by at least one study reaching the opposite conclusion using a different, if less rigorous, method.

A skeptic pressing this argument further would also point out, correctly, that the financial-harm studies this book leans on most heavily measure correlation with genuine statistical sophistication but cannot fully rule out a harder counterfactual problem: state legalization decisions are not randomly assigned, and a state's decision to legalize, along with the timing of that decision, may itself correlate with other economic conditions — a state under fiscal pressure looking for new revenue, a regional economy already showing signs of household financial strain — that could independently drive some of the bankruptcy and debt-collection trends these studies attribute to betting access. Both the Baker-Meer team and the Hollenbeck team used difference-in-differences and related quasi-experimental designs specifically built to address this concern, comparing legalizing states against otherwise-similar non-legalizing states over the same period rather than simply comparing a single state before and after its own legalization date — real methodological effort to isolate the effect, not a naive before-and-after comparison. But no research design fully eliminates the possibility that some unobserved factor correlated with both a state's decision to legalize and its residents' financial trajectory is doing part of the work these studies attribute to betting access itself, and reasonable economists reading the same papers have flagged that residual uncertainty as a live methodological question rather than a settled one.

There is a final, more uncomfortable argument the steelman makes, and this book owes it a direct answer rather than a polite mention. Critics of this book's overall framing could reasonably note that a narrative built around chapters on in-play betting design, VIP whale-hunting, and behavioral engineering is, almost by its choice of subject matter, going to find engineering wherever it looks — the same way a book about food additives will always be able to find an additive, and a book about advertising will always be able to find manipulation, regardless of whether the underlying product is, on net, worse than its predecessor. This is a fair methodological caution, and this book concedes it rather than arguing around it: choosing to write ten chapters about product design and financial harm, rather than ten chapters about the consumer-welfare gains of a taxed, monitored, dispute-resolvable betting market, is itself an editorial choice that shapes which facts end up in front of the reader, even when — as this book has tried to ensure throughout — every individual fact presented is real, dated, and checkable.

What this book does not concede, because the evidence assembled in its earlier chapters does not support conceding it, is that the specific mechanisms documented there — same-game parlays engineered around the reality that a bettor overestimates the true combined probability of several correlated outcomes, in-play micro-betting cadences modeled explicitly on slot-machine reinforcement schedules, VIP outreach programs that identify and specifically cultivate the heaviest-losing customers rather than discouraging them — are ordinary, incidental features of a competitive consumer product rather than deliberate design choices aimed at maximizing handle among the customers least equipped to sustain it. The steelman in this chapter is strongest exactly where it stays empirical: the illegal market this legal one replaced really did lack every consumer protection this book has found inadequate in the legal market, Nevada really did run a regulated sportsbook industry for decades without collapsing into scandal, and the academic case for large-scale, population-wide harm really is less unanimous than a reader who has only encountered this book's own chapter 14 might assume. The steelman is weakest where it tries to extend those true facts into a claim this book's earlier research does not support: that because the replacement is measurably better than what it replaced on several real dimensions, the specific product decisions examined in chapters 6 through 10 are therefore defensible, or incidental, rather than what the evidence in those chapters shows them to be — a business that discovered, the same way slot-machine manufacturers discovered a half-century earlier, that engineering a product around near-misses, frictionless re-betting, and continuous small rewards produces more revenue from the same population than a slower, plainer version of the identical product would, and built accordingly.

Both of those things are true at once, and holding them together, rather than collapsing them into a single verdict, is the harder and more honest position this book has tried to take from its opening pages. The legal market is a real, measurable improvement over the illegal one on self-exclusion, on dispute resolution, on organized crime's reduced reach, and on Nevada's own long, comparatively boring regulatory track record. It is also, simultaneously, a product whose specific engineering choices — not its mere existence, but its particular design — this book's evidence shows were selected because they increase how much and how often a subset of vulnerable bettors lose money, in ways the pre-2018 bookie, working by hand, one customer at a time, from a notebook rather than a machine-learning model, never had the scale or the tools to replicate. A reader who finishes this chapter convinced that legalization was, on net, worth doing is not wrong. A reader who finishes it convinced that being worth doing settles every question about how the resulting product should be built, taxed, or restrained is missing the distinction this book's remaining chapters are built to make.
