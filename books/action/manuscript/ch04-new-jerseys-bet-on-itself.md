# New Jersey's Bet on Itself

Atlantic City had been dying for a long time before Chris Christie decided that legal sports betting was worth staking his administration's credibility on to try to save it. The Boardwalk casino industry that New Jersey had legalized in 1976, in a referendum sold to the state's voters as the mechanism that would rescue a decayed resort town on the strength of gambling revenue alone, had spent its first quarter century essentially unchallenged as the only legal full-scale casino destination on the East Coast. That monopoly ended, decisively and permanently, once Pennsylvania, Delaware, New York, and other neighboring states began licensing their own casinos through the 2000s, and Atlantic City's revenue collapsed under competition it had never been designed to withstand: casinos closed, thousands of jobs disappeared, and the boardwalk that had once drawn millions of visitors a year from Philadelphia and New York began to look, in press coverage from the period, less like a resort and more like a cautionary tale about what happens to a one-industry town when that industry's regional monopoly ends. Christie, who took office as governor in January 2010 in the middle of that collapse, needed something Atlantic City did not already have, and sports betting — legal in Nevada, illegal almost everywhere else, and already flowing through Atlantic City's economy anyway in the form of an illegal market nobody seriously denied existed — looked, to Christie and to the New Jersey legislature, like the most obvious available answer. The scale of what Atlantic City had actually lost by the time Christie fully committed to the fight is worth stating in specific terms, because it explains the urgency behind a legal strategy that most outside observers, at the time, considered a near-certain loser. Four of the resort city's twelve casinos closed within 2014 alone — the Atlantic Club in January, the Showboat in August, Revel on September 2, and Trump Plaza on September 16 — throwing roughly eight thousand people out of work in a single year, and a fifth, the Trump Taj Mahal, would close before the end of 2016, putting another three thousand jobs at risk. The Atlantic City metropolitan region lost more jobs over a twelve-month span during this period than any of the more than three hundred other metro areas the federal Bureau of Labor Statistics tracked nationally, a distinction that made the city, briefly, the worst-performing local labor market in the entire country. Christie's push for sports betting cannot be separated from that collapse; it was not a speculative side project pursued out of general enthusiasm for gambling expansion, but a targeted attempt to give a specific, visibly dying local economy one more legal product to sell before the boardwalk's casino industry shrank past the point of no return.

The political groundwork came first, in the form of a nonbinding 2011 ballot referendum asking New Jersey voters whether the state constitution should be amended to permit sports wagering at casinos and racetracks. It passed by a margin of roughly two to one, with about 64 percent of voters in favor, a result decisive enough that Christie, who had already signaled his support before the vote, treated it as a clear public mandate rather than a close call requiring further deliberation. "The fact is now gaming is everywhere in many states across the country, so there's no reason why sports gaming in my view should be restricted," Christie told reporters around the time of the vote, framing the issue less as a moral question about gambling than as a question of competitive fairness between states — an argument that would remain the core of New Jersey's public case for the next seven years. The legislature followed the referendum with New Jersey's 2012 Sports Wagering Act, which authorized sports betting at the state's licensed casinos and racetracks, and Christie signed it, fully aware that the law he was signing directly violated a federal statute that had been on the books for two decades.

That was not an accident or an oversight; it was the entire strategy. New Jersey had, in fact, once had a legal path to exactly this outcome without any confrontation at all. PASPA, when Congress passed it in 1992, included a narrow, time-limited exemption written specifically with New Jersey's existing, decades-old casino industry in mind: the state had one year from the law's effective date to pass its own sports-wagering authorization and join Nevada as a second permanently legal jurisdiction. New Jersey's legislature let that year pass without acting, a piece of institutional inertia that, in hindsight, cost the state a quarter century of legal sports betting revenue it would spend enormous political and legal capital trying to recover the hard way. By 2012, the only route left to New Jersey ran through open defiance of federal law, betting — in the most literal sense the state's own governor would later invoke — that a direct legal challenge could succeed where a missed legislative deadline two decades earlier had failed.

The major professional sports leagues and the NCAA responded exactly as PASPA's own text empowered them to: within weeks of the 2012 Sports Wagering Act's passage, the NFL, NBA, NHL, MLB, and NCAA jointly sued the state of New Jersey in federal court to block the law, later joined by the U.S. Department of Justice as a co-plaintiff defending the federal statute itself. Christie's legal team did not dispute the basic factual premise of the leagues' case — New Jersey's 2012 law plainly violated PASPA's prohibition on state authorization of sports betting, and nobody involved pretended otherwise. Instead, New Jersey's lawyers built their entire defense around a structural argument about federal power rather than a substantive argument about gambling policy: PASPA, they contended, did not merely regulate an activity in the way ordinary federal legislation does, but instead commandeered state legislatures themselves, forcing New Jersey's own lawmaking body to maintain a state prohibition on sports betting that its own elected representatives no longer wanted, on pain of federal enforcement if the state tried to repeal it. That argument invoked a constitutional doctrine known as anticommandeering, rooted in the Tenth Amendment's reservation of powers not delegated to the federal government to the states, and articulated most clearly by the Supreme Court in a pair of 1990s cases holding that Congress cannot directly order state governments to enact or enforce a federal regulatory program. New Jersey lost this first round decisively — federal district and appellate courts sided with the leagues, reasoning that PASPA merely prohibited certain state action rather than affirmatively commandeering states to do anything, a distinction the courts found constitutionally significant even if it struck New Jersey's lawyers as a difference without much practical meaning.

New Jersey's second attempt, a narrower 2014 law that repealed certain state-law prohibitions on sports betting at casinos and racetracks rather than affirmatively authorizing and regulating the activity, was a more careful bet on the same underlying legal theory: if PASPA only barred states from affirmatively licensing sports betting, New Jersey reasoned, then a state should still be free simply to repeal its own criminal prohibitions and step back, leaving sports betting neither licensed nor criminally banned within its borders. The leagues sued again, and once more the lower courts sided against New Jersey, this time reasoning that even a selective repeal of state prohibitions still amounted to prohibited state "authorization" under PASPA's broad language. By the time the case, now captioned Christie v. National Collegiate Athletic Association, worked its way toward the Supreme Court, Christie himself had left office — New Jersey term-limits its governors to two four-year terms, and Christie's second term ended in January 2018 — and his Democratic successor, Phil Murphy, inherited the litigation Christie had started, giving the case its final name by the time the Supreme Court actually ruled: Murphy v. National Collegiate Athletic Association, a bureaucratic accident of timing that nonetheless meant the governor whose name appears on the most consequential gambling-law decision in American history was not the governor who had actually staked his political career on bringing the case.

The Supreme Court agreed to hear the case in 2017 and heard oral argument on December 4, 2017, with New Jersey's side represented by Theodore Olson, the former U.S. solicitor general who had argued and won Bush v. Gore in 2000 and who brought to the case the kind of elite appellate credibility that signaled New Jersey was treating its long-shot constitutional argument with full seriousness rather than as a delaying tactic. Justices across the ideological spectrum, during argument, pressed hard on the practical difficulty of distinguishing a law that "regulates" state conduct from one that "commandeers" it, a line New Jersey's opponents argued was illusory and New Jersey's lawyers argued was exactly the distinction the Constitution required courts to police. The Court's decision came down on May 14, 2018, and it was not close in its ultimate direction even if the underlying doctrine had divided lower courts for years: a 6-3 majority, in an opinion written by Justice Samuel Alito and joined by Chief Justice John Roberts, Justice Anthony Kennedy, Justice Clarence Thomas, Justice Elena Kagan, Justice Neil Gorsuch, and, for most of the opinion, Justice Stephen Breyer, held that PASPA's prohibition on state authorization of sports gambling schemes violated the Tenth Amendment's anticommandeering principle by directly ordering states not to exercise their own legislative authority over an activity within their borders — precisely the kind of federal command over state lawmaking that the Court's earlier anticommandeering precedents had already forbidden. The majority's reasoning treated the distinction lower courts had drawn between "regulating" and "commandeering" state conduct as a formalism without constitutional substance: a federal law that tells a state legislature it may not repeal its own prohibition on an activity is, functionally, ordering that legislature how to legislate, and the Constitution does not care whether Congress accomplishes that command through an affirmative mandate or through a prohibition on repeal.

Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg's dissent, joined in full by Justice Sonia Sotomayor and in part by Justice Breyer, did not primarily contest the anticommandeering analysis itself; instead, Ginsburg argued that even accepting the majority's constitutional conclusion about PASPA's anti-authorization provision, the correct remedy was to sever that specific unconstitutional provision from the rest of the statute rather than strike the entire law down, preserving PASPA's other prohibitions — such as its ban on individuals operating sports betting schemes — while excising only the piece that ran afoul of the Tenth Amendment. "When a statute reveals a constitutional flaw," Ginsburg wrote, "the Court ordinarily engages in a salvage rather than a demolition operation," a line that captured, more concisely than anything in the majority opinion, just how total the practical consequences of the ruling would turn out to be. The majority disagreed on severability as thoroughly as it had agreed on the underlying constitutional violation, reasoning that Congress's evident purpose in PASPA had been a uniform national policy against state-authorized sports betting, a purpose that could not survive in any coherent form once the core anti-authorization provision fell, and that salvaging the statute's remaining pieces would leave behind a law that did not resemble anything Congress had actually intended to pass. The practical effect of the majority's severability holding, rather than Ginsburg's preferred narrower one, is difficult to overstate: because the Court struck PASPA in its entirety rather than surgically removing only the offending clause, every state in the country became free, immediately, to legalize and regulate sports betting on whatever terms its own legislature chose, rather than merely free to repeal existing prohibitions while remaining barred from actively licensing and taxing the resulting industry. New Jersey did not win a narrow permission slip to do what it had already tried to do in 2014; it won, for itself and for every other state simultaneously, the much larger prize of full regulatory authority over an entirely new industry.

The decision's timing intersected with something that would have seemed almost unthinkable to the men who wrote PASPA in 1992: by 2018, the major professional sports leagues that had sued to keep the law in place were no longer uniformly opposed to the outcome the Supreme Court had just handed down, because several of them had already, quietly and separately, begun repositioning themselves for a legal betting market years before the ruling arrived. NBA commissioner Adam Silver had published an op-ed in the New York Times in November 2014 — three and a half years before Murphy v. NCAA was decided, and while his own league remained a named plaintiff in the litigation attempting to preserve the very ban his op-ed argued against — calling for Congress to legalize and regulate sports betting nationally rather than leave it confined to Nevada and an unregulated black market. Silver was the first sitting commissioner of a major American professional league to take that position publicly, and the seeming contradiction between the NBA's litigation posture and its commissioner's public advocacy reflected a genuine split within the leagues themselves: the institutional, legal position defending PASPA as written coexisted, especially inside the NBA, with a business calculation that a properly regulated national betting market, structured to funnel a share of its revenue back to the leagues through data licensing and sponsorship deals, would ultimately serve the leagues' financial interests better than continuing to fight a ban that an enormous illegal market was already ignoring. Major League Baseball's relationship with the industry ran even deeper and further back than Silver's op-ed suggested: MLB had been a financial investor in DraftKings since 2013, a full year before Silver's piece ran and five years before PASPA fell, a business relationship the league maintained even as it stood alongside the NBA, NFL, NHL, and NCAA as a named plaintiff seeking to preserve the federal ban on the very activity DraftKings was quietly angling to enter. The NBA's own financial stake in the coming legal market arrived not long after Silver's op-ed, when the league took an ownership position in FanDuel in November 2014, the same month Silver's argument for legalization was published — a sequence of events that makes considerably more sense as coordinated corporate positioning than as coincidence, and one this book's fifth chapter examines in full, since DraftKings and FanDuel's own transformation from daily-fantasy operators into the two dominant sportsbook brands in the country is the direct commercial consequence of the legal opening Murphy v. NCAA created.

The leagues' public reaction to their own courtroom defeat completed the pivot Adam Silver's 2014 op-ed had started, and it is worth registering how quickly the rhetoric shifted from opposing legal sports betting to negotiating a cut of it. The NFL's statement following the ruling called on Congress to "enact a core regulatory framework for legalized sports betting" while insisting the league's "long-standing and unwavering commitment to protecting the integrity of our game remains absolute" — language that managed to sound continuous with decades of anti-gambling posture while quietly conceding that the fight to keep betting illegal was over. Major League Baseball struck a similar note, warning of "profound effects" from the ruling while pledging to seek "proper protections" for the sport in partnership with the other leagues. The concrete form those "protections" took, once state legislatures actually began drafting their own sports-betting bills in the following months, was more transactional than the integrity language suggested: the NBA and MLB specifically lobbied statehouses to include what they called an "integrity fee," typically proposed at around one percent of total handle, paid directly to the leagues by licensed sportsbooks in exchange for the leagues' cooperation and data, alongside a push to be designated the mandatory, exclusive source of the in-game data operators would need to run in-play betting products. Most states ultimately rejected the integrity-fee proposal outright, regarding it as a thinly disguised revenue grab dressed in the language of anti-corruption vigilance, though the leagues' underlying interest in controlling and monetizing official data streams survived in a different, more durable form: today's data-licensing and official-partnership deals between the leagues and the major sportsbook operators, the subject of a later chapter, accomplish commercially much of what the rejected integrity fee would have accomplished legislatively, just without a state government as the intermediary setting the price.

What the ruling did not do, and what its own majority opinion took some care to note, was declare a constitutional right to bet on sports, or suggest that Congress had lost all authority over the subject. Anticommandeering doctrine constrains how Congress can regulate — it cannot conscript state legislatures into doing its enforcement work for it — but it does not prevent Congress from regulating sports betting directly through its own federal apparatus, the way it regulates banking or securities markets nationally without relying on state legislatures as intermediaries. Congress could, in theory, still pass a new federal sports-betting law tomorrow that set uniform national standards, so long as that law operated directly on individuals and businesses rather than commandeering state governments to do the regulating on its behalf. That Congress has, in the years since 2018, chosen not to exercise that remaining authority in any comprehensive way — leaving each state to write its own licensing rules, set its own tax rates, and decide for itself which products, from same-game parlays to in-play micro-betting, its licensed operators may offer — is itself a consequential choice this book's later chapters return to, because a national market built from fifty separate, uncoordinated state regulatory regimes behaves very differently than one national law would have produced, and the gaps between those regimes are exactly where much of the product-design story in the chapters ahead unfolds. For New Jersey and Atlantic City, though, the immediate consequence of May 14, 2018, was simpler and more direct than any of that longer-term regulatory question, and it arrived with a twist that must have stung a little in Trenton: New Jersey was not even the first state to actually take a legal bet after its own Supreme Court victory. Delaware, one of PASPA's three other grandfathered states and by then eager to expand its own limited sports-lottery product into full single-game wagering, moved faster through its existing regulatory apparatus and opened first, with Governor John Carney placing the state's inaugural legal sports bet at Delaware Park on June 5, 2018, three weeks after the ruling. New Jersey followed nine days later, on June 14, when Monmouth Park racetrack, partnered with the British bookmaker William Hill, opened its own sportsbook to a large and reportedly celebratory crowd, with Governor Phil Murphy placing the state's first legal wager himself — a small irony embedded in the sequence of events, since the state that had spent six years and hundreds of thousands of dollars in legal fees fighting all the way to the Supreme Court to win this right watched a smaller neighbor beat it to the actual opening by more than a week, simply by moving faster once the legal path was clear. Within days of Monmouth Park's launch, additional sportsbooks began opening across Atlantic City's surviving casino floors, and a gamble Chris Christie had made on behalf of a dying resort town — one that outlasted his own governorship and had to be finished by a governor from the opposing party who inherited a lawsuit rather than chose one — had paid off for the industry it was designed to rescue, even if Atlantic City itself would spend the following years discovering that legal sports betting alone was never going to be a large enough business, on its own, to rebuild what regional casino competition had already taken away.
