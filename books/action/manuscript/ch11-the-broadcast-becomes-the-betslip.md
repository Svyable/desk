# The Broadcast Becomes the Betslip

On April 17, 1963, Commissioner Pete Rozelle stood before reporters and announced that he was suspending two of the NFL's more recognizable players, Paul Hornung of the Green Bay Packers and Alex Karras of the Detroit Lions, indefinitely, for betting on games. Neither man had bet against his own team; Rozelle said so explicitly, and the amounts involved were modest by any later standard — Hornung was accused of wagering around $500 across three seasons, Karras of placing several bets of fifty or a hundred dollars. Five other Lions were fined for betting on a single championship game, and the team itself was fined for having ignored earlier reports of the gambling among its own players. Both Hornung and Karras were reinstated after a year, their careers largely intact, Hornung eventually reaching the Hall of Fame. But the message Rozelle sent in 1963 was not really about the dollar amounts. It was that professional football's relationship with gambling was to be one of maximum public distance: a league could not survive, in Rozelle's judgment, if fans believed the games might not be on the level, and the surest way to protect that belief was to treat even a modest, victimless personal wager by a player as an offense serious enough to end a season. That posture, with periodic exceptions and controversies, held for the next fifty years.

Major League Baseball's version of the same posture was carved even deeper into the sport's institutional memory, because baseball had already lived through the consequence of not enforcing it. Pete Rose finished his playing career as the sport's all-time hits leader and went on to manage the Cincinnati Reds, and in 1989, following an investigation led by special counsel John Dowd, Commissioner Bart Giamatti announced that Rose had accepted a lifetime ban from the game. Giamatti was an unusual figure to be the one delivering that verdict: a scholar of English Renaissance literature who had spent his academic career studying Edmund Spenser before becoming, in 1978, the youngest president in two centuries of Yale University's history, he left academia for baseball's presidency in 1986 and had held the commissionership itself for barely five months when he announced the Rose decision. He died of a heart attack eight days later, at fifty-one, the shortest tenure of any commissioner in the sport's history — a coincidence of timing that would come to color how the Rose ban was remembered inside the institution for the following three and a half decades, less as one disciplinary ruling among many than as the last act of a commissioner who had, in the sentimental version of the story, given his life defending the game's integrity. The Dowd Report, a 225-page document submitted to Giamatti that May, concluded that Rose had bet on baseball games — including, the investigators found, at least fifty Reds games during the 1987 season, wagering a minimum of $10,000 a day on the days he bet. Rose accepted the ban as part of a negotiated agreement that stopped short of baseball formally ruling on whether he had bet on games at all, a carefully constructed ambiguity that let both sides avoid a fuller public airing of the evidence, and he spent the following fifteen years publicly denying the central finding before admitting it himself in a 2004 memoir. Giamatti died of a heart attack eight days after announcing the suspension, and the image that calcified around the episode — a commissioner who gave his life, in some sentimental tellings, defending the integrity of the game against its greatest star — made Rose's ban into something closer to sacred doctrine inside baseball's institutional culture than an ordinary disciplinary ruling. For thirty-five years, no commissioner touched it.

Adam Silver broke with this half-century of institutional posture in a short New York Times opinion piece published on November 13, 2014, titled "Legalize and Regulate Sports Betting." At roughly 437 words, it was not a lengthy argument, and Silver was careful to frame it as pragmatic rather than enthusiastic: an enormous, unregulated black market for sports betting already existed, he wrote, and the NBA would be safer, its games more trustworthy, and its fans better protected if that activity were pulled into a licensed, monitored system with real integrity safeguards rather than left to operate in the shadows where the league had no visibility into who was betting what, or how much. Silver called specifically for federal legislation that would let states legalize and regulate sports betting under strict rules — mandatory monitoring of unusual line movement, licensing requirements for operators, age verification, geographic controls. It was, at the time, a genuinely radical position for a sitting commissioner of a major American league to stake out in public, the first time any of them had done so, and it drew swift pushback from executives at other leagues who continued to treat gambling proximity as a threat rather than an opportunity. Within four years, the ground Silver had staked out alone became the ground every league would eventually occupy.

The Supreme Court's Murphy v. NCAA decision in May 2018 did not require the NBA, NFL, or MLB to change anything about their own conduct; it simply struck down the federal law that had confined legal single-game sports betting to Nevada, handing the question of whether and how to legalize it back to individual states. But the leagues moved almost immediately to claim a commercial stake in the market the decision had opened. On July 31, 2018, barely ten weeks after the ruling, the NBA announced that MGM Resorts International had become its first "official gaming partner," a three-year deal worth $25 million that gave MGM the rights to league branding and broadcast highlights alongside a direct NBA data feed — the first time in the league's history that it was compensated, contractually and explicitly, for a betting operator's use of its product. The NHL followed within three months, structuring its own MGM partnership as non-exclusive and declining to describe the arrangement as a direct cut of gambling revenue, a more cautious posture than the NBA's but a clear signal that hockey, too, now considered betting-related partnership revenue worth pursuing openly. The NFL and MLB, the two leagues with the deepest historical scar tissue around gambling, moved more slowly and negotiated harder, but by 2021 both had struck exclusive, multi-year, nine-figure-plus data and marketing agreements of their own — the NFL's initial deal with Genius Sports, described in this book's earlier chapter on in-play betting, and MLB's exclusive partnership with Sportradar, running from the 2019 season through a subsequent extension to 2032. The league that had banned Pete Rose for life for betting on his own team's games was now, three decades later, a contractually committed long-term supplier to the industry that runs bets on those same games. MLB went further than simply licensing its data: in July 2021 it made DraftKings a co-exclusive Official Sports Betting Partner alongside BetMGM, building a feature the league called Bet & Watch that let fans with open accounts on both MLB.com and DraftKings stream live games directly inside the sportsbook's own app, collapsing the distance between broadcast and betslip into a single screen years before ESPN or FanDuel would attempt the same thing at national scale. Individual franchises followed the league's lead into naming-rights territory the sport's older gambling taboo would never have permitted: BetMGM was named the Boston Red Sox's own official sports-betting partner, one of dozens of similar team-level deals struck across MLB, the NBA, and the NFL in the years following the leagues' initial 2018 partnerships, each one putting a specific operator's name on a specific team's in-stadium signage, radio broadcast, or promotional calendar.

ESPN was not the first network to try welding its own brand directly onto a sportsbook, and the earlier attempt is worth recounting because its failure previewed almost exactly what would later happen to ESPN Bet. Fox Sports announced its own venture in May 2019, barely a year after Murphy v. NCAA, taking a stake of just under five percent in The Stars Group — the gambling company that would shortly become part of Flutter Entertainment — in exchange for a long-term commercial agreement to launch a co-branded product called Fox Bet. The app rolled out later that year and eventually reached bettors in New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Colorado, and Michigan, integrated into Fox's own sports programming much as ESPN Bet would later be integrated into ESPN's. It never gained meaningful traction against DraftKings and FanDuel, and the commercial relationship between Fox and its betting partner grew strained enough that Flutter and Fox jointly announced Fox Bet's permanent shutdown in July 2023 — four months before ESPN Bet would even launch, and more than two years before ESPN Bet would meet the same fate. Two of the country's largest sports broadcasters, in other words, each spent several years and a meaningful ownership stake trying to convert their own brand equity directly into betting-market share, and each concluded that owning the broadcast did not automatically translate into owning the betslip.

The clearest public marker of how completely broadcast media had absorbed this shift arrived in August 2023, when ESPN announced a ten-year partnership with Penn Entertainment to launch ESPN Bet, a sportsbook carrying the network's own name across its programming and its app. The deal's structure signaled how much value ESPN believed its brand carried in a betting-saturated market: Penn agreed to pay ESPN $1.5 billion in cash over the life of the agreement, plus roughly $500 million in warrants entitling ESPN to purchase about 31.8 million shares of Penn common stock, in exchange for the right to rebrand Penn's existing Barstool Sportsbook operation under the ESPN name. ESPN Bet launched on November 14, 2023, across seventeen states — a network that had spent decades as the broadcast home of highlight reels and pregame analysis now had its own name on a betting product, integrated directly into its own programming, with on-air personalities discussing odds and same-game parlays during the same broadcasts that once would have kept any mention of a point spread carefully off-limits under long-standing network policy. The partnership did not survive its full ten-year term. ESPN Bet's market share settled at around three percent nationally, well short of what either side had projected, and in November 2025 ESPN and Penn mutually agreed to terminate the arrangement effective December 1, 2025 — the contract had reportedly included a provision letting either side exit after the third year if specific market-share thresholds went unmet, and Penn and ESPN both pointed to that underperformance rather than any other factor in explaining the early end. ESPN pivoted immediately, signing DraftKings as its new exclusive "official sportsbook and odds provider," a title that made explicit what ESPN Bet's marketing had implied for two years: the network's own on-air product and a specific betting operator's odds feed were now, contractually, the same thing. The unwinding produced one small, almost novelistic irony at the center of the whole arrangement: as part of the deal ending ESPN's involvement, Dave Portnoy, the founder who had sold Barstool Sports to Penn in stages beginning in February 2020 — an initial 36 percent stake for roughly $163 million, valuing the company at $450 million, followed by Penn's purchase of the remaining shares for approximately $388 million in February 2023, just before Penn rebranded the whole operation under ESPN's name — bought Barstool back from Penn for one dollar, reclaiming the media company whose sports-betting ambitions had briefly worn one of the most recognizable brand names in American sports before the partnership underneath it came apart.

The NFL's own posture on advertising tracked the same trajectory as its data-rights strategy, moving from cautious permission to active integration in the space of about three years. Having struck its first US sportsbook marketing partnerships in 2021, the league began allowing operators to purchase in-game commercial inventory and other previously restricted media placements, a shift reflected starkly in the raw spending numbers: national television advertising from sportsbook brands rose from roughly $21.4 million in 2019, while the market was still confined mostly to Nevada, to more than $314.6 million in 2022, after legal betting had reached most of the country — more than a fourteenfold increase in three years, concentrated overwhelmingly in the broadcasts of the league that had spent the preceding half-century keeping gambling references off its air entirely. The volume of money spent to build and sustain this level of integration between broadcast and betting product has been enormous by any standard applied to a new consumer category. Industry estimates put total US gambling-advertising spend at roughly $1.8 billion in 2022 alone, with sportsbook and gambling brands spending nearly a billion dollars across media in 2023 specifically. FanDuel alone spent more than a billion dollars annually on marketing during this period; DraftKings spent close to $200 million on advertising in a single quarter of 2023. The top four brands by spend — FanDuel, BetMGM, DraftKings, and PrizePicks — accounted for more than sixty percent of the roughly billion-dollar 2023 total, with roughly half a billion dollars of that concentrated in television advertising, the medium most directly tied to the live sports broadcasts these companies had spent the preceding four years building formal partnerships with. A viewer watching a Sunday afternoon NFL broadcast in 2018 would have seen commercials for trucks, beer, and insurance. The same viewer watching the same broadcast slot five years later would see, interspersed with the trucks and the beer, an advertisement for the same league's own official betting partner, discussing the exact point spread the broadcast's own on-screen graphic was displaying.

The integration reached furthest, in the end, not at the national network level but at the regional one, where FanDuel simply bought the broadcast infrastructure outright rather than negotiating a partnership with it. The company's parent, Flutter Entertainment, acquired a controlling stake in what had been the Diamond Sports regional-network bundle and, in 2025, rebranded fifteen separate regional sports networks — the local channels carrying the day-to-day, season-long broadcasts of MLB, NBA, and NHL teams across their home markets — under the name FanDuel Sports Network, building in on-screen odds graphics and a live bet-tracking feature that lets a viewer watching a Cincinnati Reds or Detroit Tigers game follow their own open FanDuel wager in real time on the same screen as the broadcast itself. The company's own data on the feature makes the underlying commercial logic explicit: viewers who engage with the on-screen betting integration watch nearly twice as many games across a season as viewers who do not, and remain active users of the product for more than forty additional days on average. This is the fullest expression of the trend this chapter has traced from Adam Silver's 2014 op-ed forward — not a broadcaster renting its name to a sportsbook for a few years, the way ESPN and Fox each tried and each ultimately abandoned, but a sportsbook owning the regional broadcast infrastructure itself, permanently, with the game and the betslip sharing not just a screen but a single corporate parent.

Individual leagues have not applied their newly commercial relationship with gambling uniformly to their own athletes' conduct, and the contrast is instructive. Calvin Ridley, a wide receiver for the Atlanta Falcons, was suspended indefinitely by the NFL in March 2022 — a suspension covering at minimum the entire 2022 season — after the league found he had placed a series of parlay bets, including bets on his own team to win, during a five-day stretch in November 2021 while he was away from the team on a break addressing his mental health. The league's own investigation found no evidence Ridley had used inside information or that any teammates or coaches were aware of the betting, and the total amount wagered came to roughly $1,500. Ridley maintained he did not have a gambling problem and was reinstated the following year. The gap between Ridley's punishment — a year-long suspension for a few thousand dollars in parlay bets placed on his own team while on leave, with no evidence of manipulation — and the modern NFL's own multi-hundred-million-dollar commercial embrace of the same betting product, marketed continuously during the same league's broadcasts to millions of fans, is not a contradiction the league has ever had to resolve in public, because the two categories of conduct are governed by entirely separate logics: an athlete betting on his own sport remains an integrity threat requiring severe punishment, precisely because the league's own financial interest in betting's growth depends on public confidence that the games themselves remain unaffected by it.

Pete Rose's own case resolved, unexpectedly, after his death. Rose died in September 2024 at the age of 83, having spent his final decades lobbying unsuccessfully for reinstatement under a series of commissioners who each declined to revisit Giamatti's 1989 ruling. In May 2025, Commissioner Rob Manfred announced that Rose, along with sixteen other individuals on baseball's permanently ineligible list — including all eight players banned for their role in the 1919 Black Sox World Series scandal — had been removed from that list. Manfred's stated reasoning was structural rather than a reversal of the underlying factual findings: a person no longer living, he wrote, cannot represent an ongoing threat to the integrity of the game, and permanent ineligibility, in his reading of the rule, therefore ends at death. The decision followed a petition from Rose's attorney, Jeffrey Lenkov, and arrived amid public pressure that included commentary from President Donald Trump, though Manfred's ruling did not frame the decision as a response to either. The practical effect was to make Rose eligible for consideration by the Hall of Fame's Classic Baseball Era Committee, whose next scheduled vote falls in December 2027, with induction, if it happens, arriving no earlier than 2028 — meaning the sport that helped build the modern legal betting industry's data and marketing infrastructure may soon enshrine, in its own hall of honor, the player whose gambling on the game had defined its most severe possible punishment for the preceding thirty-six years.

Silver himself, asked about his 2014 op-ed a decade on, said he did not regret it — the internet-era reality he had described, of a betting market that would exist whether or not the leagues participated in regulating it, had not changed, and legalization had at least brought that market into view rather than leaving it entirely underground. But the same commissioner who had opened the door was, by 2024 and into 2025, also the one publicly banning Jontay Porter for life over a prop-betting manipulation scheme the next chapter examines in detail, and calling, in subsequent public comments, for tighter regulation of exactly the product his own league had spent the preceding six years helping to build. That is not a contradiction so much as the honest shape of the position Silver staked out from the beginning: legalization was never presented, even by its earliest and most prominent institutional advocate, as costless. It was presented as the lesser of two imperfect options, chosen in preference to an illegal market the league could not see into or influence at all. Whether the visible, regulated, heavily monetized version of that market has actually delivered the integrity protections Silver promised in 2014, or has simply moved the same risks into a more profitable and more heavily advertised form, is a question this book's later chapters on integrity, public health, and the industry's own numbers take up one at a time, rather than one this chapter can resolve on the strength of a single commissioner's decade-old prediction.

It would be too simple to read the Rose reinstatement as baseball quietly making peace with gambling because gambling has become profitable, and the timing invites exactly that cynical reading without quite supporting it as a full explanation. Manfred's own rule change applies prospectively to anyone who dies while on the permanently ineligible list, not merely to Rose, and it leaves the underlying prohibition on betting on one's own sport fully intact for every living player, coach, and executive — nothing about the decision authorizes a current player to do what Rose did. Ridley's suspension, decided by a different league entirely but under the same basic logic, shows that logic is still enforced today, and enforced harshly, even against a modest wager placed on a leave of absence. What has genuinely changed is not the rule that governs athletes and insiders — betting on your own sport, or providing inside information to someone who does, remains close to the one absolute taboo the modern, betting-saturated sports business has preserved intact. What has changed is everything surrounding that narrow prohibition: the broadcast booth discussing the live line, the league's own app pushing a same-game parlay during the game it is airing, the network whose name sits on the sportsbook itself. The taboo Rozelle built his 1963 suspensions around was that gambling should not come near the game at all — not the players, not the broadcast, not the sponsorship inventory, nothing. The taboo that survives in 2026 is much narrower and much more specific: that gambling should not come near the mechanism that determines who wins. A player cannot bet on his own league. An insider cannot feed a bettor confidential information. Everything else — the broadcast partner, the regional network's own name, the on-screen bet tracker following a fan's open wager in real time, the hundreds of millions of dollars in television advertising — has been not merely permitted but actively, expensively, and enthusiastically built, by the same institutions that spent the preceding half-century insisting the opposite was the only way to protect the sport at all. Rozelle would have recognized the narrow taboo that remains. He would not have recognized almost anything else about how close the rest of the betting industry has been allowed to stand.
