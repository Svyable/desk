# The States That Said Yes

New York taxes a sportsbook's gross gaming revenue at 51 percent. Nevada taxes the same category of revenue, earned on the same kind of bet, at 6.75 percent. A dollar of profit a sportsbook makes taking a bet on the Buffalo Bills in a bar in Buffalo is worth roughly seven and a half times more to the state government where it was earned than the identical dollar earned on the same game by a sportsbook on the Las Vegas Strip. Nothing about the bet itself explains that gap — not the odds, not the bettor, not the risk the book is carrying. The gap exists because two state legislatures, working from the same 2018 Supreme Court ruling and the same industry begging to be let in, arrived at almost opposite theories of what a state should be trying to do with a newly legal vice.

That gap is the subject of this chapter, and it is worth sitting with before moving to the more dramatic material — the ballot fights, the tribal lawsuits, the states that still have nothing at all — because the tax rate is where a state's real intentions show up, stripped of the press-release language about consumer protection and responsible gaming that every legalization bill carries regardless of what it actually does. A state writing a 51 percent tax rate into law has decided that sports betting is primarily a revenue instrument, to be squeezed as hard as the operators will tolerate before they simply stop offering promotions and, in the extreme case, stop operating. A state writing 6.75 percent has decided something closer to the opposite: that betting is one leg of a broader entertainment economy — hotel rooms, restaurants, shows, table games — and that squeezing the sportsbook itself would be like taxing the parking lot at a casino instead of the casino.

Nevada's number is not a typo and not an accident. It is the number Nevada set decades before any other state had to think about the question, back when sports betting existed nowhere else in the country and the state had every incentive to keep its books thin-margin, high-volume, and structurally tied to the rest of the gaming floor — a sportsbook in Nevada has historically functioned as a loss leader that gets a bettor into a building with slot machines, blackjack tables, and a steakhouse, not as a stand-alone profit center to be taxed for its own sake. When Murphy v. NCAA opened the rest of the country in 2018, Nevada didn't have to renegotiate that theory; it already had one, built for a market where the state's cut came from everything a visitor did once they walked through the door, not from the betting slip specifically. New Jersey, the state most directly responsible for winning the Supreme Court case that made all of this possible, started from a similar place — a 13 percent tax on online sports betting revenue when the market launched — reflecting Atlantic City's own casino-anchored logic, distinct from the New York model. But by 2025 New Jersey's math had changed. Governor Phil Murphy's administration proposed raising the state's online tax rate to 25 percent as part of the fiscal year 2026 budget; the legislature negotiated it down, and the number that took effect on July 1, 2025, was 19.75 percent — applied uniformly to online sports betting, online casino gambling, and fantasy sports contests, which had been taxed at 13, 15, and 10.5 percent respectively just months earlier. New Jersey, in other words, didn't abandon Nevada's casino-floor logic so much as decide the number on that logic needed to be considerably higher than anyone had guessed in 2018.

New York took the opposite bet from the start, and it is worth being precise about what "the opposite bet" actually meant in practice, because 51 percent is not merely a higher number than Nevada's — it changes what kind of company can profitably operate in the state at all. When New York opened mobile sports betting in January 2022, the state ran a competitive bidding process among operators, and the bidders who wanted in had to accept the tax rate as a condition of entry rather than lobby it down the way operators had lobbied every other state's legislature. The result was the platform economics of legal sports betting laid bare: DraftKings, FanDuel, Caesars, and the rest concluded that access to roughly twenty million adults in the country's largest media market was worth operating at a fraction of the margin they earned everywhere else, and they came in anyway. The state, for its part, got a windfall. New York collected more than $1.8 billion in mobile sports wagering tax revenue in the market's first two full years, and by the 2024 calendar year the cumulative total directed to New York's education aid formula — the statutory destination for the state's gambling tax revenue, sports betting included — had passed $2.6 billion since the January 2022 launch. Governor Kathy Hochul's office has repeated a version of the same statistic in press release after press release: sports betting funds roughly 15 percent of the state aid New York sends to its public schools, a figure that turns a consumer product into a line item lawmakers now have a direct incentive to protect and expand rather than restrain.

Illinois offers a case study in what happens when a state decides a moderate initial tax rate under-collected and moves to fix that mid-course rather than at the outset. Illinois's original 2019 sports betting law set a flat 15 percent tax; five years later, facing a budget gap, Governor J.B. Pritzker's administration and the state legislature replaced it, in the middle of the night during a 2024 budget session, with a graduated system running from roughly 20 percent at the bottom of the revenue scale up to 40 percent for the largest operators — a structure explicitly designed so the tax burden would land hardest on exactly the two companies, DraftKings and FanDuel, that control most of the state's handle. The two companies did not simply absorb the increase. The following year, Illinois added a second, more unusual mechanism on top of the graduated tax: a per-wager fee, twenty-five cents on each of a book's first twenty million bets taken in the state annually and fifty cents on every bet after that, collected regardless of whether the bet wins or loses for the house. FanDuel announced it would pass a fifty-cent transaction fee directly to customers on every bet placed starting in September 2025; DraftKings followed with its own sliding surcharge, starting at twenty-five cents and rising to fifty, with exemptions carved out for larger parlay and single-game wagers and for certain loyalty-tier customers — a pricing structure that, whatever its stated purpose, has the practical effect of nudging casual bettors toward the same larger, riskier bets the industry's own product design has been pushing them toward since chapter 6 of this book. Illinois bettors placed roughly 370 million individual wagers in 2024 alone, DraftKings and FanDuel each accounting for more than 150 million of them — volume large enough that a fifty-cent friction charge on every ticket is not a rounding error so much as a second, quieter tax the state never had to legislate directly, because the operators did the collecting on its behalf.

Ohio shows how fast a state's theory of the tax rate can change once the first year of real numbers comes in. Ohio launched legal mobile sports betting on January 1, 2023, at a flat 10 percent tax rate, a figure set the way most first-mover states set their initial number: low enough to attract every major national operator to the launch, on the assumption that a thriving, competitive market would eventually be worth more to the state than a higher rate applied to a smaller or more cautious one. Governor Mike DeWine did not wait to find out whether that assumption would hold. Barely eight months into the state's first year of legal betting, his administration pushed a doubling of the rate, to 20 percent, through the legislature as part of a budget bill, and it took effect that July — a state literally within its first year of experience with the product deciding its original number had been too generous to the operators and correcting it while the ink on the original licenses was still fresh. DeWine tried the same move again in his 2025 budget proposal, this time seeking to double the rate a second time, to 40 percent, which would have made Ohio's the highest sports betting tax rate in the country outside New York. That second increase died in the legislature amid pushback from lawmakers who argued the operators, having already absorbed one doubling, needed stability more than the state needed another windfall — a rare instance of an industry successfully holding a tax line once a state had already shown its willingness to move it.

Pennsylvania sits at the high end of the spectrum by a different route than New York's. Pennsylvania taxes online sports betting revenue at 36 percent — 34 percent to the state, 2 percent to local governments — making it, on paper, the second-highest rate of any sizable commercial market in the country after New York. In practice the number is softer than it looks, because Pennsylvania, unlike New York, allows operators to deduct promotional spending and bonus credits before calculating taxable revenue; industry analysts estimate the effective rate, once free-bet promotions and deposit-match bonuses are netted out, lands closer to 25 percent. That gap between the statutory rate and the effective rate is its own small lesson in how these laws actually function: a state can write an aggressive-sounding number into its code and still leave the operators a meaningful lever — how generously they promote — to manage their real tax exposure, which is precisely the same free-money apparatus chapter 6 of this book examined for its effect on bettor behavior, here doing double duty as a tax-avoidance tool for the operators offering it.

Line these four states up — Nevada's 6.75, New Jersey's 19.75, Pennsylvania's 36 (24.6 effective), New York's 51 — and the range alone tells a reader something the marketing language around "responsible, regulated sports betting" tends to flatten: there is no consensus among American state governments about what this product is or what a fair public claim on its revenue looks like. A bettor in Reno and a bettor in Buffalo are using functionally the same app, placing functionally the same same-game parlay on the same Sunday night football game, and the two states hosting them have reached entirely different conclusions about how much of the operator's take belongs to the public. Neither state has run the natural experiment the other represents — nobody has cleanly isolated how much of New York's revenue haul comes at the cost of worse promotional offers, tighter lines, or product features quietly withheld from New York customers because the state's cut makes them unprofitable to offer there — and this book has not found rigorous, published research that isolates that specific effect. What is verifiable is simpler and, in its way, more useful: the tax rate is the one number in this entire industry that a state legislature controls directly, unmediated by app design or algorithm, and forty-some state legislatures have used that lever to say forty-some different things about what they think sports betting is for.

The states that have said no outright are, in a sense, easier to explain than the range among the states that said yes, because the reasons cluster around a small number of recognizable forces rather than forty independent judgments about tax policy. As of this book's research in September 2026, eleven states have no legal sports betting in any form — Alabama, Alaska, California, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Minnesota, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Texas, and Utah — and the two largest of them, California and Texas, account for a combined population larger than every legal sports-betting state except New York and Florida put together. Neither holdout looks likely to move soon, and neither is holding out for the same reason.

California's absence is the direct, durable consequence of the single most expensive ballot-measure fight in American history, and it is worth walking through exactly what happened in 2022, because the popular shorthand — "California voters rejected sports betting" — obscures a fight that was never really about whether Californians wanted to bet on football. Two competing measures appeared on the November 2022 ballot. Proposition 26, backed by a coalition of roughly two dozen of California's federally recognized gaming tribes, would have legalized in-person sports betting exclusively at tribal casinos and the state's four licensed horse-racing tracks, while also expanding tribal casino games to include roulette and dice. Proposition 27, backed by a coalition of national mobile operators — DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM, and others — would have legalized statewide online and mobile sports betting through partnerships with tribes, opening the market to the same national brands already live in three dozen other states. The tribes that had spent Prop 26 sending mailers and testimony to Sacramento turned around and spent against Prop 27, because a market where any tribe could license its name to a national operator's app threatened the in-person, on-reservation exclusivity California's gaming tribes had spent decades building and litigating to protect. The two sides and their allies spent an estimated $450 to $460 million on the campaign — more than either major-party candidate spent on the 2020 California gubernatorial recall combined with the original 2003 recall that put Arnold Schwarzenegger in office — and voters rejected both measures decisively: Prop 26 lost with roughly 68 percent voting no, and the operator-backed Prop 27 fared even worse, losing with over 83 percent opposed, an unusually lopsided verdict for a measure that had over $100 million behind it. By September 2026, California's tribal gaming leadership had made its position for the near term explicit: James Siva, chairman of the California Nations Indian Gaming Association, told reporters the tribes would not place a sports betting measure on the 2026 ballot at all, and Pechanga Band of Mission Indians representative Catalina Chacon named 2028 as the earliest realistic target, contingent on building a coalition broad enough across California's more than one hundred tribes to avoid a repeat of 2022's tribe-against-tribe campaign. Until then, California — the single largest state by population without legal sports betting of any kind, and the fifth-largest economy in the world if it were a country on its own — simply has none, and the reason has almost nothing to do with public appetite for the product and almost everything to do with an intramural fight over who gets to control it.

Missouri, which sits almost exactly between Texas and California in population rank but shares neither state's structural obstacle, is the clearest available control for the experiment California ran and lost in 2022 — the same operator playbook, tried again two years later, in a state without a powerful tribal gaming bloc positioned to fight it. Missouri Amendment 2 went before voters as a constitutional amendment on November 5, 2024, after two earlier legislative efforts had stalled and an initiative had failed to qualify for the ballot in prior cycles. DraftKings and FanDuel funded almost the entire yes campaign themselves, spending roughly $43 million — a record for a Missouri ballot measure, though barely a tenth of what the two sides spent fighting each other in California two years earlier — betting that a state whose commercial casino industry runs through riverboat licenses rather than a politically organized tribal gaming sector would not generate the kind of well-funded, motivated opposition that had buried Proposition 27. The bet paid off, though only barely: Amendment 2 passed with 50.05 percent of the vote, a margin of a few thousand ballots in a state of six million people, close enough that a modest shift in turnout in St. Louis or Kansas City could have produced the opposite result. State regulators were required to have the market live within thirteen months of the vote, and Missouri's first legal sportsbooks opened on December 1, 2025, becoming, depending on how a given tracker counts states that authorize but haven't yet launched, either the thirty-eighth or thirty-ninth state with a live legal market. The contrast with California is not that Missourians wanted sports betting more than Californians did — Amendment 2's margin suggests they barely wanted it at all — but that Missouri lacked the one force capable of matching a national operator's ad budget dollar for dollar and, more importantly, lacked the specific grievance, a threat to an existing tribal gaming monopoly, that gave California's tribes a reason to spend nine figures fighting a measure most of them might otherwise have ignored.

Texas's absence traces a different, more conventionally partisan path. Texas allows no legal sports betting, mobile or otherwise, and the state's legislature — which meets only in odd-numbered years, a structural quirk that means any given failure locks the state out for a minimum of two more years — killed the latest push during its regular 2025 session before it reached a floor vote in the chamber that mattered most. Lieutenant Governor Dan Patrick, who controls the flow of legislation through the Texas Senate as its presiding officer, has been the single most consistent obstacle, publicly and repeatedly opposing any expansion of gambling regardless of the specific bill's structure. The more interesting story is what happened in the House, which had actually passed a sports-betting bill in the 2023 session only to watch it die in the Senate. Ahead of the 2025 session, Governor Greg Abbott intervened in a series of Republican primary races, helping replace a bloc of comparatively moderate incumbents with more conservative challengers — a purge whose motivations extended well beyond gambling policy specifically, but whose effect on this particular question was concrete and measurable: roughly two dozen House Republicans who had voted yes in 2023 were gone by 2025, replaced by newcomers who ran, in part, on opposing any gambling expansion, and several returning members who had voted yes in 2023 announced they would vote no the second time. A bill that had cleared the House with room to spare two years earlier never got close in 2025. Lobbyists on both sides of the issue have already shifted their planning horizon to the 2027 session, the next opportunity Texas's biennial calendar allows.

The remaining nine holdout states divide along familiar lines that this book's earlier chapters have already sketched in other contexts: states with strong religious or cultural opposition to gambling generally (Utah has no legal gambling of any kind, full stop, and Hawaii is one of only two states, alongside Utah, with no commercial or tribal casino industry to build on in the first place), states where a small number of veto points — a single skeptical committee chair, a governor's office uninterested in spending political capital on the fight — have simply stalled repeated attempts (Minnesota's legislature has taken up sports betting bills in four consecutive sessions through 2026 without passing one, the sticking point in most years being a dispute between the state's eleven tribal nations, which hold exclusive rights to Class III gaming under their existing compacts and want that exclusivity to extend to any mobile sports betting license, and the state's horse tracks and card rooms, which want a cut of a market they currently have no legal claim to), and states where a specific procedural hurdle has proven harder to clear than legislative will alone. Georgia is the clearest recent example of the last category: unlike most states, Georgia's own courts have suggested that authorizing sports betting requires amending the state constitution, which in turn requires a two-thirds supermajority in both legislative chambers before the question can even reach voters — a bar no ordinary majority bill can clear regardless of how many members personally favor the idea. A 2026 effort, House Bill 910, tried to route around that requirement entirely by structuring the product as an extension of the existing state lottery rather than a new form of gambling requiring constitutional authorization — an online-only model, no physical sportsbooks or casinos attached, with the Georgia Lottery issuing up to eighteen licenses at a $100,000 application fee and $1.5 million annual license fee, taxed at 25 percent of adjusted gross gaming revenue. The bill still needed a two-thirds House supermajority to advance, a hangover requirement from the same constitutional question it was designed to dodge, and on March 6, 2026, it fell far short, drawing just 63 of the 120 votes required, effectively ending Georgia's 2026 push before the Senate ever took it up.

What connects Georgia's procedural wall, Texas's primary-driven Senate opposition, Minnesota's tribal-versus-racetrack standoff, and California's $460 million tribal civil war is not any shared judgment about whether legal sports betting is good or bad policy. It is that in each case, a state's decision has turned on a local political structure — a supermajority threshold, a lieutenant governor's veto power over the legislative calendar, a gaming compact negotiated a generation ago, a coalition of tribes with more leverage than any national sportsbook operator — that has nothing to do with the product-design questions chapters 5 through 13 of this book spent most of their pages on. A resident of Sacramento or Austin or Minneapolis who wants to place a same-game parlay on Sunday can still do it, in practice, by crossing a state line, using a VPN and an out-of-state billing address, or simply finding one of the offshore or unlicensed books chapter 17 will return to — the same workarounds bettors used everywhere in the country before 2018, now persisting in the handful of states too large, too internally divided, or too structurally resistant to have joined the other thirty-nine. The patchwork this chapter has traced — a tax rate that varies by a factor of seven, a product available in most of the country and unavailable, for entirely different reasons, in nine large and often populous states that never reached the same yes — is not a temporary transitional phase on the way to some cleaner national standard. Nearly a decade after Murphy v. NCAA, it is simply what American sports betting policy is: fifty separate answers to the same question, negotiated fifty separate times, with a Congress that — as the next chapter shows — has had every opportunity to impose a single answer and has, so far, declined to try.
