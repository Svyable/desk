# Building a Sportsbook You Could Defend

A Connecticut study, commissioned by the state's own gaming regulators and examined in some detail by researchers tracking the state's post-2018 betting market, found that fewer than seven percent of Connecticut residents who gamble meet the clinical threshold for problem or at-risk gambling — and that this same small group generates roughly 71 percent of all the state's legal gambling revenue. Narrow the lens to sports betting specifically, the product this book has spent twenty chapters examining, and the number gets starker still: 51 percent of all sports betting revenue collected in Connecticut comes from problem gamblers alone, not from the at-risk category surrounding them, but from the smaller population that has already crossed into diagnosable disorder. That is not a number about a product with a flaw. It is closer to a description of a business model. A restaurant that took most of its revenue from a small number of customers with a diagnosed eating disorder would not be described as having a marketing problem. A casino that took most of its floor revenue from a handful of self-identified compulsive gamblers would already have federal and state regulators asking hard questions about the design of its slot floor. Sports betting, on the evidence chapter 14 through chapter 20 of this book have assembled, resembles the casino far more than it resembles the restaurant, and Connecticut's own commissioned research is not an outlier finding — it is corroborated by the credit-bureau bankruptcy studies chapter 17 examined, by the NCPG's own long-running survey data, and by the plain mathematics of an industry that reports record annual revenue in the same years its state-funded problem-gambling helplines report record call volume.

This chapter does not propose banning the product that generates those numbers. Chapter 17 made the strongest available case for why that would be a mistake, and this book has not found evidence in the chapters since that would change that conclusion: the illegal market this legal one replaced offered no self-exclusion, no dispute resolution, and, as the November 2025 New Jersey indictment of a Lucchese-linked bookmaking ring recruiting college athletes showed, has never actually disappeared — it has simply lost the customers a regulated operator will still serve, while continuing to recruit the ones a regulated operator, under current rules, is allowed to keep serving anyway. The honest problem this book has documented across twenty chapters is not that a legal, taxed, monitored sports betting market exists. It is that the specific market fifty states independently built, with no federal floor beneath any of them, was engineered — deliberately, at the product level, using the same behavioral research slot-machine manufacturers pioneered a half-century earlier — to maximize how much and how often its heaviest users bet, and that Connecticut's own numbers show the resulting revenue concentrating exactly where a defensible product would try hardest to prevent it from concentrating. What follows is not a wish list. Every mechanism this chapter proposes is already operating somewhere, in a market recognizably similar to the American one, generating real, measured results this book's earlier chapters have already cited. The question this chapter asks is narrower and more answerable than "should sports betting exist": what would it take to build the version of this product that a state regulator, a company's own board, or a member of Congress could actually defend in public, with the same rigor this book has tried to bring to every claim in its preceding chapters?

Start with the affordability check, because it is the single mechanism with the clearest, most recent evidence behind it. Britain's rollout, chapter 19 detailed, applies an automated, credit-bureau-anchored check once a customer's net losses cross defined thresholds, built specifically to intervene without friction for the overwhelming majority of customers who show no signs of harm — 95 percent of the lowest-tier checks in the pilot resolved invisibly, using data the credit bureaus already held, and the Gambling Commission's own first-quarter 2026 data found median losses among customers who did trigger an intervention fell by roughly 22 percent compared with the year before. Nothing about that architecture is specific to British law or British credit infrastructure; Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion, the three bureaus that built the technical backbone of Britain's system, are the same three bureaus that already underwrite most American consumer lending. A federal minimum standard requiring every licensed American sportsbook to run a comparable, tiered check — invisible at low stakes, escalating only as losses cross real thresholds — would not require inventing new technology or new institutions. It would require Congress to do what the SAFE Bet Act has proposed twice now and failed to move twice now: write the requirement into federal law, funded and structured closely enough to Britain's already-tested model that American regulators would not have to run their own multi-year pilot from scratch before finding out whether it works.

The self-exclusion gap this book has returned to in three separate chapters is the second mechanism, and it may be the easiest to fix relative to how much harm its absence currently permits. Nevada, this book's own steelman chapter noted, has no statewide law letting a gambler exclude themselves from in-person casino gambling at all — only a voluntary, toothless arrangement that stops direct marketing without stopping the gambling itself. Most other states do somewhat better, requiring some form of self-exclusion from licensed operators, but every state's list stops at its own border: a bettor who self-excludes in New Jersey can open an account in Pennsylvania the same afternoon, and a bettor excluded from every licensed operator in the country can still find the exact kind of bookmaker federal prosecutors were still indicting in November 2025. Australia's BetStop register, chapter 19 showed in real, government-published numbers, demonstrates a national, single-registration system is not a hypothetical proposal awaiting some unbuilt technology — it is a three-year-old piece of working government infrastructure that had drawn nearly 49,400 cumulative registrations by September 2025, with roughly 32,000 active exclusions at that point, run by a national telecommunications regulator in a country whose legal and administrative traditions sit close enough to America's that the excuse "no one has built this" is simply false. A small coalition of daily fantasy sports operators — PrizePicks, Underdog, Dabble, and Splash Sports — had already proven the private-sector version of the same idea feasible in the American market by March 2026, launching a voluntary, US-wide, fifty-state self-exclusion system among themselves without waiting for any legislature to require it. What a federal minimum standard would add is not the technology, which four private companies had already built voluntarily, but the legal requirement that every operator, not just the ones willing to join a voluntary coalition, actually honor it.

The advertising and promotional-design questions this book's middle chapters spent the most pages on are harder to legislate cleanly than an affordability check or a shared exclusion list, because — unlike a credit-bureau threshold, which is a single number a regulator can simply require — an advertising rule has to draw a line through language, timing, and format, exactly the kind of line the Ohio-versus-Massachusetts-versus-New York patchwork chapter 16 documented in specific dollar-fine detail has struggled to draw consistently even within a single country. Australia's 2026 compromise, chapter 19 showed, offers the clearest existing model for how a legislature might thread that needle without attempting an outright ban that a lobbying-heavy industry — the American Gaming Association spent more than $2.9 million on federal lobbying across five recent quarters, chapter 16 found, and Australian sports leagues gave politicians roughly A$245,000 in tickets and hospitality while fighting their own country's version of this fight — will resist regardless of how the specific threshold is set. A hard cap on the number of in-broadcast ads per hour, paired with a flat ban on advertising during the live window of the game itself, would not eliminate sportsbook marketing from American sports broadcasts, any more than Australia's version eliminated it from NRL and AFL broadcasts. It would end the specific pattern chapter 11 of this book examined at length — the broadcast becoming the betslip, in real time, mid-play — without requiring Congress to solve the much harder question of banning sports sponsorship altogether, a fight Australia's own three-year, still-unfinished legislative process shows can consume years even with a single national legislature and a single national broadcast regulator, neither of which the fifty-state American system has.

Money is the fourth mechanism, and it is the one this book's earlier chapters have shown American states are, collectively, handling worst. GamingToday's 2026 survey of state problem-gambling funding found New Jersey — a state that collected roughly $2.1 billion in combined online gaming and sports betting revenue in 2025 alone — allocated less than a tenth of one percent of that revenue to treatment and prevention; Pennsylvania dedicated roughly 0.2 percent, Michigan roughly 0.3 percent. Against that baseline, the states that have actually built something defensible are not hypothetical models borrowed from another country; they are sitting in the same fifty-state system chapter 15 spent an entire chapter describing as an uncoordinated patchwork. Massachusetts directs 9 percent of its sports betting revenue specifically to a Public Health Trust Fund built to research and reduce problem gambling — a figure roughly ninety times New Jersey's own share of a considerably larger revenue base. Illinois requires 25 percent of its sports betting tax revenue, the same tiered, per-wager-surcharged tax structure chapter 15 examined in detail, to fund problem-gambling prevention, education, treatment, and provider training and credentialing. The National Council on Problem Gambling's own recommended floor — at minimum 1 percent of gross gaming revenue dedicated to treatment, a threshold most states, chapter 17 showed, do not clear even now — is not an aspirational target invented for this book's closing chapter. It is a number the industry's own likeliest customers, on Connecticut's own commissioned math, are already generating many times over in tax revenue alone; the money exists, in every state examined in this book, in amounts vastly exceeding what would be required to fund treatment at the level the industry's own trade group would have a hard time calling unreasonable. What does not exist, in forty-some of fifty states, is the legal requirement that any specific share of it actually gets spent that way rather than absorbed into a general fund a state legislature can redirect toward anything else it wants funded in a tight budget year.

None of these four mechanisms — the affordability check, the shared exclusion list, the advertising cap, the dedicated funding floor — requires resolving the harder jurisdictional fight chapters 16 and 18 of this book spent two full chapters documenting. A federal minimum standard along these lines does not have to settle whether Kalshi's sports contracts are swaps or bets, a question sitting, unresolved, in front of the Supreme Court as this book goes to press; it would apply to any operator taking a sports wager from an American customer, gaming license or derivatives registration alike, closing the exact loophole chapter 18 showed a company can currently exploit by choosing the cheaper of two federal postures. It does not require solving the Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering tension chapter 16 flagged in the SAFE Bet Act's own legal analysis, if the standard is written, as tobacco and consumer-lending regulation eventually were, as a floor states remain free to exceed rather than a ceiling states must be brought down to — New York's 51 percent tax rate and Massachusetts's 9 percent problem-gambling set-aside would not need to change under a federal floor requiring, say, half that funding share and Britain's tiered affordability-check architecture; they would simply already clear a bar most other states currently do not. And it does not require taking chapter 17's steelman any less seriously than that chapter demanded: an affordability check built on Britain's own invisible-for-95-percent design does not slow down the recreational bettor risking twenty dollars on a Sunday parlay any more than it currently slows down a British bettor doing the same thing; it engages only once a specific customer's losses cross a threshold Connecticut's own data suggests is disproportionately concentrated among a small, identifiable, already-struggling population in the first place.

What stands between the mechanisms this chapter has described and their actual passage is not a missing idea. Every one of them exists, tested, in a market whose government has already generated its own real evidence of what happens when it is applied. What stands between them and American law is the same gap chapter 16 spent an entire chapter describing: a $2.9 million-and-rising annual lobbying effort defending the value of an unregulated federal space that a fully coordinated market would close, a Congress that has now let two separate bills — one restricting the product, one funding the cleanup its own existing 1951 excise tax could pay for without raising a single new dollar in taxes — sit in committee for going on two years apiece, and fifty state legislatures each weighing, correctly, that unilateral action costs their own operators a competitive disadvantage against every neighboring state that declines to match it. That collective-action problem is precisely the shape of failure a federal minimum standard exists to solve, and precisely the shape of failure Britain's Gambling Commission and Australia's ACMA never had to solve at all, because neither country had fifty separate legislatures independently deciding whether to move first.

Return, for a final image, to something this book has already shown the reader rather than invented for the occasion. In a betting shop on an ordinary British high street, the machine that used to accept £100 in a single spin — the machine campaigners spent years calling the crack cocaine of gambling, the machine that was, by some bookmakers' own accounting, generating close to half their profit before 2019 — is still there. It still lights up, still spins, still pays out. The screen still asks the same question it always asked. What changed is a single number, printed now on the machine's own interface: a maximum stake of two pounds, not one hundred, chosen not because the industry offered it voluntarily but because Parliament, faced with Jack Ritchie's story and thousands like it that never made a headline, decided the difference between those two numbers was the entire argument. Nothing about the machine's underlying appeal was removed. The bright screen, the twenty-second cycle, the near-miss animation this book's earlier chapters showed American app designers borrowing from slot-machine research decades old — all of it is still exactly as engineered as it was in 2018, still built by the same behavioral logic, still standing in the same shop. Only the ceiling on how much a single spin can take from a single person in a single moment of bad judgment actually moved. That is not a solution to gambling. It is not even a solution to fixed-odds betting terminals, which still exist, still profit, still occasionally still hurt somebody in a shop on a Tuesday afternoon. It is a demonstration that an industry this book has spent twenty-one chapters showing knows exactly how to engineer a product toward maximum extraction can be told, by a government willing to say so and mean it, precisely how far that engineering is allowed to go — and that the industry, however loudly it predicts catastrophe beforehand, generally finds a way to keep operating anyway. The American sportsbook industry has never once been told that. It has been asked, occasionally, by a state commission here or a stalled bill in Washington there. It has never once, since May 14, 2018, actually been told. This book's argument, across every chapter that came before this one, is that the difference between asking and telling is not a technicality. It is the entire remaining distance between the legal market this country actually built and the one it could still choose to defend.
