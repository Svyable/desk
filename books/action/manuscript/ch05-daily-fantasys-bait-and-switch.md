# Daily Fantasy's Bait and Switch

Season-long fantasy football is old enough that its own legal protection was written into federal law almost as an afterthought, tucked into a 2006 statute that was aimed at an entirely different target. The Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act, signed by President George W. Bush on October 13, 2006, was designed to choke off the booming offshore online-poker and casino industry by making it a crime for banks and payment processors to knowingly handle transactions connected to illegal internet gambling — a financial chokehold rather than a direct ban, since Congress had learned by then that prosecuting individual offshore websites was a losing game of jurisdictional whack-a-mole. Buried inside that law was a carve-out for fantasy sports, written with the blessing of the major professional leagues, exempting games that met several conditions: prizes had to be established in advance and not tied to the number of entrants or the amount wagered, outcomes had to reflect the relative knowledge and skill of participants rather than chance, and — the provision that would matter most a few years later — winning outcomes could not be based on the score, point spread, or performance of any single real-world team or combination of teams. Congress's actual target was the traditional, decades-old hobby of season-long fantasy football, played among friends and coworkers for modest stakes, drafted once in August and managed casually across an entire NFL season. Representative Jim Leach, the UIGEA's principal author, would later say plainly that the carve-out was meant to relieve banks of an enforcement burden around a hobby nobody in Congress considered gambling in any meaningful sense, not to bless an entirely new industry that did not yet exist.

That new industry arrived because a small number of entrepreneurs read the statute's actual text rather than its evident intent, and noticed something the law's drafters had not anticipated: nothing in the UIGEA's fantasy-sports exemption required a contest to last an entire season. A fantasy competition that started and finished within a single day or a single week of games met every technical condition Congress had written into the carve-out just as well as one that ran for four months. FanDuel, launched on July 21, 2009, by the Scottish entrepreneur Nigel Eccles as a spinoff of an earlier prediction-market venture called Hubdub, built its entire business on that observation, offering daily contests in which a user drafted a new lineup of real athletes against a salary cap before each day's games and won or lost money based on those players' statistical performance that single day, rather than across a season. DraftKings, founded in Boston in February 2012 by three former colleagues from the printing company VistaPrint — Jason Robins, Matthew Kalish, and Paul Liberman — entered the same space a few years later and, together with FanDuel, spent the following several years locked in a spending war for market dominance that would come to look, in retrospect, like a dress rehearsal for the promotional arms race that would consume the legal sportsbook industry after 2018.

The legal theory underneath daily fantasy sports was, from the beginning, a claim of skill over chance, and it is worth taking that claim seriously rather than dismissing it outright, because the industry's entire legal survival depended on regulators and courts accepting some version of it. A traditional sports bet asks a simple binary question resolved entirely by events outside the bettor's control — will Team A beat Team B, will the total go over or under a number — and a bettor's skill, to whatever extent it exists, consists entirely of correctly judging probabilities the bettor has no ability to influence. A daily fantasy contest, by contrast, asks a user to build a full roster of individual athletes within a fixed salary cap, competing against a field of other users who built their own, different rosters from the same pool of players and the same cap — a genuinely more complex analytical exercise, involving player-selection strategy, salary-cap optimization, and an understanding of statistical variance across dozens of simultaneous games, that plausibly rewards research and skill in a way a simple win-or-lose sports bet does not. Whether that skill component was large enough, relative to the underlying randomness of any single day's athletic performances, to legally distinguish daily fantasy sports from gambling was exactly the question that regulators in dozens of states would spend the following several years fighting over, without ever reaching a clean, uniform national answer — a regulatory patchwork that let DraftKings and FanDuel operate as legal, unregulated, essentially untaxed businesses in most states for years, collecting entry fees and paying out prizes on a scale that soon dwarfed anything the season-long hobby Congress had actually meant to protect had ever generated.

The scale of that operation, by the fall of 2015, had become impossible for anyone with a television to ignore. DraftKings and FanDuel spent a combined $205.9 million on national television advertising between the start of 2015 and October 5 of that year alone — DraftKings accounting for $131.4 million of that across more than forty thousand individual national ad airings, FanDuel for the remaining $74.5 million across another twenty-one thousand — a level of spending that, for a stretch of weeks around the opening of the NFL season, had the two companies collectively outspending the entire American beer industry on national television advertising, an industry that had used football broadcasts as its signature marketing showcase for decades. DraftKings alone spent roughly $20 million on advertising during the NFL's opening week that September, making it, by some measures, the single biggest television advertiser in the country that particular week, a fact that startled media reporters covering the ad-spend data more than it startled the millions of football viewers who had, by then, simply stopped being able to watch a broadcast without a DraftKings or FanDuel commercial appearing between nearly every set of downs. The blitz worked in the narrow sense that it was designed to work — both companies' user bases and entry-fee volumes grew explosively through that stretch — and it worked in a second sense that would matter enormously two and a half years later: it built two of the most recognized consumer brand names in American sports, at a cost of hundreds of millions of dollars in venture capital, well before either company had a legal product to sell nationally that was actually called sports betting. When Murphy v. NCAA opened that door in 2018, DraftKings and FanDuel did not need to build brand recognition from nothing the way a genuinely new entrant would have; they needed only to redirect an audience that already knew their names, trusted their apps enough to have entered a daily contest at least once, and had already, in many cases, given the companies their banking information.

The state-by-state legal patchwork that followed the 2015 crisis eventually cohered into something resembling a settled framework, but only after years of company-funded lobbying in dozens of statehouses simultaneously. Virginia became, in March 2016, the first state to pass a law specifically legalizing and regulating daily fantasy sports as its own defined category, distinct from traditional gambling, and other states followed in a steady trickle rather than a rush: New York's legislature passed its own DFS-legalization bill in June 2016, resolving the standoff Schneiderman's cease-and-desist letters had created the previous fall, and by the end of 2017 roughly eighteen states had enacted some form of DFS-specific regulatory framework, with Ohio's governor signing that state's version in the final days of December 2017 and Pennsylvania formally following in 2018. The pattern across these bills was consistent enough to read almost as a template: each state's legislature had to decide, explicitly, whether it believed daily fantasy sports was predominantly a game of skill or a game of chance, and states that accepted the skill-based framing tended to legalize and lightly regulate the activity, typically with a modest licensing fee and consumer-protection rules, while states that rejected it tended to treat DFS as unlicensed gambling subject to whatever criminal penalties already existed on the books. DraftKings and FanDuel, having survived the 2015 crisis as functioning national businesses despite the New York shutdown, entered 2018 with a hard-won, expensively purchased education in exactly the kind of state-by-state legislative combat — coalition-building, lobbying spend, competing skill-versus-chance narratives tailored to each statehouse's particular political culture — that legal sports betting itself would demand from both companies again, immediately, at a scale the DFS fight had merely previewed.

The industry's first serious crisis arrived not from a regulator or a court but from its own employees, in a story that broke on October 5, 2015, when the New York Times reported that a mid-level DraftKings content manager named Ethan Haskell had accidentally published internal data — specifically, aggregate figures showing which players were most heavily used across DraftKings' own user-submitted lineups in that week's NFL contests — before the deadline for the week's games had passed. The information would have been valuable to anyone building a competing lineup, since knowing which players the field was overweighting or underweighting can meaningfully change optimal roster-construction strategy in a contest where a user's score is measured relative to everyone else's. What made the story catch fire nationally rather than remain an internal DraftKings embarrassment was Haskell's own activity that same week: he had entered, and won $350,000 in, a contest on FanDuel — DraftKings' chief and, at the time, essentially only major competitor — raising the immediate and obvious question of whether an employee with privileged internal data at one company had used it to win money at the other. DraftKings and FanDuel both responded by barring their employees from playing daily fantasy sports for cash anywhere, at either company, while an outside law firm, Greenberg Traurig, led by a former U.S. attorney named John Pappalardo, investigated the specific incident and ultimately concluded that Haskell had not actually used the leaked data to his advantage, because he had already locked his own FanDuel lineup roughly forty minutes before the information became available to him. The exoneration on the narrow factual question did little to blunt the broader damage: the episode had, almost overnight, made vivid and concrete an entire category of risk — employees at companies running the games having access to information ordinary players did not — that a genuinely well-regulated gambling product is specifically designed to prevent, and that an unregulated one, by definition, had no external mechanism to catch until a reporter happened to notice.

The regulatory reckoning that followed the insider-trading story was swift and, in New York specifically, close to fatal for the industry's existing business model. New York Attorney General Eric Schneiderman opened an investigation into both companies and, on November 10, 2015, issued formal cease-and-desist letters ordering DraftKings and FanDuel to immediately stop accepting entry fees from New York residents, on the theory that daily fantasy sports met his state's statutory definition of illegal gambling regardless of what Congress's 2006 carve-out had said about the activity federally. Schneiderman's public reasoning cut directly against the skill-versus-chance defense the industry had built its entire legal existence on: DFS customers, he argued, were "clearly placing bets on events outside of their control or influence, specifically on the real-game performance of professional athletes" — a formulation that treated the skill involved in constructing a lineup as legally beside the point, since the actual outcome still depended on how real athletes performed in real games nobody paying an entry fee could control. Both companies fought the order in court, and lost: on December 11, 2015, a New York state court judge ordered DraftKings and FanDuel to cease operations in the state entirely, repeatedly describing the activity in his ruling using the precise language of New York's own gambling statute — "risking something of value" on an outcome the customer does not control — language that read, to industry observers, like a judge deliberately refusing to grant the companies the skill-based exemption they had spent years cultivating in the press and in friendlier state legislatures. New York, at the time one of the largest and most lucrative state markets for both companies, remained closed to them for months while the state legislature eventually passed a law explicitly legalizing and regulating daily fantasy sports — a resolution that validated the industry's underlying business but only after forcing it through the exact kind of formal state licensing and taxation regime it had spent its first several years of existence successfully avoiding.

New York was not an isolated skirmish; it was the leading edge of a state-by-state legal reckoning that played out across the country through 2015, 2016, and beyond, with regulators in several other states reaching similar conclusions on their own timelines and their own legal grounds. Illinois's attorney general issued a formal opinion in December 2015 concluding that daily fantasy contests amounted to illegal gambling under state law — a conclusion the Illinois Supreme Court would eventually reverse years later, after the legislature had already intervened, but one that shut DraftKings and FanDuel out of another major state market in the meantime. Nevada, of all places, followed a similar path: the state's Gaming Control Board declared in October 2015 that paid daily fantasy contests constituted gambling requiring a Nevada gaming license to offer legally, a ruling with a particular irony given that Nevada was, at that exact moment, the only state in the country where a sportsbook could legally take the kind of single-game bet DraftKings and FanDuel had spent years insisting their product was legally nothing like. Texas's attorney general reached the same conclusion in a January 2016 advisory opinion, framing the legal standard in blunt terms that cut straight through the skill-versus-chance argument the industry had built its business on: it is prohibited gambling in Texas, the opinion held, if you bet on the performance of a participant in a sporting event and the house takes a cut, regardless of how much strategic skill goes into picking that participant beforehand. Dozens of other state legislatures moved in the opposite direction over the same stretch, passing bills that explicitly carved daily fantasy sports out from their gambling statutes and imposed a licensing and consumer-protection framework instead, leaving DraftKings and FanDuel to operate, for the better part of three years, as legal, licensed businesses in some states and prohibited operators in others, sometimes changing status in the same state more than once as legislatures, courts, and regulators took turns weighing in. By the time this patchwork settled into something resembling stability, DraftKings and FanDuel had spent tens of millions of dollars on lobbying and litigation defending a business model whose actual legal footing varied, sometimes significantly, from state to state — a fragmented regulatory experience that gave both companies a hard, expensively earned education in exactly the kind of state-by-state licensing apparatus they would need to master again, at even larger scale, once sports betting itself became legal a few years later.

The two companies' own competitive rivalry nearly ended in a merger before it ended in the sportsbook pivot that actually defined their next decade. DraftKings and FanDuel announced plans to combine their businesses in November 2016, a deal that made obvious commercial sense to both companies' investors — years of expensive customer-acquisition competition between what were, by then, essentially the only two meaningful players in the DFS market had left both companies burning cash to fight over the same finite pool of customers, and a merger promised to end that war overnight. Federal antitrust regulators saw it differently. The Federal Trade Commission, joined by the attorneys general of California and the District of Columbia, sued in June 2017 to block the merger, arguing that a combined DraftKings-FanDuel would control more than 90 percent of the entire paid daily fantasy sports market in the United States, a concentration level the agency considered a clear violation of federal antitrust law regardless of how the industry's gambling-versus-skill status was ultimately resolved. Rather than fight the FTC's case to trial, the companies abandoned the merger on July 13, 2017, and the agency dismissed its complaint — a decision that, less than a year later, would turn out to have inadvertently preserved exactly the kind of head-to-head competitive dynamic that made both companies fight so hard, and spend so much money, for market share once a much larger prize than daily fantasy sports suddenly became legally available to both of them simultaneously.

Murphy v. NCAA, decided in May 2018, handed both companies almost precisely the business opportunity their merger would have foreclosed them from needing: rather than consolidating two competitors fighting over a capped daily-fantasy market, both DraftKings and FanDuel could now expand into an entirely new, vastly larger product category — licensed, legal sports betting — using infrastructure, brand recognition, and millions of existing daily-fantasy customers they had already spent years and enormous sums acquiring. The pivot was less a strategic surprise than the culmination of positioning both companies had quietly been doing for years: DraftKings had already built out technology and licensing relationships that translated directly into sportsbook operations, while FanDuel had, in 2018, merged its own U.S. business with the American operations of Paddy Power Betfair, the Irish-British gambling conglomerate that would rename itself Flutter Entertainment the following year — a deal in which Paddy Power Betfair paid roughly $158 million for an initial 58 percent stake in FanDuel, giving the fantasy-sports company access to genuine, decades-deep sportsbook operating expertise from a company that had never needed America's fantasy-sports carve-out to begin with, because bookmaking was simply legal where it came from. Flutter would go on to buy out most of the remaining stake, paying roughly $4.2 billion in 2020 for an additional 37 percent of FanDuel that brought its total ownership to about 95 percent, a valuation that made unmistakably clear how much larger the sports-betting opportunity had become than the daily-fantasy business FanDuel had actually built its brand on. DraftKings took a different but parallel route to the same scale, going public in April 2020 through a merger with a special-purpose acquisition company alongside the sports-betting technology provider SBTech, a transaction that gave the company the public capital markets access it would spend the next several years drawing on heavily — the subject, along with FanDuel's own aggressive promotional spending, of this book's next chapter — to fund the customer-acquisition war that both companies had already fought once, over a much smaller prize, before Murphy v. NCAA changed the size of the game entirely.

The leagues' own relationship with the two companies completes the story of how thoroughly a "fantasy sports carve-out" ended up seeding the two dominant brands in American legal sports betting, and it is worth returning to because of how directly it contradicts the leagues' formal litigation posture during the PASPA fight. Major League Baseball had been a financial investor in DraftKings since 2013, and by 2015 had signed a multiyear sponsorship deal naming DraftKings its "official daily fantasy game" — a commercial relationship the league maintained continuously even as it stood, that same year and the years following, as a named plaintiff in litigation seeking to preserve PASPA's ban on the broader sports-betting industry DraftKings was already positioning itself to enter. The NBA's relationship with FanDuel followed an almost identical arc, with the league taking an equity stake in the company in November 2014, the same month Commissioner Silver's op-ed calling for legalization ran in the Times. Neither league's investment in a fantasy-sports company technically required it to abandon its formal opposition to sports betting in court, since the legal categories were genuinely distinct at the time. But the practical effect, once PASPA fell, was that two of the four major professional leagues had already placed direct financial bets, years in advance, on the exact two companies that would go on to dominate the legal sports betting market their own litigation had spent years trying to prevent from existing — a contradiction that says less about hypocrisy on any individual's part than about how clearly sophisticated market participants inside the sports and gambling industries could see, well before the Supreme Court ruled, which way this particular fight was eventually going to end.
