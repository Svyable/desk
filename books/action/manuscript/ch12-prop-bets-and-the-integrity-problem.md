# Prop Bets and the Integrity Problem

Jontay Porter left the court after three minutes on March 20, 2024, during a game between his Toronto Raptors and the Sacramento Kings, citing an illness. For a player of Porter's standing — a fringe rotation big man who had spent much of his career shuttling between the Raptors and their G League affiliate — a short, unremarkable exit from an unremarkable regular-season game would ordinarily draw no attention at all. It drew attention this time because of what had been bet on him beforehand. A prop wager on Porter's individual statistical output that evening, worth $80,000 to whoever had placed it, had been flagged by a sportsbook's own monitoring systems before the game even finished, and the payout was frozen rather than processed. The wager had been unusually large and unusually specific for a player of Porter's profile, exactly the kind of anomaly the industry's automated integrity systems exist to catch, and catching it turned out to be the first thread in unraveling a scheme that ended, a month later, with Commissioner Adam Silver handing down a lifetime ban.

What investigators found, once they pulled the thread, was more extensive than a single suspicious bet. Between January and March of 2024, while traveling with either the Raptors or their G League team, Porter placed at least thirteen bets on NBA games using an associate's betting account rather than his own — wagers ranging from $15 to $22,000 apiece, totaling $54,094 wagered and $76,059 returned, a net gain of $21,965. He had also, according to the league's findings, disclosed confidential information about his own health status to a bettor he knew to be actively wagering on his performance, and had limited his own playing time in at least one game — the March 20 contest against the Kings, the one that had triggered the frozen $80,000 payout — specifically to influence the outcome of prop bets built around his statistics. In July 2024, Porter pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud, admitting in his plea that he had prearranged with bettors to remove himself from games early, citing fabricated injuries, so that the bettors — who knew in advance what he planned to do — could safely wager the under on his individual prop lines and collect a guaranteed profit.

The Porter case is worth sitting with in detail because it is not, in its basic structure, a new kind of fraud. Point-shaving and game-fixing are as old as organized betting itself. Eight members of the 1919 Chicago White Sox, Shoeless Joe Jackson among them, were accused of accepting bribes to lose the World Series to Cincinnati; a Chicago jury acquitted all eight in 1921 after key evidence disappeared from the grand jury record, but the sport's first commissioner, Kenesaw Mountain Landis, banned them from the game for life the very next day regardless of the verdict, a precedent Bart Giamatti would invoke explicitly seven decades later in banning Pete Rose. College basketball had its own reckoning a generation after that, when a Manhattan College player named Junius Kellogg reported a bribery attempt in 1951, an act that unraveled a point-shaving ring reaching into City College of New York's own recent national-championship team, whose players admitted taking at least a thousand dollars a game to shave points off games they were favored to win comfortably anyway. What is new, in the legal, data-driven, prop-saturated market this book has been describing, is not the underlying temptation. It is the specific vulnerability player-prop betting creates, one that did not exist in the same form when a bettor could only wager on which team would win or by how much. A team's final score depends on the coordinated effort of an entire roster, a head coach's game plan, and the other team's own performance — manipulating it convincingly requires either a conspiracy spanning multiple players or a single player willing to visibly and repeatedly sabotage a team result in a way teammates, coaches, and broadcasters are likely to notice. A player's own individual statistical line, by contrast, is something a single athlete can influence unilaterally, in ways that are difficult to distinguish from an ordinary bad shooting night or a coach's legitimate decision to limit a role player's minutes. Jontay Porter did not need to convince ten other players to cooperate. He needed only to take himself out of a game and claim he felt sick, a plausible, common, entirely unremarkable event in professional basketball that happened, in his case, to have been arranged in advance with the people betting against his production. The same asymmetry applies to a baseball pitcher's strikeout total, a quarterback's passing yards, a golfer's stroke count on a single hole — any statistic granular enough to be bet on individually is granular enough for the single person generating it to influence alone, without needing a teammate's cooperation or a coach's knowledge.

A parallel category of prop-style products has grown up alongside licensed sportsbooks specifically by avoiding the sportsbook label altogether, and it complicates any assumption that the integrity infrastructure described later in this chapter covers the entire market where individual-player props are actually sold. Companies including PrizePicks and Underdog built daily-fantasy-style pick'em products that let a customer wager on whether a specific player's statistical output will land over or under a set line — functionally identical, from a bettor's perspective, to a sportsbook player prop — while claiming the legal status of a game of skill rather than a bet, a classification this book's earlier chapter on daily fantasy sports traced back to the industry's own 2015-era legal strategy. Regulators in a growing number of states rejected that classification once the pick'em products scaled: seven states banned the prop-style pick'em format in 2023 alone, Florida forced PrizePicks, Underdog, and a third operator called Betr to stop offering the product in March 2024, and New York's Gaming Commission required PrizePicks to pay nearly $15 million for operating without a license after formally classifying prop-style daily fantasy contests as unlicensed sports wagering. Several operators responded not by exiting the market but by restructuring the product into a peer-to-peer format, pitting customers against each other rather than against the house, a structural tweak several of the same states that had issued cease-and-desist orders subsequently accepted as compliant. Whatever their legal classification in any given state, these products depend on exactly the same individual-player statistical data this chapter has already described as the core vulnerability behind Porter, Rozier, and the college basketball conspiracy — and it is considerably less clear that a product operating under a daily-fantasy license, in a state that has not yet forced it into the sportsbook regulatory category, is being monitored by the same integrity infrastructure Sportradar and the leagues built for their own licensed sportsbook partners.

The league's response, beyond banning Porter, was to restrict the market that had made the scheme profitable in the first place. Following the case, the NBA and its sportsbook partners moved to stop offering prop bets specifically on players like Porter — low-salary, limited-role players whose statistical lines could be manipulated with the least practical difficulty and the least likelihood of drawing attention, since a fringe rotation player logging three unexpected minutes attracts far less scrutiny than a starter doing the same. The industry's own shorthand for the change, adopted quickly in sports-betting trade coverage, became "the Jontay Porter rule" — an informal recognition that the case had exposed a specific, addressable seam in how prop markets were being constructed, rather than a broader retreat from prop betting as a category. The rule addresses the most easily exploited version of the vulnerability without eliminating the underlying incentive structure: a star player's individual props remain heavily bet and heavily marketed, and the same basic mechanism — an athlete with knowledge of his own health, workload, or game plan, selling or acting on that knowledge before it becomes public — remains available to any player, at any salary level, willing to risk his career the way Porter did.

The Jontay Porter rule did not end the problem it was named for, and the clearest evidence of that arrived a year and a half later, at a considerably higher level of the sport. On October 23, 2025, federal prosecutors unsealed indictments against thirty-four people in two overlapping investigations the FBI described as connected to organized-crime figures, and among those arrested were Miami Heat guard Terry Rozier and Portland Trail Blazers head coach Chauncey Billups, a Hall of Famer whose playing career had ended more than a decade earlier. The two cases were related but distinct. Billups was accused of serving as what investigators called a face card — a recognizable name whose presence helped lure wealthy targets into rigged, mob-linked poker games in Manhattan, Las Vegas, Miami, and the Hamptons, games in which hidden technology allegedly let insiders see other players' hands. Rozier's case tracked far closer to Porter's, but at a larger scale and with a much more prominent player at its center: prosecutors alleged that between December 2022 and March 2024, Rozier tipped associates to his own availability for prearranged early exits from games, including a March 2023 game against the New Orleans Pelicans in which he left after nine minutes citing an injury, after which his co-conspirators placed roughly $200,000 in wagers on his statistical unders. A superseding indictment filed in May 2026 added a specific allegation that Rozier accepted a $100,000 bribe in exchange for altering his on-court performance. Both Rozier and Billups were placed on immediate leave by their teams, and the NBA said it would continue cooperating with the federal investigation, using language nearly identical to what the league had said about Porter — a signal, whether intended or not, that the mechanism uncovered in Porter's case had not been an isolated aberration the league could treat as solved, but a pattern serious enough to recur, within roughly a year, involving a far more visible player and a far larger alleged payout.

College sports produced its own version of the integrity problem in the same period, in a case that involved not a player but a head coach. Brad Bohannon, Alabama's baseball coach, was fired on May 4, 2023, after casino monitors at a sportsbook in Cincinnati flagged suspicious betting activity connected to an April 28, 2023 game between Alabama and LSU. The pattern that triggered the alert was, in its outline, similar to what would later unfold with Porter: a bettor named Bert Eugene Neff attempted to place a $100,000 wager on the Alabama-LSU game, a size that immediately drew the sportsbook's attention, and staff limited the actual bet accepted to $15,000. The subsequent investigation found that Bohannon had disclosed information about an injury to his starting pitcher to Neff before the game, information that would have been directly relevant to how a bettor might price Alabama's chances that day. The NCAA's Committee on Infractions later found that Bohannon had violated the association's core principles of honesty and sportsmanship, and imposed a fifteen-year show-cause order — a penalty severe enough that any NCAA member institution hiring Bohannon in that window would be required to suspend him from coaching for the entirety of the baseball regular season across his first five years of employment there, an effectively career-ending sanction dressed in the language of a suspension rather than an outright ban. Alabama itself received three years of probation, a $5,000 fine, and a requirement to retain an outside firm to provide gambling education to its student-athletes, coaches, and administrators going forward.

The Bohannon case differs from Porter's in a detail worth pausing on: nothing in the public record establishes that Bohannon himself placed a bet or profited financially from the tip he gave Neff. His violation was disclosure — sharing inside information with someone he knew or should have known was going to act on it — rather than direct wagering or direct financial gain, which places it in a different legal and ethical category even as it produced a comparably severe institutional punishment. That distinction matters for understanding the shape of the integrity risk college sports faces as legal betting has spread into markets far smaller, far less scrutinized, and far more easily influenced than a marquee professional league's flagship product. A college baseball assistant coach, an athletic trainer, a hometown friend with access to a locker room — any of these people sit close enough to genuinely private information, injury status, lineup decisions, effort levels in practice, to move a betting line if they choose to share it, and the market for wagering on college sports has expanded rapidly enough since 2018 that plenty of bettors are now willing to act quickly on exactly that kind of tip, particularly in the prop-bet and player-performance markets that reward granular inside knowledge most directly.

Bohannon's case and Rozier's case, taken together, still understated the scale of what federal investigators were finding across college basketball by the start of 2026. On January 15, 2026, prosecutors in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania unsealed an indictment describing a scheme that dwarfed anything the Porter case had suggested was possible: more than thirty-nine players across at least seventeen NCAA Division I men's basketball programs had allegedly rigged, or attempted to rig, more than twenty-nine games, in a conspiracy prosecutors tied to earlier game-fixing in the Chinese Basketball Association as well. Twenty current and former college players and six alleged fixers, including a former NBA player named Antonio Blakeney, were charged with wire fraud, conspiracy, and bribery in sporting contests; one of the fixers pleaded guilty within weeks of the indictment being unsealed. The mechanics were the same basic pattern as the Porter and Rozier cases scaled up and systematized: payments to individual players ranging from $10,000 to $30,000 per game, in exchange for deliberately underperforming in ways a bettor already positioned against the team's cover number could profit from. What distinguished this scheme from the higher-profile professional cases was who the fixers targeted. Reporting on the indictment noted that the players recruited came disproportionately from smaller, lower-profile programs — Kennesaw State, New Orleans, Nicholls State, Robert Morris, Coppin State, Tulane, Northwestern State, Abilene Christian — rather than the marquee basketball powers that dominate national broadcasts, on the theory, prosecutors suggested, that players at mid-major programs without the lucrative name-image-and-likeness endorsement income flowing to stars at bigger schools would be more receptive to a cash offer for underperforming in a game few people outside their own campus were watching closely. If the Porter case had exposed how easily a single low-salary professional's individual statistics could be manipulated, the January 2026 indictment showed the same underlying economic logic operating across an entire tier of college sports simultaneously: wherever the gap between an athlete's market value and a fixer's cash offer is widest, the individual-performance prop market creates the same vulnerability, at whatever scale the fixers are organized enough to exploit.

The infrastructure built to catch these cases did not exist, in anything like its current form, before the legal market required it. The NCAA describes its own integrity-monitoring effort as the largest global program of its kind, screening more than 22,000 NCAA contests a year for the statistical signatures of manipulation, work done substantially through Sportradar, the same data company whose exclusive rights deals with the NBA, NFL, and MLB were examined in this book's earlier chapter on in-play betting — the identical real-time data pipeline that makes same-game parlays and micro-betting possible also generates the forensic record that later makes fraud detectable. Individual conferences have built parallel, sometimes overlapping monitoring relationships of their own; the Big Ten, for instance, contracts separately with a firm called IC360, formed in October 2023 when U.S. Integrity, one of the sector's earlier specialized monitoring companies, merged with a compliance firm called Odds On Compliance. Sportradar itself launched what it calls its Universal Fraud Detection System in February 2021, a platform designed to flag anomalous betting patterns across a much broader swath of the legal market than any single league or conference could monitor on its own, cross-referencing betting-line movement against publicly available information about injuries, lineup changes, and other events that might legitimately explain a sudden shift in a market's price. Illinois has gone further than most states by statute, barring prop bets on its own in-state college teams entirely, a targeted response to exactly the asymmetric-vulnerability problem the Porter and Bohannon cases both illustrate. NCAA President Charlie Baker has pushed publicly for a broader version of the same restriction, arguing that prop bets on individual college athletes should be prohibited nationally rather than left to a patchwork of state-by-state rules that leaves some college markets far more exposed than others.

It is worth being honest about what this monitoring infrastructure can and cannot actually accomplish, because the industry's own marketing of its integrity systems sometimes implies a level of prevention the underlying mechanics do not really support. Sportradar's Universal Fraud Detection System and the NCAA's monitoring program are both fundamentally detection tools, not prevention tools: they work by identifying statistical anomalies in betting patterns after unusual money has already moved, which means the systems are structurally better at catching a fraud already partway underway than at stopping one before it starts. The $80,000 prop bet on Jontay Porter was frozen before it paid out, which the industry rightly points to as a success story — the monitoring system worked exactly as designed. But the bet was placed, and Porter had already left the game, before anyone outside the conspiracy knew anything was wrong; the system caught the fraud in progress rather than preventing the underlying arrangement between Porter and his bettors from forming in the first place. A genuinely motivated insider willing to keep bet sizes modest, spread wagers across multiple accounts, and avoid the kind of single, glaringly oversized bet that triggered the Porter alert would face a meaningfully harder detection problem, and nothing in the public record of either the Porter or Bohannon cases suggests the industry's current monitoring tools would reliably catch a more disciplined version of the same scheme.

The Rozier and Billups arrests make that limitation explicit rather than merely theoretical, because the case was not built the way Porter's was. Nothing in the public reporting on the October 2025 arrests describes a sportsbook's own anomaly-detection software flagging Rozier's betting line the way Sportradar's or a book's internal risk team flagged Porter's $80,000 prop. The Rozier and Billups cases instead grew out of a broader FBI investigation into organized-crime-linked gambling operations — rigged poker games, loan-sharking, the kind of racketeering enforcement federal prosecutors have run for decades, largely independent of any legal sportsbook's own monitoring infrastructure — that happened to intersect with allegations of on-court manipulation once investigators started examining who the mob-linked operation's associates actually were. That is a meaningfully different detection pathway than the one this chapter has otherwise credited with catching Porter and Bohannon: an anomalous betting pattern flagged by an automated system built specifically for that purpose, versus a wiretap and an informant network built for an entirely different kind of organized-crime prosecution that happened to surface a sports-integrity case as a byproduct. The industry's own integrity infrastructure deserves credit for what it actually caught. It should not be credited, on the strength of the Rozier case, with catching something federal organized-crime investigators found first.

There is a real counter-case embedded in all four stories, and it deserves acknowledgment rather than a footnote: Porter, Rozier and Billups, Bohannon, and the thirty-nine college players named in the January 2026 indictment were all caught. Every one of these cases was caught by systems that did not exist in anything like their current form before the post-2018 legal market required their construction, and every one resulted in swift, publicly documented federal or league discipline rather than the kind of quiet, internal handling that characterized much of organized sports' historical relationship with gambling corruption, including the years Major League Baseball spent managing the Black Sox scandal's aftermath largely out of public view before Landis's ban made the sport's position unambiguous. The legal, regulated market's critics sometimes describe prop betting's integrity risk as though it were a novel danger legalization introduced into a previously clean system; the more accurate account is that manipulation risk has always existed wherever money can be won by influencing an athletic outcome, running in an unbroken line from 1919 to 1951 to 2024, and that the illegal or semi-regulated markets those earlier fixers exploited offered no comparable forensic trail and no comparable institutional mechanism for catching what happened as quickly as it was caught in Porter's case, in Rozier's, or in the sprawling college conspiracy unsealed in January 2026.

What should temper any straightforward relief at that record is the trajectory rather than any single case in isolation. The Jontay Porter rule, adopted specifically to close the exact seam Porter had exploited, was in place for a year and a half before federal investigators unsealed a case involving a far more prominent player allegedly taking a six-figure bribe, and before a second case surfaced describing more than ten times as many manipulated games across more than twice as many teams as anyone had previously acknowledged in American college basketball. Each scandal produced a response narrower than the scandal that provoked it — a rule restricting props on low-salary players, a round of arrests, a fifteen-year show-cause order — and each response was, within roughly a year, followed by a new case operating at a larger scale than the one the response had been built to prevent. Whether the detection systems examined in this chapter are actually keeping pace with a growing market's growing incentive to exploit them, or are instead simply getting better at cleaning up scandals of ever-increasing size after the fact, is a question this book's remaining chapters — particularly its account of the industry's own numbers — return to from a different angle.
