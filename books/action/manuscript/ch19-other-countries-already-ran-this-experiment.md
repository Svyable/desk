# Other Countries Already Ran This Experiment

The United States did not invent legal, technology-mediated sports betting, and it is not the first country to discover that a legal, regulated betting market can still be engineered aggressively enough to require a second round of regulation. Britain legalized off-course betting shops in 1961, built a national regulatory framework under the Gambling Act 2005, and spent the years since learning, expensively and in public, exactly how far a legal industry will push a permissive rulebook before a government intervenes. Australia took a more piecemeal path — legal wagering long predates any single national framework there — but arrived, over the past three years, at some of the most specific advertising and inducement restrictions anywhere in the developed world. Neither country's experience maps onto the American one perfectly; both have national health systems, different advertising and media regulatory traditions, and betting markets shaped by decades of legal, land-based wagering that most of the United States never had before 2018. But both ran a real experiment this book's earlier chapters can only gesture toward hypothetically, and both produced results specific enough, and recent enough, to be worth taking seriously as a preview of where the American fight is likely headed next.

Britain's clearest, most concrete lesson concerns a machine, not an app, and it is worth walking through in detail because the mechanism of harm it revealed generalizes directly to the phone-based products chapters 8 through 10 of this book examined in the American market. Fixed-odds betting terminals — electronic machines installed inside licensed betting shops, offering rapid-fire casino-style games including roulette, with results generated every twenty seconds or so — became, through the 2000s and into the 2010s, the single largest source of profit for Britain's largest bookmakers, contributing roughly half of some chains' entire high-street revenue. The machines allowed a maximum stake of £100 per spin, a limit high enough that a bettor could, in principle, lose thousands of pounds in a single sitting at a machine installed in an ordinary storefront on an ordinary high street, with no dealer, no other players, and no natural pause in play beyond the twenty seconds the machine itself imposed. British anti-gambling campaigners and, eventually, members of Parliament from multiple parties began calling the machines the "crack cocaine of gambling," a characterization that became so widely repeated in British press coverage and parliamentary debate that it functioned, by the mid-2010s, as the terminals' de facto public name. The campaign built for years before it produced action: the government's own Gambling Commission published statistics showing 33,360 of the specific machine category involved, known in industry classification as B2 machines, operating across Great Britain as of May 2019, generating a combined gross gambling yield of roughly £1.5 billion annually from that category alone. Behind the campaign that eventually forced Parliament's hand were specific families, not an abstract statistic, and one of them is worth naming because the connection between the product and the harm it named is exactly the connection this book has tried to trace in the American market. Jack Ritchie, a young man from Sheffield, began betting on fixed-odds betting terminals at sixteen or seventeen, while still at school; he killed himself in 2017, at twenty-four, after years struggling with a gambling disorder his parents, Liz and Charles Ritchie, would later say they had not recognized in time to intervene. The following year, the Ritchies founded a charity called Gambling with Lives, built around supporting other families bereaved by gambling-related suicide and campaigning for the same legislative changes this chapter has already described — the stake cut, and later a broader push for restrictions on gambling advertising modeled on Britain's own 2005 ban on tobacco advertising. The charity's advocacy became a visible, repeated presence in the parliamentary debate that led to the 2019 stake reduction, and the Ritchies were awarded MBEs in 2023 for that work. Nothing in Jack Ritchie's story proves, on its own, that fixed-odds betting terminals caused his death in some simple mechanical sense the way a single dose of a contaminated drug might cause a single, traceable overdose; gambling disorder, like most mental-health crises, arrived alongside other pressures this book has no way of fully reconstructing from public reporting. What his story does establish, and what made it politically effective in Britain in a way this book's earlier chapters have shown American harm statistics alone have not yet managed in Congress, is that the debate over a £100 stake was never actually abstract to the people fighting hardest to change it — it had a name, a school, a family, and a campaign that outlasted the loss it was built around.

Parliament eventually acted through secondary legislation rather than a fresh act — the Gaming Machine (Miscellaneous Amendments and Revocation) Regulations 2018, which took effect April 1, 2019, cut the maximum stake on a single spin from £100 to £2, a reduction of 98 percent applied virtually overnight to a product that had been generating close to half the profit of Britain's largest bookmaking chains.

The industry's own response to the stake cut is itself a data point worth taking seriously, because it demonstrates just how load-bearing the highest-stake, highest-velocity product had become to the underlying business model. William Hill said publicly it might have to close as many as 700 shops and put 4,500 jobs at risk; Ladbrokes Coral's parent company GVC projected roughly 900 shop closures threatening 5,000 jobs; Betfred forecast up to 500 closures and a headcount reduction of 2,500. Those were not idle threats — Britain's high-street bookmaking footprint did contract meaningfully in the years following the stake cut, as betting shops whose economics had depended on the £100-stake machine found the same physical retail space no longer supported profitably by a £2-stake replacement. That contraction is precisely the trade-off worth sitting with rather than glossing over: a regulator cut the maximum loss available to a single vulnerable customer in a single sitting by 98 percent, and the industry's own numbers suggest that decision cost thousands of retail jobs built around a product whose profitability depended specifically on the largest, fastest losses a machine could extract from the customers least able to stop playing. Britain's Parliament, faced with that trade-off explicitly rather than hypothetically, chose the customers' side, and the country's high street looks different today as a direct, acknowledged consequence.

Britain's second and more recent lesson concerns not a single machine but the regulatory architecture around online betting, and the details matter because two different systems are easy to confuse. The Gambling Commission's **financial vulnerability checks** are the live system. They began at a £500 net-deposit threshold in a rolling thirty-day period in August 2024 and moved to £150 on February 28, 2025. Those checks use public-record information — bankruptcy orders, county-court judgments and similar indicators — and do **not** use credit-reference-agency data. The separate **Financial Risk Assessment** project is aimed at a much smaller group of high-spending customers and would use limited credit-reference data to identify current financial difficulty. That system went through a pilot with operators and credit-reference agencies rather than a national live rollout. In July 2026 the Commission announced that it would proceed with staged implementation: when fully implemented, it expects fewer than three percent of accounts to require an assessment, and says about 97 percent of those assessments should be frictionless and document-free. That is meaningful evidence that the architecture is technically workable. It is not yet evidence that the system has reduced losses by a particular percentage, and this book should not pretend otherwise.

The distinction changes what Britain can honestly be said to prove. It already proves that a national regulator can impose a consistent, low-friction public-record vulnerability check across licensed remote operators and can pilot a more targeted credit-data system at national scale. It does **not**, as of this book's September 2026 research cutoff, provide a completed national outcome study showing that Financial Risk Assessments reduced median customer losses. The policy fight also remains real: bookmakers, punters' groups and horse-racing interests have argued that intrusive checks can push legitimate high-spending customers toward unlicensed offshore operators, while the Commission argues that high-spending customers are disproportionately likely to have defaults and debt-management plans that operators have historically failed to identify. The useful lesson for the American market is therefore narrower and sturdier than a headline efficacy claim: automated financial-risk screening can be built to reach only a small fraction of accounts and remain frictionless for almost all of those it reaches, but whether the intervention changes gambling harm at scale still has to be measured rather than assumed.

Australia's experience runs on a different axis entirely — not stake limits or loss-triggered checks, but the sheer saturation of gambling advertising itself, a problem American readers watching NFL broadcasts saturated with sportsbook ads since 2018 will recognize immediately. Australian betting advertising during live sports broadcasts had, by the early 2020s, become dense enough to generate sustained public and political backlash, and in June 2023 a federal parliamentary inquiry, chaired by the Labor MP Peta Murphy before her death from cancer later that year, recommended a complete phase-out of all online gambling advertising — a full ban, modeled loosely on the restrictions Australia had already placed on tobacco advertising decades earlier, to be implemented gradually over roughly three years. The Murphy report's full-ban recommendation did not survive the legislative process intact. The government that eventually acted, under Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, fell short of a complete ban, and it took another three years past the report's publication for legislation to pass at all: the Interactive Gambling Amendment (Gambling Reform) Bill 2026 and its companion cost-recovery bill cleared both houses on August 19, 2026, and the main bill received royal assent on August 26 as Act No. 72 of 2026. Most of the core advertising and inducement provisions commence on January 1, 2027, which means this book is describing enacted rules before their first real outcome data exist.

The final law gives some sense of how far the compromise traveled from the Murphy report's original full-ban recommendation. It prohibits wagering advertising during live sports coverage across the day and overnight, subject to statutory exceptions, and caps television wagering advertisements outside that context at no more than three in any sixty-minute period between 5:00 a.m. and 8:30 p.m. It also restricts online wagering advertising to logged-in adults who have not opted out, bans wagering advertising in sporting venues and on uniforms subject to transitional arrangements, bars the use of notable people such as athletes and celebrities, and prohibits promotion of odds in wagering advertising. Those rules are more substantial than the narrower compromise this book's first research pass described, but they still stop short of the Murphy inquiry's proposed full advertising ban.

Where the Australian reform also cuts decisively is on inducements — the free bets, deposit matches, and bonus credits chapter 6 of this book examined in detail as the American industry's primary tool for converting a curious first-time visitor into a funded, repeat customer. Under the 2026 reforms, licensed wagering providers will be restricted from directly marketing inducements to a customer during the first fourteen days after account registration, to someone who deregistered from BetStop within the previous three months, and to customers identified as at risk of gambling-related harm. The reforms also ban commissions to staff or affiliates that are based on customer gambling activity. Those measures were enacted in August 2026 but, like the main advertising restrictions, are too new to have generated credible harm-reduction outcomes by this book's research cutoff. The correct comparison with Britain is therefore institutional rather than statistical: both countries have moved specific product and marketing controls from voluntary practice into nationally enforceable rules, while the measurable effects of several of the newest rules remain open questions.

Australia's other national mechanism, BetStop, is older than the 2026 advertising reform and already has a real usage record worth examining on its own, because it is the single closest thing to the national self-exclusion database chapter 16 of this book showed does not exist anywhere in the American market. BetStop launched on August 21, 2023, as a single federal register letting any Australian resident exclude themselves from every licensed wagering operator in the country in one registration, rather than the operator-by-operator or state-by-state process that remains the default in the United States. By June 30, 2026, the Australian Communications and Media Authority reported 65,430 cumulative registrations and 40,160 active exclusions. Those are not enormous numbers relative to Australia's population, and registration counts by themselves do not measure prevented financial harm. ACMA's user research has nevertheless found self-excluded gamblers reporting improved quality of life, while enforcement cases also show the system is not self-executing: in May 2026 the regulator found more than 500 breaches by Entain businesses involving customers registered with BetStop. The useful American comparison is therefore not that BetStop is perfect. It is that a national, single-registration exclusion credential is functioning at meaningful scale, produces auditable compliance obligations, and gives one regulator visibility into failures that a state-by-state system can leave fragmented.

## What America can actually import

The temptation in comparative policy writing is to turn another country into a vending machine for solutions: identify a rule that sounds sensible, quote the foreign regulator, then recommend that Congress buy the same thing. Sports betting is a good example of why that is too easy. The mechanisms above can travel. Their institutional surroundings often cannot.

The most portable idea is not a particular British threshold or Australian advertising hour. It is **interoperability**. BetStop works as a national exclusion system because an exclusion does not have to be renegotiated with every operator. The British vulnerability-check rule works as a national standard because every licensed remote operator has to know what the same threshold means. Australia's advertising law can define a live-sport window because broadcasters and wagering companies cannot select a weaker state rule every time a national match crosses a border. In each case, the safety feature becomes materially stronger because the customer does not have to re-create it company by company.

That is the point American self-exclusion architecture currently misses. A person whose gambling has become dangerous is asked to act like a compliance officer: identify the relevant regulator, understand whether the exclusion covers online wagering or casinos or both, repeat the process in neighboring states, learn whether each operator has an additional proprietary tool, and then keep doing all of that at the precise moment impaired control is the problem being treated. The American system does not need a federal gaming commission to recognize how perverse that is. States can adopt an interoperable credential. Tribal gaming authorities can decide by compact or reciprocal agreement how to honor it. Operators can be required to treat a verified exclusion as portable even where the underlying legal authority remains local.

The second portable idea is **graduated friction**. Britain is useful not because £150 is a magic number; it almost certainly is not. The useful design principle is that the regulatory system distinguishes between the overwhelming majority of ordinary accounts and the much smaller group whose spending or financial circumstances justify another layer of review. The political caricature of an affordability regime imagines a bureaucrat demanding bank statements before somebody can place a twenty-pound football bet. The architecture the Gambling Commission has actually described is aimed at avoiding precisely that outcome. Public-record checks are designed to run quietly. The more ambitious credit-reference assessment is intended for a small minority, with the overwhelming majority of those assessments completed without documents from the customer.

An American version should be judged by the same standard. If a state builds a risk check that creates material friction for millions of low-spending recreational bettors, it has designed the check badly. If it builds one that never changes anything for a customer depositing thousands of dollars into an account while carrying obvious debt distress, it has also designed the check badly. The point is not maximum friction or minimum friction. It is to put friction where the evidence of risk is concentrated.

The third portable idea is **auditable failure**. BetStop's Entain enforcement case is useful precisely because it is embarrassing. A national exclusion register did not prevent every prohibited contact or wager. It created a common rule, a record of who should have been excluded, and therefore evidence that a licensee failed to honor the rule more than five hundred times. Without the common register those same failures could dissolve into disconnected customer-service complaints, each apparently local and anecdotal. The system's imperfection made enforcement possible because the standard was legible enough to be violated.

American regulation often measures compliance at the level easiest for an operator to document: the warning was displayed, the hotline number appeared, the self-exclusion button existed, the employee completed training. The foreign systems point toward a harder question: did the protection survive contact with the customer across time and across companies? Did the excluded person remain excluded? Did the risky account actually encounter a different intervention? Did the advertising exposure fall in the window the statute was written to protect? Those are outcome-shaped questions rather than paperwork-shaped ones.

A fourth idea is **sequencing**. Britain did not jump directly from no financial checks to a single intrusive national credit test. It required a limited public-record vulnerability check, gathered implementation data, piloted the more ambitious assessment with operators and credit-reference agencies, reported technical results, and then announced staged implementation. That path can look maddeningly slow to people who believe the underlying harm is already obvious. It also creates the kind of record a durable rule needs: how many accounts are touched, how fast checks return, where false friction appears, how operators misunderstand the data, and which interventions still require outcome evaluation.

There is a lesson here for both sides of the American debate. Reformers weaken their case when they convert a promising pilot into a proven health outcome before the evidence exists. Industry weakens its case when it treats the existence of unresolved implementation questions as proof that a mechanism cannot work. A pilot is neither a victory nor a failure. It is a way to make the argument smaller and more empirical.

What does **not** travel cleanly is just as important. Britain's regulator licenses a national remote-gambling market. Australia has a federal communications regulator with national authority over its online wagering register and advertising system. The United States distributes relevant authority across state legislatures, state gaming commissions, tribal governments, federal commodities law, broadcast and communications law, and ordinary consumer-protection law. The institutional map is not a nuisance around the policy. It is part of the policy.

That means a credible American reform cannot simply write "national BetStop" at the top of a bill and consider sovereignty solved. A tribal nation with its own gaming authority has legitimate reasons to resist a database designed as if state jurisdiction were the only jurisdiction that matters. A state that built a strict self-exclusion regime has legitimate reasons not to accept a weaker reciprocal standard. A federal rule drafted so broadly that it re-creates the anti-commandeering problem at the center of *Murphy* can lose years in constitutional litigation before helping a single bettor.

The more practical route may look less elegant: common technical standards, reciprocal recognition, federal incentives, minimum customer-facing requirements where Congress has clear authority, and state and tribal systems that remain sovereign while agreeing on the few signals that must travel. An exclusion credential does not need to reveal a person's entire betting history. A risk intervention report does not need to become a federal gambling dossier. An advertising cap does not require one national tax rate. Interoperability can be narrower than uniformity.

That distinction may be the single most useful thing the foreign comparison contributes. The United States does not have to choose between fifty incompatible systems and one omnipotent national regulator. It can decide which protections fail when they stop at a border and make those protections portable while leaving much of the surrounding regulatory architecture where it already sits.

A fair reading of both cases also has to account for why the American system resists this kind of single-stroke national fix more structurally than either comparison country's system did, rather than treating the difference as simply a matter of political will. Britain's Gambling Commission and Australia's ACMA each answer to one national government, regulate one national market, and can, once a reform clears a single national legislature, apply it uniformly. The United States has neither a single gaming regulator nor a single legislature with clear authority to write one, for the specific constitutional reason chapter 16 of this book already traced: *Murphy v. NCAA*'s anti-commandeering holding is precisely what makes a British- or Australian-style top-down national mandate constitutionally awkward for Congress to impose on state gaming regulators who were never required to accept federal standards to begin with. Layered on top of that federalism problem is the tribal sovereignty question chapters 15 and 18 both examined at length — a Minnesota or a New Mexico cannot simply adopt Britain's architecture by state statute the way Britain adopted its national rules, because a meaningful share of the market in states with significant tribal gaming runs through compacts negotiated government-to-government between a state and a sovereign tribal nation, an intergovernmental relationship with no real analogue in either Britain's or Australia's regulatory history. None of that makes the British or Australian reforms irrelevant to the American debate; it means the honest lesson to draw from them is narrower than "simply copy the £150 check nationwide." The more defensible lesson is that the specific mechanisms — national vulnerability screening, a single-registration self-exclusion system, live-sport advertising restrictions and television frequency caps — are buildable in markets recognizably similar to the American one, even though the American system would likely have to assemble something closer to those results through state action, interstate coordination, tribal compacts, or federal minimum standards capable of surviving constitutional review.

What both countries' experiences share, and what makes them worth a full chapter in a book otherwise focused on the American market, is the specific shape of the political process that produced each reform — a shape the United States, per chapters 15 and 16, has not yet replicated at national scale. Britain forced a £2 stake ceiling onto fixed-odds betting terminals and later standardized vulnerability screening across licensed remote operators while building a more ambitious credit-data assessment system in stages. Australia built BetStop nationally and in 2026 enacted advertising and inducement restrictions that begin taking effect in 2027. The evidence is therefore strongest where enough time has passed to measure an operating system and weakest where the law has only just changed. That asymmetry is useful rather than embarrassing. It means the American debate no longer has to proceed entirely in the hypothetical, but it also means another country's new law should not be treated as an outcome before the outcome exists. The £2 stake is an operating policy. The £150 public-record check is an operating policy. BetStop is an operating national exclusion system with published usage and enforcement data. Britain's credit-reference Financial Risk Assessments and Australia's 2026 advertising reforms are newer stages of the experiment whose effects still need to be earned by evidence. That is the comparison worth carrying forward: not that Britain or Australia solved gambling, but that they made particular design choices legible enough to test.
