# Other Countries Already Ran This Experiment

The United States did not invent legal, technology-mediated sports betting, and it is not the first country to discover that a legal, regulated betting market can still be engineered aggressively enough to require a second round of regulation. Britain legalized off-course betting shops in 1961, built a national regulatory framework under the Gambling Act 2005, and spent the years since learning, expensively and in public, exactly how far a legal industry will push a permissive rulebook before a government intervenes. Australia took a more piecemeal path — legal wagering long predates any single national framework there — but arrived, over the past three years, at some of the most specific advertising and inducement restrictions anywhere in the developed world. Neither country's experience maps onto the American one perfectly; both have national health systems, different advertising and media regulatory traditions, and betting markets shaped by decades of legal, land-based wagering that most of the United States never had before 2018. But both ran a real experiment this book's earlier chapters can only gesture toward hypothetically, and both produced results specific enough, and recent enough, to be worth taking seriously as a preview of where the American fight is likely headed next.

Britain's clearest, most concrete lesson concerns a machine, not an app, and it is worth walking through in detail because the mechanism of harm it revealed generalizes directly to the phone-based products chapters 8 through 10 of this book examined in the American market. Fixed-odds betting terminals — electronic machines installed inside licensed betting shops, offering rapid-fire casino-style games including roulette, with results generated every twenty seconds or so — became, through the 2000s and into the 2010s, the single largest source of profit for Britain's largest bookmakers, contributing roughly half of some chains' entire high-street revenue. The machines allowed a maximum stake of £100 per spin, a limit high enough that a bettor could, in principle, lose thousands of pounds in a single sitting at a machine installed in an ordinary storefront on an ordinary high street, with no dealer, no other players, and no natural pause in play beyond the twenty seconds the machine itself imposed. British anti-gambling campaigners and, eventually, members of Parliament from multiple parties began calling the machines the "crack cocaine of gambling," a characterization that became so widely repeated in British press coverage and parliamentary debate that it functioned, by the mid-2010s, as the terminals' de facto public name. The campaign built for years before it produced action: the government's own Gambling Commission published statistics showing 33,360 of the specific machine category involved, known in industry classification as B2 machines, operating across Great Britain as of May 2019, generating a combined gross gambling yield of roughly £1.5 billion annually from that category alone. Behind the campaign that eventually forced Parliament's hand were specific families, not an abstract statistic, and one of them is worth naming because the connection between the product and the harm it named is exactly the connection this book has tried to trace in the American market. Jack Ritchie, a young man from Sheffield, began betting on fixed-odds betting terminals at sixteen or seventeen, while still at school; he killed himself in 2017, at twenty-four, after years struggling with a gambling disorder his parents, Liz and Charles Ritchie, would later say they had not recognized in time to intervene. The following year, the Ritchies founded a charity called Gambling with Lives, built around supporting other families bereaved by gambling-related suicide and campaigning for the same legislative changes this chapter has already described — the stake cut, and later a broader push for restrictions on gambling advertising modeled on Britain's own 2005 ban on tobacco advertising. The charity's advocacy became a visible, repeated presence in the parliamentary debate that led to the 2019 stake reduction, and the Ritchies were awarded MBEs in 2023 for that work. Nothing in Jack Ritchie's story proves, on its own, that fixed-odds betting terminals caused his death in some simple mechanical sense the way a single dose of a contaminated drug might cause a single, traceable overdose; gambling disorder, like most mental-health crises, arrived alongside other pressures this book has no way of fully reconstructing from public reporting. What his story does establish, and what made it politically effective in Britain in a way this book's earlier chapters have shown American harm statistics alone have not yet managed in Congress, is that the debate over a £100 stake was never actually abstract to the people fighting hardest to change it — it had a name, a school, a family, and a campaign that outlasted the loss it was built around.

Parliament eventually acted through secondary legislation rather than a fresh act — the Gaming Machine (Miscellaneous Amendments and Revocation) Regulations 2018, which took effect April 1, 2019, cut the maximum stake on a single spin from £100 to £2, a reduction of 98 percent applied virtually overnight to a product that had been generating close to half the profit of Britain's largest bookmaking chains.

The industry's own response to the stake cut is itself a data point worth taking seriously, because it demonstrates just how load-bearing the highest-stake, highest-velocity product had become to the underlying business model. William Hill said publicly it might have to close as many as 700 shops and put 4,500 jobs at risk; Ladbrokes Coral's parent company GVC projected roughly 900 shop closures threatening 5,000 jobs; Betfred forecast up to 500 closures and a headcount reduction of 2,500. Those were not idle threats — Britain's high-street bookmaking footprint did contract meaningfully in the years following the stake cut, as betting shops whose economics had depended on the £100-stake machine found the same physical retail space no longer supported profitably by a £2-stake replacement. That contraction is precisely the trade-off worth sitting with rather than glossing over: a regulator cut the maximum loss available to a single vulnerable customer in a single sitting by 98 percent, and the industry's own numbers suggest that decision cost thousands of retail jobs built around a product whose profitability depended specifically on the largest, fastest losses a machine could extract from the customers least able to stop playing. Britain's Parliament, faced with that trade-off explicitly rather than hypothetically, chose the customers' side, and the country's high street looks different today as a direct, acknowledged consequence.

Britain's second and more recent lesson concerns not a single machine but the entire regulatory architecture surrounding online betting, and it is still being written as this book goes to press, which makes it the more directly instructive case for an American reader trying to understand where a similar fight might lead. The Gambling Act 2005 had governed British gambling regulation for nearly two decades by the time the government published its formal review in 2023, under the title "High Stakes: Gambling Reform for the Digital Age" — a title whose ambition the reform's actual content mostly lived up to. The white paper's central proposal, more consequential for online betting specifically than any single stake limit, was a system of financial-risk checks tied to how much money a customer had actually lost, rather than to any single wager size: a customer whose net losses crossed defined thresholds would trigger an automated background check, drawing on credit-reference data, assessing whether continued losses at that pace posed a realistic risk to the customer's financial stability, with escalating scrutiny at higher thresholds. The first concrete threshold went live nationally on February 28, 2025: a mandatory £150 financial-vulnerability check, applied automatically once a customer's net deposits crossed that level at any UK Gambling Commission-licensed online operator, casino included. The Commission built the system deliberately to minimize friction for the overwhelming majority of customers who show no signs of harm — a twelve-month pilot run with major operators and the country's three main credit-reference agencies, Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion, found that roughly 95 percent of these lowest-tier checks resolved automatically, in the background, without the customer ever noticing an interruption, using data the agencies already held rather than requiring a customer to submit new paperwork. A full national rollout followed in the first quarter of 2026, and the Gambling Commission's own early data from that quarter showed something this book has not found an equivalent for anywhere in the American market: among customers whose accounts triggered an affordability intervention, median monthly losses fell by roughly 22 percent compared with the same quarter the year before — a real, measured behavioral effect, tied to a specific, dated policy change, in a market structurally similar enough to the American one that the comparison is not merely aspirational.

It would be a mistake to read Britain's affordability-check system as an unqualified, uncontested success story, and this book should say so plainly rather than let the 22 percent figure stand in for the whole picture. The reform took years to move from the 2023 white paper to a functioning national system precisely because British bookmakers, punters' rights groups, and horse-racing interests — an industry with its own centuries-old dependency on betting revenue this book has not had occasion to examine — fought the specific threshold levels hard, arguing that even a £150 check risked driving legitimate high-spending customers toward unlicensed offshore operators outside the Gambling Commission's reach entirely, an argument with an obvious structural parallel to the illegal-market concerns chapter 17 of this book took seriously in the American context. The system as implemented also required years of technical negotiation between the Gambling Commission and the credit bureaus over exactly what data could be shared and under what privacy safeguards, delays a smaller or faster-moving regulator might not have tolerated. What the British case actually demonstrates, in other words, is not that affordability checks are costless or simple, but that they are technically and politically buildable at national scale, over a period of years rather than decades, by a country whose betting market — mobile-first, promotion-heavy, in-play-driven — looks considerably more like the post-2018 American market than like Nevada's old casino-floor sportsbooks. No American state, and certainly no federal proposal examined in chapter 16, has yet built anything resembling this system's automated, mostly invisible, credit-bureau-anchored architecture; the SAFE Bet Act's affordability language remains, as chapter 16 showed, a stalled bill rather than an operating system with a full year of real loss-reduction data behind it.

Australia's experience runs on a different axis entirely — not stake limits or loss-triggered checks, but the sheer saturation of gambling advertising itself, a problem American readers watching NFL broadcasts saturated with sportsbook ads since 2018 will recognize immediately. Australian betting advertising during live sports broadcasts had, by the early 2020s, become dense enough to generate sustained public and political backlash, and in June 2023 a federal parliamentary inquiry, chaired by the Labor MP Peta Murphy before her death from cancer later that year, recommended a complete phase-out of all online gambling advertising — a full ban, modeled loosely on the restrictions Australia had already placed on tobacco advertising decades earlier, to be implemented gradually over roughly three years. The Murphy report's full-ban recommendation did not survive the legislative process intact. The government that eventually acted, under Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, fell short of a complete ban, and it took another three years past the report's publication for legislation to pass at all: the Interactive Gambling Amendment (Gambling Reform) Act 2026 and a companion National Self-Exclusion Register (Cost Recovery Levy) Amendment Act cleared Parliament on August 19, 2026, more than three years after the Murphy inquiry's original recommendation, in a compromise industry critics and harm-reduction advocates both described, from opposite directions, as unsatisfying — restricting rather than eliminating the volume of wagering advertising permitted online and during broadcasts, while leaving the door open to gambling advertising's continued, if reduced, presence around Australian sport.

The final law's specific advertising limit, once it emerged from three years of negotiation, gives some sense of how far the compromise actually traveled from the Murphy report's original full-ban recommendation: broadcast betting advertisements are now capped at no more than three per hour between 6:00 a.m. and 8:30 p.m., with a complete ban during the live broadcast of a sporting event itself within that window — a rule that stops an ad from interrupting the game a viewer is actually watching while leaving the surrounding hours of pre- and post-game coverage, and the hours outside the 6:00-to-8:30 window entirely, considerably more open than the outright ban campaigners had pushed for. The gap between what was proposed and what passed is not hard to explain once the money involved is visible. A 2026 report from the Foundation for Alcohol Research and Education found every club in Australia's National Rugby League, and five clubs in the Australian Football League, carried an active gambling sponsorship, arrangements the report estimated were worth roughly $50 million a year to the NRL and $30 to $40 million a year to the AFL combined — revenue neither league was eager to see legislated away, and revenue that gave both leagues a direct commercial incentive to lobby against the stronger version of the reform Peta Murphy's committee had originally proposed. That lobbying was not merely rhetorical: reporting from April 2025 found Australian sports bodies had given federal politicians roughly A$245,000 worth of event tickets and hospitality in the period leading up to the ad-ban debate, a detail that echoes, on a different continent and in a different currency, the same access-buying dynamic chapter 16 of this book documented in the American Gaming Association's own lobbying spending against the SAFE Bet Act.

Where the Australian reform cut more decisively was on inducements — the free bets, deposit matches, and bonus credits chapter 6 of this book examined in detail as the American industry's primary tool for converting a curious first-time visitor into a funded, repeat customer. The new law bars operators from directly marketing any inducement to a customer for fourteen days after that customer registers a new account, extends that ban to three months for anyone who has deregistered from BetStop, Australia's national self-exclusion register, and imposes an indefinite, permanent ban on inducement marketing to any customer an operator has identified as at risk of gambling-related harm. The law goes further than anything currently proposed in the stalled American federal bills chapter 16 described, or in any single state's advertising rules chapter 16 also detailed, by additionally banning commission-based compensation for any staff member or affiliate marketer whose pay had been tied to how much a referred customer actually gambled — closing off, in a single stroke, the exact financial incentive structure that had made aggressive, individually targeted inducement marketing profitable for the people delivering it, not just the companies behind it. Whether Australia's compromise — advertising volume restricted rather than eliminated, inducements sharply curtailed rather than banned outright — produces measurable harm reduction comparable to Britain's 22 percent affordability-check figure is a question this book's research, conducted within days of the Australian law's passage, cannot yet answer; the reform is simply too new to have generated its own outcome data.

Australia's other national mechanism, BetStop, is older than the 2026 advertising reform and already has a real usage record worth examining on its own, because it is the single closest thing to the national self-exclusion database chapter 16 of this book showed does not exist anywhere in the American market. BetStop launched on August 21, 2023, as a single federal register letting any Australian resident exclude themselves from every licensed wagering operator in the country in one registration, rather than the operator-by-operator or state-by-state process that remains the default in the United States. The government's own regulator, the Australian Communications and Media Authority, has published quarterly usage statistics since the register's launch, and the growth curve is steady rather than explosive: roughly 17,400 people had registered by the end of January 2024, climbing to about 30,500 by the end of that September, about 44,800 by June 2025, and about 49,400 by the end of September 2025, with just under 32,000 of those registrations still active as exclusions at that point, the remainder presumably having lapsed or expired under the register's own time-limited exclusion periods. Those are not enormous numbers relative to Australia's roughly twenty-seven million population, and this book has found no study directly measuring how much gambling-related financial harm BetStop's roughly 32,000 active exclusions have actually prevented, a limitation honest enough to state plainly rather than paper over with an inferred success story. What the numbers do establish is more modest and, for the American comparison, more useful: a national, single-registration self-exclusion system is not a theoretical proposal requiring some unbuilt technology. It is a functioning, three-year-old piece of government infrastructure, used by tens of thousands of real people, in a country whose regulatory and legal traditions are close enough to the American ones that the excuse chapter 16 implicitly raised — that no comparable system exists because none has ever been built — has a straightforward answer sitting in a different country's own public statistics.

A fair reading of both cases also has to account for why the American system resists this kind of single-stroke national fix more structurally than either comparison country's system did, rather than treating the difference as simply a matter of political will. Britain's Gambling Commission and Australia's ACMA each answer to one national government, regulate one national market, and can, once a reform clears a single national legislature, apply it uniformly the next morning. The United States has neither a single gaming regulator nor a single legislature with clear authority to write one, for the specific constitutional reason chapter 16 of this book already traced: Murphy v. NCAA's anti-commandeering holding is precisely what makes a British- or Australian-style top-down national mandate constitutionally awkward for Congress to impose on state gaming regulators who were never required to accept federal standards to begin with. Layered on top of that federalism problem is the tribal sovereignty question chapters 15 and 18 both examined at length — a Minnesota or a New Mexico cannot simply adopt Britain's affordability-check architecture by state statute the way Britain adopted it by national regulation, because a meaningful share of the market in states with significant tribal gaming runs through compacts negotiated government-to-government between a state and a sovereign tribal nation, an intergovernmental relationship with no real analogue in either Britain's or Australia's regulatory history. None of that makes the British or Australian reforms irrelevant to the American debate; it means the honest lesson to draw from them is narrower than "simply copy the £150 check nationwide." The more defensible lesson is that the specific mechanisms — a loss-triggered affordability check, a single-registration self-exclusion system, a capped hourly ad limit — are proven, buildable, and measurably effective in a market recognizably similar to the American one, even though the American system would likely have to assemble something closer to Britain's and Australia's results through fifty separate state actions, or through the kind of federal minimum-standards bill chapter 16 showed has stalled twice now, rather than through the single legislative stroke that worked in London and Canberra.

What both countries' experiences share, and what makes them worth a full chapter in a book otherwise focused on the American market, is the specific shape of the political process that produced each reform — a shape the United States, per chapters 15 and 16, has not yet replicated at any level. In both Britain and Australia, a national government with a single regulatory body and a single legislative process eventually forced a specific, measurable design change on an entire national betting industry at once: a £2 stake ceiling applied identically in every betting shop in the country, an affordability-check threshold applied identically at every licensed online operator, an inducement-marketing ban applied identically to every operator holding an Australian license. The United States, by contrast, has fifty separate gaming regulators, fifty separate advertising regimes — the Ohio-versus-Massachusetts-versus-New York patchwork chapter 16 documented in specific dollar-fine detail — and, as of this book's research, no federal body that has forced any single design change on the industry nationwide, ever, at any point since 2018. A hypothetical American SAFE Bet Act, fully enacted with Britain's affordability-check architecture built in wholesale, would be the first American policy response to actually match the scale and uniformity of what Britain and Australia have each, after years of public fights this chapter has tried to render in enough detail to be useful rather than merely inspirational, already built. Nothing currently moving through Congress, per chapter 16's account of the SAFE Bet Act's stalled committee status, is close to becoming that. But the fact that two other large, wealthy, English-speaking democracies with betting markets recognizably similar to the post-2018 American one have already run this experiment — one on a machine, one on a threshold, one on an advertisement — means the American debate no longer has to proceed entirely in the hypothetical. The £2 stake, the £150 check, the fourteen-day inducement ban: these are not thought experiments. They are operating policies, in operating markets, generating real data, some of it — Britain's 22 percent loss reduction chief among it — considerably more encouraging than this book's earlier chapters, focused entirely on what the American industry has built rather than what another country has already restrained, had reason to expect.
