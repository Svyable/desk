# In-Play

On the last Tuesday of July 2018, MGM Resorts International announced that it had become the National Basketball Association's first "official gaming partner," a three-year arrangement worth $25 million that gave MGM the rights to league marks, broadcast highlights, and something no casino had ever been able to buy directly from a professional sports league before: a live data feed straight from the league's own scorers' tables. The NBA, for the first time in its history, was being paid for the thing bettors actually wanted, which was not the box score published the next morning but the score as it happened, second by second, foul by foul. Commissioner Adam Silver had already spent nearly four years building toward that moment. In November 2014 he had published a short opinion piece in the New York Times, titled "Legalize and Regulate Sports Betting," arguing that a black market was going to keep booking NBA games whether the league liked it or not, and that the league would be safer, and the games more trustworthy, if betting on them ran through licensed, monitored channels instead. It was a strange position for a commissioner to stake out in public. Every major American league had spent decades treating gambling as a threat to be kept as far from the product as legal language and stadium signage could manage. Silver's op-ed did not create legal sports betting — the Supreme Court would not strike down the federal ban for another three and a half years — but it marked the moment a sitting commissioner said, on the record, that his league's relationship to gambling was about to become a revenue line rather than a liability to be managed.

What Silver was actually selling, once Murphy v. NCAA cleared the way in May 2018, turned out to be more valuable than the marketing rights the MGM deal nominally covered. It was data. Specifically, it was the right to be the first, fastest, most reliable source of the small facts a sportsbook needs to keep a betting market open while a game is still being played: who has the ball, what the score is at this instant, whether a player just left the floor holding his hamstring. A season-long win-loss bet does not need any of that. A bet placed during the third quarter, on whether the next possession ends in a made basket, needs it updated within a fraction of a second, verified, and distributed simultaneously to dozens of competing sportsbooks who cannot legally coordinate with each other but who all need to move their in-game prices at the same moment or risk being picked apart by bettors watching the broadcast a few seconds ahead of the odds. That infrastructure did not exist in American sports before 2018, because there had been no legal market large enough to justify building it. Within five years it had become one of the more lucrative pieces of the entire industry, and two companies, Sportradar and Genius Sports, had positioned themselves as its plumbing.

Sportradar, a Swiss company with roots in European soccer-data distribution, had already been supplying real-time statistics to media outlets before the American market opened, which gave it a head start once betting data became a separate, more valuable product to sell on top of that infrastructure. In November 2018 the NBA struck non-exclusive data-rights agreements with both Sportradar and Genius Sports, allowing either company to distribute official NBA and WNBA data to licensed operators. Those initial deals kept terms private, in the way early-stage league partnerships in a brand-new legal market often did, with neither side eager to set a public price before anyone knew what the market would bear. By 2021, the price had become clear enough that the NBA agreed to a deal reported at roughly six years and $250 million with Sportradar alone, and the league also took a small equity stake in the company, a detail worth pausing on: a professional sports league becoming a part-owner of the company that supplies betting data about its own games, so that the league's financial interest in betting-market activity is no longer indirect. By 2023, with Sportradar three years into being a publicly traded company on Nasdaq, the two sides signed an eight-year extension reported at more than a billion dollars in cash and stock — a number that would have been almost unimaginable to describe to an NBA general counsel in 2014, when the league's official position was that gambling on its games was a problem to be litigated against, not a product line.

The NFL moved more cautiously and extracted more money. Its April 2021 agreement with Genius Sports made Genius the exclusive worldwide distributor of NFL data to the regulated betting market and to media, on a six-year structure — four guaranteed years followed by two option years the league controlled — reported at roughly $120 million, plus an equity position in Genius Sports itself. The NFL's caution reflected its own history more than any doubt about the money: this was the league of Paul Hornung and Alex Karras, suspended in 1963 for betting small amounts on games in which no evidence ever surfaced that either man had bet against his own team, and the league's leadership had spent the following half-century treating even the appearance of gambling proximity as an existential threat to the product. By 2021 that caution had not disappeared so much as been repriced. In June 2025, with four years of the original agreement behind it, the NFL extended and expanded the Genius Sports partnership through the 2029 season, adding not just data distribution but the exclusive right to sell in-game advertising inventory around the league's low-latency live-streaming product, a sign of how far the relationship had moved past a simple data-licensing arrangement toward something closer to a joint commercial venture between the league and the company reselling its second-by-second information. Major League Baseball, whose own history with gambling is the deepest and most scarred of any American league, reached its first Sportradar data agreement in time for the 2019 season and extended that exclusive partnership through 2032 — a decade-plus commitment from the league that had banned Pete Rose for life in 1989 for betting on his own team's games, now selling the real-time data those same kinds of bets would be built from.

The NHL followed the NBA within three months, announcing its own MGM partnership in October 2018, structured deliberately as non-exclusive rather than an outright sale of league-wide rights — MGM would get access to NHL data that could eventually include puck- and player-tracking information, plus the marketing rights to use every team's logos, but the league was explicit that it was not taking a direct cut of MGM's gambling revenue the way the arrangement would later be described, and that other operators would be free to negotiate similar access. The distinction mattered less in practice than in press-release language; within a few years, essentially every operator of scale had struck some version of a data or marketing agreement with essentially every league, and the individual terms of who got exclusivity and who got a broader, shared arrangement mattered mostly to the vendors competing for the business, not to the bettor on the other end of the phone, who experienced all of it as the same thing: a scoreboard that had, without anyone announcing it as such, become a live betting terminal.

The practical effect of these deals, for a bettor holding a phone, was that a sportsbook could offer thousands of new things to wager on inside a single game, updated continuously, priced by a computer rather than by a human trader watching the action. This is the product category the industry calls in-play or live betting, and its smallest, fastest-moving subcategory has its own name: micro-betting, a wager on the outcome of the very next unit of play — the next pitch, the next play call, the next NBA possession — rather than the game as a whole. A company called Simplebet, founded in 2018, built its entire business around generating these micro-markets using machine-learning models that ingest the official data feed and produce a new price every few seconds, fast enough that a sportsbook can post odds on whether the next play will be a run or a pass and take bets on it before the ball is snapped. By the time Caesars Sportsbook rolled out Simplebet-powered micro-betting in December 2022, the technology had partnerships across DraftKings, FanDuel, and Caesars simultaneously — three competitors who do not normally share infrastructure, all buying the same underlying pricing engine because none of them could build it faster alone. The data-rights deals with the leagues were the precondition; Simplebet and companies like it were what got built on top of that precondition once it existed.

The specific shape of micro-betting differs by sport in ways that track each sport's own internal rhythm, which is part of why the technology needed a separate, dedicated buildout for each league rather than one generic in-play engine. Baseball offered the most natural fit: a pitch is a discrete, cleanly bounded event with a beginning and an end, a pause of ten to twenty seconds before the next one, and a limited, well-understood range of outcomes — ball, strike, foul, or ball in play — which makes it comparatively easy for a pricing model to generate a fresh, defensible number between pitches and for a bettor to place a wager in the window before the next one is thrown. Football offered a similar built-in pause between plays, long enough for a bettor to decide whether the upcoming snap will be a run or a pass, though the huddle-and-snap rhythm varies enough by team and situation that pricing models have to account for tempo the way a human bookmaker never had to. Basketball is the hardest of the three, because play is close to continuous — no natural pause exists between one possession and the next the way it does between pitches or snaps — which is why NBA-focused micro-betting tends to center on possession-level outcomes settled quickly rather than the truly granular next-dribble markets that would require a stoppage baseball and football provide for free. None of this variation was an accident of nostalgia; it reflects an underlying truth about which sports could support the fastest-growing, highest-margin betting products the moment the data infrastructure existed to support them, and helps explain why baseball, a sport many casual fans had treated as sports betting's slow, old-fashioned category, became one of the more aggressively marketed in-play products once Sportradar's and Genius Sports's data pipelines came online.

None of this would matter much to the book's argument if in-play betting and micro-betting were simply a more exciting way to watch a game. They are also, for reasons that follow directly from how they are priced, a meaningfully more profitable way for a sportsbook to take a bet than the traditional point spread ever was. A standard point-spread wager at the familiar -110 odds carries a house edge of roughly 4.5 percent — meaning that if a sportsbook could somehow attract perfectly balanced action on both sides of every such bet forever, it would keep about four and a half cents of every dollar wagered, win or lose, purely from the vig baked into the price. That is a real business, and it was the business Nevada sportsbooks ran for decades, with statewide hold hovering close to five percent through most of the twentieth century and into the twenty-first; in 2018, the year the federal ban fell, Nevada's own hold came in at 6.01 percent, the best figure the state had posted since 2007. A single straight bet on one outcome is, mathematically, close to the simplest product a sportsbook offers. A same-game parlay — betting, in one combined wager, that a specific quarterback throws for over 275 yards and his team wins by more than a field goal and a specific receiver scores a touchdown — is not simple at all, because those outcomes are correlated with each other in ways that are difficult for an ordinary bettor to estimate and that a sportsbook's pricing model is built specifically to exploit. If the quarterback throws for 275 yards, his team is more likely to be winning; if his team is winning by more than a field goal, that specific receiver scoring is not an independent event from the other two legs the way a bettor doing rough parlay math in his head would assume. A sportsbook that priced the combined bet as if the three legs were statistically independent — simply multiplying the payout odds together, the way an old-fashioned three-team parlay card at a Nevada book effectively worked — would be handing money back to sharp bettors who understood the correlation. Modern same-game parlay pricing does the opposite: it prices the correlation in, which lets the sportsbook advertise seemingly generous individual odds on each leg while retaining a combined hold that industry analysts estimate can run from the low twenties to well above thirty percent on a several-leg ticket, several times the hold on a single straight bet.

The arithmetic is worth walking through once, because it is not intuitive and the whole product depends on it not being intuitive. Three independent bets, each priced at even money with a small vig built in, combine on paper into odds of roughly seven-to-one on all three hitting together — take three coin flips at close to fifty-fifty each, multiply the probabilities, and the combined payout should reflect roughly a one-in-eight chance. If a book actually paid seven-to-one on a same-game parlay whose three legs were as correlated as a quarterback's passing yardage, his team's margin of victory, and his top receiver's touchdown, it would be giving away money, because those three things are not independent: a big passing day and a big margin of victory tend to arrive together, which means the true combined probability of all three hitting is meaningfully higher than the independent-legs math suggests, and a bettor who understood that could find real value in the mispriced parlay. Sportsbooks do not make that mistake anymore. Their correlation-aware pricing models shade the payout down from what naive independent math would offer, closer to what the correlated legs actually deserve — and then, because the whole point of running a book rather than a fair exchange is to keep a margin, shade it down further still, past fair value, before publishing the number a bettor sees on the app. The bettor experiences this as a menu of appealingly boosted-looking individual odds bundled into one ticket. What has actually happened is that the same correlation which makes the parlay fun to bet — everything on the ticket tends to happen together, because it is all riding on the same team having a good game — is precisely the feature the pricing model uses to collect a larger toll than three separate straight bets ever could have generated.

The financial consequence of that math, multiplied across a national customer base betting on their phones every night of the football, basketball, and baseball seasons, has been the single biggest driver of sportsbook profitability growth since legalization. Same-game parlays made up under a fifth of major operators' gross gaming revenue as recently as 2021; industry estimates for the past two seasons put that figure at somewhere between 35 and 40 percent. Flutter Entertainment, FanDuel's parent company, told investors that its customers placed 262 million same-game bets in the fourth quarter of 2023 alone, a figure up nearly 75 percent from the same quarter a year earlier. In states that break out the data, multi-leg parlays — same-game and multi-game combined — now account for something close to a quarter of total betting handle but more than half of total sportsbook revenue, a gap between share-of-volume and share-of-profit that exists precisely because parlays are priced to hold so much more of every dollar wagered on them than a straight bet does. None of this requires any bettor to do anything differently than they would otherwise want to do; a same-game parlay is, for a fan watching the game anyway, a genuinely more interesting way to have money on the outcome, tying together three or four things happening in front of you rather than just the final score. That is precisely what makes the product effective. It did not require deception to become the most profitable structure in the sportsbook's menu. It required only that the fan's own instinct — that watching the fourth quarter with a same-game parlay live is more fun than watching it with a single spread bet already decided — turned out to align with the bet that returns the house the most money.

In-play betting operates on the same logic at an even faster tempo, and by 2025 it had become, for the industry's largest operator, no longer a side product but the main one. DraftKings reported that live betting exceeded half of its total handle for the first time in the first quarter of 2025, a threshold that would have sounded implausible to anyone describing the American sports-betting market as recently as 2019, when nearly every dollar wagered went down before kickoff or tip-off, on a bet that would then simply exist, unchanged, until the game ended. The shift toward live betting matters for the same underlying reason same-game parlays matter: a market that moves continuously, repriced every few seconds by an algorithm reacting to the last pitch or the last possession, generates far more total betting events per game than a market that is set once before the opening whistle and left alone. A bettor who would have placed one spread bet on a Sunday afternoon football game in 2018 can now place forty separate micro-bets on that same game without ever putting down more total money than the original spread bet would have cost — but each of those forty bets carries its own hold, its own vig, its own small toll collected by the house. Multiply the toll by the transaction count rather than by the dollar volume, and the arithmetic of why in-play betting became the industry's fastest-growing category becomes considerably less mysterious.

It would be a mistake to read any of this as evidence that the leagues, the data vendors, or the sportsbooks sat down in 2018 and engineered a system explicitly designed to raise the effective hold on the average bet, in the way a slot-machine manufacturer might tune a payout table. The leagues sold data-distribution rights because sportsbooks needed official, verified data to run a legal, monitored market rather than the unregulated, occasionally fraud-prone data streams offshore books had relied on for decades, and because refusing to sell that data would not have stopped anyone from betting on NBA games — it would only have meant sportsbooks priced those bets off slower, less accurate feeds, with worse odds for everyone and a real integrity risk if a book's in-play price lagged the actual game by several seconds, a gap sharp bettors would find and exploit within days. Simplebet and its competitors built micro-betting because bettors, given the choice, kept choosing it — the same-game parlay is popular because fans like combining bets on things they are already watching, not because anyone forced that preference into existence. But intent and effect are different questions, and the effect, whatever the intent behind each individual deal, has been a market where the fastest-growing, most heavily marketed products are also the products that separate money from bettors' pockets at several times the rate of the point-spread bet the entire legal industry was originally built to offer. The data pipeline the leagues sold in 2018 made that market technically possible. What sportsbooks built on top of the pipeline made it the most profitable part of the business.

The people setting these in-play prices are, worth noting, not doing anything resembling what a Nevada oddsmaker did in 1985, quietly adjusting a number on a board based on judgment and years of experience. In-play and micro-betting prices are generated by automated systems reacting to the official data feed in real time, with a human trading team monitoring for anomalies rather than setting each individual number by hand — there are simply too many markets, updating too fast, for any staff of human oddsmakers to price them one at a time the way a spread was once set. A modern sportsbook's trading floor looks less like the folding tables and grease pencils of an old Las Vegas sports book and more like an options-trading desk: rows of monitors tracking exposure across thousands of simultaneously live micro-markets, risk managers watching for the statistical signature of a bettor who has found a mispriced number before the algorithm corrects it, and a hard cap on how much action any single in-play market will accept before it is pulled and repriced. That automation is itself part of why in-play markets can sustain a higher hold than a human-set spread market could: a computer repricing a market every few seconds does not need to build in the same margin for the possibility that new information will move against it before the next update, because the next update is seconds away rather than a day away.

There is a genuine benefit buried inside this same infrastructure, and the book's later chapter on prop-bet integrity returns to it directly: an official, verified, continuously logged data feed makes it considerably easier to spot the kind of manipulation that thrives on ambiguity. A sportsbook that knows, to the second, exactly when a given prop market opened, how the price moved, and who bet it, has a far better chance of catching an athlete or insider feeding information to a bettor than a bookmaker working off a radio broadcast and a legal pad ever did. The same real-time pipeline that makes a three-leg same-game parlay possible to price also makes it possible, after the fact, to reconstruct exactly what a suspicious bet looked like and when it was placed — a forensic capability that simply did not exist in the pre-2018 market, legal or illegal. That the infrastructure cuts both ways, making both the highest-margin products and the cleanest fraud detection possible at the same time, is a fair complication to hold alongside the rest of this chapter's argument rather than a contradiction of it.

The league data-rights deals of 2018 through 2021 did not just make in-play betting possible. They made it possible to price in-play betting the way modern in-play betting is actually priced — continuously, automatically, and at a hold that a bettor placing one careful spread bet before kickoff, the way most bettors did as recently as the last legal Nevada monopoly, would never have generated in the first place. What that hold means for any individual bettor is, on a single Tuesday night, close to nothing — a few dollars here, a slightly worse number there, indistinguishable from ordinary bad luck. What it means aggregated across tens of millions of bettors placing dozens of micro-wagers a night, night after night, across a full season, is the subject the next two chapters take up directly: who those dollars are actually coming from, and why the industry has become so good at knowing, before a bettor does, exactly how much of them it is likely to collect.
