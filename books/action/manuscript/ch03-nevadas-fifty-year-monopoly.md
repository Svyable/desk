# Nevada's Fifty-Year Monopoly

In the mid-1950s, a bookmaker who called himself Jimmy "the Greek" Snyder ran a modest storefront operation in Las Vegas called the Vegas Turf and Sports Club, one of a handful of freestanding "turf clubs" — small, often shabby rooms with chalkboards and a cashier's window, licensed separately from the casino floor — where a bettor in the one state where this was legal could put money on a horse race or a football game. By his own later account and by the estimate of people who watched him work, Snyder was moving something like two million dollars a week through that room at the peak of his run, an extraordinary sum for a business with no computers, no television screens, and no marketing beyond word of mouth among men who already knew where to find it. Snyder would go on to a long career as a television personality, predicting football games on network broadcasts for a national audience that had no legal way to act on his predictions unless they happened to be in Nevada, which was the entire point: for most of the twentieth century, professional oddsmaking and legal sports betting existed almost nowhere else in the United States, and the men who built that industry inside Nevada's borders did so in near-total isolation from the rest of the country's gambling culture, developing techniques and institutions that the rest of America would not encounter, legally, until decades after Snyder himself was dead.

Nevada's status as the sole legal home for American sports betting did not arrive as a triumphant act of policy; it arrived almost by accident, as a side effect of the state's broader 1931 decision to legalize casino gambling generally, followed by a slower, messier process through which race and sports wagering specifically found a legal footing. Off-track betting on horse racing became legal in Nevada in the 1940s, and it was through that opening that the infrastructure for sports betting first took root, much of it initially built and controlled by men with connections to organized crime. Benjamin "Bugsy" Siegel, the mobster most famous for his role in developing the Flamingo hotel-casino, ran a race-information wire service that supplied odds, entries, and results to Nevada's bookmakers, a service that reportedly generated him something like twenty-five thousand dollars a month from Las Vegas operations alone before his 1947 murder. The wire service business, and the "turf clubs" that depended on it, occupied an uneasy space for years afterward — licensed in the narrow technical sense that Nevada law permitted the activity, but run substantially by figures the rest of the country's law enforcement regarded as organized-crime associates, a reputation that would dog Nevada's sports betting industry's public image for decades even as the state itself worked to professionalize and distance the business from its wire-service origins.

The federal government came close to killing the entire enterprise only two decades after it started, and the near-death experience is worth understanding in detail because it illustrates how fragile legal sports betting's existence actually was for most of its history, long before the Supreme Court or Congress ever debated PASPA. The Revenue Act of 1951 imposed a ten percent federal excise tax on the total amount wagered — not on revenue or winnings, but on handle, the total sum bet — at any legal sportsbook, along with a fifty-dollar occupational stamp requirement for anyone taking bets. Congress's stated purpose was to make illegal gambling operations easier for state prosecutors to identify and shut down, on the theory that requiring bookmakers to register and pay a federal tax would flush illegal operators into the open. What it actually did, in Nevada, was very nearly destroy the state's only legal sportsbook industry: a ten percent tax on total handle, rather than on profit, is a genuinely punishing rate for a business that typically holds only four or five percent of everything wagered as its own revenue before paying out winners, meaning a legal Nevada sportsbook could easily owe the federal government more in excise tax than it kept for itself after paying off bettors who won. Licensed operators shut down or went underground rather than absorb a tax rate that made legal operation mathematically unsustainable, and legitimate, licensed sports wagering in Nevada spent much of the 1950s and 1960s as a shrunken, marginal business, squeezed by the same federal government that had, nominally, made it legal in the first place. Relief did not come for more than two decades: Congress finally cut the excise tax rate to two percent, effective October 15, 1974, a change that allowed Nevada's legal sportsbook industry to begin operating on economics that actually made sense, and the modern Las Vegas sports betting industry — the one that would eventually employ professional oddsmakers, attract national attention during major events, and become the training ground for every regulated sportsbook that opened after 2018 — essentially dates its real beginning to that tax cut rather than to any earlier legalization.

It was into this newly economically viable industry that the modern point spread, as a mass-market betting product, took hold, though its actual origin predates Nevada's tax relief by a generation and belongs, somewhat surprisingly, to a Chicago securities analyst and part-time math teacher named Charles K. McNeil. Sports betting before McNeil's innovation was priced almost entirely on the moneyline: a bettor picked a straight winner, and the bookmaker adjusted the payout odds to reflect each side's perceived chance of winning, so that betting on a heavy favorite might return only a few dollars of profit on every hundred dollars risked, while betting on a decided underdog could pay out several times the original stake. That system works reasonably well for evenly matched contests but becomes commercially awkward for lopsided ones, because almost nobody wants to risk a large sum for a tiny potential return on a heavily favored team, which is exactly the kind of bet a mismatched college or professional schedule produces constantly. McNeil, who had made enough money beating his own bookie's moneyline prices that the bookie eventually limited how much action he would accept from him, responded by opening his own operation and inventing what he called "wholesaling odds": instead of adjusting the payout, he adjusted the margin of victory required to win the bet, offering a favorite and an underdog at even money each, with the favorite required to win by more than a set number of points, and the underdog allowed to lose by less than that number and still pay out. The mechanism did something psychologically important as well as mathematically important — it let a bettor take his favorite team at a simple, appealing even-money price rather than laying an unattractive moneyline, and it gave the bookmaker a tool for balancing action on both sides of a game regardless of how mismatched the two teams actually were. The point spread spread from McNeil's Chicago operation through the loose national network of bookmakers over the following two decades and had become the dominant way of pricing American football and basketball by the time Nevada's legal industry began to professionalize in earnest during the 1970s and 1980s.

Professionalization, once it began, had a face, and that face belonged for most of two decades to a Vancouver-born statistician named Michael "Roxy" Roxborough, who founded a company called Las Vegas Sports Consultants in 1982 and ran it until 1999. Roxborough's insight was that individual Nevada sportsbooks, most of which had neither the staff nor the specialized expertise to build a credible line on every college and professional game on a given weekend from scratch, would pay for a centralized oddsmaking service that did the analytical work for them and let them focus on managing risk and taking bets rather than on the far more specialized work of setting an opening number. By the time his company was at its peak, Las Vegas Sports Consultants supplied the opening lines used by more than ninety percent of Nevada's licensed sportsbooks, meaning a single company, and effectively a small team of analysts working for it, set the price that governed the overwhelming majority of America's only legal sports betting market for nearly two decades. Roxborough landed the assignment that made his reputation in 1983, when the Stardust — at the time the most respected and highest-limit sportsbook in Las Vegas, a book that took action from the state's most sophisticated professional bettors without flinching — hired him to set its opening line, a credential that made every other book in the state eager to follow. Roxborough brought computer modeling and statistical rigor to an oddsmaking discipline that had previously depended heavily on individual bookmakers' personal feel for a matchup, and the shift he represents — from an intuitive, experience-based craft to a quantitative, model-driven one — anticipated, on a much smaller and more analog scale, the algorithmic pricing systems that would come to dominate legal sports betting after 2018.

What Roxborough and the other Nevada oddsmakers of his generation were actually doing, mechanically, deserves to be explained plainly, because the underlying arithmetic of a sportsbook's business model has not changed in any fundamental way since their era, even as the products built on top of it have multiplied. A standard point-spread or total bet in the United States is typically priced at odds of -110 on each side, a figure quoted as "eleven to win ten": a bettor risks $110 to win $100 in profit, or equivalently smaller multiples of the same ratio, such as $11 to win $10. If a coin-flip bet — a genuinely fifty-fifty proposition — were priced fairly, both sides would pay out at even money, or +100 in American odds notation, meaning a $100 bet wins $100. Pricing both sides at -110 instead means the implied probability embedded in the price is not fifty percent per side but roughly 52.4 percent per side, and since only one side can actually win, the two sides' implied probabilities sum to about 104.8 percent rather than a mathematically honest 100 percent. That extra 4.8 percentage points is the vigorish, commonly shortened to "vig" or called "the juice," and it translates into a hold — the percentage of total money wagered on a given market that the book expects to keep, on average, regardless of which side wins — of roughly 4.5 percent on a standard, perfectly balanced two-way market at -110 odds. A sportsbook does not need to predict winners correctly to profit from this arithmetic; in the cleanest theoretical case, it does not even need to know anything about the underlying sport at all. If it can attract equal money on both sides of a market priced at -110/-110, it collects $110 from bettors on the losing side, pays out $100 in winnings plus the original $10 stake back to bettors on the winning side, and pockets the remaining $10 regardless of the actual outcome on the field. The entire discipline of oddsmaking, from McNeil's original innovation through Roxborough's computer models to the algorithmic systems used by today's regulated operators, exists to get as close to that perfectly balanced outcome as possible — not to predict games accurately for its own sake, but to set a price that induces roughly equal money on both sides, at which point the vig does the rest of the work automatically.

The point spread was never the only way Nevada priced a game, and a full accounting of the state's oddsmaking legacy has to include the moneyline, the older and in some ways more intuitive wager that never disappeared even after McNeil's invention took over as the default way to bet football and basketball. A moneyline bet asks a bettor to pick a straight winner, with no margin of victory attached, and prices each side according to its implied probability of winning outright. A heavy favorite might be priced at -300, meaning a bettor has to risk three hundred dollars to win one hundred, reflecting a market view that the favorite wins roughly three times out of four; the corresponding underdog might be priced at +240, meaning a hundred-dollar bet would return two hundred forty dollars in profit if the underdog pulls the upset, a payout large enough to compensate for how rarely the market expects it to happen. Moneylines remained the standard way to bet baseball and hockey throughout Nevada's monopoly era, sports where scoring is low enough and single-run or single-goal outcomes common enough that a point spread makes less intuitive sense than it does in football or basketball, and the same basic vig-based arithmetic that governs a point-spread market governs a moneyline market too: a book prices both sides so their implied probabilities sum to slightly more than 100 percent, collecting its edge from that overage regardless of which side of the bet ultimately wins.

The professionalization Roxborough came to symbolize did not happen in a regulatory vacuum, and the state's own oversight apparatus deserves credit as a genuine, if belated, achievement in its own right. Nevada did not pass its foundational Gaming Control Act until 1955, nearly a quarter century after casino gambling itself became legal in 1931, and for those intervening decades the state's licensing and enforcement regime was thin enough that organized-crime figures like Siegel could operate with only nominal state supervision. The Gaming Control Board's response, once it had statutory teeth, became one of the more distinctive instruments of American gambling regulation: in 1960 the board published the first edition of what came to be called, for the plain reason that its original cover was bound in black tape, the "Black Book" — a formal List of Excluded Persons, initially just eleven names, each one a figure with documented organized-crime ties, who were permanently barred from setting foot in any licensed Nevada gaming establishment, sports pools and race books included. Early inductees included men like Tony "The Ant" Spilotro and Sam Giancana, names that belonged as much to FBI organized-crime files as to any casino's guest list, and the list's existence sent a specific, public message: a bettor's money was welcome in Nevada, but the criminal networks that had built the state's early wire-service and turf-club infrastructure were not, at least not visibly, going forward. The Black Book has added names in every subsequent decade, and its criteria broadened over time beyond organized-crime affiliation to include cheaters, chip counterfeiters, and others deemed threats to what Nevada's regulators describe as public confidence in the integrity of licensed gaming — a regulatory concept, worth remembering, that reappears in nearly identical language in the licensing statutes every other state would eventually write when it legalized sports betting for itself after 2018.

That distinction — a sportsbook's core business model rewarding balanced action rather than accurate prediction — is precisely what created the market for professional bettors, the "wise guys" whose presence became as central to Nevada's identity as the casinos themselves, and whose most successful member, a Kentucky-born former car salesman named Billy Walters, is worth understanding in some detail because his career illustrates both what Nevada's market made possible and the limits it eventually placed on anyone who succeeded too visibly within it. Walters became a central figure in what was known as the Computer Group, a loosely organized syndicate that in the 1980s used early computer modeling — genuinely novel technology for sports betting at the time — to identify games where the market's posted line diverged from the group's own calculated probability, then moved large amounts of money through a network of runners placing bets at sportsbooks across the state before those books could adjust their lines in response. The FBI investigated the Computer Group beginning in 1984 on suspicion of illegal bookmaking, and Walters was charged and ultimately acquitted, beating that case and several similar indictments over the following years. His success bred a specific and lasting consequence that shapes the industry to this day: sportsbooks, wary of losing consistently to bettors with better information or better models than the house itself possessed, began limiting how much action they would accept from Walters and bettors like him, refusing large bets or shading lines specifically against known sharp bettors, a defensive practice that predates the current generation of regulated mobile sportsbooks by decades and that this book's seventh chapter shows has, if anything, become more aggressive and more automated in the app-based era rather than less.

Nevada's monopoly began eroding, in practical if not legal terms, well before PASPA fell in 2018, and the erosion came from a direction the state's own licensed industry could do little about: the internet-enabled rise of offshore sportsbooks based in the Caribbean and Central America during the 1990s. As those operations matured, some of the sharpest and most experienced oddsmakers Nevada had trained — men like Ron "Cigar" Sacco, a longtime American bookmaker who relocated his operation to the Dominican Republic in the late 1980s and became known within the industry as a godfather figure to the offshore business — took their pricing expertise with them to jurisdictions with no functional American regulatory reach at all, where they could accept enormous bets from American bettors, wise guys and casual players alike, without the licensing constraints or bet-size limits Nevada law imposed. By the 2000s, offshore books, along with newer, more disciplined operators like Pinnacle, had become the de facto source of the earliest and sharpest opening lines for many sports, effectively displacing Nevada as the market's true price-discovery mechanism even though Nevada remained the only place in America where betting on those same lines was actually legal. Nevada sportsbook directors, a role that had once meant setting an original line from scratch, increasingly became line-followers themselves, watching what offshore and market-making books were doing and adjusting Nevada's own numbers to match rather than to lead — a subordination of the legal domestic industry to an illegal, unregulated offshore one that stands as one of the stranger and less-discussed consequences of PASPA's decades-long ban, and a dynamic this book's seventh chapter shows persists today in a different, legalized form, with Circa Sports and a handful of other market-making operators now filling the role that offshore books once played almost by default.

Nevada's own regulators and sportsbook operators would eventually watch their fifty-year exclusivity dissolve with a speed that must have been disorienting even to those who had spent years arguing for exactly this outcome. Seven states had some form of legal sports betting operating by the end of 2018, the year PASPA fell; that number reached fifteen within another year, and would keep climbing toward the roughly forty states with legal markets in place by the mid-2020s. Nevada's total sports betting handle kept growing in absolute terms through this period — operators there would go on to report record-setting individual events, including handle north of $150 million on a single college-football bowl slate in one recent season alone — but its share of the national market, the thing that had made Nevada synonymous with legal sports betting for two generations, collapsed by simple arithmetic: a fixed population betting in a state with roughly three million residents cannot keep pace with a national market being built simultaneously in states with populations in the tens of millions. By the mid-2020s, industry trackers were ranking Nevada outside the top ten states by sports betting revenue in some years, an almost unthinkable demotion for the state that had, for fifty years, been the entire market rather than one competitor within it.

By the time New Jersey's Chris Christie began the political fight that would eventually reach the Supreme Court, Nevada's sports betting industry had spent roughly fifty years building nearly everything a legal, national sportsbook industry would eventually need — the point-spread pricing convention, the vig-based business model, a professional oddsmaking discipline, and a hard-won institutional understanding of how to manage the risk posed by sharp bettors — while remaining, in market-share terms, almost an afterthought next to the offshore industry it had effectively lost the pricing war to. What Nevada could not solve on its own, because no single state's regulatory authority extended past its own borders, was distribution: a fifty-year head start on the mechanics of legal sports betting meant very little to the roughly ninety-eight percent of the American population who could not legally walk into a Nevada sportsbook on a given Sunday. That problem — how to take an industry that had matured in one desert state and scale it to fifty — is where this book turns next, to a New Jersey governor who looked at a dying Atlantic City and decided the only way to save it was to break, deliberately and publicly, the very federal law that had protected Nevada's monopoly for as long as most working oddsmakers had been alive.
