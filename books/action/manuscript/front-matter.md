# Front Matter

## Action

### How America Turned Every Game Into a Bet, and What the House Learned About Everyone Else

**Sven Hardy Benson**

This book is about a legal product, not an illegal conspiracy.

That distinction matters because the modern American sports-betting market does not need secret coordination to be worth examining. The important machinery is mostly visible. State legislatures passed statutes. Regulators issued licenses. Public companies described customer acquisition, hold, parlay mix, live-betting growth and retention to investors. Leagues sold data rights and sponsorships. Sportsbooks advertised bonuses in public. Courts published opinions. Researchers measured household financial effects, risky gambling, dark patterns and credit distress. The story is not hidden. It is distributed.

The central problem is therefore one of assembly.

After *Murphy v. NCAA* in 2018, the United States ran fifty versions of the same policy experiment at different speeds. Some states taxed heavily; others competed for operator entry. Some limited college betting; others did not. Some launched mobile wagering almost immediately; others kept betting tethered to casinos. The product evolved faster than the regulatory language used to describe it. A market once organized around the outcome of a game became increasingly organized around the next possession, the next pitch, the next player event and the next personalized offer.

This manuscript follows that transition from law to software.

It also makes a distinction that public debate often refuses to make. Legalization can be better than prohibition on several dimensions and still create new harms. A licensed market can displace illegal bookmakers, generate tax revenue, create integrity-monitoring data and give customers enforceable rights. The same market can also make gambling radically easier to access, increase wagering frequency, concentrate revenue in heavy users and create incentives to retain customers whose behavior is becoming dangerous. Those claims do not cancel each other out.

The book treats six questions separately:

1. **What changed legally?** The Supreme Court did not legalize sports betting nationwide. It invalidated the federal rule that prevented most states from authorizing it, returning the decision to state governments. The opinion is public: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/584/16-476/
2. **What changed economically?** Handle, operator revenue, tax receipts and customer-acquisition spending are different measures. A large handle is not state revenue. A large tax payment is not evidence that most bettors are harmed.
3. **What changed technologically?** Mobile betting, in-play markets, microbetting, same-game parlays, automated pricing and personalized messaging changed the cadence of the product even when the legal category remained “sports wagering.”
4. **What changed behaviorally?** Product features can alter session length, re-engagement and wager frequency without determining any individual bettor’s fate.
5. **Who bears the downside?** Average effects can conceal concentration. Household-finance research repeatedly suggests that the largest adverse effects are not distributed evenly across users.
6. **What would falsify the book’s argument?** If high-frequency product features do not meaningfully change wagering intensity, if financial-harm estimates collapse under stronger designs, if risky-play detection consistently reduces harm without unacceptable false positives, or if heavily regulated jurisdictions show no meaningful improvement over lighter regimes, several conclusions here should be revised.

That last question matters most.

A book about gambling should not imitate gambling by hiding the conditions under which it loses.

## Evidence posture

The manuscript gives preference to court opinions, statutes, regulator reports, company filings, peer-reviewed research, central-bank and university working papers with disclosed methods, and contemporaneous journalism that links to underlying records. Trade publications are useful for product mechanics and industry language but are not treated as neutral referees. Company statements are evidence of what a company says, sells or reports, not automatic proof that its preferred interpretation is correct. Advocacy organizations are useful for service-demand and policy data but carry their own institutional incentives.

Working papers are labeled as working papers. Lawsuits are allegations unless adjudicated facts are available. Helpline contacts are not diagnoses. Handle is not loss. Gross gaming revenue is not profit. A correlation is not promoted into causation because it fits the thesis.

The source ledgers live with the manuscript in `research/source-ledger-A.csv`, `research/source-ledger-B.csv`, and `research/source-ledger-C.csv`. They are intentionally more mechanical than the prose. Their job is to preserve the chain from sentence to source while the manuscript is still changing.

## A note on people

The phrase **problem gambler** is convenient and often dehumanizing. This book prefers descriptions of behavior when possible: a person experiencing gambling disorder, a customer showing risky play, a bettor chasing losses, a household experiencing financial distress. The distinction is small in grammar and large in consequence. The subject is not a species of person. It is a pattern that can emerge from an interaction among vulnerability, circumstance, product design and access.

Most people who place sports bets do not develop gambling disorder. Some people derive genuine entertainment from wagering and regard losses as a controlled leisure expense. That population belongs in the book because any serious regulation has to preserve the possibility of ordinary use while reducing concentrated harm.

The book is therefore neither an abolition brief nor an industry defense.

It is an argument that a market sophisticated enough to price the probability of the next pitch can also be asked to measure what its own design does to the people using it.

## Dates and moving targets

Sports betting is unusually hostile to static publication. State rules change. Tax rates change. Operators enter and exit. Prediction markets blur regulatory categories. Leagues renegotiate data arrangements. Product features migrate between apps quickly. Figures in this edition are tied to dated sources wherever possible rather than presented as permanent facts.

Readers should treat the manuscript as a documented snapshot of the post-*Murphy* expansion through 2026, not as a promise that every market count will remain current.

The more durable subject is the machine underneath the count: permission, distribution, pricing, product design, segmentation, regulation and the incentives connecting them.

That machine is the action.
