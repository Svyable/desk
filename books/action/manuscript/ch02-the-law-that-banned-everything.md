# The Law That Banned Everything

On February 18, 1951, the Manhattan district attorney Frank Hogan announced the arrests of three basketball players from the City College of New York, a commuter school whose team had, less than a year earlier, done something no team before or since has managed: won both major national college basketball tournaments, the NCAA championship and the National Invitation Tournament, in the same season. Ed Roman, Alvin Roth, and Ed Warner had helped CCNY beat Bradley University for both titles in 1950. Within a year, all three were under arrest, along with a player from New York University and a former Long Island University player named Eddie Gard who had served as the go-between, for taking cash from gamblers to shave points — not to lose outright, which invites suspicion, but to win by less than the posted spread, or lose by less, keeping the final score inside a margin that satisfied a bettor without costing the team a victory it might otherwise have earned honestly. The players told investigators they had taken as much as a thousand dollars a game. What started as a Manhattan story spread within weeks to Toledo, to Bradley itself, to more than thirty players at seven schools, and became, in the retelling that followed, the first great proof that college basketball's closeness to legal and illegal gambling money was not an occasional embarrassment but a structural vulnerability — one that a Rhodes Scholar and Olympic gold medalist named Bill Bradley would still be invoking, nearly forty years later, when he stood on the floor of the United States Senate to argue that Congress needed to ban sports betting everywhere in America except the handful of places where it was already legal. The punishment, when it came that November from a state judge named Saul Streit, was severe by any standard: several players received jail sentences, the gambler who had organized much of the scheme, Salvatore Sollazzo, drew a term of eight to sixteen years, and the NBA quietly declined to draft any of the implicated players regardless of their talent, treating the scandal as a permanent professional disqualification rather than a mistake a young player might live down. CCNY's own basketball program never recovered its former stature; the school was barred from playing at Madison Square Garden, the sport's premier New York venue and the site of its championship a year earlier, and the university eventually dropped down from major-college competition entirely. The NCAA, which before 1951 had treated point-shaving as a remote and largely theoretical risk, began building the institutional infrastructure — compliance offices, athlete education programs, closer relationships with state gaming regulators — that would define its approach to gambling for the rest of the century, treating the CCNY scandal much the way American commercial aviation would later treat a fatal crash: not as an isolated failure, but as a signal that the entire system's assumptions needed to change.

Bradley knew the sport from the inside in a way few legislators ever know the industries they regulate. He had been an All-American at Princeton, deferred an NBA contract to study at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, then spent a decade with the New York Knicks, winning two championships, before entering politics and eventually the Senate as a Democrat from New Jersey. He had grown up in the same college-basketball culture the 1951 scandal had scarred, and he came of age professionally in an NBA that had spent decades anxious about its own association with organized gambling, an anxiety that predated even the CCNY case: the 1919 Black Sox scandal, in which eight members of the Chicago White Sox were paid by a gambling syndicate reportedly connected to the New York gambler Arnold Rothstein to intentionally lose the World Series to the Cincinnati Reds, had already taught American professional sports the lesson that a fixed championship could poison public trust in an entire league for a generation. The eight players — among them the outfielder Shoeless Joe Jackson, whose actual role in the fix remains disputed by historians to this day — were acquitted at a 1921 criminal trial after key evidence went missing from the grand jury file, but baseball's new commissioner, the federal judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis, banned all eight from the sport for life anyway, a single administrative act that did more to define American professional sports' relationship with gambling for the rest of the twentieth century than any statute Congress would pass. Rothstein's own role in the scandal has never been fully settled by historians — he was indicted but never convicted, and always maintained that others had used his name and reputation without his direct involvement in organizing the fix — but the scale of what he personally wagered is well documented: he bet roughly a quarter of a million dollars on Cincinnati to win the series, a sum that made him, whatever his exact operational role, the scandal's financial center of gravity and the model, for two generations of anti-gambling legislators afterward, of exactly the kind of figure a law like PASPA was meant to keep away from amateur and professional sport alike. The players who actually took part, by contrast, mostly saw a fraction of what they had been promised — the pitcher Eddie Cicotte reportedly collected only $1,000 of a promised $10,000 after the first game — a detail that would recur, in miniature, in nearly every point-shaving scandal that followed: the athletes doing the actual corrupting of the game were rarely the biggest financial winners in their own conspiracy.

The pattern the Black Sox scandal set — insiders with access to outcomes, gamblers with cash and patience, a scheme that requires only a handful of participants to work — recurred with enough regularity in college basketball specifically that by the time Bradley introduced his bill, it read less like an isolated historical embarrassment and more like a genre. In the 1978-79 season, three Boston College players, most prominently a forward named Rick Kuhn, agreed to shave points in nine games between mid-December and early March at the instigation of a Pittsburgh-connected gambler named Paul Mazzei and, once the operation needed muscle and financing, a Lucchese crime family associate named Henry Hill — the same Henry Hill whose later cooperation with federal prosecutors, after his own arrest on unrelated charges, would eventually become the basis for the film Goodfellas. Hill's testimony, once he began cooperating, is what actually exposed the Boston College scheme to prosecutors; without his decision to talk, the point-shaving might never have surfaced at all, since a shaved game, unlike a fixed loss, leaves no obviously anomalous result for an outsider to notice. Kuhn was convicted and served time in federal prison. Six years later, in early 1985, a similar scheme surfaced at Tulane, where five players, including a star center named John "Hot Rod" Williams, took a combined eighteen thousand dollars to shave points in games against Southern Mississippi and Memphis State at the instigation of a non-athlete student with a cocaine-dealing sideline named Gary Kranz. The fallout was severe enough that Tulane's president recommended disbanding the men's basketball program entirely, and the head coach and two assistants resigned after admitting they had separately been paying players, a violation distinct from but tangled up with the point-shaving case itself. Williams, tried separately, was acquitted by a jury, but the program he had played for did not survive the scandal in anything like its previous form.

The case that would prove most useful to reformers making the argument Bradley wanted to make, though, came nearly a decade after his bill became law, which is itself worth noting: PASPA did not end college point-shaving, because point-shaving is a crime committed by desperate or greedy insiders and enabled by an illegal betting market that federal legislation regulating legal, licensed sportsbooks was never actually positioned to touch. In 1994, a campus bookmaker at Arizona State named Benny Silman recruited the Sun Devils' leading scorer, a guard named Stevin "Hedake" Smith, to shave points in four Pac-10 games, paying Smith roughly twenty thousand dollars a game to help retire a gambling debt Smith had run up with Silman himself. What makes the Arizona State case unusually well documented, and unusually useful for understanding how legal, licensed sports betting actually functions as a check on this kind of fraud rather than merely an enabler of it, is who caught it: not campus security, not the NCAA, but Las Vegas oddsmakers, who noticed unusual, heavy, suspiciously well-timed betting action pushing the line on Arizona State games in ways that did not match the team's on-court form, and flagged it to Nevada gaming regulators, who alerted the FBI. Smith and an accomplice pleaded guilty in December 1997; Silman received a sentence of nearly four years. The detail worth sitting with, because later chapters in this book return to it from the opposite direction, is that a legal, regulated, closely monitored betting market — Nevada's, the only one that existed anywhere in the country at the time — was the mechanism that actually surfaced the fraud. An illegal betting market, by contrast, has no institutional incentive to report anomalous action to a regulator, because there is no regulator to report it to, and every incentive to stay quiet, because the whole enterprise depends on staying invisible. This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence the pro-legalization side would eventually deploy, decades later, against Bradley's own law, though Bradley himself, arguing in the early 1990s, was working from a different and in some ways more defensible set of facts: at the time he wrote his bill, the CCNY, Boston College, and Tulane scandals were the most recent and vivid proof points available, and all three involved amateur, college-age players who had been corrupted specifically because a market for betting on their games existed somewhere, legally or otherwise, for a gambler to exploit.

Bradley's bill, introduced in the Senate as S. 474 by Arizona Democrat Dennis DeConcini on February 22, 1991, with Bradley as its lead champion, was formally titled the Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act, though it would be known for the rest of its life by two other names: PASPA, the acronym, and the Bradley Act, after its sponsor. Its operative mechanism was simple and, in retrospect, constitutionally fragile in a way nobody fully appreciated at the time: rather than banning sports betting outright, which would have raised obvious federalism problems by directly criminalizing conduct states have traditionally regulated themselves, PASPA made it unlawful for a state to "sponsor, operate, advertise, promote, license, or authorize by law" any scheme based on the outcome of a competitive sporting event. The distinction sounds technical, but it would become the entire basis of the Supreme Court case that undid the law a quarter century later, because it meant PASPA did not directly ban sports betting so much as it commandeered state governments into banning it themselves, on the federal government's behalf, by making state authorization the illegal act. The bill passed the Senate on a lopsided 88-5 roll call vote on June 2, 1992, and was signed into law by President George H.W. Bush that October. The lopsidedness of the vote is itself a piece of evidence about the political moment: this was not a contested, closely fought piece of legislation. It was something close to a consensus position among both parties, reflecting a broad, bipartisan discomfort with gambling's proximity to professional and amateur sports that the CCNY-to-Tulane run of scandals had done a great deal to manufacture. Among the five senators who voted no was Iowa Republican Charles Grassley, whose objection anticipated, by more than two decades, the exact argument that would eventually kill the law: Grassley opposed PASPA not because he favored sports betting but because he believed the bill unfairly favored the four states it chose to exempt over the forty-six it did not, and because the Justice Department itself, in commenting on the bill's original wording, had already raised concerns about the federalism problem of Congress dictating what state legislatures could and could not authorize within their own borders. Nobody in 1992, including apparently Grassley himself, framed that concern in the specific constitutional language — the Tenth Amendment's anticommandeering doctrine — that would eventually carry the day at the Supreme Court in 2018. But the structural objection was there from the bill's first Senate vote, a minority position for twenty-six years before it became, almost overnight, the majority one.

There is a contradiction sitting inside PASPA's moral framing that neither its supporters nor most contemporary press coverage dwelled on at the time, and it is worth naming because it helps explain why the law's legitimacy eroded so completely over the following quarter century. Congress passed a law in 1992 built substantially on the premise that betting on the outcome of a competitive event corrupts it, or at least creates an unacceptable risk of corruption, at almost exactly the moment when state governments across the country were aggressively expanding a different form of legalized gambling: the state lottery. By 1992, roughly three dozen states operated their own lotteries, openly advertised, often earmarked for popular causes like education funding, generating billions of dollars a year in state revenue from the same population PASPA was nominally protecting from the corrupting influence of wagering. A state government could, under PASPA, legally run and advertise a numbers game with worse odds than most sports bets offer, while being federally barred from licensing a sportsbook operator to take a bet on a football game with a transparent, published price. That asymmetry did not go unnoticed by gaming-industry lobbyists and, later, by the lawyers who would argue New Jersey's case before the Supreme Court, but in 1992 it was not yet the load-bearing argument it would become. What Congress was reacting to, more than any consistent theory of what forms of gambling corrupt sport and which do not, was a specific and recent run of scandals involving amateur athletes and organized gambling interests — a narrower, more visceral fear than a general anti-gambling philosophy, and one that a booming, state-sanctioned lottery industry did nothing to trigger because lotteries have no game on the field whose outcome a bettor might try to influence.

PASPA's exemptions were where the law's practical shape actually lived, and they deserve more attention than the headline "sports betting was banned" usually gets, because the exemptions reveal what Congress was actually protecting rather than what it claimed to be protecting. Nevada, where licensed sports pools had operated openly since 1949, was exempted outright, on the theory that a state with fifty years of existing regulatory infrastructure and a legal sportsbook industry already embedded in its economy could not fairly be forced to dismantle it. Oregon, Delaware, and Montana were exempted as well, because each ran some limited form of state-sanctioned sports lottery or parlay game at the time the law passed, and Congress chose to grandfather existing arrangements rather than force an abrupt shutdown. New Jersey received the most consequential exemption of all, and also the one it ultimately failed to use: the law gave New Jersey a one-year window from PASPA's effective date to pass its own sports-betting authorization and join Nevada as a second fully legal state, a provision written specifically because Atlantic City's casino industry, already licensed and regulated, made New Jersey's case for an exemption structurally similar to Nevada's. The New Jersey legislature did not act within that year. The exemption lapsed, unused, and it would take New Jersey another two and a half decades, a fight that reached the Supreme Court, and a different governor entirely to finally claim the legal sports betting industry Congress had once been willing to hand it for free — a piece of legislative history this book's fourth chapter picks up directly, because the frustration of watching an opportunity expire through simple inaction shaped how aggressively Chris Christie's administration would later pursue the same goal by force of litigation rather than by asking politely.

It is worth pausing on what PASPA's supporters were and were not arguing, because the moral-panic framing that later critics applied to the law retroactively is not entirely fair to the case its proponents actually made at the time, even if the case turned out to rest on shakier legal ground than anyone anticipated. NBA commissioner David Stern testified before Congress that the interstate character of sports betting — bets placed across state lines, odds transmitted across state lines, the entire apparatus of a national betting market touching games played in every state — made federal rather than state-by-state regulation the only coherent response, a genuinely serious institutional argument distinct from mere gambling-averse moralizing. The major professional leagues and the NCAA, who would spend the next quarter century as PASPA's principal institutional defenders and would eventually become named defendants in the Supreme Court case that killed it, framed their support in terms of protecting the integrity of competition rather than in terms of protecting anyone's soul from the sin of gambling itself — a framing that let PASPA's supporters sidestep the messier, more moralistic anti-gambling arguments that had dominated American gambling policy for much of the twentieth century and instead present the law as a narrow, technical measure to prevent the CCNY and Boston College scenarios from recurring at scale. Whether that framing was sincere or convenient is a question this book cannot fully settle, because both things can be true simultaneously: a genuine concern about fixed games and a convenient cover story for a broader cultural discomfort with gambling can motivate the same piece of legislation without contradiction.

What PASPA actually did, once enacted, was create a strange and unstable equilibrium that would hold for twenty-six years without ever quite resolving the tension built into its own design. Sports betting itself did not disappear; it simply moved, overwhelmingly, into forms the law could not reach. Illegal bookmaking continued in every American city, largely untouched, because PASPA regulated state authorization rather than individual conduct and left enforcement of underlying gambling law to states that mostly had bigger enforcement priorities than a neighborhood bookie taking action on Sunday's games. Offshore sportsbooks, based in the Caribbean and increasingly reachable by the mid-1990s through the newly commercial internet, absorbed an enormous share of the demand that PASPA had pushed out of any domestic legal channel, operating from jurisdictions like Antigua and Costa Rica in a regulatory gray zone American law enforcement mostly lacked the tools to touch. Nevada, meanwhile, kept operating exactly as it had before, its licensed sportsbooks generating tax revenue and its professional oddsmakers refining a pricing discipline that the rest of the country's bettors could access only by flying to Las Vegas or calling an offshore number — the entire subject of this book's next chapter. And daily fantasy sports, a product that did not exist in any form Bradley or Congress could have anticipated in 1992, would eventually find a separate legal loophole entirely, built not on PASPA but on a different and later federal statute, that let two companies build something close to a national sports-betting product without technically violating PASPA at all, a story this book's fifth chapter tells in full.

The CCNY scandal, the Black Sox before it, and the Boston College, Tulane, and Arizona State cases after PASPA's passage share a structural lesson that both sides of the modern sports-betting debate have tried, at different moments, to claim as their own. Point-shaving is not primarily a story about legal markets; every scandal this chapter describes was enabled by illegal or quasi-legal betting arrangements existing entirely outside any state's regulatory reach, arrangements a federal ban on legal state authorization did essentially nothing to disrupt. That fact would become central to the argument legalization advocates made a generation later: that driving betting further underground does not make fixed games less likely, it makes them harder to detect, because a regulated sportsbook, unlike a bookie or an offshore operator with no relationship to American law enforcement, has both the technical capacity and, increasingly, the legal obligation to flag suspicious betting patterns to the leagues and to state gaming commissions the moment they appear — precisely the mechanism that caught the Arizona State scheme in 1994, three years before that state's own booking industry would have been legal anywhere Smith and Silman could have reached it. Bradley's law, built substantially on the trauma of scandals that illegal and quasi-legal betting markets had produced, would spend the next twenty-six years failing to prevent the conditions that produced them, while one grandfathered state kept building the exact regulatory infrastructure — licensed books, monitored lines, a direct channel to law enforcement — that would eventually prove the strongest argument for tearing the whole law down.
