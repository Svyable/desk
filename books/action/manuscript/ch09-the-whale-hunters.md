# The Whale Hunters

Christopher Sage and Terry Thompson met their VIP hosts the way most sportsbook customers do: not by asking for one, but by being found. Both men were Pennsylvania residents who had signed up for DraftKings and FanDuel accounts like millions of other Americans after the state legalized mobile sports betting, placing ordinary bets on ordinary games. At some point in each man's account history, a signal crossed a threshold somewhere inside the companies' customer-data systems, and a human being was assigned to make sure that customer kept betting. Thompson's FanDuel host, identified in his subsequent lawsuit only as Morgan, and his DraftKings host, identified as Donahue, began reaching him directly on his personal phone. Sage's DraftKings host, Dyleisha Lewis, did the same. The hosts sent promotional offers timed to his account activity, arranged tickets and hotel accommodations to Super Bowl LVI in Los Angeles, and, on one occasion, sent a bottle of champagne priced at $500. By the time the two men filed suit in Philadelphia's Court of Common Pleas in March 2026 — a complaint brought by the Public Health Advocacy Institute that named the VIP hosts themselves as individual defendants, apparently the first time an online sportsbook lawsuit in the United States had done so — Thompson alleged combined losses of more than $1.8 million across the two platforms, and Sage alleged losses of $40,000. Both men, according to the complaint, had developed diagnosable gambling disorders during the period their hosts were courting them.

The complaint did not name only the two companies and their hosts. It also named the NFL and Genius Sports as defendants, tying the VIP-targeting allegations directly to the same real-time data infrastructure this book's previous chapter traced back to the 2021 NFL-Genius Sports agreement — the plaintiffs' argument being, in effect, that the league's own data feed helped power the product their hosts were encouraging them to keep using. It is a claim that has not been tested at trial and may not survive the companies' motion to dismiss, but the structural argument underneath it, connecting the data pipeline to the marketing apparatus built on top of it, is one this book has been assembling piece by piece since the last chapter.

The lawsuit is contested; DraftKings and FanDuel have moved to dismiss it, and nothing in a complaint constitutes a proven fact until a court says so. But the mechanism the complaint describes — a company identifying its most active bettors and assigning a real person to keep them close, with gifts, personal attention, and access calibrated to how much that specific customer was worth — did not originate with online sports betting. It is the oldest piece of casino marketing there is, older than the slot machine's LED lights and older than the loyalty-card scanner at the entrance to the pit. What has changed, in the transition from a Las Vegas casino floor to a phone in a bettor's pocket, is not the underlying idea. It is the precision.

The person most responsible for turning casino customer relationships into a quantitative science was not a casino boss in the traditional sense at all, but a Harvard Business School professor named Gary Loveman, who wrote an unsolicited letter to Harrah's Entertainment's chief executive in 1997 offering unrequested advice on how the company might grow, and was hired as chief operating officer the following year. Loveman had no gaming-industry background; what he had was a conviction, drawn from his academic work, that most casino operators were running their customer-relationship decisions on intuition rather than evidence, and that a company willing to apply the same statistical rigor to gambler behavior that airlines and credit-card issuers were already applying to their own customers could build a durable competitive advantage. The vehicle he built to test that conviction was Total Rewards, a loyalty program that eventually enrolled more than 40 million members and that, by the time Caesars Entertainment (as Harrah's had renamed itself) filed for bankruptcy protection in 2015, was valued by outside analysts as something close to a billion-dollar asset in its own right — not because of any points liability sitting on the balance sheet, but because of what the underlying database of individual gambler behavior was worth to whoever controlled it. In a 2003 interview, by then chief executive, Loveman put the company's approach in terms that read, two decades later, like an unintentional preview of the sportsbook industry's entire VIP apparatus: Harrah's used database marketing and decision-science-based analytical tools, he said, specifically to widen the gap between his company and competitors who still based customer incentives on intuition rather than evidence.

What that decision science actually found, once Harrah's had enough data to find anything, surprised people inside the industry who had spent their careers assuming the high roller in the private baccarat pit was the customer worth chasing. The truly profitable segment, Total Rewards' own analysis showed, was not the whale flying in on a comped jet for a single weekend of enormous bets. It was the ordinary, repeat slot player — a retiree visiting three times a month, wagering modest amounts, but doing so with a consistency and total lifetime volume that the occasional high roller, for all the drama of a single big session, usually could not match. This finding reoriented the entire industry's marketing spend over the following two decades, away from the glamorous, easily mythologized whale and toward the much larger, much less cinematic population of steady, moderate players whose aggregate value, tracked patiently across hundreds of visits, dwarfed any single dramatic night at the tables. The insight required, to act on it, exactly the kind of infrastructure Total Rewards was built to provide: a loyalty card that followed a player from the slot floor to the buffet to the hotel check-in desk, generating a continuous behavioral record that let the casino calculate, for any given customer, approximately how much that person was worth over a projected lifetime relationship — and, from there, exactly how much in comps, attention, and inducements it was worth spending to keep that value flowing.

Loveman did not stop at building a database; he insisted on testing it the way a pharmaceutical company tests a drug. Harrah's began running what amounted to thousands of controlled marketing experiments — one group of similar customers offered a free hotel room, a comparable group offered a modest amount of free slot play instead, a third group offered nothing at all as a control — and then measured, with the same statistical discipline an epidemiologist would apply to a clinical trial, which intervention produced more gambling from which type of customer. One outside observer studying the company at the time made exactly that comparison explicit, describing Harrah's approach as resembling clinical trials for a drug's efficacy more than anything a traditional casino marketing department had done before. The results reshaped how the company allocated its entire promotional budget: internal analysis found that the top 26 percent of Total Rewards customers by value were generating 82 percent of the company's total revenue, a concentration ratio that made the case for pouring disproportionate marketing attention into that top tier self-evident once the number existed to point to. Sports betting apps did not need to invent this approach. They needed only to notice that a mobile phone generates a far richer, far more continuous behavioral record than a physical loyalty card ever could, because the phone is present for every bet, every login, every moment of hesitation between opening the app and placing a wager, in a way no swipe of a physical card at a casino kiosk ever captured. DraftKings describes its own VIP function in almost clinical terms on its careers site, seeking analysts to build customer segmentation and value models, to design what the company calls treatment plans for high-value customers, and to develop revenue forecasts specifically for that segment — language borrowed, whether consciously or not, directly from the customer-lifetime-value vocabulary Loveman's team pioneered at Harrah's, now applied to a product where the underlying data arrives in real time, every day, rather than accumulating one physical casino visit at a time. The company's own recruiting materials describe the VIP team as handling a significant share of the company's total revenue and having outsized visibility into the bottom line as a result — an unusually candid admission, in a job posting rather than a lawsuit, of how concentrated a modern sportsbook's profitability actually is among a relatively small population of its most active customers.

One of the more concrete places this tension surfaces is the deposit limit, a tool every regulated sportsbook offers and every responsible-gaming advocate recommends: a customer sets a cap on how much money can enter their account in a given week or month, a genuine measure of self-protection built directly into the product. The same apps that offer the limit, however, typically make raising it a matter of a few taps inside the account settings, available on demand, with no waiting period and no independent review — a structural asymmetry in which the guardrail a customer builds for themselves can be dismantled by that same customer, in a moment of exactly the impulse the guardrail was meant to interrupt, faster than it took to put up. A VIP host who has been assigned to a customer approaching or exceeding a self-imposed limit is, in the ordinary course of the relationship, positioned to notice the limit and to reach out — not necessarily to enforce it, but to offer the kind of personalized attention, bonus, or encouragement that makes raising it feel like a natural next step in an ongoing relationship rather than a decision made alone. Nothing about that sequence requires bad faith on the host's part. It requires only that the host's job, as the industry's own recruiting language describes it, is to grow the customer's engagement, and that a self-imposed limit sitting unmoved is, from a pure revenue standpoint, engagement not yet grown.

That concentration is not a secret internal to the industry; it shows up, whenever regulators have looked closely enough to measure it, as a strikingly lopsided distribution of where the money actually comes from. British reporting on the country's far more mature online-gambling market, regulated for longer and studied more aggressively than the American one, has found roughly a third of total operator revenue coming from around one percent of customers — a concentration ratio that would be extraordinary in almost any other consumer business, and that makes plain why a company's VIP program, rather than its ordinary marketing to casual bettors, is where an outsized share of its actual profit originates. It is worth being precise about what that concentration implies. A customer who is losing money steadily, reliably, and in amounts large enough to be worth a personal host's attention is, by definition, the kind of customer a public company's shareholders most want retained. The incentives inside a VIP program are not designed around identifying a customer in trouble and slowing them down; they are designed around identifying a customer's value and protecting it, and for a large share of VIP customers, "value to the company" and "vulnerability to harm" are simply two descriptions of the same underlying behavior pattern, observed from two different vantage points.

The United Kingdom's Gambling Commission has spent the better part of the past decade producing the closest thing available to a public record of what happens when that incentive goes unchecked, because Britain's regulator has both the authority and the institutional appetite to fine operators over exactly this failure mode in a way no American state regulator has yet matched. In 2023 the Commission imposed a record £19.2 million penalty on William Hill's online operation, after finding — among a list of "alarming" and "widespread" failings, in the regulator's own characterization — that one customer had been allowed to open an account and lose £23,000 within twenty minutes without any affordability or welfare check taking place, and another had been permitted to lose £18,000 within twenty-four hours under the same absence of scrutiny. Those are not VIP-program failures in the narrow sense of a host behaving badly; they are failures of the systems that are supposed to flag a customer for exactly the kind of review a responsible operator would apply before that customer's losses accelerated further — systems that, not coincidentally, are the same underlying customer-monitoring infrastructure a VIP program uses to identify a valuable customer in the first place, repurposed, in a well-run company, to also catch a customer in danger. William Hill's parent company, 888 Holdings, had already been fined roughly £7.8 million in an earlier enforcement action tied specifically to how it handled VIP customers, including accounts based in the Middle East that the Commission found had been allowed to continue gambling at harmful levels without adequate checks. Betfred, a smaller UK operator, was separately fined £825,000, with an additional multimillion-pound penalty against its parent company, for its own social-responsibility and anti-money-laundering shortcomings. None of these cases allege that the companies built VIP programs with the explicit goal of harming customers. They allege something more mundane and, in a way, more damning: that the same data systems capable of identifying a customer's rising value to the company were not required, and in practice were not used, to trigger a proportionate response when that same data pattern indicated rising harm to the person generating it.

American sportsbooks operate under regulatory regimes that are, state by state, considerably less mature than Britain's, and no American regulator has yet produced a penalty on the scale of William Hill's £19.2 million for VIP-program failures specifically. What exists instead, so far, is private litigation — the Sage and Thompson case being the most detailed public account to date of what an American VIP relationship actually looked like from the inside, at least according to one complaint that the defendants dispute. The specificity of that complaint is itself instructive: a company does not need to explicitly instruct a VIP host to target a customer showing signs of a gambling disorder in order for the system to produce that outcome. It needs only to instruct the host, as the industry's own job postings describe the role, to maximize a given customer's engagement and lifetime value, and to give that host real-time visibility into exactly which levers — a personalized bonus, a well-timed phone call, a gift calibrated to the customer's own recent losses — are most likely to keep a specific, named individual actively betting. A customer whose losses are mounting because they are struggling to stop is, from the pure vantage point of a value model blind to that context, difficult to distinguish from a customer who simply enjoys the product and can comfortably afford to keep using it. Distinguishing between those two customers is possible — some UK-regulated operators build affordability checks and voluntary loss limits precisely to draw that distinction — but doing so runs directly against the commercial logic of a VIP program built to identify and protect the company's most valuable relationships, which is exactly why regulators, rather than the industry itself, have been the primary source of pressure to build the distinction in.

The mechanics behind how a company like DraftKings decides which customer gets a host in the first place borrow directly from a discipline retailers and credit-card issuers have used for decades to sort customers by value: a scoring approach built around recency, frequency, and monetary value — how recently a customer last placed a bet, how often they bet, and how much they have wagered and lost over a given window. A customer who scores high enough across those three dimensions crosses a threshold that triggers human attention, the same basic logic Harrah's applied with slot-floor loyalty cards two decades earlier, now running continuously against a mobile betting feed instead of a periodic casino-visit log. Churn-prediction models, built on the same underlying data, flag customers whose betting frequency is starting to decline — the earliest sign, in a value-model's eyes, that a previously reliable revenue source might be drifting away — and it is precisely at that moment of drift that a personalized retention offer, timed and sized by the same model, tends to arrive. Whether the customer is drifting away because their finances have improved, because a new hobby has taken betting's place, or because a gambling problem has begun to frighten them into pulling back is not a distinction the churn model is built to make; it is built to notice the drift and respond to it, regardless of cause.

The United States has, so far, produced nothing resembling the UK Gambling Commission's enforcement record, and part of the reason is structural rather than a matter of one country's regulators simply trying harder. The UK Gambling Commission is a single national authority with jurisdiction over every licensed operator in the country, empowered to fine, restrict, or revoke a license across the entire British market at once. The United States has no equivalent body. Sports betting is regulated state by state, under whatever combination of a gaming commission, a lottery agency, or a horse-racing board a given legislature happened to assign the job to when it legalized wagering, which means a VIP-program failure identified by, say, one state's gaming enforcement division has no automatic bearing on how the same company's VIP program operates in the dozens of other states where it holds a separate license under separate rules. A company found to have mishandled a vulnerable VIP customer in one jurisdiction faces, at most, a state-level penalty and a public-relations problem, not anything resembling the UK Commission's power to treat a single finding as evidence about a company's national practices. Congress has, so far, left the industry almost entirely to this patchwork; proposals like the SAFE Bet Act that would impose uniform national standards on VIP marketing, affordability screening, or self-exclusion have not become law. It is worth asking, given that structure, what the country's actual response mechanism looks like where it exists at all. Every state that has legalized mobile sports betting requires operators to offer some form of self-exclusion, typically a voluntary registry a bettor can join to have their account access blocked, usually for a period of at least a year. In practice, the tool is used by a strikingly small fraction of the population it exists to protect: one state's 2026 regulatory report found close to 300,000 online sportsbook accounts had used a short, three-day "cool off" period during the prior year, but fewer than 500 customers had made the more serious commitment of a full self-exclusion lasting a year or more. Self-exclusion registries are also, by design, state-specific and operator-specific in a country where a bettor can cross a state line or open an account with a different licensed operator and simply resume — there is no federal registry, and proposed legislation to create one, the SAFE Bet Act, had not passed as this book was being written. A customer who has self-excluded from DraftKings in New Jersey faces no technical obstacle to opening a FanDuel account the same afternoon, a gap that stands in obvious tension with the same companies' VIP programs, which face no comparable friction in identifying and retaining a valuable customer across every product the company offers.

There is a genuine counter-case here, and it deserves to be stated plainly rather than waved away. Not every heavily comped, frequently contacted VIP customer is a person in crisis; plenty of high-volume bettors are wealthy, disciplined, and entirely capable of losing tens of thousands of dollars a year on sports betting the way another person might spend the same sum on a boat or a wine collection, without any of it representing harm in a meaningful sense. A casino host's attention, similarly, is not inherently predatory — hospitality, personalized service, and genuine relationships between hosts and long-term customers are real features of the industry, not merely euphemisms for exploitation, and the legal, regulated version of this relationship is unambiguously preferable to the unlicensed bookmaker who extended credit to a losing customer with no oversight and no obligation to intervene at all.

The strongest version of the industry's case, in fact, points to what happened when Britain tried to impose the opposite extreme. When the Gambling Commission pushed operators toward more aggressive affordability checks in 2024 — background financial-verification screening triggered automatically once a customer's losses crossed certain thresholds — Britain's horse-racing industry, whose wagering base skews older and wealthier than sports betting's, reported a real-terms decline of roughly £3 billion in online betting turnover over two years, a drop the sport's governing body attributed directly to the checks. A survey of nearly 15,000 racing bettors found that a quarter had already been required to undergo a check, and more than half said they would bet less or stop entirely if further checks were introduced; among bettors who said they had turned to an unlicensed, unregulated black-market bookmaker as a result, the share who blamed the affordability checks specifically rose from roughly half to nearly two-thirds. That finding cuts directly against a simple story in which more aggressive intervention is always the safer choice: a check intrusive enough to drive a comfortable, financially secure bettor away from the regulated market and toward an operator with no self-exclusion registry, no affordability screening, and no license to lose does not obviously make anyone safer, even if it looks, on a regulator's dashboard, like responsible-gambling activity increasing. The honest version of this chapter's argument is not that VIP programs are secretly malicious. It is that the same data infrastructure a company builds to find its most profitable customers is, by construction, extremely good at finding the people losing the most money the fastest — and that nothing in a public company's ordinary incentive structure asks that infrastructure to slow down once it has found them, unless a regulator, a plaintiff's attorney, or the company's own responsible-gaming team is empowered to push back against the revenue team pursuing the same customer from the other direction. Gary Loveman's insight in the early 2000s was that the casino industry had been leaving enormous amounts of money on the table by not knowing, with real statistical confidence, exactly who its best customers were. Two decades and one legal sports-betting boom later, that same insight, running now on a phone rather than a loyalty card, means the industry also knows, with real statistical confidence, exactly who its most damaged customers are — usually before those customers know it about themselves.
