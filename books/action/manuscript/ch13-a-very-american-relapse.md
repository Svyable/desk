# A Very American Relapse

The country has done this before, although never with a product this fast.

Every gambling expansion in American history has arrived carrying the same pair of arguments in opposite hands. One says the activity already exists, so bringing it into daylight is more honest than pretending prohibition works. The other says normalization changes the activity itself: more access, more advertising, more participation, more people discovering they are worse at stopping than they expected. Both arguments can be true at the same time. That is what makes sports betting harder to think about than a morality play.

The post-2018 market did not invent gambling disorder. It did not invent bookmakers, compulsive wagering, debt, relapse, or the family member who learns too late that the checking account has been emptied. What changed was the delivery system. The old American sportsbook was geographically scarce, socially marked and often illegal. The new one is an authenticated software session. It sits beside banking, messaging, maps and weather. It can reopen in less time than it takes to remember why you closed it.

That difference is not cosmetic.

A person trying not to drink can still encounter alcohol everywhere, but the bottle does not send a personalized push notification because the local team has just fallen behind by seven. A casino customer who leaves the floor has at least crossed a threshold. A sportsbook customer can leave by swiping upward and return by tapping glass. The recovery environment and the gambling environment occupy the same object.

This is where the word **relapse** belongs in the book, but carefully. Relapse is a clinical concept before it is a metaphor. Gambling disorder is recognized in modern psychiatric classification as a behavioral addiction, which is important precisely because the thing being consumed is not a chemical. The object of reinforcement is the wagering cycle itself: anticipation, risk, outcome, arousal, regret, pursuit, occasional reward, repetition. A product can intensify that cycle without putting a molecule into the bloodstream.

The strongest recent American evidence is not a clean national count of newly addicted sports bettors. We do not have one. What we have is a set of indicators that rise together imperfectly and should not be collapsed: treatment demand, helpline contacts, self-exclusion behavior, household financial stress, and survey measures of risky gambling. Each sees a different part of the population.

The National Council on Problem Gambling reported that contact with the national helpline system rose sharply during the first years of widespread mobile sports betting. Its modernization material records large increases in calls, texts and chat contacts after 2020, with total contact volume nearly doubling by 2023. That number is easy to misuse. A helpline contact is not a diagnosis. Awareness campaigns can raise calls even if underlying prevalence stays flat. Better routing can make an old problem more visible. A national hotline becoming easier to find can produce more measured demand without creating more disorder.

But the direction of the signal is difficult to ignore when the same pattern appears in states immediately after launch. Ohio's gambling-help network reported a large jump in contacts during its first year of legal sports wagering. New Jersey's Council on Compulsive Gambling has reported helpline growth since 2018 and a caller population increasingly dominated by younger men presenting sports-betting problems rather than the casino and lottery patterns counselors were more accustomed to seeing. National reporting has described the same demographic shift: men in their twenties and thirties appearing more often in services built in an earlier era for a different gambling profile.

That does not prove the app created the vulnerability. It establishes something narrower and more useful: the market is recruiting a population into high-frequency gambling quickly enough that treatment systems notice the composition of their callers changing.

The distinction between prevalence and severity matters here. Imagine a country where the share of adults meeting the strictest diagnostic threshold moved only modestly, but the number of people exposed to continuous wagering tripled and the most affected subgroup lost money faster. A prevalence chart could look reassuring while the treatment burden worsened. The reverse is also possible: publicity around problem gambling could bring people into treatment earlier, increasing helpline use while reducing severe downstream harm. That is why a responsible account does not choose one indicator and call it the answer.

The household-finance studies make the problem harder to dismiss as a counseling-system artifact, but they need the same discipline. The NBER working paper **“Gambling Away Stability: Sports Betting’s Impact on Vulnerable Households”** used transaction data and staggered state legalization to estimate how online sports betting changed household financial behavior. The reported pattern was not that every legal-state household became poorer. It was that spending on sports betting displaced savings and investment and that the adverse credit effects were concentrated among financially constrained households. The paper later moved into the peer-reviewed literature, but even good quasi-experimental work remains an estimate built from assumptions about comparison groups and timing rather than an X-ray of each bettor’s motive. Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w33108

A separate line of work from researchers associated with Brigham Young University examined roughly 184,000 households and reported that net brokerage investment fell after sports-betting legalization, with the heaviest bettors reducing investment deposits much more than average. Again, this is not the same question as addiction prevalence. A person can make a financially damaging habit without meeting clinical criteria for gambling disorder. A person can meet those criteria before a bankruptcy appears. The overlap is real but not complete. Source summary: https://news.byu.edu/intellect/byu-study-legalization-of-sports-betting-has-led-to-households-saving-significantly-less-money

The New York Federal Reserve added another layer in its staff report **“Sports Betting Across Borders: Spatial Spillovers and Credit Distress.”** The striking part was not merely the association between legalization and worse credit outcomes; it was evidence that distress could spill across state lines, consistent with people in non-legal states crossing borders or otherwise gaining access to nearby legal markets. That matters because it weakens one of the simplest state-policy assumptions: that gambling exposure stops at the legal border. A phone notices location. A household economy does not. Source: https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1184.pdf

None of these studies, by themselves, tells us how many people relapsed after years of abstinence because a sportsbook app became legal in their state. That question is surprisingly undermeasured. It is also exactly the question a product-safety regulator ought to care about.

Consider the person who self-excluded from a casino in 2016. The exclusion list may have been property-specific or state-specific. By 2024 the same person could face a completely different set of operators, products and jurisdictional rules. Some states maintain centralized self-exclusion programs. Others rely more heavily on operator-level tools. There is no single national self-exclusion switch covering every legal sportsbook in the United States. A person can be disciplined enough to recognize a problem and still be required to repeat that recognition across systems designed by different regulators.

That fragmentation would be inconvenient in any addiction context. In a mobile market it is an architectural flaw.

The most revealing comparison is not with alcohol or tobacco but with financial fraud prevention. Banks assume that a person who has flagged a card as stolen should not need to contact every merchant individually. Credit bureaus let a consumer freeze access across the system because identity theft does not respect corporate boundaries. Sports betting regulation, by contrast, often treats exclusion as a relationship between one gambler and one operator or one gambler and one state. The burden falls on the person at the moment when impaired control is the exact condition under discussion.

This is why “responsible gambling” can become a strangely circular phrase. The customer is told to set limits, take breaks, use cool-off tools, self-exclude, avoid chasing losses and treat betting as entertainment. All good advice. But the person most in need of those tools is, by definition, the person whose ability to impose those rules on himself is becoming unreliable. A safety system that works only while the user is fully capable of self-regulation is less of a safety system than it appears.

The operators know this at least indirectly because they already maintain detailed behavioral models for a different purpose. A sportsbook can detect when a customer has stopped wagering, when deposits have changed, when preferred sports shift, when bet size increases, when engagement falls, when a promotion reactivates play, and when a high-value account deserves a human VIP host. The data infrastructure required to detect risky change is not science fiction. The argument is about what the system should optimize for once risky change appears.

That question becomes uncomfortable around the word **VIP**. The industry's most profitable customers are not evenly distributed. In gambling generally, a relatively small share of users contributes a disproportionate share of revenue. American sportsbook companies have imported loyalty segmentation, host programs and personalized retention from casino economics. DraftKings has publicly described VIP analytics and “treatment plans” for high-value customers in its own recruiting material. That phrasing is ordinary customer-relationship language inside the company. Read from the outside, in an industry dealing with addiction risk, it has an accidental double meaning.

A high-value customer can be a wealthy recreational bettor who understands the odds, enjoys the product and loses amounts he can comfortably afford. He can also be a customer whose “value” is rising because loss-chasing and impaired control are accelerating. Revenue data alone does not distinguish those people.

The British market offers a warning because its regulators encountered this problem earlier. Repeated enforcement cases there found operators failing to intervene when customers deposited or lost extraordinary sums in short periods, including VIP accounts. The United States does not have to copy the British regulatory model wholesale to learn the narrower lesson: customer-value systems and harm-detection systems will sometimes identify the same person for opposite reasons.

That is the product-design conflict in one sentence.

The industry response is that regulated operators are better positioned to see and manage risky behavior than illegal bookmakers ever were. This is one of the strongest arguments for legalization, and it is true as far as it goes. A licensed sportsbook has identity data, account history, deposit records, exclusion obligations, geolocation and regulatory reporting. An offshore site or neighborhood bookmaker may have none of those constraints. Legalization creates the possibility of effective intervention.

Possibility is not the same as incentive.

The state wants tax revenue. The operator wants handle and retention. The bettor wants entertainment and, usually, profit. The regulator wants a legal market that does not produce scandals severe enough to threaten the policy settlement. Those incentives overlap until they do not. A bettor beginning to spiral is exactly where they diverge. The operator makes more money if the customer continues, unless regulation or internal policy changes the payoff. The state may collect more tax if the customer continues. The customer may insist he wants to continue. The harm-control system is the only participant whose success can require less gambling.

A serious regulatory design therefore needs measures that are not generated by the operator's own revenue dashboard. How many customers hit deposit limits? How many voluntarily self-exclude? How many later attempt to reverse exclusion? How often does an operator initiate contact because of risk markers rather than marketing opportunity? What happens after that contact? Does betting fall? Do customers simply move to another app? How many excluded users appear at competitors within thirty days? How many accounts receive promotional messages during or immediately after a cooling-off period?

Those are not philosophical questions. They are database joins.

## The missing relapse dashboard

A regulator trying to measure relapse would not need to decide what is happening inside every customer's head. It would need to stop throwing away the sequence of events the market already records.

Start with the simplest timeline. A customer stops betting for ninety days. The customer returns. The first session contains one deposit, then another, then another. The average stake rises. A promotion is redeemed. The account reaches a loss threshold. A responsible-gambling message appears. A VIP host sends a separate message. The customer sets a limit, changes it, cools off, returns, and eventually self-excludes.

Every one of those events has a timestamp.

Today they can live in different operational silos and be interpreted according to different incentives. Marketing sees reactivation. Payments sees deposit velocity. VIP sees account value. Safer-gambling staff sees a risk score. Compliance sees whether the required message was sent. A regulator sees whatever the operator is required to report.

The same customer can therefore be four different customers depending on which dashboard is open.

A relapse-resistant system would force those views together.

It would ask how often a customer who has taken a cooling-off period receives a promotion within the first week back. It would measure whether a voluntary deposit limit is followed by attempts to raise the limit and whether those attempts are approved immediately or after a delay. It would compare the intervention rate for customers whose losses are rising with the retention rate for the same group. It would record whether a person flagged by one operator for acute risk opens an account at another operator during the same period.

That last measurement is where the state-by-state structure becomes impossible to defend as merely an administrative inconvenience. If the market can share information to verify identity, prevent duplicate promotional abuse, geolocate a phone, detect payment fraud and comply with anti-money-laundering rules, then the claim that it is technically impossible to recognize a customer who has explicitly asked not to be allowed to gamble everywhere deserves a much higher burden of proof.

Privacy matters here. A national risk database containing every bettor's detailed behavior would create its own danger and would be an invitation to misuse data this book has spent chapters showing is commercially valuable. The answer is not to build one enormous government dossier of wagers.

The answer is to define the smallest pieces of information that have to travel.

An exclusion can be a yes-or-no credential. A cooling-off period can have an expiry date. A regulator can require operators to submit aggregated intervention outcomes without publishing customer identities. Independent auditors can inspect the underlying records under confidentiality rules. Researchers can receive de-identified data with enough structure to test whether interventions work without being handed a list of named gamblers.

The distinction matters because “privacy” otherwise becomes a strangely convenient argument in one direction. The same customer may already be profiled aggressively enough to receive individualized odds boosts and retention offers, yet suddenly become too private to protect across operator boundaries. A defensible market cannot claim both positions without explaining the asymmetry.

It also needs a failure metric.

If an operator sends a warning and the customer loses twice as much in the next thirty days, the fact that the warning was delivered is not evidence that the system worked. If a self-excluded customer moves to a competitor, the first operator's compliance is not the same thing as a successful exclusion regime. If a customer repeatedly crosses a risk threshold and is simultaneously upgraded into higher-value service, the presence of a responsible-gambling team somewhere in the organization is beside the point.

The outcome has to be behavioral.

Did the risky pattern slow? Did losses stop accelerating? Did the customer take a break that lasted? Did cross-operator access close when the customer asked it to? Did marketing pressure decline at the same moment the risk system said it should?

Those questions would also make the legalization argument stronger where it deserves to be stronger. An illegal bookmaker cannot easily produce audited intervention rates, cross-operator exclusion performance, standardized loss-concentration reports or evidence that a risk alert actually reduced harm. A legal market can.

That is the bargain legalization should have demanded: not merely visibility for taxation, but visibility for outcomes.

The best version of a legal market would make them auditable.

It would also stop pretending that a helpline number at the bottom of an advertisement is the endpoint of responsible-gambling policy. The helpline matters. Treatment matters. But by the time a customer calls, several upstream systems have already failed or been overwhelmed: personal limits, family awareness, operator intervention, self-exclusion architecture, credit friction, perhaps state rules around promotions and VIP handling. A public-health approach moves attention earlier in the chain.

The phrase **very American relapse** in the title is not meant to imply a national weakness of character. It is about the national habit of rebuilding an old risk in a more efficient commercial form and then locating responsibility mainly inside the consumer. We did not invent gambling. We did not invent compulsive gambling. We did build a market in which every professional game can become a sequence of wagers, every customer can be segmented, and every quiet evening can be converted into a re-engagement opportunity.

That is new enough to deserve measurement on its own terms.

There is also a danger in getting the story too neat. Most sports bettors will not develop gambling disorder. Many will use the apps for years, lose modest amounts, and consider the losses the price of entertainment. Some people who once bet illegally now have a safer, more transparent product with enforceable rules and clearer recourse. Some customers will use limit-setting tools successfully. Some increases in helpline contacts may reflect better awareness and less stigma rather than more underlying harm. A legalization regime can simultaneously reduce one kind of danger and increase another.

That is not a contradiction. It is the actual policy problem.

The question is not whether legal sports betting is secretly the same thing as an illegal bookmaker. It is whether legalization was treated as the end of the regulatory project when it should have been the beginning.

The first generation of American sports-betting law focused on permission: which operators may enter, what tax rate they pay, which leagues and bets qualify, how geolocation works, how revenue is reported. The second generation will have to focus on behavior: product speed, customer concentration, cross-operator exclusion, VIP intervention, personalized marketing, credit distress and what counts as evidence that a market is producing avoidable harm.

A mature market should be able to answer those questions without treating every critic as a prohibitionist and every bettor as a patient.

That requires something the first legalization wave did not demand strongly enough: a shared dataset capable of telling us not only how much America wagered, but how the wager changed the people placing it.

The house already measures almost everything else.
