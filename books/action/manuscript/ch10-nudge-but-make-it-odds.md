# Nudge, But Make It Odds

B.F. Skinner spent a considerable part of his career at Harvard studying pigeons in boxes, and one of the more durable findings to come out of that work had nothing to do with pigeons at all. Skinner was interested in what happens to behavior under different schedules of reinforcement — different rules for when a response gets rewarded — and he found that a reward delivered after a fixed, predictable number of responses produces steady behavior that stops almost as soon as the rewards stop coming. A reward delivered after a variable, unpredictable number of responses produces something else entirely: behavior that persists at a high, stubborn rate, and that keeps going for a remarkably long time even after the rewards are withdrawn altogether, because the animal — or the person — has no way of knowing whether the next response, right now, is the one that finally pays off. Skinner himself pointed to slot machines as the clearest real-world illustration of the principle, and he was reportedly confident enough in the mechanism that he claimed he could, in principle, train a pigeon into something resembling a pathological gambler using nothing but a variable-ratio schedule and enough time in the box. The apparatus itself was simple enough to build on a lab bench: a small enclosed box, a lever or a key the animal could peck or press, and a mechanism that delivered a food pellet according to whatever schedule the experimenter had programmed. Skinner ran these experiments at Harvard and later the University of Minnesota and Indiana University across the 1930s and 1940s, and part of what made the variable-ratio finding so durable was how mundane the setup looked next to how stubborn the resulting behavior turned out to be — a pigeon pecking a key hundreds of times an hour, for a reward that might not arrive until the fortieth, the tenth, or the first peck, with no way to tell in advance which one it would be. Later researchers have pushed back on the strongest version of that claim — more recent work in rats has failed to show that variable-ratio training alone reliably produces the compulsive, harm-generating pattern clinicians associate with disordered gambling in humans, which means Skinner's confidence outran what the evidence can actually support. But the narrower finding has held up for seventy years and underlies nearly every gambling product built since: on a variable-ratio schedule, there is no safe moment to stop, because the very next pull, the very next bet, might be the one that pays.

The person who did the most to translate that laboratory finding into an account of how an actual casino floor works was not a psychologist but an anthropologist, Natasha Dow Schüll, who spent fifteen years embedded in Las Vegas studying slot-machine players before publishing her account, Addiction by Design, in 2012. Schüll's central argument was not simply that slot machines exploit variable-ratio reinforcement, which by her telling was already a well-understood piece of industry engineering by the time she began her fieldwork. It was that the entire physical and digital environment of a modern slot machine — the seating, the screen's pacing, the sound design, the mathematical structure of near-misses — had been engineered around a psychological state she called the machine zone: a kind of absorbed equilibrium in which a player's awareness of time, money, and even their own body recedes, and in which the goal of play stops being to win and becomes simply to keep playing, for as long as possible, at the machine's own designed tempo. Schüll's most unsettling contribution was her insistence that this was not a bug in slot-machine design, some regrettable side effect of an otherwise neutral entertainment product, but close to the entire point: modern machine gambling, in her account, is engineered for what she called continuous productivity, a state of extended, uninterrupted play extracted from the player with the same design intent an engineer brings to any other system built to maximize a specific output. Addiction, in this framing, is not purely a property sitting inside a vulnerable individual's brain chemistry. It is something that emerges from the interaction between a person and a machine built, deliberately, to produce it in as many people as the mathematics of variable-ratio reinforcement will allow.

A sports-betting app is not a slot machine. It does not sit in a windowless room designed to obscure the passage of time, and the outcome of an NBA game is not generated by a random-number generator tuned to a fixed payout percentage; it depends on twenty-two athletes and the actual, unscripted events of a real basketball game. But the underlying reinforcement structure a bettor experiences scrolling through a modern app is close enough to Schüll's description of the slot floor that the comparison is not merely rhetorical. A same-game parlay, discussed in this book's earlier chapter on in-play betting, converts a single game's outcome into three or four separate variable-ratio events instead of one — a missed shot with two minutes left doesn't just cost a bettor the game bet, it kills the parlay in a moment that arrives unpredictably, and the very next bet, on the very next game an hour later, carries exactly the same promise the last one did, unresolved by the fact that the last one lost. In-play and micro-betting, described in the same earlier chapter, compress the interval between one bet resolving and the next opportunity appearing down to seconds rather than a full game, which is precisely the kind of tempo increase Schüll's research on slot-machine design identified as central to prolonging play: the faster the interval between one bet's resolution and the next bet's availability, the less room exists for a player's own judgment to interrupt the sequence before the next variable-ratio pull is already underway.

The near-miss is the piece of slot-machine psychology with the longest research history behind it, and it maps onto the same-game parlay almost too neatly to be coincidental. Mark Griffiths, a British psychologist who has studied gambling behavior for decades, helped establish that a near-miss — a slot-machine reel that stops one symbol short of a jackpot — is not simply a disappointing loss to the player experiencing it; it is processed, behaviorally and even in brain-imaging studies of reward circuitry, as something closer to a win than an ordinary loss, and it increases rather than decreases the motivation to keep playing, in direct contradiction of how ordinary reinforcement theory says a failed outcome should function. A same-game parlay that wins its first three legs and loses its fourth on a missed field goal as time expires is, structurally, a manufactured near-miss delivered on a schedule the bettor cannot predict — the ticket was never actually close to paying out in a mathematical sense, since a parlay either cashes in full or fails in full, but the lived experience of watching three legs hit before the fourth one collapses is close to indistinguishable from Griffiths' description of a slot machine's near-jackpot. Sportsbooks have built a specific promotional product directly on top of that experience: parlay insurance, an offer, common across the major operators, that returns a bettor's stake or issues bonus bets specifically when a multi-leg parlay loses on its final leg. The promotion is marketed as a consumer-friendly cushion against bad luck. It also has the effect of converting the single most reliable trigger for continued play that gambling psychology has identified — the near-miss — into an officially compensated event, ensuring that the parlay bettor who experiences it most often is the one who keeps a small reward flowing back in, and keeps building the next ticket.

Where the comparison to Schüll's work becomes considerably less speculative is in the deliberate design choices sitting on top of the underlying betting mechanics — the features a product team adds specifically to shape when and how often a bettor opens the app, independent of whatever game happens to be on television that night. A peer-reviewed taxonomy published in the journal Addiction in 2025 catalogued these design choices across online gambling platforms generally, sorting them into categories researchers have taken to calling sludge, dark patterns, and dark nudges — friction deliberately added to discourage an action the operator does not want, such as withdrawing money, alongside friction deliberately removed to encourage an action the operator does want, such as depositing more. A separate scoping review of the same literature catalogued specific mechanisms recurring across gambling apps: onboarding flows engineered to minimize the steps between download and first deposit, loss-framed messaging that recasts a losing session as nearly having won, and interface choices that make a deposit limit easy to raise and hard to lower. None of these individual choices is illegal in most American states, and few of them are unique to gambling apps — a food-delivery app that makes canceling a subscription three menus deep while making reordering a single tap is running a version of the same playbook. What makes the pattern more consequential in a betting app is simply what is being manipulated: not a subscription fee, but the pace and frequency of an activity whose underlying mathematics guarantee that, over any sufficiently long run, the house keeps a predictable share of everything wagered.

Gamification sits alongside the dark-patterns literature as a related but distinct design layer, less concerned with friction or urgency than with borrowing the reward structures of a video game and layering them over a betting product that would otherwise consist of nothing but a stream of wins and losses. DraftKings' loyalty structure, called Dynasty Rewards, sorts customers into five named tiers — Bronze, Silver, Gold, Diamond, and Onyx — with betting activity across the company's sportsbook, casino, and other products accumulating credits that move a customer up the ladder, unlocking better multipliers, weekly prize chests, and, at the top tier, a dedicated VIP host of exactly the kind described in this book's previous chapter. FanDuel, which does not run an equivalent tiered sportsbook loyalty program, launched a peer-to-peer prediction product called FanDuel Picks in 2025 specifically to give users, in the words of the trade press covering the launch, a daily reason to open the app that is not tied to whether a game happens to be on that night. Both approaches do the same underlying work: a tier ladder or a daily-missions structure generates a sense of progress and status independent of a bettor's actual won-loss record, so that a customer who is losing money in the only sense that matters — dollars leaving their bank account — can nonetheless experience climbing to Gold or completing a daily streak as a form of winning. Points, streaks, and badges simulate progress even while a customer is losing money, in the description one industry analysis gave the pattern, and the description is not intended as praise.

Push notifications are the most direct, least subtle instrument in this toolkit, and they are also the one with the clearest documented behavioral effect. Research on betting-app usage has found that users report placing more impulsive bets specifically after receiving a notification, and that problem gamblers are both more likely to leave notifications enabled and less likely to actively disable them than recreational bettors are — a finding that inverts the intuition a product designer might otherwise have, that the people most at risk would be the first to turn notifications off. A notification's content and timing are not arbitrary; a lapsed customer, someone who has not opened the app in several days, is a natural target for a message offering a free bet or a limited-time promotional boost, and the same behavioral-science literature that flags this pattern as a dark pattern also notes the uncomfortable overlap between "lapsed customer worth re-engaging" and "customer who may have stepped back because betting had become a problem for them." A win-back campaign built purely around maximizing reactivation does not distinguish between those two populations, because nothing in ordinary engagement data tells the model which lapsed user is which.

The cash-out feature occupies a stranger position in this taxonomy, because it genuinely does give a bettor something real: the ability to lock in a partial win, or cut a partial loss, before a bet's outcome is fully determined, rather than waiting helplessly for a final score that might reverse a bet's fortunes in the last minute. Sportsbooks market cash-out as a responsible-gambling feature, a tool that hands control back to the customer, and in a narrow sense that framing is accurate — a bettor who cashes out is choosing certainty over the variance they would otherwise be exposed to. But the same feature, examined through Schüll's framework, does something else as well: it generates a fresh, live decision point in the middle of every single bet, at the exact moment a bettor is watching the action unfold and is most emotionally engaged with the outcome, converting what used to be a single binary event — the bet resolves, win or lose, at the final whistle — into an ongoing sequence of smaller decisions, each one an opportunity to re-engage with the product rather than simply wait. Researchers studying the feature have described it as capable of prolonging a betting session and creating what amounts to an illusion of control, the sense that constant, active management of a live bet is itself a skill being exercised, when in practice the sportsbook has priced the cash-out offer to preserve its own margin regardless of which choice the bettor makes.

Friction on the way out of the product tells a similar story from the opposite direction. Deposits on a modern sportsbook app are close to frictionless — a saved card, a biometric login, funds available for betting within seconds. Withdrawals, by contrast, are frequently subject to rollover requirements buried in terms-of-service language most users never read closely, along with per-transaction withdrawal caps and mandatory waiting periods, commonly cited in the twenty-four-to-forty-eight-hour range, between one withdrawal request and the next. None of these frictions is advertised the way a deposit bonus is advertised; they exist, functionally, as the withdrawal side's mirror image of the deposit side's ease, and the asymmetry between the two experiences is itself one of the clearest markers researchers use to distinguish an ordinary business practice from a deliberately engineered dark pattern — a company genuinely indifferent to whether a customer keeps their money in the account or takes it out would presumably make both processes comparably easy.

None of this design vocabulary is unique to gambling, and the broader regulatory conversation about it predates the sports-betting boom by several years. The Federal Trade Commission published a staff report in September 2022, "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light," documenting a rise in what it called sophisticated design features aimed at tricking or trapping consumers across e-commerce, subscription services, and children's apps, and sorting the practices into categories that map closely onto the gambling-specific taxonomy researchers would publish a few years later: design that creates false beliefs, such as a countdown timer implying scarcity that does not really exist; design that buries important information inside lengthy terms of service; and design that leads to charges a consumer did not clearly authorize. The FTC's 2022 report was not written about gambling apps specifically, and it named no sportsbook by name, but the categories it built have since become the standard vocabulary researchers and regulators reach for when examining any app — betting-related or not — whose interface seems engineered to produce a specific financial behavior rather than simply to inform a user's own choice.

Massachusetts offers the most concrete public accounting available of what happens when this design machinery goes unsupervised by the regulator nominally responsible for it. State law required the Massachusetts Gaming Commission to review sports-wagering advertising materials before operators distributed them to the public; an audit released in August 2025 found the commission had not been doing so, and that in the roughly thirteen months between March 2023 and March 2024, fifty-one separate incidents were logged in which sportsbook advertising reached underage users or people who had identified themselves as struggling with gambling addiction — precisely the population the pre-clearance requirement existed to protect, using precisely the personalized-marketing infrastructure the rest of this chapter has described, operating for more than a year with no one at the state level actually checking whether the safeguard was functioning. The gap was not a matter of Massachusetts lacking a rule; the rule existed, on paper, from the moment the state legalized mobile betting. It was a matter of enforcement capacity, and of a regulator standing up a sports-betting market fast enough to meet the political timeline lawmakers wanted without standing up, at the same pace, the oversight staff the same law had promised would be watching.

What all of this design machinery is ultimately built to sustain, and what makes the stakes different from a shopping cart abandoned mid-checkout, is a population of bettors for whom the product is not working as advertised entertainment. A literature review commissioned by the National Council on Problem Gambling found that among online sports bettors specifically, as distinct from bettors overall, roughly 16 percent met clinical criteria for gambling disorder and another 13 percent showed some signs of problem gambling without yet meeting the full clinical threshold — together putting close to a third of online sports bettors somewhere on the spectrum of gambling-related harm, a rate the same review found running at least twice as high as the rate among gamblers generally. The same body of research describes the bettor most likely to fall into that clinical category in terms that will be familiar by the time this book reaches its later chapters on public health and demographics: disproportionately young, male, and online-native, exactly the population a mobile-first product with continuous notifications, tiered loyalty rewards, and frictionless deposits is best designed to reach at the highest possible frequency.

It would also be incomplete to describe behavioral design in gambling products as though the same toolkit only ever points in one direction. Several states now require sportsbook operators to build voluntary protective tools using precisely the nudge techniques this chapter has otherwise described as exploitative — a pop-up that interrupts a live betting session after a set amount of time has elapsed to remind a customer how long they have been playing, a mandatory prompt before a first deposit that asks a customer to set a budget rather than simply accepting money, optional messaging modeled on Canada's GameSense program that reframes a losing streak in explicitly cautionary rather than encouraging language. These tools use the identical behavioral vocabulary — a timed interruption, a default nudged toward a protective choice, a reframe of the bettor's own recent history — in service of the opposite goal from a win-back push notification. That such tools exist, and that some jurisdictions mandate them, is a genuine complication to any account of the industry as monolithically indifferent to the psychology it deploys. It is also true, and worth stating plainly, that these protective nudges are opt-in, easy to dismiss, and rarely defaulted to the most restrictive setting, while the deposit flow, the notification cadence, and the loyalty-tier ladder are switched on by default and require active effort from the customer to weaken — an asymmetry in which the industry's own default settings reveal, more reliably than its responsible-gambling marketing does, which behavioral tools it expects most customers to actually encounter.

It would be dishonest to present all of this as proof that every feature in a modern betting app was designed by product teams sitting in a room with a printed copy of Skinner's variable-ratio research and Schüll's fieldwork on their desks, deliberately engineering harm. Some of it almost certainly was designed with exactly that kind of behavioral-science literacy — the gambling industry has employed psychologists and behavioral-design consultants for decades, going back well before the first sportsbook app existed, and slot-machine manufacturers in particular have a long, well-documented history of hiring researchers specifically to study what keeps a player in the chair. But plenty of the same design choices could also emerge from something less sinister and more familiar: an ordinary product team running ordinary conversion-rate experiments, testing which notification copy gets more clicks, which onboarding flow gets more completed signups, which button color gets more deposits — the same optimization process every consumer app on a smartphone runs constantly, applied without any specific intent to exploit gambling psychology at all, but landing, again and again, on the same answers a deliberate behavioral engineer would have reached, because the underlying human responses to variable reward, urgency, and friction do not care why a designer built the feature that triggers them. A/B testing a push-notification's timing to maximize the click-through rate is standard practice at any consumer software company; it becomes something else entirely only because of what the click leads to on the other end.

What the accumulating academic literature on gambling-app dark patterns does establish, with reasonable confidence, is that the difference between a betting app and an ordinary shopping app is not that one uses persuasive design and the other doesn't. Nearly every app on a modern phone uses some version of variable reward, urgency framing, and friction asymmetry to shape behavior; that much of contemporary product design descends from the same behavioral-science lineage Skinner helped establish, whether or not any individual designer has ever read a page of his work. A streaming service's autoplay countdown, a social feed's infinite scroll, a mobile game's daily login bonus — all of it runs on some version of the same variable-reward architecture, refined over fifteen years of consumer-app competition for a finite amount of daily attention, long before a sportsbook product manager ever opened a specification document. The difference is what happens at the end of the loop the design is built to prolong. A shopping app's loop ends in a purchase whose price the customer can see and compare before committing. A social-media app's loop ends in more scrolling, a cost measured in attention rather than money. A sports-betting app's loop, run enough times, ends — for the mathematically guaranteed portion of its user base large enough for the industry's hold percentages to function as a business model at all — in a loss whose size the bettor typically cannot see clearly until well after the fact, prolonged by exactly the same behavioral machinery Schüll spent fifteen years documenting on the casino floor, now carried in a pocket, available at four in the morning, with no pit boss, no closing time, and no walk to the parking lot to interrupt the sequence before the next variable-ratio pull is already loading on the screen.
