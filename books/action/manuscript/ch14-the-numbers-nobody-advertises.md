# The Numbers Nobody Advertises

The most useful number in American sports betting is almost never the handle.

Handle is the number that photographs well. It is the giant pile of money wagered: billions through New York in a month, tens of billions nationally in a year, a number that can be printed next to a picture of a phone and made to look like proof of a new American pastime.

The sportsbook does not keep the handle. It keeps the hold: wagers minus payouts, before expenses. The state taxes some version of that revenue. The operator spends heavily to acquire and retain customers. Bettors recycle winnings into new bets. One dollar can pass through the handle multiple times.

So the first thing to do with a huge betting number is take it apart.

The second thing is harder.

Ask where the money came from before it became a bet.

That question did not have a good answer during the first years after *Murphy*. States could publish tax receipts. Operators could publish revenue. Industry groups could estimate national handle. All of those figures described the gambling system from the point of view of the gambling system.

Household finance requires a different camera.

Did the money replace spending on movies? Beer? Lottery tickets? Restaurants? Stocks? Emergency savings? A credit-card payment? Groceries next week?

The same $100 wager can mean almost nothing or quite a lot depending on which balance-sheet line surrendered it.

By 2024, researchers finally had enough staggered state legalization, bank-transaction data, credit-bureau data, and mobile-betting adoption to begin estimating that substitution instead of arguing from anecdotes.

The findings are not identical. They are also not especially comfortable.

## The account before the bet

A team led by Scott Baker used transaction-level financial data from 183,821 households—more than four million household-quarters—to study what happened when online sports betting became legal. Their paper, [*Gambling Away Stability: Sports Betting’s Impact on Vulnerable Households*](https://doi.org/10.3386/w33108), began as an NBER working paper in 2024 and was published in the *Journal of Financial Economics* in 2026.

The useful feature of the dataset is that a sportsbook deposit sits beside everything else.

You can see investment contributions. Credit-card payments. Consumption. Transfers. The household does not arrive as a survey answer about whether gambling feels affordable. It arrives as a flow of money.

Legalization increased average quarterly betting expenditures by about $29.46 across the full household sample. That number looks small enough to end the conversation if averaged over everyone, including people who never bet.

It should not.

Betting was concentrated. Participation grew after legalization. Frequency grew. The average includes a large zero-bet population surrounding a much smaller group whose behavior changed substantially.

The researchers found that online sports betting reduced saving and investment rather than simply displacing another form of entertainment. Among financially constrained households, the changes were sharper: credit-card debt increased, available credit fell, and overdraft frequency rose.

This is the first denominator the advertising never shows.

The marginal sports-betting dollar is not drawn proportionately from every American household. It is drawn heavily from the people who bet, and the financial consequences are concentrated again among those with less slack.

A market can therefore look modest in aggregate consumption data while being severe inside a narrow slice of household balance sheets.

Both descriptions can be true.

## The credit report

A second research team—Brett Hollenbeck, Poet Larsen, and Davide Proserpio—approached the question through a massive consumer-credit panel. Their [*Financial Consequences of Legalized Sports Gambling*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4903302) uses roughly seven million U.S. consumers and the staggered rollout of sports betting to estimate changes in credit health.

The paper is still important to read with its status visible: it has circulated as a working paper and has been revised materially. That is not a reason to ignore it. It is a reason not to turn one coefficient into scripture.

Its strongest result is the difference between access and friction.

General legal sports-betting access is associated with a modest reduction in average credit scores. When a state moves from retail betting to online/mobile access, the estimated decline is much larger. The authors’ late-2025 specification reports roughly a 0.7-point decline for general access and about a 12-point decline associated with the retail-to-online transition, alongside increases in bankruptcy, collections, credit-card delinquency, and auto-loan delinquency.

Twelve points is large enough that it deserves replication, alternative specifications, and hostile reading.

But notice the direction of the design question even before deciding whether that exact estimate survives.

The law does not merely legalize the wager.

The interface changes the cost of reaching it.

Driving to a casino is a friction.

Walking to a counter is a friction.

Being observed placing a large wager is a friction.

Closing time is a friction.

A geofenced app with stored credentials, instant deposits, live markets, personalized prompts, and a bet button under your thumb removes those frictions one by one.

If the financial effect of legalization is materially larger when the product becomes mobile, then “legal gambling” is too crude a treatment variable. The product architecture belongs in the policy model.

## A one-point decline that matters

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York supplied a third angle in 2026.

Jacob Goss and Daniel Mangrum’s [*Sports Betting Across Borders: Spatial Spillovers, Credit Distress, and Fiscal Externalities*](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr1184) asks a question state law tends to pretend does not exist: what happens at the border?

Mobile sports betting is geofenced, not walled.

People drive.

Counties near a legal state can generate bets even when their own state has not legalized the market. The researchers find that legalization increases sportsbook spending by roughly a factor of ten and raises participation by about 3.1 percentage points. In non-legal counties within fifteen miles of a legal border, spillover betting is about 14 percent of the direct effect, falling toward zero by roughly sixty miles.

The financial result is smaller in absolute units than the most dramatic working-paper estimates, which is exactly why it is useful.

Median credit scores decline by roughly one point. Overall delinquency rises by about 0.3 percentage points from a base near 10.7 percent. Nearby non-legal counties show a smaller delinquency spillover too.

One point sounds trivial.

Across an entire treated population, it is not necessarily trivial at all.

Average effects are where concentrated harms go to hide.

Suppose 97 percent of people experience no credit effect and 3 percent experience a large one. The mean will advertise calm while the tail absorbs the event.

That is approximately the structure a sportsbook would prefer us to discuss: total tax revenue on one side, average consumer on the other, heavy users dissolved inside both.

## The refrigerator

Credit scores are abstract enough that almost anything can be made to sound manageable.

Food is harder.

In June 2026, Xiaohui Guo, Lizhong Peng, and Chad Meyerhoefer released an NBER paper called [*Wagering the Bread Money: Sports Betting Legalization and Food Sufficiency*](https://doi.org/10.3386/w35305). Using Household Pulse Survey data and the staggered state rollout from 2021 through 2023, they estimate that legalized sports betting reduced food sufficiency by 2.1 percent among working-age adults without a college degree.

Because only a minority of households actively bet, they translate the estimate into a much larger implied effect among active bettors: roughly a 10.5 percent decline.

The effect was not flat through the year.

It followed football.

The estimated reduction persisted for roughly three to five months during NFL seasons and was larger among households with adults aged twenty-five to forty-four and among racial and ethnic minorities.

This result should receive the same skeptical treatment as every other causal paper in this book. Household Pulse Survey responses have their own sampling problems. State legalization is not randomly assigned. Translating an intent-to-treat effect into an active-bettor effect requires assumptions about who actually participates.

But the seasonal pattern is difficult to wave away with a generic story about worsening household finances.

A sports-betting effect that grows during football season is at least pointing in the direction its proposed mechanism predicts.

That is what makes it worth taking seriously.

The ugly phrase *bread money* is not moral theater when the outcome being measured is food sufficiency.

It is an accounting question.

## The helpline denominator

Financial distress is not gambling disorder. Gambling disorder is not the only way gambling can create financial harm.

Those categories overlap without being identical.

The public-health signal nevertheless moved in the same period.

The U.S. National Problem Gambling Helpline’s 2025 annual report recorded an average of more than 31,000 calls per month, and nearly half of people contacting the system were between eighteen and thirty-four. A 2026 *Information Systems Research* paper, [*Let It Ride!*](https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2024.1526), reports that problem-gambling hotline calls were strongly associated with the legalization of online sports betting, while physical sportsbook legalization did not show the same relationship.

That distinction should sound familiar by now.

Access is not binary.

A sportsbook is not simply present or absent. It has a distance, a clock, a login, a payment rail, a notification system, a menu depth, and a latency between impulse and execution.

The public-health argument becomes stronger when it stops saying *gambling is addictive* and begins specifying which product changes alter exposure.

## The counter-number

There is a serious counterargument, and the book needs it in the room before the conclusion.

A 2025 Progressive Policy Institute report looked at aggregate consumer data and argued that legal sports betting had not produced a visible national financial catastrophe. It noted that gambling spending as a share of personal consumption expenditures was about 1.04 percent in 2024 versus 1.07 percent in 2017, essentially flat at that level of measurement. It also pointed out that bankruptcies declined in early-adopter states over the post-2019 period rather than exploding.

That evidence matters.

It does not directly contradict the household studies as cleanly as either side may want.

A flat national share of consumer spending can coexist with a new gambling product replacing other gambling expenditures.

Falling aggregate bankruptcies can coexist with a causal increase relative to what bankruptcies would have been without online betting.

A small average credit effect can coexist with a large effect among frequent bettors.

And a legal market can reduce harms associated with illegal bookmaking while creating different harms through easier access.

The argument is not settled by choosing the scariest chart or the most reassuring one.

It is settled by matching denominators.

National consumption is a national denominator.

State tax revenue is a state denominator.

Credit scores are a consumer denominator.

Bank-transaction studies can isolate bettors.

Helplines isolate help-seekers.

Food-sufficiency surveys isolate households with particular constraints.

None is a complete picture. Together they are much harder to market around.

## Concentration is the missing denominator

There is another denominator missing from nearly every public argument: how revenue is distributed across customers.

This is different from asking what the average bettor loses.

Imagine two legal markets that each produce $1 billion in annual sportsbook revenue. In the first, ten million customers each lose about one hundred dollars over the year. In the second, nine million customers break roughly even or lose small amounts while a much smaller group supplies most of the billion dollars. The state tax receipt can be identical. The operator's income statement can be identical. The public-health problem is not remotely identical.

That is why averages are unusually dangerous in a business whose customers do not contribute evenly.

A mean can describe the market while describing almost nobody in it.

The correct picture is a distribution: percentiles, tails, duration, frequency, and concentration. How much of revenue comes from the top one percent of losing accounts? How much comes from people who deposit every week? How much comes from customers who have already used a limit-setting tool? How much comes from customers whose wagering accelerated after a large loss? How much comes from accounts assigned a VIP host?

Those questions change the meaning of familiar numbers.

Suppose an operator reports that the median customer loses very little. That can be true in a market where the business depends heavily on a small group losing a great deal. Suppose a state reports that most registered accounts place only occasional wagers. That can also be true while most taxable revenue comes from the minority who wager constantly. “Most users are fine” and “the revenue model is concentrated in the users least fine” are not mutually exclusive statements.

The same issue appears in household research. When legalization produces a small average decline in saving across a giant sample, the natural response is to call the effect economically modest. Sometimes that response is correct. But if the treatment effect is concentrated among active bettors, and concentrated again among financially constrained active bettors, the average is doing exactly what averages do: dividing a narrow event across people who never experienced it.

The policy response should therefore publish both.

Give the public the average effect. Then give it the tail.

Publish median annual loss, but also the 90th, 95th, 99th percentile, and the share of operator revenue attributable to each band. Publish account counts, but separate dormant accounts from monthly actives and monthly actives from high-frequency customers. Publish gross gaming revenue, but show how much was generated by customers who triggered a safer-gambling intervention during the same period.

That last number would make the debate considerably less theatrical.

If it is tiny, the industry would have unusually strong evidence that its profitability is not materially dependent on the customers its own systems identify as risky. If it is large, regulators would finally know that a meaningful share of tax receipts arrives from exactly the accounts policy is supposed to protect.

Either result is more useful than another press release announcing handle.

## A tax receipt is not an impact assessment

The other accounting mistake is to treat tax revenue as if it were automatically a measure of public benefit.

Tax revenue is real. It can fund schools, transit, pensions, treatment, or a general fund. Legal operators can also displace illegal ones, create jobs, buy advertising, pay licensing fees, and provide a regulated product to adults who want it. A serious critique of sports betting does not need to pretend those benefits are imaginary.

It does need to keep the ledger open past the revenue line.

If a state collects a dollar in sports-betting tax while a household reduces retirement contributions, carries more revolving debt, misses a payment, or needs treatment, those are not all the same kind of cost and should not be mechanically subtracted from one another. But neither should the state count its dollar and stop measuring.

The fiscal question is downstream of the household question.

Where did the taxable loss originate? What else changed in the customer's finances? Which customers generated the revenue? What public costs, if any, followed? How much of the tax receipt was dedicated to measuring or reducing those costs?

A state could answer those questions without deciding that sports betting is socially good or socially bad. It could simply treat the market like an economic activity whose externalities are worth measuring rather than a vending machine that happens to remit taxes.

This is already how governments think about other industries. A fuel tax is not a complete transportation policy. Alcohol excise revenue is not a complete alcohol policy. Cigarette taxes do not tell you whether smoking prevalence, cessation, or treatment is improving. The tax is one instrument and one data point.

Sports betting arrived in many statehouses with tax revenue doing far more rhetorical work than that.

The number was easy to forecast, easy to campaign on, easy to publish monthly, and politically legible. The harder measures—loss concentration, substitution away from saving, delinquency, treatment demand, exclusion, product-specific risk—arrived later because the institutions collecting the tax were not built to collect them.

That sequencing matters. What a government measures first becomes what the market learns to optimize around.

If the official scoreboard is handle, operators can celebrate handle.

If it is tax revenue, states can celebrate tax revenue.

If the scoreboard also includes how concentrated losses are and whether the concentration is rising, the definition of a successful market gets harder in exactly the way it should.

## Revenue from whom

States typically advertise sports betting in two numbers: handle and tax revenue.

The first makes the market look enormous.

The second makes it look public-spirited.

Missing is the distribution that connects them.

If sportsbook revenue came from millions of people each losing fifty dollars a year, the policy question would be relatively simple. It would look like a broad entertainment tax voluntarily paid by consumers.

If a large share comes from a narrow band of high-frequency users, including people carrying revolving debt and repeatedly redepositing after losses, the same tax revenue has a different economic character.

The state does not need to intend that result for the incentive to exist.

A tax on gross gaming revenue makes the treasury a minority partner in sportsbook losses.

Responsible-gambling programs may be funded from those proceeds. Treatment can therefore be financed by the activity producing some of the treatment demand. That is not unique to gambling; tobacco, alcohol, and lotteries have their own versions of the same loop.

But sports betting adds an unusual feature.

The state can watch the product become more efficient at producing the taxable event.

More markets.

More live bets.

More parlays.

Faster settlement.

Less friction.

A tax system that treats every dollar of operator revenue as equivalent has no built-in reason to prefer slow, low-risk betting over a product architecture that increases loss velocity.

The policy can be legal and still be poorly instrumented.

## The number worth publishing

A defensible sports-betting market would publish a number the current system mostly leaves inside operator databases.

Not merely total handle.

Loss concentration.

What percentage of net sportsbook revenue comes from the top 1 percent, 5 percent, and 10 percent of losing accounts?

How does that concentration change for in-play betting, parlays, VIP-managed customers, and users who have triggered responsible-gambling indicators?

How many bettors are net losers over one month, six months, one year, and three years?

How much of a state's tax revenue is generated by accounts that are simultaneously carrying revolving credit-card balances, repeatedly using overdraft, or making deposits immediately after payroll?

Some of those questions create legitimate privacy issues. They can still be answered in aggregate under regulatory supervision.

Operators already possess much of the behavioral data.

The absence is not technical.

It is institutional.

A market that can calculate, in milliseconds, the probability that a backup point guard gets his third rebound before the next commercial break can calculate the distribution of customer losses.

The numbers nobody advertises are not unavailable.

They are simply the numbers most capable of changing what the public thinks the product is.
