# Free Money

For the first several years of legal, mobile sports betting, the fastest way to understand what a company like DraftKings actually was, as a business, was not to read its marketing copy but to read the line item on its own income statement labeled "sales and marketing." In 2020, DraftKings spent nearly $500 million on sales and marketing against total revenue of roughly the same order of magnitude, a ratio close to eighty cents of marketing spend for every dollar the company brought in. The ratio did not meaningfully improve for years afterward: in the first quarter of 2022, DraftKings spent $313 million on sales and marketing to generate $417 million in revenue, a marketing-to-revenue ratio of roughly 75 percent, and by the first quarter of 2023 that figure had only eased to around 68 percent. For a normal, mature consumer business, spending numbers like these would signal a company in crisis, hemorrhaging money to stay relevant against competitors. For DraftKings and its principal rival FanDuel through the first half of the 2020s, the numbers signaled something closer to strategy: a deliberate, venture-funded decision to lose money on every customer acquired today in exchange for owning that customer's attention, and their betting habit, for years to come.

The mechanism behind that spending, the thing a viewer of any NFL broadcast during this period would have seen advertised dozens of times a Sunday, was the promotional offer — most commonly structured, by the mid-2020s, as some version of "bet five dollars, get two hundred in bonus bets" or "your first bet is on us, up to a thousand dollars." The appeal to a new customer is straightforward and genuinely was not a scam in the crude sense: a person who deposited a small amount and placed a qualifying wager really did receive additional betting credit, sometimes worth many multiples of their own initial stake, and that credit had genuine value if used. The mechanics underneath the offer, though, were built to convert that appeal into a durable, repeat-betting customer rather than a one-time windfall, and the structure did that work through terms most new users skimmed past on their way to claiming the promotion. Bonus-bet credit is, almost universally in this industry, non-withdrawable in its raw form: a customer cannot simply claim two hundred dollars in bonus bets and cash them out as two hundred dollars of real money. Instead, the credit has to be wagered — often subject to a specific rollover or "playthrough" multiplier requiring the customer to bet the bonus amount, or some multiple of it, before any resulting winnings convert to withdrawable cash — and even winning bonus-bet wagers typically pay out only the profit, with the original bonus stake itself excluded from the payout rather than returned alongside it. A new customer chasing a headline number like "$200 in bonus bets" was, in practice, being handed not two hundred dollars but the obligation to place a further round of real wagers in order to have any chance of converting that credit into money they could actually withdraw — a structure that guarantees additional betting activity as the literal condition of the promotion's value, regardless of whether that additional activity is something the customer would otherwise have chosen to do.

Bonus bets were not the only promotional lever operators pulled, and the second major one, the "odds boost" or "profit boost," deserves separate mention because it targets existing customers rather than new ones, doing for retention what the sign-up offer does for acquisition. An odds boost takes a standard market — a player to record a certain number of receiving yards, say, normally priced at roughly even money — and temporarily inflates the payout, often substantially, usually limited to a single use per customer per day and frequently capped at a modest maximum stake. The inflated odds cost the operator real money on the specific bets that win, but the promotion is cheap in aggregate for a reason that mirrors the same-game-parlay economics described in this book's opening chapter: a boosted price on one leg of a bet still leaves the operator's overall margin, spread across an entire day's betting menu and an entire customer base, comfortably intact, while the boost itself does something a bonus-bet offer cannot — it gives an operator's app a reason to send a push notification to an existing, already-acquired customer on a random Tuesday in November, pulling that customer back into the app to check today's featured boost even on a day with no major game the customer had otherwise planned to bet on. Where the bonus-bet offers described above put new customers in the door, the daily rhythm of odds boosts, delivered directly to a phone's lock screen, is what kept the app open once the free-money phase of any given customer's relationship with the operator had already ended.

The economics of this promotional strategy only make sense against the backdrop of a specific number every operator, investor, and industry analyst tracked obsessively during this period: customer acquisition cost, the total marketing and promotional spend required to bring one new paying, verified user onto the platform. Precise figures varied by company, by state, and by year, and operators were not always eager to publish the number in isolation, but the broader pattern showing up across earnings calls and industry analysis throughout the early 2020s was consistent: acquiring a single new sports-betting customer, once state licensing fees, bonus-bet giveaways, affiliate marketing payouts, and traditional advertising were all accounted for, routinely cost operators well over one hundred dollars per customer, and considerably more in newly launched states where competitors were all racing simultaneously to lock in market share before habits solidified around whichever app a given bettor happened to download first. The logic driving that spending, as later articulated by FanDuel's own finance leadership looking back on the period, was close to explicit: company executives at FanDuel would go on to say that, with the benefit of hindsight, they regretted not spending even more aggressively on customer acquisition during the initial land-grab years, because the long-term value of a customer who becomes a habitual bettor — the industry term is lifetime value — so thoroughly outweighed even a costly upfront acquisition price that underspending, not overspending, turned out to be the more expensive mistake in a market where early-mover brand loyalty proved unusually durable.

The capital that funded this entire strategy has an identifiable, traceable origin, and it is worth tracing because the list of names behind it complicates any story that treats sports betting's promotional war as something imposed on an unwitting sports establishment from the outside. DraftKings raised a modest $1.4 million seed round in early 2012, followed by conventional venture rounds from firms like Atlas Venture, GGV Capital, and Redpoint Ventures through 2013 — the ordinary early trajectory of a Boston tech startup, indistinguishable at that stage from any other daily-fantasy operator. The round that changed the company's trajectory, and that reveals how early the broader sports establishment had begun positioning itself for a legal betting future still three years away, closed in July 2015: a $300 million Series D round in which Fox Sports alone contributed roughly $150 million for an 11 percent stake in the company, joined in the same round by Major League Baseball, the National Hockey League, Major League Soccer, the company that owns Madison Square Garden, and the Kraft Group, the investment vehicle controlled by New England Patriots owner Robert Kraft. Three of the leagues that would spend the next three years as named plaintiffs fighting to preserve PASPA in federal court — MLB and the NHL among them — were, simultaneously, financial investors in the company positioned to benefit most directly from PASPA's repeal, a contradiction this book's fifth chapter examines from the daily-fantasy side and that resurfaces here from the financial one: the same capital relationships that would later fund DraftKings' promotional war for sports-betting customers were assembled years before that war had a legal product to fight over, by investors who included some of the very institutions publicly insisting, in court filings, that legal sports betting posed an unacceptable threat to their sport's integrity.

DraftKings' own financial disclosures make the scale of the resulting bet legible in a way few other consumer internet businesses have ever had to disclose so plainly, because as a publicly traded company since its April 2020 debut, DraftKings has been required to report its losses in exhaustive, audited detail every quarter. The company's own S-1 registration filing, prepared ahead of that public listing, showed net losses of $76 million in 2017, $76 million again in 2018, and $143 million in 2019 — a company that was already losing tens of millions of dollars a year while still operating primarily as a daily-fantasy-sports business, before the far larger capital requirements of a nationwide sportsbook rollout had even begun. Those numbers accelerated sharply once the sports-betting expansion was fully under way: DraftKings' operating losses reached $1.56 billion in 2021 alone, and the company guided investors to expect negative adjusted earnings of up to $925 million for the full 2022 fiscal year, figures that would have been alarming for almost any other consumer business but were treated, by the growth-focused investors who had funded DraftKings' SPAC listing and continued buying its stock through this period, as the expected and even necessary cost of building durable market share in a new industry before state-by-state competition locked in. FanDuel's parent company Flutter Entertainment reported broadly comparable losses on its own American operations across the same stretch, including an EBITDA loss of roughly $552 million attributed to FanDuel across 2021 and 2022 combined — evidence that the promotional arms race was not a DraftKings-specific strategy but an industry-wide response to a competitive environment in which no major operator could afford to spend meaningfully less than its rivals without risking permanent, hard-to-reverse market-share losses.

What makes this spending pattern worth placing in a broader American business context, rather than treating it as a quirk unique to sports betting, is how closely it mirrors a strategy that had already reshaped several other major consumer industries in the preceding decade, funded by an overlapping pool of venture capital and growth-equity investors making a similar underlying bet. Uber, before its 2019 initial public offering, accumulated more than $12 billion in operating losses dating back to 2014, subsidizing rides below their actual cost for years in order to train an entire generation of consumers to open a ride-hailing app by reflex rather than consider a taxi, and to starve slower-moving competitors of the capital needed to survive a price war Uber's investors were willing to fund indefinitely. DoorDash followed a similar playbook in food delivery, reporting a net loss of $667 million in 2019 alone while raising roughly $2.5 billion in venture funding ahead of its own December 2020 public offering, money that subsidized delivery fees and driver payments well below what the underlying unit economics of a single delivery could sustainably support, precisely so that DoorDash could out-loss regional and national competitors until enough of them folded or sold that the remaining players could eventually raise prices without losing customers to a cheaper alternative. Sports betting's promotional war followed the identical structural logic, adapted to a product where the "subsidy" took the form of bonus bets rather than discounted rides or waived delivery fees, and where the eventual payoff for surviving operators was not merely market share in the abstract but a specific, durable base of bettors whose behavioral patterns — which sportsbook app they open first, which one already has their banking details saved, which one they associate with the promotional windfall that got them started — the industry's own research consistently shows are remarkably sticky once established, converting an expensive acquisition cost paid once into years of recurring betting activity paid for entirely out of the customer's own pocket thereafter.

The unusual feature of the sports-betting version of this strategy, compared with Uber's or DoorDash's, is what the subsidized product actually is. A venture-funded ride-hailing subsidy makes a real, useful service — transportation — artificially cheap for a period of years, a wealth transfer from investors to consumers that, whatever its other effects on the taxi industry or on driver pay, at least delivers the literal thing being subsidized: a ride, at a price below its true cost. A sports-betting promotional offer subsidizes something structurally different: not the cost of a genuinely useful service delivered below cost, but the customer's own initial exposure to a product whose entire business model, as chapter three explained and chapter seven will return to, is built around the mathematical certainty that the house wins slightly more than it loses in aggregate over any sufficiently large number of bets. Uber's investors were betting that enough subsidized riders would eventually become full-price riders once competitors were driven out and prices normalized. DraftKings' and FanDuel's investors were betting that enough bonus-bet recipients would eventually become customers wagering their own money, at odds mathematically engineered to keep somewhat more of it than they get back, often enough and in high enough volume that the operator's built-in edge, compounded across millions of individual wagers, would recoup the acquisition cost many times over. Both bets required losing money upfront to build a customer base. Only one of them required that customer base to keep losing money, by design, indefinitely, for the underlying business model to work at all once the promotional subsidies stopped.

The subsidies did, eventually, start to stop, and the endpoint of that unwind is itself instructive about how the entire era should be understood in retrospect. DraftKings reported full-year 2025 revenue of $6.05 billion, up from $4.77 billion the year before, and posted net income of $3.7 million for the year — a modest figure in absolute terms, but the company's first full year of profitability since its 2012 founding, a milestone thirteen years and, by the company's own cumulative disclosures, well over a billion dollars in accumulated losses in the making. Fourth-quarter 2025 results showed the shape of what a maturing version of this business now looks like: revenue up 43 percent year over year to nearly $2 billion for the quarter alone, with adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization of $343 million, roughly four times the prior year's comparable figure, as marketing spend as a share of revenue continued the steady decline that had been under way since the early-2020s peak. Not every operator chased customer acquisition through television advertising and bonus bets alone; some bought the audience outright. Penn National Gaming, a regional casino operator without a nationally recognized consumer brand of its own, completed the acquisition of a 36 percent stake in Barstool Sports — the irreverent, sports-obsessed digital media company built around founder Dave Portnoy's large and highly engaged young male following — for approximately $163 million in February 2020, explicitly framing the deal as a customer-acquisition strategy rather than a media investment: Penn's own announcement cited reductions in customer acquisition and promotional costs as a direct benefit of gaining exclusive rights to attach the Barstool name and audience to its sportsbook product. Penn expanded its stake toward majority ownership over the following three years before ultimately paying $388 million in February 2023 to buy out the remaining interest and take full control of Barstool outright — only to sell the Barstool brand back to Portnoy for a nominal sum later that same year, having already secured a different, larger media partnership it judged more valuable: a ten-year deal in which Penn agreed to pay ESPN $1.5 billion for the rights to operate a sportsbook under the ESPN Bet name, wagering that the audience attached to the most recognized brand in American sports media would prove a more durable acquisition engine than Barstool's smaller, if more devoted, following had been. Beneath these headline media deals, a much larger and less visible ecosystem of affiliate marketing did similar work at smaller scale: sports-content websites, individual handicappers, and betting-focused publishers across the internet earned commissions from operators, typically structured as either a flat fee for every new depositing customer they referred or a percentage of that customer's ongoing losses over time, an arrangement that gave a large and diffuse network of independent media publishers a direct financial stake in how many readers they could convert into betting customers, and in how much those customers ultimately lost.

Not every regulator watched this promotional language pass unchallenged, and the pushback that did occur is worth recording as a genuine, if partial, corrective rather than omitting it in the interest of a cleaner narrative. Ohio's Casino Control Commission, reviewing operator advertising after the state's own legal market launched on January 1, 2023, issued formal notices of violation that January to BetMGM, Caesars, and DraftKings, alleging that each company had advertised promotions as "free" or "risk-free" when a bettor was, in fact, required to risk their own money or accept a real loss in order to obtain the promised reward — precisely the gap between marketing language and bonus-bet mechanics this chapter has already described. The commission also cited operators for advertising that failed to include legally required problem-gambling messaging clearly and conspicuously, and for mailing promotional materials to addresses associated with people under Ohio's legal betting age of twenty-one. The settlements that followed in February 2023 carried real financial consequences rather than merely symbolic ones: DraftKings agreed to pay $500,000, Barstool Sportsbook $250,000, and the state's casino regulator went on to collect roughly $1.3 million in total advertising-related penalties from operators in the eighteen months or so after Ohio's market opened — a small fraction of any operator's marketing budget, but a rare instance of a state regulator using its licensing leverage to force specific, verifiable changes to how sports-betting promotions could be worded, rather than leaving the industry to police its own advertising claims.

DraftKings' own path to the public capital markets that eventually funded so much of this spending took a route that was, in 2020, still an unusual one for a company of its size and visibility: rather than pursuing a traditional initial public offering, with its accompanying roadshow and underwriter-driven pricing process, DraftKings went public on April 24, 2020, through a reverse merger with a special-purpose acquisition company, combining simultaneously with the sports-betting technology provider SBTech in a three-way transaction that let all three entities begin trading as a single public company almost overnight. SPAC mergers, which exploded in popularity across the broader stock market in 2020 and 2021 before falling sharply out of favor once regulators and investors grew warier of the structure's looser disclosure requirements, offered DraftKings a faster and, at the time, less scrutinized path to the public listing it needed to keep funding a promotional strategy that traditional private venture capital alone could not have sustained indefinitely. The timing mattered as much as the mechanism: DraftKings' SPAC listing closed just as the first full wave of post-Murphy state legalizations was accelerating, giving the company access to public equity markets — and the ability to raise additional capital through follow-on stock offerings whenever its cash position required it — at exactly the moment its marketing spending was about to climb into the hundreds of millions, then billions, of dollars a year.

The transition from an era of nine-figure quarterly losses to modest, then rapidly expanding, profitability marks the effective end of the free-money phase this chapter describes: the promotional offers did not disappear, and new-state launches still bring aggressive bonus-bet blitzes to this day, but the industry-wide, blank-check subsidization of essentially every new customer's first several months of betting activity — the period when DraftKings and FanDuel were, in effect, paying people to develop a habit — had by 2025 given way to a more selective, more targeted, and ultimately more calculated version of the same underlying strategy, one this book's ninth chapter examines closely: rather than subsidizing every new customer indiscriminately, operators increasingly directed their most generous promotional spending toward the specific customers whose betting patterns marked them, in the industry's own internal terminology, as its most valuable.
