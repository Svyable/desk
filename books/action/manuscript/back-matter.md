# Back Matter

The easiest ending would be a verdict.

Legal sports betting was good. Legal sports betting was bad. The Supreme Court opened the door. The states rushed through it. The companies exploited everyone. The companies merely served demand that already existed.

None of those sentences survives the evidence intact.

The legal market solved real problems. It moved a large amount of wagering into systems with identity checks, geolocation, transaction records, regulator access and integrity monitoring. It created tax revenue where illegal betting created none. It gave leagues, operators and law enforcement better visibility into suspicious wagering patterns. A licensed book can freeze an account, produce records and answer a subpoena. An offshore site may do none of those things.

The legal market also changed the object being regulated. This is the part the first wave of legislation underestimated. A bookmaker taking bets before kickoff and a mobile platform continuously generating live markets are both called sportsbooks, but they are not behaviorally equivalent products. Same-game parlays, microbetting, automated cash-out offers, personalized promotions, VIP segmentation and push reactivation alter frequency, session structure and the economic value of a customer. Regulation that controls only licensing and tax rates misses the layer where much of the modern market’s power lives.

That is the book’s final claim: **sports-betting policy has to regulate the product that exists, not the noun lawmakers legalized.**

## What the evidence currently supports

The strongest evidence in this manuscript supports several statements with different confidence levels.

**High confidence:** the post-2018 legal market grew rapidly; mobile wagering became dominant; operator economics increasingly favor higher-hold products such as parlays; leagues and sportsbooks developed deep commercial relationships around data and advertising; legal operators possess far more behavioral data about customers than old retail sportsbooks did; state rules remain materially fragmented.

**Substantial but still developing evidence:** legal online sports betting changes household financial behavior at least for some groups; adverse effects appear concentrated among financially constrained or heavy-betting households; treatment and helpline systems have seen changes in volume and caller demographics during the legalization era; faster and more personalized product design can increase engagement and impulsive use.

**Important but unresolved:** the exact national increase in gambling-disorder prevalence attributable specifically to sports-betting legalization; how much of measured financial distress represents substitution from illegal gambling versus genuinely new exposure; which interventions reduce harm without pushing users toward offshore markets; how effectively operator risk models can distinguish wealthy high-volume recreational bettors from customers losing control.

The distinction between those categories should remain visible after publication. Certainty is not improved by flattening the evidence.

## The measurements regulators should require

If the next generation of regulation is serious, it needs better denominators.

States already report handle, revenue and tax. Those measures tell governments how large the market is. They do not tell governments how risk is distributed inside it.

A more useful public reporting regime would include, at minimum:

- distribution of net losses by customer percentile, reported in privacy-preserving aggregate form;
- share of revenue generated by the highest-spending 1, 5 and 10 percent of customers;
- number and rate of voluntary deposit-limit activations;
- number and rate of cooling-off periods and self-exclusions;
- operator-initiated risky-play interventions, with follow-up outcomes;
- promotional contacts sent to customers who previously used harm-reduction tools;
- VIP enrollment and removal rates;
- migration between operators after exclusion or intervention;
- average wager frequency and session duration by product class;
- separate hold and loss distributions for straight bets, parlays, same-game parlays and live micro-markets;
- links, where legally and ethically possible, to independent household-finance and treatment-demand datasets.

The industry already measures much of the upstream behavior for commercial reasons. The missing step is making enough of the aggregate system visible that researchers can test whether claimed safeguards work.

## Falsification agenda

Several conclusions in this book should be abandoned or narrowed if future evidence contradicts them.

**1. Product-speed hypothesis.** If high-frequency in-play and microbetting products do not produce meaningfully different risky-use patterns after controlling for customer selection, the argument that product cadence is itself a major harm variable weakens.

**2. Concentration hypothesis.** If independent data show sportsbook revenue is broadly distributed across recreational bettors rather than concentrated in high-volume customers, the concern that operator incentives systematically conflict with harm reduction at the top end should be revised.

**3. Household-finance hypothesis.** If stronger longitudinal work fails to reproduce savings, credit, collections or bankruptcy effects after legalization, those claims should be treated as artifacts of early study designs rather than settled findings.

**4. Regulatory-friction hypothesis.** If stricter affordability checks, marketing limits or centralized self-exclusion consistently push substantial numbers of users into less safe illegal markets without reducing total harm, the policy case for those interventions changes.

**5. Detection hypothesis.** If operator risk models cannot reliably distinguish dangerous escalation from ordinary high-value play without unacceptable false positives, expectations for automated intervention need to be lower and more human review will be necessary.

A policy book that cannot say what would change its mind is only advocacy with footnotes.

## A better market is a testable proposition

The phrase **responsible gambling** has become too easy for everyone involved.

Operators can use it as a marketing category. Regulators can point to required links and voluntary limits. States can earmark a fraction of tax revenue for treatment. Bettors can be reminded to play responsibly while the product continues optimizing for engagement.

A defensible market would make the phrase measurable.

Did the intervention reduce losses among at-risk customers? Did it reduce reactivation after self-exclusion? Did a new product materially increase session frequency? Did a marketing restriction reduce risky play without moving customers offshore? Did centralized exclusion work across operators? Did VIP-host policies catch deterioration earlier? Did customers understand the actual hold of the products they used?

Those are empirical questions.

The most interesting future sportsbook is therefore not the one with the most restrictions or the fewest. It is the one whose safety claims can survive external measurement.

That would be a profound change in posture. The first legalization wave asked operators to prove they could run a fair game, hold customer funds, identify users, comply with location rules and pay taxes. The next wave should ask them to prove that the design choices generating the most revenue are not invisible to the public institutions licensing them.

There is no reason a legal market cannot answer that challenge.

The technology is already there.

So is the data.

What remains is deciding who gets to see enough of it to know what the action costs.
