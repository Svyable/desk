# When the Line Blurs Into a Market

Every legal sportsbook examined in this book's earlier chapters exists because a state government decided to let it exist. A DraftKings license in New Jersey, a FanDuel license in New York, a BetMGM license in Michigan — each one is a state-issued permission slip, backed by a state licensing fee, subject to a state tax rate chapter 15 showed can range from 6.75 to 51 percent, and revocable by a state gaming control board if the operator breaks the state's rules. That is the entire regulatory architecture this book has described, from Nevada's 1949 head start through the fifty-state patchwork Murphy v. NCAA set in motion in 2018. In January 2025, a company called Kalshi began offering a product that let a customer put money on the outcome of an NFL game, structured not as a bet regulated by any state gaming commission, but as a financial derivative regulated by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission — a federal agency whose statutory job, since its creation in 1974, has been to police the market for commodity futures and swaps, not to police gambling, a category the CFTC's own governing statute explicitly excludes it from regulating. Whether that distinction is real or merely semantic is now the subject of an active, unresolved fight playing out simultaneously in at least three federal circuit courts, and this chapter's job is to describe that fight as precisely as the available record allows — including, where the record does not yet allow precision, saying so plainly, because this is the single fastest-moving legal question in this entire book and any claim of false certainty about it would go stale within months of publication.

The product itself is simple to describe even though the legal fight over it is not. Kalshi calls what it sells an event contract: a customer pays a price for a contract that resolves to one dollar if a specified event happens and zero dollars if it doesn't, with the price itself moving up and down before resolution based on what other traders are willing to pay — mechanically identical, in almost every respect a customer would actually experience, to buying a "yes" side of a proposition bet at a sportsbook, except that Kalshi calls the transaction a trade rather than a wager, calls its customers traders rather than bettors, and operates under a federal designation as a contract market rather than a state gaming license. Kalshi's own event-contract catalog, since its sports expansion began, has included contracts on which NFL team wins a given game, whether a specific player will hit a statistical threshold, and other markets that would be instantly recognizable to anyone who has placed a same-game parlay through the apps chapter 8 of this book examined in detail. Polymarket, a rival platform that built its early reputation on political and cultural-event contracts rather than sports, took a more circuitous path back into direct competition: after exiting the U.S. market in 2022 and paying a $1.4 million fine to settle CFTC allegations that it had operated an unregistered derivatives platform, Polymarket spent $112 million in July 2025 to acquire QCX, a small but already CFTC-licensed derivatives exchange and clearinghouse, and used that acquisition as its route back into legal U.S. operation — the CFTC granted the resulting entity, QCX LLC, an amended order of designation in November 2025, and by early 2026 Polymarket was again offering event contracts to American customers, this time as a fully registered exchange rather than an unregistered offshore platform.

The core legal question both companies are litigating, in parallel, in front of different courts, is whether a sports-outcome contract is a swap under the Commodity Exchange Act — the federal statute that gives the CFTC its jurisdiction — or whether it is, regardless of its financial-derivative packaging, a bet under state gambling law, which every state but Nevada's pre-2018 monopoly has always regulated as a matter of core state police power rather than federal commerce authority. If it is a swap, the Commodity Exchange Act's preemption provisions arguably bar any state from applying its own gaming law to it at all, and a federally registered exchange could offer sports contracts nationwide without seeking a single state license, collecting no state tax, and answering to no state gaming regulator — the entire fifty-state architecture chapters 15 and 16 of this book described, simply routed around. If it is a bet, then Kalshi and Polymarket are two more sportsbooks that happen to have found a novel federal designation to hide behind, and every state that has spent since 2018 building its own licensing, taxation, and consumer-protection regime around sports wagering has a legitimate claim to regulate them exactly as it regulates DraftKings and FanDuel.

Kalshi was the company that opened this fight, but it was never the only one fighting it, and the roster of companies now offering sports-outcome contracts under the same federal theory has grown considerably since January 2025. Robinhood, the retail trading app best known for stocks and cryptocurrency, launched its own event-contract offering through a subsidiary called Robinhood Derivatives, LLC, structured on the identical CFTC-registration theory Kalshi relies on; Crypto.com built a comparable product on its own regulated derivatives arm. By December 2025, Connecticut's Department of Consumer Protection had issued a joint cease-and-desist order against all three companies at once, accusing each of conducting unlicensed sports wagering under the state's gambling statute regardless of the CFTC registration each cited in its defense — Robinhood's public response was to state plainly that its event contracts were federally regulated through a CFTC-registered entity and therefore, in the company's view, outside Connecticut's reach entirely, while Kalshi responded to Connecticut the way it had responded to Nevada and New Jersey: by suing the state in federal court seeking an injunction. Connecticut was not an outlier state acting alone. By the time of that order, Maryland had become the sixth state, after New Jersey, Nevada, Ohio, Illinois, and Montana, to send some version of the same cease-and-desist letter to one or more of these companies, with Arizona issuing its own shortly after — a rolling, state-by-state confrontation that, by the end of 2025, had drawn in most of the same states chapter 15 showed taking the most aggressive tax and licensing postures toward the conventional sportsbook industry, now aiming the identical enforcement posture at a set of companies that had never applied for a state license at all.

The federal side of the fight has its own internal politics, separate from the courts, and they matter because a change in CFTC leadership can reshape the agency's posture faster than any circuit court can rule. Caroline Pham, appointed acting chair of the CFTC in early 2025, staked out a position that tried to split the difference this chapter's court cases have since made much harder to split: the CFTC, she argued publicly, does have a legitimate role regulating event contracts as a market regulator, but the agency should be careful not to encroach on the states' own prerogative to regulate gambling as gambling — a stance that satisfied neither side fully, drawing a bipartisan warning letter that September from Senators Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada and John Curtis of Utah, joined by four colleagues, reminding the acting chair that nationwide sports contracts should not be allowed to supersede state and tribal gaming law. By January 2026, CFTC leadership had changed again: incoming Chair Michael Selig announced the agency would move forward with formal rulemaking on prediction markets, a process that could eventually produce the kind of clear federal standard the courts, split as they are, have not — though as of this book's research that rulemaking remained in its early stages, with no proposed rule yet published, meaning the fight's fastest-moving track by late 2026 was still the litigation rather than the agency process nominally meant to settle it.

Several states moved fast once Kalshi's sports contracts went live in early 2025, sending cease-and-desist letters that treated the company as an unlicensed sportsbook rather than a novel financial product — Nevada, New Jersey, and Maryland among the first, followed by others including Ohio. Kalshi's response was to go to federal court seeking injunctions against enforcement in each state, arguing the Commodity Exchange Act preempted the state actions entirely, and for the first several months of the fight, that strategy worked: federal district courts in both Nevada and New Jersey initially sided with Kalshi, finding, under a field-preemption theory rooted in the Supremacy Clause, that Congress's comprehensive federal scheme for regulating commodity derivatives left no room for a state to impose its own gambling law on a CFTC-registered product, whatever the product actually resembled in practice. That early run of favorable district-court rulings did not survive contact with the appellate courts, and the two appellate rulings that have now come down point in flatly opposite directions.

The Third Circuit ruled first, and it ruled for Kalshi. In KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty, decided April 6, 2026, a divided three-judge panel — Judge David Porter writing for the majority, joined by Chief Judge Michael Chagares, with one judge dissenting — affirmed a preliminary injunction barring New Jersey from enforcing its gambling laws against Kalshi's sports-related event contracts. The majority's reasoning tracked the field- and conflict-preemption theory Kalshi had argued from the start: Kalshi's contracts, the panel held, likely qualify as swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act, and a state cannot use its own gambling statute to override a federal regulatory scheme Congress built specifically to occupy this space, even when the underlying product looks, to an ordinary customer, exactly like a bet. The Third Circuit was the first federal appellate court to rule on the question at all, and legal commentary at the time noted it was likely to set the tone — or at least the opening argument — for the parallel cases already working their way through the Fourth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits.

It did not set the tone for the Ninth Circuit, which ruled the opposite way less than five months later. In KalshiEX, LLC v. Assad, decided August 28, 2026, a unanimous three-judge Ninth Circuit panel sided with Nevada, rejecting Kalshi's preemption argument and holding that Kalshi had failed to show a likelihood of success on the claim that the Commodity Exchange Act preempts Nevada's gaming laws as applied to sports-related event contracts. The panel's reasoning treated the product's real-world function — a customer risking money on a sports outcome, in a market Nevada has regulated as gambling since 1949 — as the operative fact, rather than the federal label Kalshi's CFTC registration placed on top of it. The Nevada Gaming Control Board, joined in its defense by problem-gambling advocacy organizations that filed supporting briefs in March 2026, had argued throughout the litigation that allowing a federally registered exchange to offer functionally identical products outside the entire state licensing, taxation, and consumer-protection framework chapters 15 through 17 of this book have spent four chapters describing would create exactly the kind of two-tiered market this book's own thesis has tried to avoid oversimplifying: a fully regulated tier operating under fifty different sets of state rules, and an unregulated federal tier, subject only to the CFTC's derivatives-market rules, that answers to none of them — no state self-exclusion list, no state advertising restriction, no state minimum age tied to a gaming license, no state tax revenue at all.

Two circuits, ruling within five months of each other on closely related facts, reached opposite conclusions — the Third Circuit finding federal preemption likely, the Ninth Circuit finding it unlikely — which is as clean a circuit split as American appellate law produces, and it did exactly what circuit splits are designed to do: it moved the fight toward the Supreme Court. In early September 2026, New Jersey's attorney general petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court to resolve the conflict, asking the Court to step into what state officials characterized as a nationwide fight over whether prediction-market platforms must follow the same gambling laws that bind every other operator in the sports betting industry. As of this book's research, that petition remains pending; the Supreme Court has not yet decided whether to grant certiorari, and this book makes no prediction about the outcome, because none is available to make responsibly. What is verifiable, and worth stating precisely, is the shape of the unresolved question itself: as of September 2026, whether a sports event contract is a federally preempted swap or a state-regulable bet is settled law in the Third Circuit (yes, preempted), unsettled the opposite way in the Ninth Circuit (no, not preempted), and genuinely open everywhere else in the country, including in the Fourth and Sixth Circuits, where parallel litigation was still working through the district courts at the time of this book's research.

The tribal gaming fight running alongside the state fight adds a second, distinct legal front that the state-versus-CFTC framing alone does not capture, and it deserves its own explanation because the underlying law is different. Tribal nations that operate gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act hold a legal exclusivity most state-licensed commercial operators do not: under compacts negotiated with their host states, many tribes have the sole legal right to offer certain forms of gambling, including in some states sports betting itself, within tribal jurisdiction — the same exclusivity chapter 15 showed driving Minnesota's stalled legislative fight and shaping California's rejected 2022 ballot measures. If a federally registered exchange can offer sports contracts on tribal land without seeking the tribe's consent, on the theory that the contracts are a federally regulated financial product rather than gambling subject to IGRA, that exclusivity is effectively void wherever a phone with a Kalshi or Polymarket account can get a signal. Multiple tribal nations have sued on exactly that theory. The Ho-Chunk Nation in Wisconsin sued Kalshi in 2025, and a federal judge found the tribe had shown a likelihood of success on its claim that Kalshi's operation on tribal land violated federal Indian gaming law — a notably different outcome, on a related but legally distinct question, than either of the state-versus-CFTC circuit rulings. Three California tribes filed a comparable suit in 2025 arguing Kalshi's sports-linked contracts function as unlicensed sports betting accessible on tribal lands in violation of IGRA. And in May 2026, four New Mexico tribal governments — the Pojoaque, Sandia, and Isleta pueblos and the Mescalero Apache Tribe — filed their own suit making the same core argument; Kalshi moved to dismiss, the case proceeded to a bench trial, and by September 4, 2026, the tribes were presenting final arguments urging the court to bar Kalshi from operating within tribal boundaries altogether, a ruling that remained pending as this book went to press. None of these tribal cases turns on the swap-versus-bet question the state cases are litigating; they turn instead on whether a company operating without a tribal gaming compact can lawfully reach customers physically located on tribal land at all, a question IGRA answers more directly than the Commodity Exchange Act does, and it is one more front on which Kalshi is defending the same underlying business model using a different legal argument in front of a different kind of court.

It is worth being precise about what actually changes for the person placing the bet, because the legal argument can make the distinction sound more abstract than it feels on a phone screen. A customer opening a Kalshi or Robinhood event-contract app sees a continuously updating price rather than American-style odds, pays an explicit per-contract fee rather than a built-in vig, and technically owns a tradeable position that can be sold back to the market before the game ends rather than a settled wager that simply wins or loses — differences real enough that this book takes no position on whether the underlying product is, item for item, more or less consumer-friendly than a conventional same-game parlay. What has not changed, on the evidence available so far, is the underlying behavior the product invites: a contract on whether a specific player records an interception in the third quarter is, to the person deciding whether to buy it, a bet on that exact same proposition, dressed in exchange-trading language rather than sportsbook language, and nothing in the CFTC's market-structure rules asks the question a state gaming commission is statutorily built to ask — whether the contract's design, its price movement, its in-game re-tradability, was built to maximize engagement among exactly the bettors least equipped to absorb the losses, the question chapters 8 through 10 of this book spent three full chapters answering about the licensed sportsbook industry. A regulatory fight over whether the CFTC or fifty state gaming commissions has jurisdiction is not, by itself, a fight about whether anyone is asking that second question at all — and so far, on the sports contracts specifically, the honest answer is that no regulator, state or federal, has yet forced Kalshi, Robinhood, or Crypto.com to answer it in the way New Jersey's Division of Gaming Enforcement or Nevada's Gaming Control Board routinely requires of a licensed sportsbook.

It is worth pausing on why this fight matters beyond its own considerable legal complexity, because the stakes are not merely definitional. Every consumer protection this book's earlier chapters have found inadequate in the regulated state market — Nevada's own missing statewide casino self-exclusion law, chapter 17 showed, is a real gap even inside the system this chapter has called comparatively strong — is, on Kalshi's own reading of its federal designation, simply inapplicable to its platform: no state self-exclusion list currently reaches a Kalshi account, because Kalshi maintains its position is not subject to state gaming law at all. No state minimum-age enforcement tied to a gaming license applies to it in the states where its preemption argument has prevailed, though Kalshi's own terms of service impose an 18-plus age requirement voluntarily rather than as a matter of state-enforced law. No state tax revenue flows from a Kalshi sports contract the way it flows, at rates chapter 15 detailed running from 6.75 to 51 percent, from every dollar wagered through a licensed sportsbook. And critically, none of the behavioral-design scrutiny this book's middle chapters applied to same-game parlays and in-play micro-betting has been subjected to any CFTC oversight process built for that purpose, because the CFTC's statutory mission has never been consumer protection against compulsive financial-product use in the way a state gaming commission's mission explicitly is — the CFTC was built to police market manipulation and ensure orderly derivatives trading among sophisticated participants, not to ask whether a specific contract design is engineered to maximize how often an eighteen-year-old college student places a bet on his phone during a commercial break, the exact question chapter 10 of this book spent an entire chapter answering about the licensed sportsbook industry.

Whether Kalshi and Polymarket ultimately win their preemption argument at the Supreme Court, lose it, or see the current circuit split simply persist unresolved for years — a genuine possibility, since the Court is not obligated to grant New Jersey's petition and circuit splits on federal preemption questions have sometimes lingered for a decade or more before resolution — the underlying commercial logic driving both companies is not going away regardless of how the litigation ends. A regulatory environment where one path to offering sports contracts costs a state license, a state tax rate as high as 51 percent, and compliance with a fifty-state patchwork of self-exclusion and advertising rules, while a second path costs a single federal registration and none of the above, is an environment that will keep producing companies willing to bet — in the most literal sense available to a business strategist — that the second path is cheaper and faster than the first, regardless of how many state cease-and-desist letters or tribal lawsuits they have to fight along the way. This book takes no position on how the Supreme Court should resolve the Third Circuit and Ninth Circuit's disagreement; that is a genuine, close legal question this book's own research has not resolved and should not pretend to. What this book can say with confidence is that the fight itself is the clearest evidence yet that the state-by-state architecture chapters 15 and 16 described — imperfect, uneven, built without federal coordination — was never as stable as it looked, because it was always vulnerable to exactly this kind of jurisdictional arbitrage: a company that looked at fifty state gambling regulators and one federal derivatives regulator, and found the federal one both cheaper to satisfy and, so far, in at least one circuit, legally sufficient on its own.
