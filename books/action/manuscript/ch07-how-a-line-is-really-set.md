# How a Line Is Really Set

A bettor opening a sportsbook app on a Sunday morning sees a number — New England minus three and a half, or the Lakers moneyline at minus one hundred sixty — presented with the flat, unexplained authority of a price tag on a grocery-store shelf. The number appears to come from nowhere in particular, which is by design: a modern retail sportsbook wants a customer to trust the line the way a shopper trusts a listed price, without wondering who set it, why, or whether it might be wrong. In practice, almost none of the major regulated operators an American bettor is likely to use — DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM, Caesars — are setting that opening number from scratch, in the way Roxy Roxborough's team once did for Nevada's books in the 1980s. They are, for the overwhelming majority of markets, copying and lightly adjusting a line that originated somewhere else, at one of a small number of operators the rest of the industry still treats as the actual source of price discovery.

Circa Sports, the Las Vegas book built specifically to court professional bettors rather than exclude them, has become one of the clearest examples of where an original number still gets made. For college football, Circa posts the first line of the week — every game on the board, all at once, every Sunday during the season — a practice the book maintains publicly as a point of pride and a marketing draw for the sharp bettors it wants walking through its doors, since being first means absorbing the earliest and most informationally dangerous action before any other book has had a chance to see how the market reacts. For NFL markets and player props specifically, Circa's numbers frequently lead the broader regulated market as well, with other operators watching where Circa's line settles before finalizing their own. Pinnacle, the Curaçao-licensed operator built almost entirely around a "winners welcome" philosophy this chapter returns to, plays a similar role internationally, and Westgate SuperBook maintains its own reputation as a serious, sharp-focused Nevada oddsmaking operation. What these three operators share, and what separates them from the recreational-facing apps most Americans actually use to place a bet, is a business model built on being right rather than a business model built on managing a large, mostly unsophisticated customer base — a distinction with consequences this chapter spends most of its length unpacking.

The mechanics of how a number actually gets built, before any bettor sees it, generally begins with a quantitative model — statistical projections built from historical performance, player-level data, injury reports, weather, and whatever proprietary adjustments a given book's trading team has developed over years of watching its own results — that produces an initial, computer-generated projection of a fair line: the number at which, in the model's estimation, a bet on either side would be a roughly equal proposition before the vig is applied. A human trader or a small trading team then reviews that computer output, adjusts for factors the model may not fully capture — a starting quarterback's status still genuinely uncertain an hour before a Sunday deadline, a public perception the trader expects to distort betting volume in a predictable direction, a market-specific quirk the model has historically mispriced — and posts the number publicly. From that moment, the line stops being a pure statistical estimate and starts being a live, responsive price, adjusting continuously in response to the single input that actually matters to a sportsbook's own profitability: where the money is landing.

That responsiveness is where the industry's own internal vocabulary — sharp money, square money, steam, reverse line movement — earns its keep, and understanding those terms precisely is the difference between watching a betting line as spectacle and understanding it as a live market. "Sharp" bettors, sometimes still called wise guys in an unbroken line of terminology from Billy Walters's era, are professional or semi-professional players whose historical betting patterns show a consistent, statistically significant ability to beat the closing line — to have identified, more often than chance would predict, that a book's posted number was mispriced relative to the true probability of the outcome. "Square" money, by contrast, describes the volume of bets placed by recreational bettors, whose wagers tend to cluster predictably around popular teams, recognizable star players, and round, appealing numbers, driven by fandom, media narratives, and simple availability bias rather than any edge over the market. A single sharp bettor's wager might be a fraction of the total dollar volume a popular team draws from thousands of casual bettors on a given Sunday, but because a book's trading team has learned, often through painful and expensive experience, which betting patterns correlate with sharp money and which correlate with square money, a comparatively small, well-timed sharp bet can move a line more than a much larger volume of scattered public action — a phenomenon that produces what the industry calls a "steam move," a rapid, uniform shift in a line across multiple books within minutes, signaling that professional money has concentrated on one side of a market with enough conviction that books are adjusting defensively rather than waiting to see how the rest of the day's action develops.

Reverse line movement is the clearest publicly visible signature of this dynamic, and it is worth walking through because it is the single most useful piece of evidence a careful bettor, or a careful reader of this book, can use to distinguish a market genuinely being shaped by informed money from one simply drifting with public sentiment. In an ordinary market, a line moves toward whichever side is attracting more bets, because a book adjusts its price to try to balance action and protect its own vig-based margin regardless of the actual outcome — more bets on the favorite typically pushes the spread higher, making that favorite a slightly less attractive bet going forward and the underdog a slightly more attractive one, nudging the two sides back toward balance. Reverse line movement is the exception that reveals something the raw bet-count data alone would hide: a line moving in the opposite direction from where the majority of individual bets are landing, which happens when a comparatively small number of very large, sharp wagers on the less popular side outweigh, in actual dollar terms, a much larger number of small public bets on the popular side. When a book sees eighty percent of its ticket count on one team but nonetheless moves its line toward the other team, that gap is a visible signal — to any bettor paying attention, not merely to the book's own trading desk — that the money behind the unpopular side is coming from bettors the book itself takes seriously enough to react to.

That last point is the pivot on which this entire chapter's central tension turns: a sportsbook's willingness to take a bet seriously enough to move its line is not the same as its willingness to keep accepting that bettor's money indefinitely, and the industry's actual practice of limiting or outright banning winning customers is one of the most persistently documented, and persistently contested, features of the modern regulated market. Reporting from outlets including the Washington Post and ESPN has described a broadly consistent pattern across the largest American operators — Bally's, BetMGM, Caesars, DraftKings, Fanatics, FanDuel, and Penn among them — of restricting how much money a bettor identified as sharp is permitted to wager, sometimes reducing an account's betting limits from thousands of dollars per wager down to a few dollars, effectively making it impossible for a flagged customer to bet in any meaningful size even though their account technically remains open. NBA player and prediction-market investor Kyle Kuzma became one of the more prominent public critics of the practice in 2026, accusing DraftKings specifically of removing customers from the platform "for winning" while, in his characterization, steering casual bettors toward the highest-margin parlay products the operator offers — a criticism that captures, in a single public complaint, the central asymmetry this book's earlier chapters have already described: an industry that markets itself around the appealing possibility of winning while operating a business model that depends on a specific, identifiable class of winning customers not being allowed to keep winning at scale.

The operators themselves do not deny that limiting happens; they dispute the characterization of why it happens, and their defense deserves to be stated in full rather than dismissed, because it rests on a genuine business logic rather than mere evasion. Sportsbook representatives interviewed by ESPN and other outlets have consistently argued that limits are not imposed simply because a customer has won money, but because of specific, identifiable behaviors: exploiting a "mistake line" — a price a trading team posted in error, perhaps failing to account for a late injury update, that a sharp bettor's faster information flow allows them to hammer before the book can correct it — or systematically abusing promotional offers in ways the terms were never intended to permit, or simply possessing, through proprietary modeling or faster data feeds, what operators describe as a better model or more information than the book's own trading team, a gap a fixed-limit retail book has limited tools to close except by reducing how much that specific customer can wager. From a sportsbook's perspective, the distinction matters enormously: a casual bettor who wins a few times through luck poses no threat to the book's long-run mathematical edge, since the underlying vig ensures the book profits from the aggregate of thousands of similar customers regardless of any single customer's short-term results, while a bettor with a demonstrated, persistent statistical edge poses a genuine and compounding threat, because that edge does not average out the way variance does — it recurs, bet after bet, for as long as the book keeps accepting the action. A consultant specializing in gambling policy, quoted in coverage of the practice, put the resulting tension in blunt consumer-facing terms: if a customer wins consistently enough, that customer effectively cannot keep playing, a reality that sits uneasily beside advertising that promises anyone the chance to win.

This is precisely the dynamic Pinnacle has built its entire brand identity around inverting, and the contrast is instructive because it demonstrates that limiting winners is a business choice rather than an unavoidable feature of sports betting itself. Pinnacle's own public educational material addresses the question directly, explaining that most retail sportsbooks restrict winning bettors because those books depend on a comparatively small number of popular markets and a customer base whose betting patterns skew heavily recreational, making a single sharp, well-informed bettor a disproportionate threat to a narrow, high-margin business. Pinnacle's low-margin model — typically citing margins in the range of two to three percent across most of its markets, compared with an industry average closer to five to seven percent and some recreational-focused operators pricing markets with a ten percent hold or higher — runs on a different logic entirely: welcoming sharp action, rather than suppressing it, because professional bettors' willingness to bet both sides of Pinnacle's lines at volume is precisely the mechanism that keeps those lines accurate, and accurate lines, priced thin enough to be genuinely competitive, are what Pinnacle depends on to attract the very high volume its low-margin model needs to be profitable at all. A market-maker, in other words, treats a sharp bettor as a source of pricing information worth paying for in the form of a thinner margin; a recreational-facing retail book treats the same bettor as a liability to be contained.

The discipline described so far — a model producing a projection, a trader refining it, a market absorbing money and adjusting in response — holds up reasonably well for a game's core markets: the point spread, the total, the moneyline, the small number of prices that determine the outcome most bettors actually care about and that draw enough betting volume, across enough of a book's customer base, to generate a genuinely useful signal about where the true number probably sits. That discipline strains, and in places simply breaks down, once a modern sportsbook's menu expands into the hundreds or even thousands of secondary markets a same-game parlay product needs to exist at all: whether a specific wide receiver records more or fewer than four and a half receptions, whether a particular reliever records a strikeout in his first batter faced, whether the game's first score comes on a field goal. No trading desk, however well staffed, can build a fully researched, independently modeled price for every one of those markets on every game on a given Sunday; a meaningful share of that long tail is priced using simplified statistical shortcuts, historical averages, or a trader's fast, experience-based estimate rather than the same rigor applied to the headline spread, and the resulting prices are, by the traders' own private admission within the industry, considerably more likely to contain a genuine mispricing that a sufficiently well-prepared bettor could exploit before the book's own systems catch up. This is not a minor technical footnote to the chapter's larger argument; it is close to its center. The same product category that generates the industry's highest margins, because a parlay's combined odds compound a book's edge across every leg, is also the product category built on the thinnest, least rigorously modeled pricing — a combination that works enormously in an operator's favor against the overwhelming majority of casual bettors placing small, single-shot parlay tickets for entertainment, and that simultaneously creates exactly the kind of soft, exploitable price a sharp, well-modeled bettor is most likely to find and hammer before a limit gets imposed.

Much of the actual risk-management work that determines who gets limited, and how quickly, no longer happens inside any individual operator's own building at all, a fact that complicates the picture of dozens of competing sportsbook brands each independently deciding how to treat a given customer. A small handful of business-to-business technology vendors — among them Kambi, a Swedish-founded firm that supplies either a complete turnkey sportsbook platform or a standalone odds-and-risk feed to operators across dozens of regulated markets, and Sportradar and Genius Sports, both of which built their original businesses supplying live data feeds to leagues and broadcasters before expanding into odds compilation and automated trading tools — now sit underneath a large share of the ostensibly separate, competing sportsbook brands an American bettor can choose among. These platforms perform, as an automated service sold to multiple client operators simultaneously, much of what a human trading desk like Circa's once did entirely in-house: compiling odds from raw statistical feeds, balancing liability across thousands of simultaneous in-play markets, and — the function most relevant to this chapter — running behavioral models trained to flag a given account's betting pattern as consistent with sharp, model-driven wagering rather than recreational play, triggering an automatic reduction in that account's limits without necessarily requiring a human trader to review the individual case at all. A bettor limited at one operator, in other words, is not always being personally singled out by that specific company's trading staff; in a meaningful number of cases, the same underlying risk-scoring technology, licensed from the same handful of vendors, is quietly doing similar work across several nominally competing books at once, a consolidation of the actual pricing-and-limiting infrastructure that sits almost entirely out of public view and that no single operator's own marketing or public statements have much incentive to draw attention to.

The practice has become contested enough, and public enough, to reach state legislatures directly rather than remaining a grievance confined to bettor forums and gambling-industry trade press. New York Assembly member Alex Bores introduced legislation in early 2026, titled the Fair Play Act, that would make New York the first state in the country to prohibit licensed sportsbooks from capping or reducing a customer's betting limits simply because that customer has been winning or betting frequently, permitting operators to restrict an account only when the restriction is tied to documented suspicious activity or indicators of problem gambling, and requiring operators to notify an affected customer within twenty-four hours with a specific explanation for any limit imposed. The bill's own premise — that a sportsbook licensed to operate in New York should not be permitted to advertise itself as a place anyone can win money while simultaneously reserving the right to quietly cap how much any specific winning customer is allowed to keep winning — captures precisely the tension this chapter has traced from Circa's opening line down through Pinnacle's low-margin counter-model to Kyle Kuzma's public complaint, and its introduction is itself evidence that the gap between a sportsbook's marketing promise and its actual account-management practice has become difficult enough to ignore that a state legislature, rather than only bettors and journalists, has started trying to close it directly.

The distinction between these two business models — market-making, built on volume, thin margins, and welcoming informed action, versus the recreational retail model most American bettors actually interact with, built on wider margins, aggressive promotional customer acquisition, and active risk suppression against sharp customers — is not a minor technical footnote. It describes two genuinely different products wearing the same interface. A bettor opening the DraftKings or FanDuel app is, in almost every practical sense, interacting with a business whose profitability depends far more on managing a large population of recreational customers toward higher-margin products like same-game parlays, described in this book's first chapter, than on the traditional bookmaking discipline of balancing two-sided action at a thin, market-accurate price — a discipline that, as this book's third chapter showed, was the entire original business model Nevada's oddsmakers spent fifty years refining, and that today survives in its purer form mainly at the handful of operators, Circa and Pinnacle among them, that a casual sports fan has likely never heard of, precisely because those operators have chosen not to spend the hundreds of millions of dollars on national advertising that made DraftKings and FanDuel household names in the first place. The line a bettor sees on a Sunday morning, in other words, was made twice: once, upstream, by a market-making book competing to get the number right, and once again, downstream, by a retail operator whose actual business interest lies less in the accuracy of that number than in how many recreational customers can be induced to bet on it, repeatedly, at odds the operator has quietly and legally arranged to keep in its own favor.
