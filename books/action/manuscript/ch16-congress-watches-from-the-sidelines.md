# Congress Watches From the Sidelines

Congress already has a sports betting law. It has had one since 1951, when lawmakers, worried about the money organized crime was making off illegal bookmaking, imposed a federal excise tax of 0.25 percent on every dollar wagered with a legal bookmaker — a handle tax, not a revenue tax, meaning it applies whether the bet wins or loses, collected regardless of the sportsbook's own profit or loss on the game. The tax survived Nevada's decades as the only legal market, survived the 1992 federal ban that made the rest of the country's bookmaking illegal, and survived, unchanged, the 2018 Supreme Court ruling that opened sports betting to every state that wanted it. It is still on the books today, still charged on every dollar a legal sportsbook takes in, in every one of the thirty-eight or thirty-nine states — trackers differ on how to count a state that has authorized betting but not yet launched it — where the product is now legal. Since 2018, sportsbooks have paid more than $500 million into this tax. And when a member of Congress asked the Internal Revenue Service, in the course of ordinary oversight, exactly where that money had gone inside the federal budget, the IRS could not say. The agency's own public reporting on federal excise tax collections has not broken out a specific dollar figure for the 0.25 percent wagering tax since fiscal year 2000 — a full eighteen years before the tax became relevant to anywhere outside Nevada, and eight years and counting since it became relevant to nearly the whole country.

That fact belongs at the front of this chapter because it is the cleanest possible illustration of what federal involvement in legal sports betting has actually amounted to since Murphy v. NCAA: a law nobody updated, generating money nobody tracks, sitting untouched while fifty state legislatures did the actual work of deciding what this industry would look like. Congress did not write the rules that govern same-game parlays, in-play micro-betting, or the VIP-outreach programs chapters 8 and 9 of this book examined in detail. It did not set a national minimum age, a national self-exclusion standard, or a national rule about how a sportsbook may advertise during a live broadcast. It did not decide how much of a state's tax revenue should be earmarked for problem-gambling treatment, a question chapter 17 of this book will show forty-some states have answered forty-some different ways, mostly badly. The one piece of standing federal law actually on the books governing this industry is a Truman-era wagering tax that generates revenue nobody in the federal government can currently account for — and, as this chapter will show, the industry's own most active current ask of Congress is not a new consumer protection but the repeal of that tax altogether.

The most serious attempt to change that — to give the country something resembling the federal baseline every other major consumer product with this much potential for harm eventually acquires — is the SAFE Bet Act, an acronym for the Supporting Affordability and Fairness with Every Bet Act, introduced by Representative Paul Tonko of New York and Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut. The bill's core theory, laid out in the sponsors' own fact sheet, is that state-by-state regulation has produced fifty different answers to questions that shouldn't have fifty different answers: whether an operator can market during a live sporting event, whether an operator can extend credit or accept payment on a credit card, whether an operator must verify a customer can actually afford the amounts they are depositing before letting the deposits continue, and whether a bettor who wants out has any way to be excluded from every operator in the country at once rather than one at a time, state by state, the limitation chapter 17 examines in more detail. The bill as introduced would ban sportsbook advertising during any live broadcast of a game, prohibit betting by anyone under 21, require affordability checks — deposit limits, spending pattern monitoring, and intervention triggers loosely modeled on the checks chapter 19 will show the United Kingdom has already built and started testing at national scale — and direct federal agencies, for the first time, to formally study the public-health effects of legal sports betting rather than relying entirely on state-funded surveys and independent academic research to fill that gap.

None of this is Congress's first attempt, which is worth knowing before treating the SAFE Bet Act as a novel idea nobody thought to try earlier. Within eight months of the Supreme Court's May 2018 ruling — while states were still writing their first licensing statutes and DraftKings and FanDuel were still mid-pivot from daily fantasy sports, the subject of chapter 5 of this book, into full sportsbooks — Senators Orrin Hatch of Utah and Chuck Schumer of New York introduced the Sports Wagering Market Integrity Act, an odd-couple pairing that made a certain sense given the two men's history: Hatch had co-authored the 1992 law the Supreme Court had just struck down, and here he was, in the last year of his Senate career, trying to build a federal replacement for the regime he had helped create. The Hatch-Schumer bill would have required every legal sportsbook in the country to use official league data when settling bets — a provision the professional leagues had lobbied hard for, since it would have functionally required operators to pay the leagues for data access — and would have set baseline federal standards for consumer protection and suspicious-activity reporting, aimed less at the affordability and marketing questions the SAFE Bet Act targets than at protecting the competitive integrity of the games themselves. It went nowhere. The bill was read twice, referred to the Senate Judiciary Committee, and never received a vote, let alone a floor debate, before that Congress ended. A House committee had held its own hearing on federal sports betting standards that same fall, an event legal-industry observers at the time described as more exploratory than purposeful — a chance for members to be seen asking questions about a fast-moving new industry rather than a serious step toward drafting shared legislation. Six years and two more election cycles later, the SAFE Bet Act arrived pursuing a different set of protections through a different set of sponsors, but the basic pattern — a bill introduced with real sponsor commitment, a committee referral, and then silence — repeated almost exactly.

Tonko and Blumenthal first introduced the bill in September 2024, too late in that Congress to move, and reintroduced it in March 2025 as S.1033 in the Senate and H.R. 2087 in the House, at the start of the 119th Congress. Neither bill has advanced out of committee as of this book's research in September 2026. Both remain formally alive — introduced, referred, and sitting — which is a different status than dead, but functionally indistinguishable from dead for the eighteen months the current bills have existed without a committee vote. The House version was referred to the Energy and Commerce Committee, with a secondary referral to Natural Resources — the committee with jurisdiction over Indian gaming compacts, a reminder of how directly any federal sports betting standard would intersect with the tribal gaming questions chapters 15 and 18 of this book both return to. The Senate version went to the Judiciary Committee, the same committee that received and buried the Hatch-Schumer bill in 2018 and that held a hearing on the SAFE Bet Act's 2024 predecessor version criticized at the time, including by some of the bill's own supporters, for being one-sided: witnesses testified about the harms of the current market, but no representative of the legal sports betting industry itself was invited to appear and answer for the product decisions being criticized, a format that let the hearing generate sympathetic headlines without ever forcing the industry to defend itself on the record or forcing the committee to weigh a direct rebuttal before deciding whether to act. Neither committee has scheduled a markup on the 2025 bills as of this book's research.

In April 2026, more than a year after reintroduction, Blumenthal and Tonko held a press event specifically to "vow to advance" the legislation, language that in ordinary legislative practice signals not momentum but its absence: a bill with real momentum gets a committee markup, not a press conference restating support for it. The SAFE Bet Act's status by the time this book went to press was, in short, exactly where it had been for nearly two years: introduced, publicly championed by its two authors, and stalled.

The American Gaming Association's response to the bill, both times it was introduced, is worth reading in full for how precisely it inverts the argument this book has made in earlier chapters. In a statement responding to the bill's original 2024 introduction, the AGA — the industry's primary trade association and the entity chapter 14 of this book already identified as the source of most of the favorable statistics operators cite in their own marketing — argued that regulated sportsbook operators were already contributing billions of dollars in state taxes while protecting consumers from what it called dangerous neighborhood bookies and illegal offshore websites. The framing does real work: it treats the existence of tax revenue and the existence of consumer protection as though they were the same accomplishment, when chapter 14's own numbers showed a state can collect record tax revenue in exactly the years its own problem-gambling helpline call volume is rising. It also treats the SAFE Bet Act's specific asks — an advertising restriction during live broadcasts, an affordability check before allowing continued deposits — as an attack on an industry that is already protecting consumers, rather than as evidence for the position chapter 10's account of behavioral design and chapter 6's account of promotional spending have already built: that the industry's product decisions and its stated commitment to consumer protection are frequently in direct tension, and that a federal bill built around exactly the features — live-broadcast ads, unlimited deposit velocity — this book's earlier chapters singled out as deliberately engineered to maximize handle is not an attack on responsible operators so much as a description of what the current product actually does.

The AGA's lobbying spending gives some sense of how seriously the trade group is treating that fight. Across roughly five quarters running from early 2025 through the first quarter of 2026, the AGA disclosed more than $2.9 million in federal lobbying expenditures, according to filings tracked by the Center for Responsive Politics' OpenSecrets database — spending that climbed rather than declined as the SAFE Bet Act sat in committee, with the group's first-quarter 2026 total, roughly $730,000, its highest single-quarter figure in more than a year. Some of that spending is general advocacy unrelated to any single bill; the AGA also lobbies on tax policy, on the federal excise tax specifically, and on a range of state-level issues that touch federal agencies only tangentially. But the pattern — sustained, and rising rather than falling, through the exact period the SAFE Bet Act has sat stalled in committee — is consistent with an industry that has concluded the current equilibrium, fifty states setting their own rules with no federal floor, is working in its favor and worth defending against the one serious attempt to change it.

The industry's own legislative priority in this same period tells a sharper version of the same story than the SAFE Bet Act fight does on its own. Rather than campaign for a new consumer-protection standard, the AGA has backed a bipartisan bill, introduced by Representatives Dina Titus of Nevada and Guy Reschenthaler of Pennsylvania, to repeal the 1951 federal excise tax outright — the same 0.25 percent handle tax this chapter opened with, the one piece of standing federal sports betting law already on the books. The argument for repeal, as the bill's sponsors and the industry both frame it, is genuinely coherent on its own terms: a handle tax, unlike a revenue tax, falls hardest on low-margin, high-volume operators and, its critics argue, makes it marginally harder for legal, taxed sportsbooks to compete on price with illegal and offshore operators who pay no such tax at all — an argument this book takes seriously, because chapter 17's steelman for the legal market rests in part on exactly this competitive logic. But set against the SAFE Bet Act, the contrast is instructive regardless of which side of the excise-tax argument turns out to be right: the industry's active legislative energy in Washington in this period has gone toward eliminating an existing federal tax, not toward building a new federal consumer-protection floor, and a Congress with limited attention for gambling policy has, so far, moved further on the industry's ask than on Tonko and Blumenthal's.

There is a second bill worth knowing about precisely because it shows what a version of federal engagement that took the money seriously, rather than the marketing, might look like — and because its own struggle to advance illustrates the same stall pattern from a different angle. The Gambling Addiction Recovery, Investment, and Treatment Act, known by its acronym GRIT, was introduced by Representative Andrea Salinas of Oregon and Senator Blumenthal, and it does not ask Congress to create any new tax or any new bureaucracy. It asks Congress to do something considerably narrower and, on paper, considerably easier: take the existing federal excise tax — the same 0.25 percent handle tax the Titus-Reschenthaler bill wants abolished — and dedicate half of what it raises specifically to gambling addiction treatment and research, with 75 percent of that half flowing to states and 25 percent funding research grants through the National Institute on Drug Abuse, over a ten-year authorization. The bill's sponsors have been explicit that GRIT is designed to be revenue-neutral to the operators and low-friction to pass, precisely because it redirects money the federal government is already collecting rather than asking for anything new. It has been introduced, reintroduced, and has not, as of this book's research, advanced further than the SAFE Bet Act has. Two bills, aimed at two different halves of the same problem — one restricting how the product can be marketed and sold, one funding the treatment of people the product has already harmed — have moved through Congress at the same speed: not at all.

The practical consequence of this inaction is not merely symbolic, and it is worth being specific about what a federal baseline would actually change rather than treating "Congress should act" as an empty rhetorical gesture. A bettor who signs up for a self-exclusion program in New Jersey today has no way to make that exclusion apply if the same person, in a moment of relapse, opens an account with a different operator in Pennsylvania, or drives across a bridge into New York, or uses a VPN to reach an operator in a state where they were never excluded in the first place — because, as chapter 17 will detail further, there is no national self-exclusion database, only a patchwork of state-level and a handful of voluntary multi-operator systems that most bettors have never heard of and that no federal law requires any operator to honor. A bettor watching a broadcast in a state that restricts in-game advertising can still see ads during the same broadcast if the network airing it is licensed nationally rather than regulated state by state, because no federal rule governs national broadcast advertising content the way the SAFE Bet Act's stalled provisions would — and the states that have tried to regulate advertising on their own have produced exactly the fragmented, operator-by-operator patchwork a federal standard is supposed to prevent. Ohio's gaming commission moved first and hardest, writing a rule in January 2023 that bars the words "free" or "risk-free" in any promotion that actually requires a bettor to wager their own money before receiving bonus credit, and it has since fined three major operators between $150,000 and $350,000 apiece for violating it. Massachusetts wrote a similar but not identical rule and fined Penn Sports Interactive $25,000 that same year over a promotion branded "Can't Lose Parlay," on the reasoning that no wager placed with a sportsbook is ever actually risk-free to the person placing it. New York, by contrast, regulates the format and disclosure language of betting ads in some detail but takes a measurably lighter touch on the underlying "free bet" framing itself. Three states, three different definitions of a misleading promotion, no two of them identical — which means the exact same national ad campaign, for the exact same product, can be a fineable violation in Columbus, a fineable violation on slightly different grounds in Boston, and simply legal in Albany, with an operator's compliance department the only body actually keeping track of which version can run where. And a researcher trying to measure the actual public-health cost of legal sports betting — the project chapter 14's numbers and chapter 17's academic dispute both depend on — has to work from a patchwork of state-funded surveys, credit-bureau studies funded by universities, and helpline call logs voluntarily reported by state problem-gambling councils, because no federal agency has been directed, resourced, or required to study the question systematically at a national level. Every one of those gaps is a direct, traceable consequence of the same fact: the only piece of federal sports betting law that predates 2018 is a wagering tax nobody in government can currently account for, and the two bills that would begin to close the gaps — one restricting the product, one funding the cleanup — have spent roughly two years each sitting in committee while the industry's own lobbying dollars flow toward repealing the one federal mechanism already in place.

The industry's resistance to the SAFE Bet Act rests on more than lobbying muscle; it also rests on an argument with a real constitutional pedigree, one this book's earlier chapters have already supplied the background for. Murphy v. NCAA, examined in chapter 2, struck down the federal sports betting ban not because the Supreme Court found a right to bet on football somewhere in the Constitution, but because it found that PASPA violated the anti-commandeering doctrine — the Tenth Amendment principle that Congress cannot force state governments to enforce a federal regulatory scheme with their own personnel and budgets. The AGA and allied state regulators have leaned on that same doctrine against the SAFE Bet Act, arguing that a bill requiring the thirty-eight or thirty-nine states that have already built their own licensing regimes to seek a federal attorney general's approval for those regimes, or to conform their own rules to federal minimum standards, risks re-creating the exact command-and-control relationship Murphy dismantled, only now aimed at the states' own regulatory choices rather than at a blanket prohibition. Legal commentary on the bill has flagged this tension directly: a federal law built on the assumption that Washington can tell a state how to run a program the state itself created and funds is precisely the kind of arrangement the Supreme Court's 2018 reasoning made harder to sustain, whatever one thinks of the policy merits. It is a genuinely uncomfortable argument for this book to sit with, because it means the same legal doctrine that opened the market nine chapters ago is now available as a shield against the one serious federal attempt to put guardrails on what that market became — a reminder that Murphy's anti-commandeering holding was never actually about sports betting specifically, and cuts in whichever direction happens to favor state autonomy at a given moment, regardless of what that autonomy is being used to protect.

None of this means state regulation has failed on its own terms. Chapter 15's tour of the tax-rate patchwork showed states making genuinely different, defensible choices about how hard to tax an industry within their own borders, and a reasonable person can look at New York's 51 percent and Nevada's 6.75 percent and conclude both reflect legitimate, if opposite, theories of what a state government owes its residents from a legal vice. What state regulation cannot do, by its nature, is set a floor beneath all fifty states at once — it cannot stop an operator licensed in a low-restriction state from advertising into a high-restriction one over a national broadcast, cannot build a self-exclusion list that follows a bettor across a state line, and cannot direct a federal research agency to study a question that, precisely because sports betting remains nominally a matter of state law under the post-Murphy framework, no single state has the jurisdiction or the incentive to fund on a national scale. That is specifically the job a federal baseline is built to do, in every other regulated industry with this kind of harm profile, from tobacco to consumer lending — and it is specifically the job Congress, nearly a decade after opening the door, has still not done.

The states, in the meantime, have not waited for Washington, because they never had the option to. Every legislature examined in chapter 15 wrote its own tax rate, its own licensing structure, its own rules — or, in California's, Texas's, and Georgia's cases, its own reasons for having none of those things at all — because the alternative to fifty separate answers was not one good federal answer arriving on some reasonable timeline. It was no answer at all, for years, while the market kept growing underneath the vacuum. That vacuum is where the next chapter's argument actually lives: the strongest case for the current, congressionally unsupervised system is not that it is well designed, but that whatever imperfect, uneven, revenue-hungry patchwork fifty states built on their own is still measurably better, on several dimensions this book has not yet fully credited, than the unregulated market it replaced.
