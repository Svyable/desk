# Kickoff

By the time the Seattle Seahawks kicked off against the New England Patriots at Levi's Stadium on the evening of February 8, 2026, most of the money that would be won and lost on Super Bowl LX had already changed hands, in the sense that matters to an industry rather than to a fan: it had already been staked. The American Gaming Association, the trade group that speaks for the country's casino and sportsbook operators, had projected before kickoff that Americans would legally wager an estimated $1.76 billion on the game, a record for any single sporting event in American history and a figure up roughly 27 percent from the $1.39 billion the same organization had estimated for the previous year's Super Bowl LIX. Some of that money arrived the way Super Bowl bets always have — a colleague's squares pool, a hundred dollars on the coin flip, a neighborhood pool where nobody involved could tell you the point spread if asked. But most of it, by every measure the industry itself publishes, now arrives through a phone: an app opened during the pregame show, a same-game parlay built during a commercial break, a same-day prop on whether the opening kickoff would be returned for a touchdown, settled and paid out or lost before the first quarter ended. The game itself turned into a rout by any football standard — Seattle's Kenneth Walker III rushed for 135 yards, the most by any back in a Super Bowl in nearly three decades, and Seattle took a 29-13 win with a defense that forced three takeaways and very nearly posted a shutout into the fourth quarter — but the betting handle did not care about the score being lopsided. It never really does. A sportsbook does not need a close game to make money; it needs volume, and volume is the one thing about American sports betting that has not stopped growing since a Supreme Court decision in May 2018 made it legal, state by state, for the first time in more than a quarter century.

The Super Bowl LX wagering estimate did not appear from nowhere. It capped a full year in which the American Gaming Association would go on to report, in its annual State of the States survey released the following month, that commercial gaming revenue across the United States had reached a record $78.72 billion in 2025, up 9.2 percent from the year before, and that sports betting alone had produced $16.96 billion of that total on a national handle — the total amount wagered, as distinct from what operators kept — of $166.94 billion, itself an 11 percent increase over 2024. Sportsbooks paid $3.71 billion in state gaming taxes on that sports betting revenue in 2025, a 32.4 percent jump from the year before, the kind of number a state lottery director or a legislator facing a budget shortfall finds easy to like. Heading into the 2025 NFL season, the trade group had projected that Americans would legally wager roughly $30 billion on NFL games alone across that single season, and actual handle came in close to that figure — a rise from about $27.6 billion the previous season — even as the AGA's forecast for the 2026 season, the one already under way as this book was being written, called for essentially flat growth for the first time since legalization began, a plateau the association itself attributed in part to a new and unregulated competitor: prediction markets, a subject this book returns to near its end. None of these are small numbers, and none of them existed, in any legal or public form, eight years before Kenneth Walker III took his first handoff in Santa Clara.

What makes the Super Bowl LX figure worth opening a book on, rather than merely another data point in an increasingly numb parade of record-breaking handle announcements, is how ordinary it had become to state it. A trade association predicting record wagering on the Super Bowl is no longer news in the way it would have been in, say, 2019; it is closer to a seasonal ritual, alongside the point spread itself and the commercial for a beer that nobody actually drinks. By the time Missouri became the thirty-ninth state to launch legal sports betting, on December 1, 2025 — joining the District of Columbia and thirty-eight other states in a market that did not exist at all, anywhere outside Nevada, as recently as the spring of 2018 — the country had mostly stopped treating the legalization of sports betting as a live argument and started treating it as background infrastructure, the way electricity or cellular service is infrastructure: something you use without thinking about who built it, who profits from it, or what it was designed to do to your behavior. This book is an argument for thinking about it again, closely, the way you might look again at a piece of infrastructure you use every day only after learning it was engineered by people with an economic interest in how much of it you use.

Start with the plain history, because the reader deserves the roadmap before the argument. For twenty-six years, from 1992 to 2018, a federal law called the Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act — known informally as PASPA, or the Bradley Act, after the New Jersey senator and former Knicks star who championed it — made it illegal for any state except Nevada to authorize sports betting in any meaningful form. Chapter two tells that story: the point-shaving scandals and organized-crime associations that gave PASPA its moral force, the states it grandfathered in and the one, New Jersey, that let its own grandfather clause lapse through legislative inaction. Chapter three describes what actually happened inside the one state where sports betting stayed legal that whole time — how a bookmaking business survived a nearly punitive federal excise tax, how the point spread itself was invented and refined, and how the vigorish, the built-in cut a sportsbook takes regardless of who wins a given game, has quietly functioned as the industry's actual business model since long before any of the app-based operators existed. Chapter four follows New Jersey's own fight to overturn PASPA, led first by Chris Christie as a play to rescue a dying Atlantic City and carried, after Christie left office, by his successor Phil Murphy all the way to the Supreme Court, which ruled 6-3 on May 14, 2018, in Murphy v. National Collegiate Athletic Association, that PASPA violated the Tenth Amendment's anticommandeering doctrine by conscripting state legislatures into enforcing a federal gambling policy they had not chosen. That decision is the hinge this whole book turns on. Before it, sports betting in the United States existed mostly as an illegal, cash-based, locally bookmade activity, plus a legal but geographically confined Nevada industry, plus a rapidly growing gray-market product called daily fantasy sports that had spent most of a decade arguing, successfully, that it was not gambling at all. After it, all three of those things collapsed into a single, converging, venture-funded, heavily marketed national industry, and the story of how that convergence happened — daily fantasy's pivot into sportsbooks, chapter five's subject; the billions of dollars of promotional losses operators absorbed to acquire customers, chapter six's; and the actual, largely invisible mechanics of how a betting line gets set, moved, and defended against sharp bettors, chapter seven's — is the second half of this book's first arc.

The thesis underneath all of it is one this book tries to state plainly enough, in its first chapter, that a reader can hold it up against the evidence in every chapter that follows and judge for themselves whether it survives contact with the record. A legal, taxed, regulated sports betting market is a real and measurable improvement over the illegal market it replaced on several dimensions that matter: consumers have legal recourse when a book refuses to pay a winning bet, states collect tax revenue that an underground bookmaking economy never generated, and law enforcement is no longer chasing illegal wire rooms with the same urgency it once devoted to a crime most Americans did not think deserved the attention. That same market has also been built, at the product level, using techniques — same-game parlays that combine many small bets into one high-margin wager, in-play betting that lets a customer place a new bet on nearly every snap of a game already under way, targeted promotional outreach to the heaviest-spending customers, and notification systems modeled on the same behavioral research the mobile-gaming and social-media industries use to maximize app engagement — that are deliberately engineered to increase how often and how much a person bets, independent of whether more frequent betting serves that person's own interest. Both of those sentences are true. The industry's own promotional material tends to lead with the first sentence and omit the second. Most of the activist and public-health literature critical of the industry leads with the second and treats the first as a talking point rather than a genuine improvement. This book tries to hold both at once, because the evidence, chapter by chapter, supports both, and because a reader who only gets one half of the picture cannot actually evaluate what a fifty-state, multi-billion-dollar experiment in legalized betting has actually done to the people inside it.

The growth curve underneath that Super Bowl figure is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is easy to let one record-breaking headline blur into the next without registering how steep the climb actually was. In 2019, the first full calendar year in which states beyond Nevada could take single-game sports bets, regulated American sportsbooks accepted a combined handle of about $13 billion. By 2023 that figure had passed $121 billion. By 2024 it was roughly $150 billion. By 2025 it was $166.94 billion — meaning the legal market grew by a factor of roughly twelve in six years, a growth rate that would be remarkable for any consumer product and is close to unprecedented for one built on top of a federal prohibition that had held for a generation. No single Super Bowl, no single NFL season, explains that trajectory by itself. What explains it is the subject of this book: a specific set of companies making specific bets, mostly correct, about which product features would convert a bettor who wagered occasionally into one who wagered constantly, and which promotional strategies would get the largest number of new customers in the door fastest, questions chapters five and six take up directly.

It also helps to be honest, in this first chapter rather than buried in a later one, about what the other side of that growth curve looks like, because a fair account of the industry has to hold the tax-revenue number and this number in the same paragraph. The National Council on Problem Gambling, the country's oldest advocacy and referral organization on the subject, reported that calls to its national helpline rose 43 percent in 2021 alone, with texts up nearly 60 percent and online chat volume up more than 84 percent, and that overall contact volume nearly doubled between 2020 and 2023 — a period that tracks almost exactly with the state-by-state rollout of legal mobile betting. The pattern shows up locally as well as nationally: in Ohio, where legal mobile sports betting launched on January 1, 2023, the regional 211 referral service serving the Cleveland area reported a 277 percent increase in gambling-related calls that same January compared with the January before legalization. Reporting on the helpline data has consistently found that callers are skewing younger, with men in their twenties and early thirties overrepresented among people describing a gambling problem connected to sports betting specifically, and the Council's executive director has said publicly that the organization sees "every reason to believe" the expansion of online sports betting is a major contributing factor to the rise in gambling problems nationally. None of that is proof, by itself, that any specific product feature caused any specific person's harm; correlation between a helpline's call volume and a state's legalization date is not the same as a controlled study, and this book treats that distinction carefully in the chapters where the harder claims live. But a rising handle and a rising helpline call count are not two unrelated stories happening to occur in the same years. They are very plausibly the same story, told from two different rooms in the same house, and a book that only visited one of those rooms would not be telling the truth about what got built.

It helps to be specific, early, about what "engineered" means in that sentence, because the word can sound like an accusation with no content — the kind of thing said about any product a critic dislikes, from junk food to social media, without much precision about mechanism. Same-game parlays are the clearest example, and they are worth understanding now because later chapters build on the mechanics. A traditional single-game bet asks one question — will Team A cover the spread, or will the total points go over or under a set number — and pays out at odds close to even money, minus the vigorish described in chapter three. A same-game parlay lets a bettor combine several individual outcomes from a single contest — the spread, the total, whether a specific player scores a touchdown, whether that same player also goes over a rushing-yardage number — into a single ticket that pays out only if every leg hits. The appeal to a bettor is obvious: a small stake on a long-shot combination can pay out at odds of 20-to-1 or more, turning a five-dollar bet into a life-changing number if everything falls right, in a way a straight spread bet essentially never can. The appeal to the sportsbook is less obvious to a casual bettor and considerably larger in practice: because the legs of a same-game parlay are not statistically independent — a quarterback throwing three touchdown passes and his team covering a large spread tend to happen together, not separately — a bookmaker who prices each leg as though it were independent, then multiplies the odds together, embeds a hold, or effective house edge, that can run into the double digits, several times higher than the four or five percent hold typical on a standard spread bet. Operators do not advertise this arithmetic; they advertise the payout. A bettor sees "20-to-1," not "this ticket carries roughly three or four times the house edge of the straight bet you'd have made five years ago, before this product existed." Same-game parlays did not exist as a mainstream, legal, mobile-first product before roughly 2019. By the mid-2020s, industry reporting and operator earnings calls routinely described parlay products, same-game and otherwise, as the fastest-growing and highest-margin segment of the entire legal sports betting business — the product operators most wanted a casual bettor to discover, because it is the product most likely to keep that bettor's money.

In-play betting works on a different lever entirely, and it deserves a full chapter of its own later in this book because its behavioral mechanics are close cousins of a casino slot machine's, but the short version belongs here too. Before legalization, and even in the first several years after it, the standard American sports bet was placed before the game started and settled after it ended: one decision, one outcome, hours apart. A modern sportsbook app instead offers a constantly updating menu of new bets available throughout the game itself — will the next drive end in a punt, will the next batter reach base, will the shot clock expire before the next basket — each one resolved within minutes rather than hours, each resolution immediately followed by a fresh menu of new bets on what happens next. The gap between placing a wager and finding out whether you won it, which behavioral-science research on gambling has identified for decades as one of the central variables driving compulsive play, shrinks from a full game to a single possession, and the number of discrete win-or-lose events available to a bettor during a single three-hour football broadcast multiplies from one to, potentially, dozens. This book's later chapters take that mechanism seriously as a design choice rather than an incidental feature, because the operators who built it were not indifferent to the difference between one bet per game and forty.

None of that mechanism explains itself without the history that made it legal and the companies that built it, which is why this book proceeds chronologically rather than thematically for its first stretch. It is tempting, writing about an industry this online and this now, to start with the app on the phone and work backward only as needed. The trouble with that approach is that it treats the current shape of legal sports betting as though it were inevitable — as though, once the Supreme Court struck down PASPA, same-game parlays and push notifications and VIP outreach programs were the only possible product any operator could have built. They were not inevitable. They were choices, made by specific companies competing for market share in a specific competitive environment, funded by specific venture capital that demanded specific kinds of growth, and every one of those choices is traceable, in the historical and financial record, to a decision someone made because it worked — because it increased handle, increased revenue, and increased the odds that a casual bettor would return the following Sunday having lost more, on average, than they had the Sunday before. Understanding that history is not a detour from the argument. It is the argument, laid out in the order the decisions were actually made.

There is a version of this book's subject that a critic could tell entirely through outrage — the predatory promotional offer, the college student who lost a semester's tuition on parlays, the state legislator who took campaign money from an operator lobbying against consumer protections a few months later. Those stories exist, they are real, and several of them appear in the chapters ahead, verified against court records, regulatory filings, and contemporaneous reporting rather than composited for effect. But a book that told only those stories would flatten an industry that is, in fact, more interesting and more genuinely contested than a simple morality tale allows. The same market that engineered same-game parlays to maximize hold also gave a bettor who gets stiffed by a shady operator an actual regulator to complain to, something nobody who lost money to an illegal bookie in 1995 ever had. The same operators who spend on VIP outreach to their highest-value customers, a term this book will show means, bluntly, their heaviest losers, also employ licensed compliance staff, fund problem-gambling helplines, and operate under state oversight that did not exist for the offshore books and neighborhood bookies that took the same bets, illegally, for decades before 2018. A reader who wants a clean villain will not find one in every chapter of this book, and that is deliberate: the evidence does not support one. What the evidence does support, consistently, across state regulatory reports, company financial filings, peer-reviewed research on gambling behavior, and the court records generated by the industry's own legal disputes, is a market that is simultaneously better than what it replaced and built, in its specific product choices, to extract more money from its most vulnerable users than almost any other legal consumer product operating today.

That claim will need to survive the chapters that give the industry's own strongest defense a full and fair hearing, which is why chapter seventeen of this book is given over entirely to the counter-argument, made by serious people who are not merely paid lobbyists, that a regulated, taxed, monitored market beats every realistic alternative, including the return of an illegal market that regulation cannot see or tax at all. It will need to survive the acknowledgment, made at several points in the chapters ahead, that most bettors — by every study this book found credible enough to cite — lose modest, bounded amounts of money on a form of entertainment they freely choose and genuinely enjoy, the way a moviegoer spends money on a ticket without expecting a return. And it will need to survive close, skeptical treatment of the harder claims: how large the population of bettors experiencing serious financial harm actually is, how that harm is measured, and how much of it is genuinely attributable to product design as opposed to preexisting vulnerability the product merely found. This book does not treat those as settled questions. It treats them as questions worth answering with real numbers, drawn from sources a reader can check, rather than with the two loudest and least careful positions already circulating in public: that legalization was an unqualified triumph because a trade association can point to record tax revenue, or that it was an unqualified catastrophe because some bettors, inevitably, lose control.

Seattle's rout of New England on February 8, 2026, will not be remembered, in football terms, as a classic — blowouts rarely are, and Kenneth Walker III's 135 rushing yards, the best mark in a Super Bowl in twenty-eight years, will likely be a trivia answer within a decade rather than a moment anyone outside Seattle replays. But the $1.76 billion wagered on it legally, and the roughly $167 billion wagered legally on American sports over the full year that surrounded it, mark something else: the completion, in under a decade, of a transformation in how a large fraction of American adults relate to the games they watch, built by companies that started the period arguing they were not in the gambling business at all and ended it running some of the most sophisticated consumer-behavior operations in the country. Understanding how that happened — the law that made it possible, the market it was built on top of, the money that funded its expansion, and the specific mechanics by which a line gets set and a bettor gets hooked — is the rest of this book's job, starting with the law that, for twenty-six years, tried to make the whole thing illegal everywhere except one desert city that had already spent half a century figuring out how to do it.
