# Front Matter

## The Airplanes We Stole

### Secret MiGs, Foreign Materiel Exploitation, and What Real Reverse Engineering Leaves Behind

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There is a photograph of a Soviet MiG-21 wearing American stars. It is the kind of image conspiracy culture should love because almost every ingredient is already there: an enemy machine, a desert base, a secret program, false markings, compartmented access, pilots who could not talk, an intelligence service working through a foreign partner, engineers measuring a technology the public did not know the United States possessed.

The photograph is not evidence of aliens. It is more useful than that.

It is evidence of what a real hidden hardware-exploitation program looks like after enough time passes for the seams to show.

In January 1968, the United States began flying a MiG-21 obtained through Israel under the code name HAVE DOUGHNUT. The aircraft had reached Israel after Iraqi pilot Munir Redfa defected with it in 1966. American pilots and engineers did not merely look at the fighter. They flew it against U.S. aircraft. They measured its performance. They tested tactics against it. They learned where it was dangerous and where it was awkward. A year later, related programs exploited MiG-17s. By the late 1970s, the logic had grown into CONSTANT PEG, a secret squadron operating Soviet-designed fighters from Tonopah Test Range so American aircrews could meet the real machines before meeting them in combat.

For decades, most Americans did not know this happened.

That fact matters. But the more interesting fact is that secrecy did not make the program metaphysically invisible. Aircraft needed fuel, spare parts, hangars, runways, maintainers, test pilots, safety procedures, acquisition channels, instructors, range time, classified reports, and eventually replacements. People were injured and killed. Airfields changed. Organizations were created. Lessons moved into doctrine even when the source of the lesson could not be named. Hardware survived. Participants aged into memoirs and interviews. Records entered archives. The Air Force eventually acknowledged CONSTANT PEG publicly in 2006. The National Security Archive later assembled hundreds of pages of declassified HAVE DOUGHNUT, HAVE DRILL, and Area 51 material.

This book begins from a simple proposition: if we want to think seriously about alleged crash-retrieval and reverse-engineering programs, we need a control sample.

The control sample should not be fictional. It should not be a hypothetical Special Access Program invented to prove that anything can be hidden. It should be a real program in which a state secretly acquired advanced foreign hardware, moved it into restricted facilities, disassembled or instrumented it, learned how it worked, protected the activity from public view, and converted the resulting knowledge into military advantage.

We have those programs.

They involved Soviet aircraft.

That does not mean an alleged recovered non-human vehicle would leave exactly the same trace as a MiG. An unknown propulsion system would create different scientific problems. A genuinely anomalous material might need different laboratories. An object of uncertain origin could trigger much tighter compartmentation. A program run through waived Special Access Programs, intelligence compartments, or contractor facilities might present different archival seams. The analogy has limits, and those limits are part of the book.

But the analogy does one thing extremely well: it destroys the lazy version of secrecy.

People often argue about hidden programs as if secrecy were a binary variable. Either a program is public, in which case it can be discussed, or it is secret, in which case the absence of evidence tells us nothing. That is not how physical programs behave. Secrecy changes where evidence appears. It does not repeal logistics.

A secret document can be locked in a safe. A secret airplane still needs tires.

That sentence will recur because it is the narrow hinge of the argument. Physical programs have a metabolism. They consume things, occupy places, create risks, generate failures, need skilled labor, and produce decisions downstream. The more exotic the hardware, the greater some of those burdens become. If an alleged program has persisted for decades while producing revolutionary propulsion, energy, materials, or field-control discoveries, then the evidentiary question is not merely whether one whistleblower says it exists. The question is what kind of institutional metabolism such a program would require and what traces that metabolism should create.

The reverse problem matters too. Real secrecy can generate false alien stories.

Area 51 is the obvious example because the site hosted aircraft that would have looked impossible to an uninformed observer. U-2s flew higher than most people thought airplanes could. OXCART aircraft moved at extraordinary speeds and altitudes. Stealth prototypes had shapes that violated familiar expectations. Soviet MiGs flew in American airspace under conditions the public did not know existed. If a witness saw something strange near a classified range and received no honest explanation, the resulting story could be sincere and wrong at the same time.

That is a harder category than hoax.

It is also more common in intelligence history than many enthusiasts or debunkers want to admit.

A person can accurately report an extraordinary observation and misidentify its cause. A cleared employee can encounter a real compartment and infer the wrong mission. A genuine program can be surrounded by false explanations because the false explanations are safer to repeat. A rumor can contain authentic technical vocabulary while attaching it to the wrong object. An official denial can be literally true while strategically misleading. A classified project can be mistaken for another classified project because the people making the inference have access to only one edge of it.

This is why epistemology belongs inside the machinery rather than in an appendix.

Throughout the book I separate four evidence classes.

**Class 1: verified, declassified, or otherwise documented facts.** These include official Air Force histories, declassified exploitation reports, archival records, court or congressional records where relevant, surviving aircraft, acknowledged organizations, and primary documents whose provenance can be checked.

**Class 2: credible but incomplete reporting or testimony.** This includes participant recollections, serious investigative reporting, claims by appropriately placed officials, and historical reconstructions that fit known records but remain only partly documented.

**Class 3: disputed allegations without independent confirmation.** This is where modern crash-retrieval claims, alleged non-human origin, unnamed legacy programs, and specific reverse-engineering success claims remain unless stronger evidence appears.

**Class 4: speculative synthesis.** This is my own attempt to derive what we should expect if a hidden exploitation program exists, how secrecy changes evidence, and which experiments or records could move a claim between classes.

These categories are not moral rankings. A Class 2 witness may be telling the truth. A Class 1 government report may be incomplete or self-serving. The categories describe the kind of evidence, not the virtue of the person producing it.

The book is therefore neither a brief for extraterrestrial retrieval nor a brief against it.

It is a book about how to tell the difference between a secret that is physically real and a story that has learned to imitate secrecy.

The United States once hid enemy airplanes in Nevada and flew them against its own pilots.

We know that now.

The useful question is not simply how they kept the secret.

It is what they could not help leaving behind.
