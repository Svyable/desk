# HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY

The MiG-21 was not enough.

One captured type can answer questions about one machine. It cannot tell you whether the lessons generalize across an adversary's fleet. In 1969, the United States extended the same logic to the MiG-17 under programs known as HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY.

The aircraft looked older than the MiG-21 because it was older. That made it easy to underestimate.

In combat, old is not the same thing as harmless.

The MiG-17 could be dangerous precisely because of the fight it encouraged. A fast American fighter that accepted a slow, horizontal turning engagement could surrender the advantages built into its own design. The captured aircraft allowed U.S. pilots to discover that relationship in controlled conditions rather than for the first time over North Vietnam.

The program reportedly accumulated hundreds of sorties across the two MiG-17 efforts. The details matter less here than the pattern: after the first valuable exploitation, the government did not conclude that the intelligence problem had been solved. It built a repeatable practice.

That shift—from singular object to exploitation ecosystem—is central to evaluating any alleged retrieval program.

The first recovered object can be handled as an emergency. The second requires comparison. The third begins to demand taxonomy.

Are they the same design? Do components interchange? Are materials consistent? Do manufacturing methods imply one source or several? Does damage differ by recovery event? Are control systems standardized? Does one vehicle contain improvements absent from another? Can parts be cannibalized? Do failures recur? Does one specimen establish that an odd feature is intentional rather than accidental?

A program possessing several anomalous craft over decades should therefore become increasingly comparative.

That creates a new documentary signature.

Engineers will begin naming variants. They will create configuration records. They will distinguish specimen A from specimen B. They will argue about whether a result applies to the class or only to one damaged object. Storage and security procedures will become standardized. Specialized test equipment will be reused. A vocabulary will emerge because human organizations cannot repeatedly handle complex hardware without naming recurring problems.

HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY lived inside that mundane organizational truth.

The captured MiGs were not valuable because they were mysterious. They were valuable because mystery could be reduced faster by putting several real aircraft into an accumulating body of knowledge.

This is one reason the absence of stable technical nomenclature in many extraordinary retrieval narratives matters.

Public stories contain many code names, but code names are not taxonomy. A code name can identify a compartment without saying anything about the object. What we would expect from repeated exploitation is technical language that becomes boring: alloy designations, connector types, specimen numbers, fixture names, failure modes, calibration routines, test envelopes, contamination categories, repair procedures.

Boring language is one of the things real technology produces.

That does not mean such language would be public. It means that if authentic documents emerge, they should contain it.

A forged memo about an exotic craft often sounds like the person who wrote it wanted the reader to understand the mystery. A working engineering document is usually less considerate. It assumes local knowledge. It worries about a measurement, a tolerance, a serial number, a leak, a fixture, a timing sequence. The extraordinary object, if real, would become ordinary to the people whose job was to keep it from falling off the stand.

That ordinariness is a testable expectation.

The MiG exploitation programs also show why recovered hardware matters beyond hardware.

A real foreign system can falsify intelligence stereotypes. During the Cold War, American analysis had to resist two recurring temptations: assuming Soviet equipment was crude because it looked different, and assuming an unfamiliar capability was superior because it was secret and threatening. Direct exploitation forced a narrower judgment. This airplane is excellent here. It is weak there. This design choice makes sense under these constraints. This component is rugged. This cockpit is difficult. This tactic works until the opponent changes geometry.

That style of judgment is what we should want around anomalous claims.

The question is not whether recovered non-human technology would be “advanced.” Advanced is too vague to test. The questions are specific. Advanced in energy density? Manufacturing precision? control bandwidth? heat rejection? materials strength? computation? sensor fusion? acceleration tolerance? reliability? Maintainability?

A technology could be extraordinary in one dimension and surprisingly primitive in another. Human engineering already works that way.

HAVE DRILL helped turn a Soviet fighter from symbol into envelope.

If a future archive ever establishes an anomalous retrieval program, the most convincing documents may do the same thing.

They will make the impossible object smaller.
