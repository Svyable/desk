# What Real Reverse Engineering Leaves Behind

We can now list the residue without turning it into a checklist that answers itself.

A real physical exploitation program begins with custody. Something moves from one owner or place to another. That movement creates security, transport, jurisdiction, and provenance problems.

Then comes characterization. The object is photographed, measured, weighed, scanned, sampled, instrumented, or operated. Those actions create data and specialized equipment.

Then comes sustainment. If the object is used repeatedly, it needs maintenance. If it cannot be maintained, the program cannibalizes, fabricates, or stops.

Then comes risk. People handle unfamiliar systems. Failures occur. Safety rules grow around experience.

Then comes translation. The program either learns something useful or it does not. If it learns something, requirements, tactics, designs, or research priorities change downstream.

Then comes counterintelligence. Adversaries care about lost strategic hardware and about what the exploiter may have learned.

Finally comes historical decay. Participants retire. facilities change. documents are reviewed. Hardware enters museums or scrap streams. classification guides age. lawsuits, memoirs, archives, and FOIA requests expose seams.

None of these lanes is individually decisive. Together they create thickness.

Thickness is what the strongest extraordinary claims currently lack in public.

There is testimony. There are congressional statements. There are alleged program names. There are stories of materials. There are reports of denied access. There are historical UAP cases and modern sensor incidents. Some of this may eventually connect to a physical exploitation program.

But connection is the work still to be done.

The retrieval signature is not a demand for a press release. It is a demand that the world described by the claim behave like a world in which the claim is physically true.
