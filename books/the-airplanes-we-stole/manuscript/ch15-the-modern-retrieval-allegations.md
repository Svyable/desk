# The Modern Retrieval Allegations

In July 2023, former intelligence officer David Grusch testified publicly before the House Oversight Committee and repeated allegations that the U.S. government had concealed a multi-decade UAP crash-retrieval and reverse-engineering program. He said his claims were based on information supplied by people he considered knowledgeable and described obstacles to congressional oversight.

The testimony is significant because of the venue, Grusch's background, and the seriousness with which some members of Congress treated the allegations.

It is not physical proof.

That distinction has been difficult to preserve in public argument. Skeptics sometimes treat secondhand testimony as worthless. It is not. Intelligence investigations often begin with testimony. Believers sometimes treat credentialed testimony as if the credential transfers to the underlying claim. It does not.

The correct category is Class 2 for the fact that serious, plausibly placed people have made the allegations, while the claimed recovered non-human hardware remains Class 3 absent independent confirmation.

This separation protects both possibilities. If the witnesses are substantially correct, the book has not dismissed them simply because they could not bring classified material to a hearing. If they are mistaken, the book has not transformed access and sincerity into a spacecraft.

The foreign-materiel control case now gives us better questions to ask.

How many alleged retrievals? Over what period? Which custody organizations? Which contractors? What kind of test infrastructure? What safety history? What scientific specialties? What downstream programs? What foreign intelligence response? Which contemporaneous records existed before the modern UAP network began circulating the same claims?

A mature allegation should become more specific under those questions.

If it only becomes more secret, the evidentiary problem remains.
