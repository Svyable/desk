# The Human Cost of Secret Hardware

Three people died in accidents connected to the Red Eagles program, according to the National Museum of the United States Air Force: Navy Lieutenant Melvin Brown, Air Force Captain Mark Postai, and Technical Sergeant Rey Hernandez.

Secrecy did not exempt the program from gravity.

This matters because risk is another trace physical programs cannot fully erase. Experimental and foreign aircraft create unfamiliar hazards. Operators lack the manufacturer's support ecosystem. Training information may be incomplete. Spare aircraft are scarce. Pressure to preserve a unique asset can conflict with the need to learn its limits. Every decision about an unknown machine contains both intelligence value and bodily risk.

Accidents create records even when the mission is classified. They create families who know someone died, units that lose personnel, medical and mortuary processes, safety investigations, wreckage handling, replacement staffing, changed procedures, and sometimes unexplained official narratives.

An alleged long-running anomalous exploitation program should therefore have a safety history. If people operate or energize unfamiliar technology, what injuries occur? If nothing is ever powered, what chemical, radiological, biological, electrical, or mechanical hazards arise during examination? What protective equipment is peculiar? Which facilities need unusual monitoring? Which occupational specialties appear repeatedly?

These questions can be abused. An unexplained illness near a classified site is not evidence of alien hardware. The direction of inference matters. We start from an independently supported program and ask what risk traces it produced; we do not start from any strange injury and use it to manufacture a program.

The same discipline applies to secrecy. A fatality does not necessarily declassify what killed the person. But over decades, accidents can create archival seams because institutions must learn from them. Real hidden programs are not only collections of secrets. They are organizations attempting to keep human beings alive while doing things ordinary safety systems were not designed to supervise.

The bodies are part of the record.
