# Three Government Answers

The U.S. government has, at different times, given three answers about modern anomalous health incidents that sound contradictory until their scopes are separated.

The first answer was clinical: people were sick.

The State Department created a response apparatus, arranged specialized medical evaluations, established care coordination, and eventually implemented compensation mechanisms authorized by the HAVANA Act. Officials repeatedly emphasized that uncertainty about cause did not negate the reality of employees' symptoms.

The second answer was mechanistic: pulsed electromagnetic energy was plausible for a subset.

The 2020 National Academies committee examined possible mechanisms and concluded that directed pulsed RF energy appeared to be the most plausible mechanism for important features of the cases it reviewed, while acknowledging substantial data limitations and heterogeneity. The 2022 Intelligence Community Experts Panel reached a related but narrower conclusion: a subset of incidents could not be easily explained by known environmental or medical conditions, and pulsed electromagnetic energy plausibly explained core characteristics, with information gaps.

The third answer was attributive: a foreign campaign was very unlikely.

The March 2023 Intelligence Community Assessment said most agencies judged that a foreign adversary was very unlikely to be responsible for the reported AHIs. Agencies found no credible evidence that a foreign actor possessed a collection system causing the incidents in the way initially hypothesized, and the large body of cases increasingly appeared heterogeneous rather than manifestations of one unique syndrome or mechanism.

Those answers can coexist.

A patient can have a genuine illness without investigators knowing its initiating cause. A mechanism can fit some reported features without evidence that it was deployed. An adversary can possess relevant science without having executed a global operation. And a global operation can be unlikely while one or a few individual cases remain unresolved.

The analytical failure begins when a statement from one layer is promoted into another.

Care becomes proof of attack.

Plausibility becomes proof of device.

Failure to attribute becomes proof of psychogenesis.

Absence of one global campaign becomes proof that no hostile incident ever occurred.

Moscow helps because it contains all three layers in historical form. The exposure was verified. The health consequences were uncertain. The purpose was disputed. Had officials treated any one layer as controlling the others, they would have produced a simpler story and a worse investigation.

The modern government record is frustrating precisely because it became more disciplined over time. The conclusions narrowed. The confidence levels appeared. The aggregate separated from the subset. That process feels like retreat if one expects government to reveal the answer. It looks more like science if one expects hypotheses to lose territory when evidence fails to arrive.

There is still a political problem.

Employees who suffered persistent symptoms can reasonably hear 'very unlikely foreign adversary' as disbelief, especially after years in which officials publicly entertained hostile mechanisms. Government agencies created that trust problem by communicating shifting judgments without always explaining the level of inference that changed.

The fix is not certainty.

The fix is better labels.
