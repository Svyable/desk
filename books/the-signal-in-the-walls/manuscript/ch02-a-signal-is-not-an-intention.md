# A Signal Is Not an Intention

Intelligence work has a bad habit of treating verbs as evidence.

Beam. Target. Illuminate. Interrogate. Attack.

Only one of those words may be physically observed. The others can be stories smuggled into grammar.

The Soviets transmitted microwave energy toward the U.S. Embassy. That much is documented. But even a directional transmission does not tell us whether the human beings in the building were the intended receiver. Antennas are directional because engineering likes gain. Radars illuminate objects they do not wish to injure. Communication systems point at apertures. Counterintelligence systems can interrogate hardware. A transmitter aimed at a building may be aimed at an antenna on the roof, a resonant cavity in a wall, a bug hidden behind plaster, or nobody in particular if the geometry is crude.

The declassified U.S. record preserved several candidate explanations. One was biological: perhaps the signal altered behavior, health, or performance. Another was electronic interference. Another was that the energy somehow activated or interrogated clandestine listening devices already planted in the chancery. The existence of actual bugs made the last category painfully concrete.

This distinction matters because the modern directed-energy debate often begins with the phenomenology of the victim. Someone reports a directional sensation or sound, followed by neurological symptoms. Investigators then search for a physical mechanism capable of producing those sensations. If pulsed radiofrequency energy can generate auditory perception, then an RF source becomes plausible. And once the mechanism is plausible, the source becomes a weapon. The syllogism feels tight because each sentence contains a true noun.

But capability is not event attribution.

We know microwave auditory effects are real. Allan Frey's early human experiments and later work by Foster, Finch, Guy, Lin, and others established that brief microwave pulses can produce perceived clicks or related auditory sensations through thermoelastic generation of pressure waves in tissue. That fact deserves to survive ridicule. It does not, by itself, establish that an embassy employee who heard a strange sound was exposed to microwaves.

Moscow offers the reverse problem. Here we have measured microwave exposure. Yet the exposure did not automatically establish a weapon.

This is a useful inversion. It prevents the investigator from believing that the hardest part is finding an exotic-enough mechanism. In many intelligence cases the hard part is connecting mechanism, source, target, dose, timing, biological response, and intent into one custody chain.

A source can be hostile without being a weapon. A surveillance system can be aggressive. A bug-interrogation beam can violate sovereignty. An intelligence service may accept incidental human exposure it does not consider clinically important. It may even exploit the fact that the target will misinterpret the signal. None of those scenarios requires a neurological attack, and none makes the transmitter benign.

This is where moral intuition can distort technical inference. People reasonably want the cause of an injury to have the shape of the injury. If a person is harmed, we search for an instrument designed to harm. But systems produce side effects. Counterintelligence equipment can create occupational exposures. Jammers can interfere with medical devices. Lasers meant for sensors can strike eyes. A weapon can also masquerade as something else. Intent is an additional variable, not a property emitted with the photons.

The disciplined question is therefore not: was the Moscow Signal malicious?

The Soviet Union was conducting clandestine activity against a diplomatic facility. Malice is too broad to help.

The better question is: what function best explains the measured parameters, geometry, persistence, technical context, and Soviet behavior when challenged?

That question is slower. It also has a chance of being right.
