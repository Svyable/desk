# The Purpose Problem

The Moscow Signal lasted too long to be explained by embarrassment.

Once American officials knew the embassy was being illuminated, they protested. Dean Rusk raised the matter with Soviet foreign minister Andrei Gromyko during the 1967 Glassboro summit. The Soviet side did not provide a satisfying explanation. The transmissions continued. In the mid-1970s additional or stronger signals were detected, and the issue became harder to contain inside classified channels.

Persistence is evidence, but it is ambiguous evidence.

A health-effects weapon aimed at diplomats could explain persistence. So could a surveillance system that remained technically useful. So could a countermeasure directed at U.S. electronics. So could a system whose political cost the Soviets considered negligible because the Americans were reluctant to publicize it.

The purpose problem is where analysts are tempted to reason backward from the most dramatic capability. If biology is possible, biology becomes intent. If espionage is already documented, espionage becomes intent. Each interpretation recruits the evidence it prefers.

A better method is to ask what observations should differ between hypotheses.

A biological weapon intended to alter human performance should be optimized around occupied spaces, human exposure patterns, and biologically effective parameters. A device-interrogation system should correlate with target hardware, technical operations, antenna geometry, and perhaps activity in particular rooms or frequencies. A jammer should correlate with communications use. A reconnaissance emitter might move or change as defensive equipment changes.

The public record is not sufficient to execute that test completely. Redactions remain. Technical details remain classified or were never preserved in accessible form. But posing the hypotheses correctly prevents the archive from becoming a Rorschach test.

The bugging context deserves more weight than it often receives. In 1964 U.S. investigators found multiple clandestine listening devices embedded in embassy walls, including sensitive areas. Soviet technical intelligence had already demonstrated both access and sophistication. Declassified U.S. memoranda later explicitly considered whether some of the microwave signals could be used to activate or interrogate audio devices.

That is not proof of function. It is a concrete alternative with a known operational ecosystem.

This distinction becomes more important when comparing Moscow to Havana. In Havana, investigators had symptom clusters and reports of unusual auditory or pressure sensations but no publicly confirmed contemporaneous measurement of a directed RF field at the moment of injury. The reasoning therefore often ran from effect to possible cause. In Moscow, investigators could run from measured cause candidate to possible effect.

The two inferential directions should not be blurred.

If Moscow teaches anything, it is that a real adversarial signal can invite the wrong purpose story for years. A future directed-energy incident could do the same. The transmitter may be a weapon. It may also be an intelligence system whose human effects are incidental, misunderstood, or exploited after discovery.

The adversary gets a vote.

Physics gets the final one.
