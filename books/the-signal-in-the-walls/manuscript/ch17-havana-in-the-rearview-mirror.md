# Havana in the Rearview Mirror

The old story came back before the new one had a name.

In late 2016, American personnel in Havana began reporting sudden, unusual sensory and neurological symptoms. Some described pressure, sound, pain, dizziness, tinnitus, visual disturbance, or cognitive difficulty. Similar reports later appeared among U.S. personnel in other countries. The phrase “Havana Syndrome” entered public language before investigators had established a syndrome, a mechanism, or an attacker. The government later adopted the more neutral term anomalous health incidents.

Moscow was waiting in the archive.

Once directed radio-frequency energy became one proposed explanation for at least some of the new cases, the historical analogy seemed almost irresistible. A foreign power had once directed microwave energy at an American embassy. The United States had secretly studied biological effects. Embassy employees had been kept uninformed. Decades later, diplomats were again reporting strange symptoms in adversarial environments.

The analogy is real.

It is also dangerous.

Historical precedent can tell us what kinds of operations states have actually conducted. It cannot identify the cause of a new event merely because the events share a vocabulary.

The Moscow Signal is strong evidence that a foreign intelligence service can deliberately illuminate a diplomatic facility with radio-frequency energy for years. It is evidence that U.S. officials have previously worried about biological consequences of such exposure. It is evidence that secrecy around an unexplained exposure can damage later trust.

It is not evidence that the Havana cases were caused by the same type of transmitter, the same waveform, the same state, or even radio-frequency energy at all.

That boundary matters more now because the official assessment has become more complicated, not less.

In December 2020, a National Academies committee reviewing illnesses reported by U.S. government employees and their families concluded that directed, pulsed radio-frequency energy appeared to be the most plausible mechanism for a distinctive subset of reported cases, while also emphasizing uncertainty and the heterogeneity of the broader population. The finding was never equivalent to attribution. A mechanism judged plausible is not an identified device, an operator, or a state sponsor.

The intelligence question evolved separately.

In March 2023, the U.S. Intelligence Community assessed that it was very unlikely a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported anomalous health incidents overall. That judgment rested on intelligence collection, analysis of possible mechanisms, and medical evidence. It was not a declaration that every patient had imagined symptoms. It was an attribution assessment.

Then, on January 10, 2025, the Intelligence Community published another update. Most components continued to assess that foreign-adversary responsibility for the events overall was very unlikely. Years of collection had not surfaced compelling intelligence tying a foreign actor to a specific incident. Most components also assessed it very unlikely that a foreign actor possessed a novel weapon responsible for even a subset.

But unanimity weakened at the edge.

One component judged there was roughly an even chance that a foreign actor had used a novel weapon or prototype device to harm a small, undetermined subset of personnel. Another judged there was roughly an even chance that a foreign actor had developed a capability that could have harmed a subset, while still considering actual deployment unlikely and expressing low confidence. One component assessed it likely that a foreign actor possessed a radio-frequency antipersonnel capability capable of producing biological effects consistent with some reported symptoms. These were minority judgments inside an assessment whose overall conclusion remained skeptical of foreign responsibility.

That is an awkward intelligence product.

It is also exactly the kind of product this book has been preparing us to read.

“Most components say very unlikely” and “one component leaves roughly even odds for a small subset” are not mutually exclusive. Intelligence agencies can agree about the aggregate and disagree about the tail. The disagreement does not prove the minority view. The majority does not erase it.

The 2025 assessment also said that medical analysis had not identified a consistent set of physical injuries across those reporting incidents, while explicitly acknowledging that affected personnel experienced genuine symptoms and suffering. Again, these are separate propositions. The absence of a common injury signature does not mean people were not ill. It weakens theories that predict one common external mechanism for the whole population.

This distinction has been difficult to preserve in public discussion.

If officials question foreign attribution, patients may hear that their symptoms are being denied.

If clinicians validate symptoms, advocates may hear confirmation of an attack.

If scientists say a physical mechanism is plausible, journalists may convert plausibility into cause.

If intelligence agencies say they cannot rule out a small subset, the residual uncertainty can become the headline.

Moscow created the same category errors in an earlier vocabulary.

There, a real exposure became evidence of suspected harm. Suspected harm became evidence of suspected intent. Secret research into the possibility became evidence that officials “knew.” The later epidemiology, because it did not produce a clean verdict, became available to both sides.

Havana arrived in an information environment already trained to make those jumps.

The comparison is most useful where it is least cinematic: in study design.

Both controversies suffer from the problem of an uncertain exposure. In Moscow, there was at least a measured external radio-frequency field. Investigators knew the building was being illuminated even if purpose and individual dose were unclear. In many anomalous health incidents, the causal exposure itself has not been independently measured at the time of the reported event. That makes retrospective mechanism testing much harder.

A suspected microwave event without recorded field data leaves investigators inferring a source from symptoms, witness reports, device feasibility, and intelligence. This is an inverse problem. Many causes can map onto overlapping symptoms.

The stronger the proposed mechanism, the more one wants contemporaneous instrumentation.

Spectrum recording.

Acoustic recording.

Environmental sensors.

Precise location and orientation.

Timing.

Medical examination immediately after onset.

Preserved biological samples.

Controls from people in the same environment who experienced no event.

The Moscow episode demonstrates what happens when those records are incomplete. Havana shows how much harder the question becomes when the exposure is not even established first.

The medical care problem should therefore be separated from causal certainty from the beginning.

Congress and federal agencies eventually did this more explicitly for anomalous health incidents. A 2024 Government Accountability Office review examined access to military health care for affected personnel and family members. It found practical problems in care coordination and the federal registry intended to support treatment and research. Of 334 patients who had qualified for care in the Military Health System in January 2024, only 33 had been entered in the AHI registry by May 2024, in part because of consent and interagency implementation problems.

That is a soberingly familiar detail.

The Moscow story contained poor individualized exposure records and fragmented medical information. Decades later, another disputed government health problem was again producing incomplete longitudinal data because agencies had not yet built a clean common system.

One need not believe the two events share a mechanism to see the institutional recurrence.

Care systems are often built after controversy begins.

Research data are then assembled from a care system that was not designed as a study.

Consent arrives late.

Agency boundaries fragment records.

The missing data later become part of the causal argument.

This is avoidable.

A government can treat people aggressively and respectfully while maintaining uncertainty about attribution. It can preserve standardized data without requiring every patient to subscribe to one causal theory. It can fund mechanism research without calling that research proof of an attack. It can investigate adversary capabilities without implying that possession equals use.

The Moscow government failed to separate some of these tasks.

The modern government has done better in places and repeated old mistakes in others.

There is another reason to keep Havana in the rearview mirror rather than make it the destination of this book. The evidence base is still evolving. Intelligence assessments can change with new collection. Medical cohorts mature. Classified records may be declassified. New technical studies may constrain feasible mechanisms. A book that treats the latest uncertainty as a solved historical fact will age badly.

The January 2025 Intelligence Community assessment itself includes explicit language about how its judgments could be wrong. That is an important feature, not a weakness. Analysts identify conditions under which new intelligence could alter the assessment.

An anomalous subject needs those conditions written down.

What would materially strengthen a directed-energy explanation for a subset of anomalous health incidents?

Contemporaneous detection of a physically plausible field during a well-documented event.

Recovered or intercepted hardware with operational relevance.

Intelligence linking an adversary unit to a particular incident.

A reproducible injury or biomarker pattern that maps to controlled exposure under matching parameters.

Independent replication of the mechanism.

A dose-response relation.

What would weaken it?

High-quality environmental monitoring across suspected incidents that repeatedly finds no plausible signal where the proposed mechanism requires one.

A medical pattern better explained by heterogeneous ordinary causes.

Reliable intelligence showing alleged adversary programs lacked the relevant capability or access.

Failure to reproduce proposed biological mechanisms under realistic constraints.

The point is not to demand impossible proof before acting.

The point is to make the thesis mortal.

Moscow’s history is valuable because some claims did die. The early primate-performance concern weakened after the exposure number was corrected. The broad epidemiological expectation of a large, obvious disease burden did not materialize convincingly. The biological-weapon theory never acquired the public evidence needed to outrank technical espionage.

Other claims survived. The beam was real. The secrecy was real. The blood sampling under a cover explanation was real. The diplomatic protests were real. The uncertainty was real.

Havana should be read with the same appetite for separation.

One of the worst things historical analogy can do is compress unlike cases into a lineage. Moscow becomes “the first Havana.” Havana becomes proof that the Soviets had the technology. Later reports become proof of a decades-long hidden weapon program. Each uncertain event lends certainty backward and forward until the chain looks stronger than any individual link.

That is not accumulation of evidence.

It is accumulation of resemblance.

Resemblance can generate hypotheses. It cannot substitute for mechanism and attribution.

There is also a strategic danger in getting this wrong in either direction.

Over-attributing ambiguous symptoms to a foreign weapon can escalate conflict, reward disinformation, and divert attention from medical care or ordinary causes. Under-attributing a genuine covert capability can leave personnel exposed and teach an adversary that ambiguity works.

Those risks are not symmetric in every case. They are both real.

The policy answer cannot be “believe everything” or “believe nothing.” It has to be instrumentation, care, intelligence collection, experimental replication, and disciplined public language.

That sounds less satisfying than a revelation because it is a program of work rather than a verdict.

The Moscow Signal lasted long enough to show the cost of waiting for a verdict before building the program.

The most important historical precedent is not that microwaves once struck an embassy.

It is that uncertainty, badly governed, became part of the injury.
