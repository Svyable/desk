# The Embassy as Laboratory

Once a government realizes its people are being exposed to something it does not understand, every ethical choice becomes entangled with an intelligence choice.

Do you tell them?

If you tell them, the adversary may learn you detected the signal. Employees may alter where they sit, how they work, or what symptoms they notice. Diplomatic pressure may force the source to change before measurements are complete. Countermeasures may reveal your instrumentation.

If you do not tell them, you are collecting data from unwitting human exposure.

The Moscow embassy lived inside that collision.

American officials monitored the fields, debated health implications, and launched research while many embassy employees remained unaware of the exposure. Years later, when Ambassador Walter Stoessel pushed for stronger action and personnel were finally briefed, the disclosure did not merely reveal a technical fact. It revealed that the government had made a judgment about who was allowed to know a risk that entered their workplace.

This is the point where secrecy begins changing the evidence rather than merely hiding it.

An ordinary occupational exposure study starts by defining the exposed population, measuring dose, establishing outcomes, and identifying a comparison group. Moscow had none of that cleanly at the outset. Exposure varied by location. The signal changed over time. Personnel rotated in and out. People had different baseline health states. Some worked near other radiofrequency sources. Records were fragmented across diplomatic, intelligence, defense, and medical systems.

Then came the politics of notification.

Before disclosure, symptom reporting is less likely to be conditioned by knowledge of the exposure. That can be useful epidemiologically, but only if investigators are already collecting standardized health data, which they were not in the way a modern prospective study would require. After disclosure, reporting changes. People reinterpret old symptoms. Anxiety rises. Physicians look harder. Newspapers call. Lawsuits and compensation questions appear. None of this makes symptoms unreal. It changes the observation process.

A good investigator has to model the observation process too.

That is one reason the Moscow Signal remains such a potent control case for later AHI claims. In Havana and elsewhere, awareness of earlier incidents spread rapidly through government communities. Reporting guidance expanded. The denominator changed. The threshold for classifying an experience as suspicious changed. The medical system learned a new diagnostic category while intelligence agencies learned a new threat category. If incidence rises after awareness rises, that can mean the attack expanded. It can also mean detection expanded. Those hypotheses are not morally equivalent, but statistically they compete.

Moscow shows that governments can create this ambiguity even when the exposure itself is real.

The embassy also forces a difficult distinction between care and attribution. Employees deserved health monitoring regardless of whether the beam was a weapon. In later AHI cases, the U.S. government eventually moved toward the same principle: provide care and compensation pathways without requiring a solved adversary attribution. That is administratively awkward but scientifically correct. Medicine answers what happened to a person. Intelligence asks who did what and why. Those questions overlap. They are not identical.

The ugly lesson is that a secret exposure can turn a workplace into a laboratory without anybody deciding to call it one.

The better lesson is that the mistake can be prevented. Measure first. Preserve raw data. Establish baselines. Separate clinical care from causal labeling. Use personal dosimetry where technically possible. Archive instrument settings. Record negative exposures, not just dramatic events. And tell people enough to protect them without pretending uncertainty is knowledge.

The Moscow embassy did not have that infrastructure.

We do now.
