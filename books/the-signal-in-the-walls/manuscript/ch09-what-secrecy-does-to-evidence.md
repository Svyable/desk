# What Secrecy Does to Evidence

Classification does not merely subtract information from the public record. It changes the ecology in which explanations compete.

Imagine three groups looking at the same event.

The first group knows there is a signal but not its suspected purpose. The second knows the suspected purpose but not the health data. The third knows the health data but not the counterintelligence operation. Each group is rational inside its compartment. Each produces an incomplete story. Years later, fragments leak or declassify. Outsiders mistake contradiction between compartments for proof that somebody lied about the whole thing.

Sometimes somebody did lie. But compartmentation can manufacture contradictory truth.

The Moscow Signal is an unusually clean example because the record accumulated across agencies with different missions. State worried about diplomats, Soviet relations, and workplace safety. CIA worried about espionage and technical threat. Defense agencies worried about biological effects and military implications. Scientists worried about experimental design. Embassy leadership worried about the people physically sitting in the building. Presidents worried about the geopolitical cost of confrontation.

There was no single institutional observer.

This is where conspiracy thinking gains genuine fuel. If an employee later learns that one office had known about an exposure another office denied knowing about, the government can look monolithic only from the outside. Secrecy makes ordinary bureaucratic fragmentation resemble coordinated deception.

The opposite error is to use fragmentation as an all-purpose excuse. Compartmentation does not erase accountability. If officials withhold a material occupational hazard from exposed personnel, the fact that the decision emerged from several agencies does not make the consequence less real.

For investigators, the practical lesson is source genealogy.

Every claim needs a parent.

Was this statement based on an instrument reading, an analyst's interpretation, a medical report, a witness recollection, a press story, or another memo quoting an earlier memo? Are five documents five independent observations, or five descendants of one uncertain measurement? Did a later official have access to more evidence, or merely more confidence?

Modern AHI reporting makes this essential. A journalist may quote a former intelligence officer who heard from a colleague that a foreign service possessed a particular device. A congressional witness may repeat the report. An internal memo may then cite the hearing as evidence of concern. Without genealogy, one rumor becomes three sources.

Moscow offers the healthier model because the physical signal generated instrument traces independent of narrative. The documents could be wrong about purpose while still being anchored to a measured exposure.

That is the gold standard for this field.

Not trust.

Not skepticism.

An independent channel that does not care what anyone believes.
