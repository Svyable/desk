# The Wrong Number

There is a special kind of scientific mistake that begins before the experiment.

The apparatus can be calibrated. The animals can be trained. The observers can follow protocol. The statistics can be performed correctly. The entire exercise can still be pointed at the wrong world because one number upstream is wrong.

Project PANDORA inherited that problem from Moscow.

The early American biological concern relied in part on estimates of the microwave field reaching the embassy. The surviving ARPA record shows that improved on-site measurements later revised those estimates downward. The corrected signal was weak enough that the behavioral degradation seen or suspected under an earlier condition did not appear when primates were tested at the more representative level.

This detail deserves its own chapter because almost every later argument about secret science contains some version of it.

Intelligence is not a laboratory input by nature. It has to be made into one.

A field officer reports a signal. A technical team estimates power density. Somebody converts units. Somebody decides whether the number is a peak, an average, or a conservative upper bound. Somebody extracts a waveform. Somebody writes a requirement for the experimental contractor. By the time the information reaches the laboratory, it may look like a clean parameter: expose at X milliwatts per square centimeter with Y modulation for Z minutes.

The cleanliness is an artifact of the handoff.

The underlying measurement may be messy. The antenna orientation may have been uncertain. The field may vary across a room. The signal may be intermittent. The equipment may have been calibrated under different conditions. There may be several transmitters. Reflections may alter local intensity. The target country may deliberately change the emission when it knows Americans are measuring.

Every one of those uncertainties can disappear when a program manager types one number into a specification.

That transformation is useful. Science needs parameters. It is also dangerous because the precision of the number can conceal the uncertainty of its origin.

The Moscow case gives us an unusually clean demonstration of the consequences. At the higher assumed field strength, a biological effect looked more plausible. At the corrected lower field strength, the specific primate-performance concern weakened. The dose-response logic is exactly what one would want science to respect. The trouble is that policy interest does not always shrink as fast as a parameter.

Programs develop momentum.

By the time a measurement is corrected, contracts exist, researchers have hypotheses, reports have circulated, and senior officials have been briefed that a potential adversary capability is under investigation. A null follow-up does not erase the first briefing from memory. It becomes “mixed results.” Mixed results can sustain a program much longer than a clean negative.

This is not necessarily bureaucratic bad faith. If the real-world signal remains unexplained, one null result does not eliminate every concern. But institutional momentum changes the burden. A question that once needed evidence to begin may later need stronger evidence to stop.

There is another problem: the wrong number can create a literature.

Suppose the initial exposure level produces a real biological effect because it is high enough to cause local heating. Researchers publish or circulate the finding. Later, the operational signal is found to be ten or twenty times weaker. The original experiment remains scientifically valid for the condition tested. It is simply less relevant to the threat. Decades afterward, a reader encounters the result without the measurement history and concludes that “the Pentagon proved the Moscow signal affected behavior.”

The laboratory fact has been detached from the intelligence correction.

This is how technical folklore forms.

A similar process occurs in weapons estimates. A missile’s range is initially overestimated. War plans are adjusted. Later measurements revise it. The early worst-case number survives in public discourse because it was attached to the dramatic moment. The correction lives in a quieter document.

PANDORA’s corrected dosimetry should therefore be treated as a major finding, not an administrative footnote.

It also complicates accusations that American officials ignored the biological evidence. At least part of the program responded exactly as one would hope: when better measurements became available, investigators changed the exposure condition and reported the less alarming result. That does not absolve the government’s disclosure failures or prove that all health concerns were handled well. It matters because the archive should be allowed to contain competent behavior too.

History becomes useless when every action is pre-sorted into prosecution or defense.

The wrong-number problem did not end in 1967 because the signal itself did not remain static. The newer transmissions detected in 1975 had different characteristics and, according to State Department descriptions, sometimes higher measured intensities and longer daily duration. A health assessment that looked reassuring for the older signal could not simply be copied forward without asking whether the new exposure still fit the tested envelope.

This is one reason Ambassador Walter Stoessel’s concern increased.

A 1975 embassy cable noted that earlier Project PANDORA work had been negative in the sense that the old TUMS levels appeared well below current U.S. safety standards. But the appearance of MUTS created a need for more precise data. The cable did not claim that the new signal was injuring employees. It recognized that the premise had changed.

A later CIA summary described the medical expert’s judgment in terms more cautious than either side of the controversy usually remembers. Current knowledge did not permit an unequivocal statement that no hazard existed, but the probability of hazard at the measured level was considered very low. After exposure duration increased and signal characteristics changed, the same general expert group judged the possibility of a health hazard to have increased.

This is what scientific uncertainty looks like when written by officials who have to act.

“Very low probability” is not “impossible.”

“Possibility increased” is not “injury established.”

Those distinctions are fragile under political pressure. Employees hear the first caveat. Diplomats hear the second. Journalists hear “microwave hazard.” Scientists hear insufficient data. Lawyers hear foreseeability.

The wrong-number problem becomes a communication problem because the public wants one quantity called safe.

Safety is conditional.

A safety statement can be robust if the mechanism is well established and exposure well characterized. For the Moscow case, both were contested at low levels. American standards reflected one interpretation of the evidence. Soviet limits reflected another. The signal varied. Exposure duration expanded. The government had incomplete individual dosimetry. A responsible statement therefore had to be narrower than the audience wanted.

Officials sometimes responded by making it broader.

That is understandable and dangerous.

When institutions fear panic, they often remove caveats to make reassurance effective. The irony is that hidden caveats later destroy reassurance when discovered. The Moscow Signal is a textbook case. If employees had been told early that an unusual low-level microwave signal was being monitored, that no established harm was expected at measured levels, and that research continued because uncertainties remained, the government might have endured short-term anxiety and retained long-term credibility. By withholding the problem, it made every later uncertainty look like evidence of concealment.

This is not to say early disclosure would have been easy. The transmission itself was entangled with sensitive counterintelligence. Revealing exactly what the Americans could detect might have told the Soviets what their equipment could evade. Publicly identifying suspected eavesdropping methods could compromise American countermeasures. A diplomatic confrontation might have affected broader negotiations. There were legitimate secrecy interests.

The failure was not that secrecy existed. It was that secrecy had biological consequences for people whose bodies were part of the unknown.

The wrong number had a human version.

The government did not know each person’s true cumulative exposure. It knew the building was being illuminated. It had measurements from particular points and periods. It knew assignments and could later reconstruct who had served there. But a personal dose history is not the same as a posting history.

When the epidemiologists arrived, they inherited categories instead of dosimeters.

That makes the later Lilienfeld study difficult to interpret. The study compared personnel assigned to Moscow with employees at other Eastern European posts and examined mortality and morbidity. It was an impressive attempt to extract signal from a natural experiment that had never been designed as one. Yet exposure misclassification remained baked in. “Moscow” was a proxy for an electromagnetic dose that varied across place and time.

If an effect depended strongly on dose, proxy exposure could wash it out.

If there were no effect, the same imperfect classification could still generate scattered associations by chance.

This asymmetry allows both sides to continue arguing. Critics can say the epidemiology was too blunt to detect a subtle effect. Defenders can say no convincing coherent pattern emerged despite a large effort. Both statements can be partly true.

The solution would be a better exposure reconstruction.

A modern research project could, in principle, revisit the archive with geospatial and computational tools unavailable in the 1970s. Digitize floor plans. Map transmitter directions by date. Recover surviving spectrum logs. Model propagation through the chancery structure. Link office and residential assignments to time periods. Estimate distributions rather than assign binary exposed/unexposed status. Then test whether health outcomes track modeled dose gradients rather than simply Moscow service.

The difficulty is data survival. Records may be incomplete, classified, lost, or too coarse. Individual medical data raise privacy issues. The building changed. The transmitters are gone. A retrospective reconstruction can improve the picture without ever making it clean.

Still, the exercise would be valuable because it attacks the right problem.

The Moscow controversy has too often been fought at the level of worldview: government cover-up versus microwave hysteria, Soviet weapon versus harmless surveillance. Dosimetry is less glamorous. It asks where the person sat.

That question can defeat a grand theory.

If the strongest reported health outcomes do not correlate with the parts of the building and years receiving the strongest fields, a simple dose-causal model weakens. If they do correlate after controlling for plausible confounders, the health question becomes more interesting. If the signal characteristics associated with symptoms differ from those used in the animal experiments, the laboratory record must be reinterpreted. If shielding reduced the field but not symptom incidence, that matters.

These are testable consequences.

An esoteric subject becomes scientific when it can lose.

The same rule applies to the espionage hypothesis. If the microwave signal was interrogating passive listening devices, one would expect technical evidence connecting field parameters to device operation. Perhaps signal timing would correlate with high-value conversations or with occupancy. Perhaps recovered hardware would resonate in the observed band. Perhaps Soviet archives would reveal the architecture. Without those links, the hypothesis remains plausible but incomplete.

The biological-attack hypothesis needs even more. It requires not only a biological effect at the measured exposure but evidence of Soviet intent to produce it. A signal that accidentally creates an effect while serving another purpose is not a biological weapon. A Soviet research literature on microwave bioeffects is not an operational order. A low national safety standard is not a targeting doctrine.

Every added claim requires an added number.

This discipline is inconvenient for secret-history storytelling. The story becomes slower. It forces the author to stop at each inferential joint and ask whether the evidence crosses it.

But the wrong number in PANDORA teaches why that inconvenience is necessary.

The first experiment did not fail because the scientists were foolish. It became less relevant because the physical world was measured better.

That is science working.

The enduring problem was that the world kept changing faster than the assurance did.
