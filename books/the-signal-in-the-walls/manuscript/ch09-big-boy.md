# Big Boy

The aircraft carrier solved one problem by creating five others.

If the embassy exposure was difficult to characterize and impossible for the United States to control, perhaps a population already living around powerful microwave equipment could offer a comparison. Naval vessels were dense electromagnetic environments. Radar systems operated at powers far above the faint field measured in Moscow, though with different frequencies, pulse structures, geometries, and duty cycles. Sailors worked in defined jobs. Some had long occupational histories around transmitters. Others were new to the ship. Medical records existed.

Project Big Boy emerged from this logic.

Declassified PANDORA material describes plans to use personnel aboard the aircraft carrier USS Saratoga as a natural occupational comparison. Observers would obtain baseline measurements from selected crew members. Investigators could compare experienced sailors with microwave exposure from radar work against recruits without the same history, and could examine groups working above and below deck.

The appeal is obvious.

The confounding is too.

Radar exposure is not Moscow exposure.

A sailor working near a shipboard radar may experience intermittent high peak powers, occupational procedures, open-air conditions, different frequencies, different whole-body geometry, and different cumulative duration from a diplomat sitting behind an embassy window. A comparison can answer whether a broad category of microwave-exposed people shows some pattern. It cannot automatically validate a mechanism tied to the specific low-level continuous or modulated fields in Moscow.

This sounds like a technical objection. It is actually the core of the study design.

When a suspected hazard is rare or poorly measured, investigators often reach for proxy exposures. Uranium miners can teach something about radon. Radiologists can teach something about ionizing radiation. Radar workers can teach something about radio-frequency exposure. The proxy is valuable when the underlying causal agent and relevant dose metric are shared.

If the mechanism differs, the proxy can mislead.

Suppose microwave harm were purely thermal above a threshold. Radar workers with higher absorbed power might be a useful upper-bound population. If they showed no adverse effect under substantially greater relevant exposure, concern about the lower Moscow field would weaken.

Suppose instead the hypothesized effect depended on a particular low-frequency modulation imposed on a carrier. A high-power naval radar with a different pulse pattern might be biologically irrelevant despite delivering more total energy. “More microwave” would not mean “more of the causal exposure.”

This is the same mistake people make when comparing chemicals by volume instead of toxicity.

Big Boy therefore sits at an important fork in the PANDORA logic. Either investigators believed microwave dose in a broad physical sense was the main variable, in which case radar workers were an informative comparison, or they believed the Moscow waveform contained something special, in which case the carrier population could provide only limited background.

The historical program appears to have entertained both possibilities because the mechanism was unsettled.

That is reasonable exploratory science.

It is also a warning against treating the eventual results as a single definitive test.

The Saratoga comparison population came with social confounders. Sailors differ by age, physical fitness, occupation, sleep, noise exposure, heat stress, solvents, aviation fuel, smoking patterns, medical screening, and the countless environmental conditions of shipboard life. Above-deck and below-deck assignments are not random. Experienced crew differ from recruits in more than electromagnetic exposure. If one group reports headaches or performs differently on a test, microwave dose is only one candidate explanation.

Good study design can adjust for some of this. It cannot turn a carrier into a laboratory.

The attraction of Big Boy was scale and realism. The weakness was the same.

Large natural experiments often feel authoritative because the subjects are real workers rather than animals in cages. But realism adds uncontrolled variables. The most persuasive occupational epidemiology usually emerges when exposure contrast is strong, dose records are good, outcomes are specific, and alternative explanations can be measured. Microwave occupational studies have often struggled with at least one of those conditions.

The PANDORA team was operating before modern personal radio-frequency dosimetry. Job title and location could stand in for exposure. That may be adequate for gross differences and poor for subtle ones.

There is also a deeper mismatch between the policy question and the medical question.

The policy question was: Are people in the Moscow embassy at risk from a Soviet signal?

The Big Boy medical question was closer to: Do groups of sailors with different histories around naval microwave equipment show measurable differences?

A negative answer to the second can reassure only to the extent that the relevant biology overlaps.

This principle seems trivial when written out. It becomes surprisingly difficult to maintain once data are scarce. Decision-makers want any comparison. Researchers want a population. A carrier exists. A ship full of radar becomes, through administrative convenience, a model of an embassy.

The name Big Boy adds to the period strangeness, but the underlying move is still common. Modern investigators studying anomalous health incidents have also faced the problem of choosing controls when the exposure itself is unknown. If one does not know the causal agent, one cannot know what population is truly unexposed. One ends up comparing jobs, buildings, cities, or cohorts and hoping the hidden variable distributes itself usefully.

Sometimes it does.

Sometimes the control group is exposed to a different version of the same thing.

Sometimes both groups share the real cause.

Sometimes neither group does.

This is why exposure science is so much less glamorous than threat attribution and so much more important.

Big Boy also complicates the popular claim that PANDORA was a straightforward attempt to build a microwave weapon. The carrier study looks more like occupational medicine and hazard assessment. That does not mean the Pentagon had no interest in military applications of microwave bioeffects. Of course it did. Any reproducible effect on cognition, behavior, or physiology could have offensive as well as defensive relevance. But a program can simultaneously protect personnel and survey potential capabilities.

Dual use begins at the question.

If a field can impair a human operator, the military wants to know how to shield its people and whether an adversary could exploit the effect. The same dose-response curve serves both concerns. Secrecy around the defensive problem can later be interpreted as secrecy around a weapon-development effort even when the surviving experiment was primarily hazard-focused.

The correct posture is neither naïve nor conspiratorial: track the tasking.

Who funded the work? What did the statement of objectives ask? What endpoints were measured? What follow-on proposals emerged from positive findings? Were engineering teams tasked to build delivery systems? Were there operational requirements for inducing the effect in adversaries? Or did the work remain in biological assessment and protection?

For PANDORA, the public record clearly establishes defense interest in the biological question. It does not, by itself, establish an operational Soviet-style “brain weapon” built from those results.

Big Boy also reminds us that the American military had enormous indigenous reasons to study microwave exposure independent of Moscow.

Radar had transformed warfare. Ships, aircraft, air-defense networks, missile systems, and communications equipment created occupational environments unlike anything in civilian life a generation earlier. Questions about cataracts, fertility, nervous-system effects, and safe work practices would have existed even if the Soviet embassy signal had never been detected. Moscow concentrated attention on a field already becoming militarily important.

This matters when tracing causation from a secret event to a research program.

A document may say PANDORA funded a particular study because of Moscow. The broader scientific community may simultaneously be studying the same effect because radar use is expanding everywhere. Later histories can mistakenly turn all microwave-bioeffects research into an echo of one embassy mystery.

The field was larger than the signal.

The reverse mistake is also possible: because microwave safety research had ordinary occupational motivations, one can overlook the extraordinary intelligence context that shaped PANDORA’s questions. The program’s distinctiveness lay in the collision.

The aircraft carrier and the embassy were two institutions filled with Americans and radio-frequency energy for entirely different reasons.

One was irradiated by its own equipment in the course of operating a weapons platform.

The other was irradiated by an adversary whose purpose was unknown.

Biologically, the body may not care about intent.

Politically, intent is almost everything.

That split explains why health studies could never fully solve the Moscow affair. Even a perfect study showing zero excess illness would leave the espionage question. Even a perfect study showing a biological effect would leave the attribution question unless the Soviet purpose were independently established.

The investigation kept trying to make one dataset answer both.

There is a useful counterfactual here.

Imagine Big Boy had found a striking, dose-dependent neurological deficit among sailors with high radar exposure, reproduced across ships and tied to measured absorbed energy. The result would have transformed microwave safety. It would also have made the Moscow exposure more concerning, though still not proof of Soviet intent.

Imagine instead that the study found no meaningful differences despite sailors receiving substantially greater relevant exposure. That would weaken broad low-level hazard claims, but a modulation-specific advocate could still say the naval waveform was wrong.

This is how an unconstrained hypothesis survives every comparison: whenever the result is negative, the special parameter moves.

A strong research program eventually has to pin it down.

What waveform? What dose? What endpoint? What latency? What dose-response? What biological transducer?

Without those constraints, “microwave effect” can mean almost anything.

The PANDORA archive shows investigators trying to narrow the space and, at times, widening it again when the result disappointed. That is ordinary exploratory science under extraordinary pressure.

Big Boy’s greatest value may therefore be conceptual rather than diagnostic.

It reveals the desperation of comparison.

The American government had a real population in Moscow, a real signal, and no clean control. It went looking for bodies already living in the spectrum.

It found a carrier full of them.

The ocean did not make the comparison simple.
