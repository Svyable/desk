# The Signal in the Walls

*The Moscow Embassy Microwaves, Project PANDORA, and the Directed-Energy Problem*

Sven Hardy Benson

There are two lazy ways to tell a story about invisible weapons.

The first is to believe everything. A diplomat gets dizzy. A strange sound is reported. Somewhere in the literature is a paper about pulsed microwaves. Somewhere else is a Russian patent. Draw three arrows and suddenly the fog has become a weapon system.

The second is to believe nothing. Microwaves are familiar. Governments overreact. Symptoms are heterogeneous. Intelligence agencies make mistakes. Therefore the entire category is hysteria with better nouns.

Both positions save time. Neither survives Moscow.

For years the Soviet Union directed microwave energy at the U.S. Embassy. That is not a rumor. American technicians measured it. Senior officials argued about it. U.S. agencies launched secret research programs partly because they did not know what the signal was doing. They exposed animals. They examined sailors already working around radar. They worried about health, espionage, mind effects, and technical interrogation of hidden devices. They briefed presidents. They did not initially brief many of the people sitting in the beam.

And then, after all that, the simplest confident story still did not arrive.

This book is about that gap.

It is not a book arguing that Havana Syndrome was caused by microwaves. It is not a book arguing that Havana Syndrome was imaginary. It is not a book about a Soviet mind-control ray. It is a book about a much stranger and more durable fact: the United States possessed the thing conspiracy stories usually lack—a measured clandestine electromagnetic exposure—and still struggled to establish purpose, biological consequence, and attribution.

That is a better starting point than belief.
