# Havana

In 2016, U.S. personnel in Cuba began reporting episodes that did not fit comfortably into existing categories. Some described unusual sounds or sensations with directional or spatial qualities. Some developed dizziness, headache, cognitive complaints, visual problems, tinnitus, sleep disturbance, and other symptoms. Early medical descriptions compared portions of the presentation to concussion or mild traumatic brain injury even though many patients had no conventional head impact.

The phrase Havana Syndrome arrived because language hates a vacuum.

The government later adopted the broader term Anomalous Health Incidents, partly because reports spread beyond Cuba and partly because the existence of one syndrome with one cause was itself uncertain.

By then, the Moscow analogy was unavoidable.

Diplomats. A hostile or competitive foreign environment. Strange sensory experiences. Neurological complaints. A historical precedent involving microwave illumination of an embassy. A known microwave auditory effect. Soviet and Russian research on radiofrequency bioeffects. The pieces assembled themselves.

But an analogy is not an exposure record.

That is the central discipline this book asks of Havana.

The early cases deserved serious investigation because some features were unusual, clustered, and reportedly acute. The National Academies committee convened by the State Department concluded in 2020 that directed pulsed radiofrequency energy appeared to be a plausible mechanism for important features of the reported illnesses. In 2022, an Intelligence Community expert panel separately concluded that pulsed electromagnetic energy could plausibly explain the core characteristics of a subset of incidents, while also finding ultrasound plausible in limited close-access scenarios.

Those were mechanism assessments, not adversary attributions.

In March 2023, the Intelligence Community issued a very different kind of judgment: most agencies assessed that it was very unlikely a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported AHIs. The assessment said the evidence pointed away from an adversary, a single causal mechanism, or a unique syndrome across the large body of cases. It also acknowledged continuing information gaps and the difficulty of proving negatives in intelligence work.

People often present those conclusions as if one must cancel the other.

They answer different questions.

A physical mechanism can be capable of producing a pattern without having produced a particular event. A government can fail to find evidence of a foreign campaign even though a mechanism remains physically possible. A subset can remain unresolved while the aggregate changes the prior probability.

Moscow is what keeps the analysis honest in both directions.

There, the source field was measured. Yet the health-weapon interpretation remained uncertain. In Havana, the health reports were real enough to generate clinical and intelligence investigations, but the publicly available record lacks the same contemporaneous instrumented source.

The symmetry is almost cruel.

Moscow had the beam without the injury proof.

Havana had the injury reports without the beam proof.

The temptation is to use one case to complete the other.

The evidence does not permit that.
