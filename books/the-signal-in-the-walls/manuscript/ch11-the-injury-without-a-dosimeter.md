# The Injury Without a Dosimeter

Forensic medicine is at its strongest when the body preserves the agent.

Lead stays. Radiation can leave dosimetric traces. Certain toxins create metabolites. Infectious agents replicate. A bullet is rude enough to arrive as evidence.

A transient electromagnetic field may leave nothing so cooperative.

That creates the injury-without-a-dosimeter problem. If a brief radiofrequency exposure causes an acute effect but nobody was carrying an instrument capable of recording the relevant waveform, later investigators may have only symptoms, geometry, witness reports, and whatever secondary biological changes remain. Those can be important. They are also less specific.

The AHI cases exposed this weakness painfully. Many of the reported symptoms—headache, dizziness, sleep disturbance, cognitive difficulty, tinnitus, fatigue—have broad differential diagnoses. Even unusual acute sensory experiences do not uniquely identify a carrier. A directional sound could be acoustic, ultrasonic under some conditions, electromagnetic through a thermoacoustic mechanism, environmental, or perceptual. Chronic symptoms can outlast the initiating event and become shaped by vestibular compensation, migraine, autonomic effects, sleep loss, anxiety, medication, or rehabilitation history.

None of this means the patient imagined the illness.

It means symptom specificity is different from symptom severity.

Moscow had the opposite evidentiary imbalance. The government could measure an RF field but struggled to demonstrate a unique health signature attributable to it. A later State Department medical review reported no clear harmful health effects in former Moscow personnel, while noting some laboratory findings whose significance was uncertain. Again, the absence of a clean syndrome did not erase the exposure.

Together the cases define what a future investigation should collect.

First, raw environmental data with enough bandwidth to detect brief pulses rather than only time-averaged power. Second, synchronized timestamps. Third, location and geometry. Fourth, immediate clinical measurements before days of secondary physiology accumulate. Fifth, samples suitable for blinded biomarker research without assuming in advance what the biomarker must be. Sixth, negative controls from comparable personnel who did not report an event. Seventh, device metadata from phones, security systems, wireless infrastructure, and RF monitors.

The key is simultaneity.

A detector installed next week is valuable for prevention. It cannot prove what happened last Tuesday.

This sounds banal until one notices how often retrospective investigations substitute capability studies for incident measurements. A lab demonstrates that a waveform can produce an effect. A defense report shows an adversary has related hardware. A patient has compatible symptoms. The triangle closes emotionally.

It is still missing a side physically.

A dosimeter is not exciting.

That is exactly why it matters.
