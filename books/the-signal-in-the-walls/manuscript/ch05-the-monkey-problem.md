# The Monkey Problem

The monkey is where the story can go wrong forever.

A primate is exposed to modulated microwave energy. Its behavior changes. Somewhere in Washington a memo circulates. Years later, a declassified paragraph appears in an archive. The result migrates into books, documentaries, hearings, and websites. Each retelling loses one piece of the experiment until what remains is a sentence: microwaves changed a monkey's behavior.

Then the exposure estimate changes.

This is not an exotic failure. It is measurement error, the most ordinary assassin in science.

The early PANDORA/BIZARRE work appears to have been influenced by CIA estimates that placed the Moscow field substantially higher than later measurements supported. The National Security Archive's document set describes an early primate experiment performed at levels keyed to those higher estimates. When the estimated Moscow power density was corrected downward, exposures at the revised levels did not produce the same degradation in task performance.

The difference between those two versions of the story is not semantic. It is the difference between a result relevant to the embassy and a result from a different dose regime.

This problem repeats across directed-energy claims because radiofrequency exposure is difficult to summarize with a single number. Average power density is not enough. Pulse width matters. Peak power matters. Duty cycle matters. Carrier frequency changes absorption. Modulation can change what biological or sensory system is stimulated. Near-field geometry complicates exposure. Reflections inside rooms can create hot and cold spots. Antenna polarization matters. Tissue geometry matters. A quoted value without waveform is often a stripped screw.

The microwave auditory effect is an excellent demonstration. Frey's early work reported auditory sensations at low average power densities, but with high peak powers in brief pulses. Later thermoacoustic models explained how rapid localized heating—too small to produce the usual sensation of heat—could generate a pressure wave that reaches the cochlea. If someone compares only average power to a continuous-wave safety limit, the phenomenon can sound impossible. If someone compares only peak power and ignores pulse energy or thermal constraints, a hypothetical weapon can sound easy.

Both are mistakes of compression.

The monkey problem also exposes a second trap: endpoint drift. A task-performance change is not brain injury. A transient physiological signal is not chronic disease. A stress response is not incapacitation. An auditory click is not a concussion. Once a domain acquires an aura of secrecy, effects from different levels of this ladder are often merged into a single category called bioeffects.

Bioeffect is one of the least informative words in the literature. Sunlight causes bioeffects. Coffee causes bioeffects. A door slamming causes bioeffects. The question is always which one, at what dose, by what mechanism, for how long, and with what consequence.

This chapter is not a defense of complacency. The opposite. If an adversary is deliberately illuminating personnel with radiofrequency energy, sloppy reasoning is a security vulnerability. Overclaiming can send detectors toward the wrong waveform, doctors toward the wrong injury model, and analysts toward the wrong actor.

The monkey was not a footnote.

The monkey was a warning about units.
