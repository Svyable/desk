# What a Microwave Can Do

The microwave oven is a terrible teacher.

It is too familiar, too loud in the imagination, and too efficient at collapsing an entire part of the electromagnetic spectrum into one household effect: heat. Put water-rich food inside a metal box, drive it with high-power radio-frequency energy near 2.45 gigahertz, and the food gets hot. From that experience it is easy to make two opposite mistakes. The first is to assume that any microwave exposure is a miniature cooking event. The second is to assume that because low-level exposures do not cook tissue, they cannot matter biologically at all.

The history of the Moscow Signal unfolded between those mistakes.

Microwaves are non-ionizing electromagnetic radiation. Unlike X-rays or gamma rays, individual microwave photons do not carry enough energy to ionize atoms or directly break chemical bonds in the same way. That does not make high-power microwave energy harmless. Electromagnetic fields can deposit energy in tissue. At sufficient absorbed power, tissue warms. Heating can produce injury. Eyes have historically received particular attention because the lens has limited blood flow and because high exposures can produce cataracts under some experimental conditions. The existence of thermal effects is not controversial.

The controversy begins lower.

Can radio-frequency energy at levels too low to produce meaningful bulk heating cause reproducible biological effects through mechanisms that are not simply ordinary temperature rise? Can modulation—the pattern imposed on a carrier—matter? Can short pulses produce sensory effects that continuous waves at the same average power do not? Can chronic exposure matter differently from an acute one? Can field interactions at membranes, nerves, or subcellular structures create effects that survive careful thermal controls?

Those questions have never been one question, although the literature often gets treated as if it were.

By the 1960s, Soviet and Eastern European research included claims of biological effects from relatively low-level microwave exposure. American researchers and defense officials took some of those claims seriously enough to investigate them. The scientific dispute was entangled with intelligence from the beginning. If Soviet laboratories had discovered a reproducible effect unknown or discounted in the West, the Moscow Signal could look different. If the Soviet literature reflected poor controls, thermal artifacts, publication bias, or regulatory caution rather than a hidden capability, the same beam looked less ominous.

The only responsible way to sort this is effect by effect.

One microwave phenomenon that is both real and frequently abused in speculation is the microwave auditory effect, often associated with Allan Frey’s early work. Under certain pulsed radio-frequency exposures, people can perceive clicks or buzzing sounds even without ordinary acoustic energy entering the ear in the usual way. Later research has generally explained the effect through rapid thermoelastic expansion: brief energy absorption produces tiny pressure waves in tissue that reach the auditory apparatus. It is remarkable. It is also not telepathy.

The distinction has to be said because exotic military narratives have repeatedly promoted a real sensory effect into a much larger capability. If a pulsed field can produce a click perceived inside the head, it does not follow that arbitrary thoughts can be read, inserted, or controlled. More elaborate demonstrations involving speech-like perception under specialized conditions require their own power, pulse, geometry, and safety constraints. A physical foothold is not the same as an operational weapon.

This pattern repeats across microwave bioeffects.

At high exposures, thermal injury can be measured. At lower exposures, studies have reported changes in behavior, electroencephalography, blood-brain barrier permeability, calcium signaling, oxidative stress, gene expression, sleep, subjective symptoms, and other endpoints. The literature spans decades and is heterogeneous in quality. Some findings have failed replication. Some are sensitive to dosimetry or temperature control. Some use exposures far above the Moscow levels. Some involve cell cultures or animals with uncertain relevance to human chronic exposure. A positive result cannot be imported into the embassy merely because the frequency band overlaps.

The blood-brain barrier offers a good example of how the argument became unstable. Claims that microwave exposure could increase barrier permeability carried obvious military and medical significance. If low-intensity fields could change what enters the brain, then exposures below thermal injury thresholds might still matter. Yet replication efforts did not always confirm the effect. A 1978 study by J. H. Merritt and colleagues, for example, attempted to replicate reported barrier changes in rats across 1.2-gigahertz exposures and found increased uptake only when animals were made hyperthermic by warm air, not under the microwave conditions themselves. That did not settle every barrier study ever performed. It demonstrated how a result that looks nonthermal can disappear when temperature is handled differently.

This is why “nonthermal” is not a mechanism.

It is a negation. It says the effect is not adequately explained by measured heating. A persuasive nonthermal claim still has to identify a repeatable biological outcome, exclude artifacts, characterize dose-response, survive independent replication, and eventually explain how field energy couples to the relevant biological system. Without that chain, “nonthermal” can become a drawer where anomalies are stored rather than an explanation.

The Moscow Signal sat inside exactly this scientific uncertainty.

The measured power densities were far below the levels used in classic thermal-injury experiments. A 1978 study of microwave-induced cataracts in rabbits and monkeys, for example, involved incident apparent power densities measured in hundreds of milliwatts per square centimeter. Moscow discussions involved microwatts to low fractions of a milliwatt per square centimeter. Those are different regimes by orders of magnitude. One cannot point to the existence of microwave cataracts at high power and infer that the embassy field caused eye disease.

Yet the low level did not end the debate because the Soviet standards were themselves lower than the American occupational limits and because some Soviet literature claimed effects below Western thermal thresholds. The political question therefore became a scientific one: was the American safety model missing something?

Project Pandora was in part an attempt to answer that.

A hidden assumption sits inside the question. Safety standards are often treated in retrospect as statements of natural law. They are not. They are decisions made from evidence, uncertainty, safety margins, exposure scenarios, and institutional philosophy. A standard can be protective without proving that the threshold immediately below it is a cliff. Two countries can choose different limits from the same underlying biology. A stricter Soviet standard did not necessarily mean Soviet scientists possessed secret evidence of a low-level weapon. A looser American standard did not necessarily mean low-level effects were impossible.

The standards nevertheless shaped interpretation because they provided numbers people could hold onto.

If the Moscow field was hundreds of times below an American industrial limit, officials could say it was small. If it approached or exceeded a Soviet public limit, employees could say the transmitting country itself would not accept that exposure. Both comparisons can be numerically true while rhetorically opposed.

The right question is not which number is scarier. It is what endpoint each standard was designed to prevent and what evidence supported that endpoint.

There is another complication: the body does not absorb a radio-frequency field uniformly.

Frequency changes penetration depth. Body size and orientation matter. Near-field and far-field conditions matter. Metal objects and structures can alter local fields. The quantity measured in air outside a building is not identical to the energy deposited in a specific tissue. Modern dosimetry often uses specific absorption rate, or SAR, to estimate power absorbed per unit mass, but even SAR can be spatially averaged in ways that hide local peaks. In the 1960s, the measurement and computational tools available for a complicated embassy environment were far less capable than current systems.

That limitation cuts both ways.

It means one should not overstate the precision of historical assurances. It also means one should not use the uncertainty as permission to imagine arbitrary hidden doses. Unknown is not infinitely large. The surviving measurements constrain the physical problem.

The same principle applies to modulation.

A carrier wave can be continuous or can have amplitude, frequency, phase, or pulse patterns imposed on it. Those patterns may matter to an electronic receiver because the receiver is designed to detect them. For biology, a modulation-specific effect requires evidence that the tissue has some mechanism capable of responding differently to those temporal patterns at the relevant field strength. This is an empirical question. “Modulated microwaves” sounds inherently more biologically sophisticated than “microwaves,” but modulation is ubiquitous in communications. The existence of modulation is not evidence of biological targeting.

Project Pandora’s investigators were drawn to modulation because Soviet claims and the Moscow signal characteristics raised the possibility that pattern mattered. Animal behavior offered an attractive endpoint. If a primate trained on a task deteriorated under one microwave condition and not another, the experiment might reveal something operationally important even before the cellular mechanism was known.

Behavior, however, is a noisy instrument.

An animal can perform differently because of stress, restraint, heating, experimenter cues, reward timing, fatigue, equipment problems, or chance. If the exposure cannot be blinded or sham-controlled properly, bias enters. If the sample is one animal or a very small number, individual variation dominates. If the parameter set is repeatedly adjusted while researchers search for an effect, the nominal significance of a positive run becomes difficult to interpret.

The declassified PANDORA and BIZARRE record contains precisely the sort of early suggestive finding that should provoke follow-up rather than proclamation. An initial primate experiment appeared to show performance degradation under microwave exposure. Later, after the Moscow power-density estimate was corrected downward, experiments at the more relevant level did not reproduce overt degradation. That sequence is scientifically ordinary. The first result becomes a hypothesis. The follow-up narrows it.

In national security, ordinary scientific revision can look like suppression.

The first positive result is exciting and may circulate among officials. The later null is buried in a progress report. Decades afterward, somebody discovers the existence of the secret program and understandably asks why the government was experimenting on monkeys with microwaves. The mere fact of classification supplies drama the final result does not. If a later whistleblower or journalist quotes the early concern without the corrected dosimetry, the story re-expands.

This is one reason classified science is uniquely vulnerable to evidentiary ghosts. The full negative space is rarely public at the same time as the exciting fragment.

There was also a genuine strategic incentive to look for something exotic. The Cold War punished technological complacency. Sputnik had taught American institutions that a rival could surprise them. Soviet missile and radar programs were real. Intelligence agencies had to consider whether apparently strange research represented a capability gap. A Soviet literature describing central nervous system effects at low microwave intensities could not simply be ignored because Western scientists found the claims implausible.

The rational response was replication.

The irrational response would have been to treat Soviet publication as proof.

Those two responses are often confused in later retellings. The Pentagon’s willingness to fund research is cited as evidence that the effect existed. But funding establishes interest, not efficacy. DARPA and its predecessors exist partly to investigate possibilities before the evidence is settled. A program can be sensible to start and sensible to terminate without ever producing a usable technology.

Project Pandora therefore belongs in a category with many frontier programs: valuable as evidence of what the government considered worth testing, weaker as evidence of what nature allowed.

The biological question also suffered from a deep asymmetry between harm and intent.

Suppose chronic low-level exposure produced a small increase in headaches, sleep disturbance, or some neurological endpoint. That finding would matter medically. It would not establish that the Soviet signal was designed to produce it. Conversely, suppose the Soviet purpose was to interfere with electronics and the signal had no measurable health effect. The exposure would still have been intentional as a technical operation. “Deliberate exposure” is not synonymous with “deliberate injury.”

Later controversies around anomalous health incidents often blur these categories. A proposed physical mechanism becomes a proposed attack; a proposed attack becomes attribution to a state; historical microwave exposure becomes precedent; precedent becomes motive. Each step requires new evidence.

The physics refuses the shortcut.

To harm biologically, energy has to couple into tissue in a way that produces an effect. To create a specific sensory phenomenon, the waveform has to satisfy constraints. To target an individual through walls or windows, geometry and attenuation matter. To cause persistent neurological injury rather than transient sensation, a plausible injury pathway is needed. To do so covertly, the system must also avoid obvious collateral signatures. An adversary may be capable of some of these things without being capable of all of them simultaneously.

This is not an argument that directed-energy weapons are impossible. High-power microwave systems exist. Millimeter-wave systems can produce intense superficial heating and have been developed for crowd-control applications. Radio-frequency energy can interact with electronics and tissue. The military significance of the spectrum is enormous.

It is an argument against using the category “directed energy” as if it were one technology.

A radar, a microwave oven, an electronic jammer, a passive-device interrogator, a high-power microwave weapon, and a pulsed source capable of inducing an auditory sensation can all involve radio-frequency energy. Their power, waveform, antenna, target, range, and effect differ radically. The phrase tells us the family, not the individual.

The Moscow Signal forced American officials to confront this before the vocabulary was ready.

They had a field that seemed too weak to fit familiar thermal-hazard models and too persistent to ignore. They had Soviet literature suggesting that low-level exposures could affect biological systems. They had an embassy already penetrated by sophisticated technical surveillance. They had no accepted explanation for the signal’s purpose. The combination was sufficient to justify experiments.

It was not sufficient to tell them what the experiments meant.

The closer one looks at microwave biology, the less satisfying the binary becomes. “Microwaves are harmless” is false at high enough exposures. “Microwaves are dangerous” is useless without dose and endpoint. “Nonthermal effects exist” is too broad to identify mechanism or risk. “No nonthermal effect has ever been proven” is too sweeping for a literature containing sensory and physiological phenomena that depend on details of exposure, some of which involve microscopic or transient heating even when people casually call them nonthermal.

The honest unit of analysis is the specific effect under the specific condition.

This is tedious. It is also the only defense against turning a spectral band into a mythological substance.

By the time the Americans named Project Pandora, the box already contained too many questions: Can low-level modulated microwaves change behavior? Did Soviet researchers know something Western researchers had missed? Were embassy employees being harmed? Was the radiation an espionage carrier? Were Soviet safety standards evidence of hidden knowledge or simply a different precautionary regime? Could animal data settle any of this? Could the problem be studied without telling the people living inside it?

The project name turned out to be better than whoever chose it could have known.

Opening the box did not release one secret.

It released a research program.
