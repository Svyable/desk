# Monkeys Under Modulation

The monkey is where the story most wants to become a movie.

A rhesus primate sits in an apparatus, trained to perform a task for food. Somewhere beyond the cage, microwave equipment is turned on. The animal makes mistakes. Men with clearances write down the errors. In another room, intelligence officers compare the exposure pattern with a signal aimed at the American embassy in Moscow. The project is secret. The codename is BIZARRE.

One hardly needs to add anything.

That is precisely why so much has to be removed.

The surviving 1967 ARPA memorandum from Richard Cesaro is useful because it strips the experiment back toward what can actually be claimed. Earlier testing had raised concern that modulated microwave exposure might degrade primate performance. New measurements of the Moscow Signal, however, indicated that the embassy field was weaker than the value used to motivate the earlier condition. When an experiment was performed at the corrected level, the report stated that there was no overt degradation in the primate’s task performance.

That sequence contains nearly the whole epistemology of PANDORA in miniature.

First, the endpoint was behavior rather than obvious injury. The experiment was looking for a change in performance that could matter operationally even if the animal did not show burns, seizures, or other gross pathology.

Second, the target condition came from intelligence measurement rather than from biology. If the Moscow estimate was wrong, biological relevance was wrong before the animal entered the apparatus.

Third, the initial suggestive result did not survive the adjustment that made the exposure more faithful to the real-world problem.

Fourth, the program did not disappear merely because one alarming version of the hypothesis weakened.

The temptation is to decide which of those facts deserves the headline. The experimental result deserves all of them.

Behavioral experiments have an odd status in weapons research because they appear closer to strategic purpose than cellular changes do. A change in an enzyme may or may not matter to a soldier. A trained animal that stops performing a task looks immediately consequential. Yet behavior is farther from mechanism and often more variable. The same feature that makes the endpoint operationally legible makes it scientifically slippery.

A primate’s performance depends on motivation, training history, fatigue, reward, restraint, stress, ambient conditions, apparatus, and the experimenter’s protocol. If a microwave field heats part of the body enough to make an animal uncomfortable, behavior can change without any exotic neural coupling. If the exposure equipment produces sound or vibration, the animal can respond to that. If investigators know when the transmitter is on, subtle cues can leak into handling. If the sample is tiny, an individual bad day can look like a signal.

The solution is not to dismiss behavioral work. It is to design it as if one expects to fool oneself.

Sham exposures matter. Blinding matters. Stable task performance before exposure matters. Adequate numbers matter. Predefined endpoints matter. Temperature monitoring matters. Repetition matters. Independent replication matters most of all.

The declassified PANDORA record does not give us, in one convenient package, a modern preregistered experiment with raw trial-level data and a complete statistical analysis. We have program reports, later recollections, and summaries. That is enough to reconstruct direction. It is not enough to pretend we possess every denominator.

The missing denominators matter because secret programs encourage anecdotal memory. The one monkey that changed behavior becomes “the monkey experiment.” The sessions in which nothing happened become invisible. A project manager’s concern survives because concern generated correspondence. Routine nulls may survive only in data files that were discarded, destroyed, or never declassified.

There is a larger lesson here about anomalous research.

When the expected effect is poorly specified, almost any unusual outcome can become interesting. A primate pauses. A rat’s EEG changes. A blood value moves. A human subject reports a headache. The anomaly is real as an observation, but the inferential space is huge. What would count as a failure? How many endpoints were watched? Which ones were chosen before the exposure? Was the same pattern seen again?

This is why falsification notes belong inside the history rather than at the end as a skeptical afterthought.

For the primate-behavior hypothesis, a strong defeat condition would have been straightforward: reproduce the exact signal parameters thought present in Moscow, run blinded sham-controlled testing across enough animals and sessions, confirm dosimetry, and show no meaningful performance difference across the range of plausible exposures. The 1967 corrected-level result moves in that direction, but the public record does not supply the full comprehensive test one might want.

For a broad “microwaves can affect behavior under some conditions” hypothesis, defeat is much harder because high-power heating alone can obviously alter behavior. The hypothesis becomes scientifically trivial unless the parameters and mechanism are constrained.

The useful claim therefore has to remain narrow.

The BIZARRE experiment did not validate the idea that the measured Moscow field reliably degraded primate task performance.

Everything else requires new evidence.

The program’s interest in modulation complicates the picture. Modulation is a natural place to search if average power seems too low for gross thermal effects and if Soviet literature suggests nervous-system sensitivity to patterned fields. It is also a natural place for false discovery. Once carrier frequency, pulse repetition, modulation depth, duty cycle, amplitude, and exposure duration become adjustable, the experimental search space explodes.

A broad search can be justified early in a field. It should eventually be followed by a locked protocol that predicts the effect before the machine is turned on.

That transition—from exploration to confirmation—is where many anomalous sciences stall.

The incentive structure is cruel. An exploratory anomaly is exciting enough to fund another experiment but too unstable to define one. The next team adjusts parameters to “better reproduce” the effect. If the result changes, the adjustment is blamed. If the result persists, the mechanism is still unknown. Each iteration generates another story about why the previous one was not decisive.

National-security secrecy adds another barrier: independent laboratories may not know the exact operational signal they are supposed to reproduce.

Suppose a university scientist is asked whether a particular modulation can affect a primate but is not told where the waveform came from. The scientist can answer the laboratory question. She cannot know whether the parameter is representative of the embassy. If the intelligence community later revises its measurement, the biological literature remains published or classified around the old condition. The research trail forks.

This is not incompetence. It is a structural cost of compartmentalization.

Cesaro’s report contains another important move: the idea that understanding human relevance might require direct human testing. That thought follows logically from the limits of animal extrapolation. It also opens an ethical chasm.

Animals are not miniature diplomats. Species differ in body size, thermoregulation, neuroanatomy, and electromagnetic absorption. A rhesus monkey trained to press or manipulate a device for reward can model aspects of attention and performance, but not the full cognitive life of a person working under chronic low-level exposure for years. If the hypothesized effect is subtle, translation becomes even harder.

Direct human testing could reduce some uncertainty, but only by creating others. What exposure is ethical? What endpoint matters? How does one obtain informed consent for a classified threat without revealing classified information? Can military or government personnel volunteer freely when the research is tied to national security? What happens if the hypothesized effects include cognition or judgment, the very capacities required for continuing consent?

These are not abstract concerns. The American Cold War record contains enough unethical research to make caution mandatory.

But the archival standard has to remain firm. A memo contemplating controlled human testing is evidence of contemplation. It is not evidence that secret human irradiation experiments were carried out under that program. If records of such experiments emerge, they belong in a different evidence class. Until then, the sentence must keep its verb.

The actual embassy employees complicate the ethics because they were already being exposed by an adversary. They were not experimental subjects in the conventional sense, yet government scientists could observe them as a naturally exposed population. The moral hazard is obvious: a government may become less urgent about stopping an exposure if the exposure is scientifically informative.

I have found no public evidence that PANDORA officials deliberately prolonged the Moscow transmissions for research. That allegation should not be invented from the structure of the problem. The United States repeatedly sought to understand and eventually stop the signal. Still, the dual role existed. The embassy was both a workplace and an involuntary field site.

That duality shows up later in the medical program.

It also explains why the monkey matters beyond the monkey.

The animal experiment was an attempt to create control where the real-world exposure had none. In Moscow, the United States did not control the transmitter, did not know the purpose, did not have perfect dose maps, and did not tell much of the population. In the laboratory, it could turn the field on and off, train a subject, control the environment, and observe behavior directly.

The laboratory seems cleaner.

Then the input value turns out to be wrong.

This is the humbling detail at the center of the story. The experimental apparatus can be exquisitely controlled and still answer the wrong question because the messy world supplied the wrong number.

Frontier national-security science lives on that boundary. The intelligence is uncertain because the target is secret. The science is uncertain because the mechanism is unsettled. The policy is urgent because people are exposed. Each institution wants the others to provide certainty first.

Nobody can.

The monkey does not reveal Soviet intent. It does not diagnose embassy employees. It does not establish a general law of microwave biology. It does something narrower and, in the long run, more valuable.

It shows how quickly a secret threat hypothesis can weaken when one measurement improves.

That is a result worth remembering because the rest of the story will repeatedly reward people for forgetting it.
