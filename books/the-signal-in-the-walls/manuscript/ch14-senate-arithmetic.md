# Senate Arithmetic

Congress arrived late enough to know there had been a secret and early enough not to know what it meant.

That is a dangerous place to investigate from.

By the late 1970s, the microwave irradiation of the U.S. Embassy in Moscow was no longer a compartmented technical problem. Employees had been briefed. Newspapers had reported the story. Scientists and physicians were arguing about low-level radio-frequency effects. Walter Stoessel’s illness had become part of the public narrative. The State Department had commissioned a large epidemiological study. Members of Congress wanted to know whether American personnel had been endangered and whether executive agencies had concealed the danger.

The Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation reviewed the history and issued a staff report in 1979. The report is valuable not because Congress possessed a magic file that settled the biology but because it had access to a broad documentary record and was willing to say two uncomfortable things at once.

First, embassy employees should have been informed earlier.

Second, the available medical studies did not provide convincing evidence that the microwave exposure had caused adverse health effects as of the time of the review.

Those findings do not cancel each other.

Much of the later literature behaves as if they must.

If the government was wrong to withhold, critics assume the health risk must have been substantial. If the health studies were reassuring, defenders assume withholding was harmless. Congress’s more awkward position is better: process can be wrong even when feared harm is not demonstrated.

This is Senate arithmetic.

It is the arithmetic of uncertainty, where zero proven injuries does not equal zero obligation.

The committee also had to deal with numbers that had become political symbols. American microwave exposure standards were far higher than Soviet limits. Depending on which value, duration, and period one selected, the embassy field could be described as well below U.S. standards and uncomfortably close to or above Soviet criteria. Critics used the contrast to imply that the transmitting state itself believed the exposure unsafe. Officials emphasized that U.S. experts did not accept the Soviet standard as proof of harm.

Both sides could perform correct division and reach opposite conclusions.

The problem was not arithmetic. It was what the denominator meant.

Safety standards are not interchangeable scientific facts. One may apply to occupational exposure, another to the general public. One may average over time differently. One may embed larger safety factors. One may be based on thermal endpoints, another on a wider interpretation of reported symptoms. Without those details, saying one standard is “a thousand times stricter” can be rhetorically explosive and scientifically incomplete.

Congressional discourse tends to reward the explosive version.

This does not make congressional concern unserious. Quite the opposite. Legislatures exist partly to challenge agencies that have normalized their own assumptions. If the Defense Department’s accepted limit was orders of magnitude above the Soviet one, it was reasonable to ask why. If Soviet researchers reported low-level effects, it was reasonable to ask whether American standards were too permissive. If agencies had conducted secret studies, it was reasonable to ask what those studies actually found.

The danger was turning the existence of disagreement into evidence for the more alarming standard.

A stricter standard may be wiser.

It may also be wrong.

The Senate report had another temporal problem. Cancer and other chronic diseases can have long latency. A study conducted soon after exposure may legitimately fail to detect effects that appear later. The committee therefore noted that long-term mortality questions could not yet be closed completely.

This caveat became another durable fragment.

Advocates of harm could say the government’s own review admitted it was too early. Skeptics could say decades of later attention still failed to reveal a coherent epidemic. Both claims require actual follow-up data, not a preference for one rhetorical direction.

Time is an evidentiary variable.

So is survivorship.

The employees most worried about exposure may be more likely to participate in later studies or preserve records. People who remained healthy may move on. Severe illness makes a memorable testimony. Absence does not testify. Congressional hearings, by design, collect salient cases rather than random samples.

That makes hearings excellent for uncovering administrative failure and weaker for estimating disease incidence.

This distinction matters because witnesses can reveal truths the statistics miss.

A former employee can establish that nobody told her about the signal, that a doctor gave an odd explanation for a blood draw, that a window was shielded, or that symptoms began during a particular posting. Those are historical facts if corroborated. The same witness cannot, from experience alone, establish the physical cause of a leukemia or neurological disorder.

Testimony and epidemiology answer different questions.

The Moscow affair has suffered whenever one is asked to replace the other.

Congress was also operating in the long shadow of American government secrecy scandals. The 1970s had exposed intelligence abuses, domestic surveillance, unethical human experiments, and executive deception. Public willingness to accept “trust us, the exposure was harmless” was understandably low.

Context changes the prior probability assigned to institutional misconduct.

It should not change the laws of electromagnetic interaction.

This is a difficult rule to maintain. Once an agency has lied about one thing, people rationally reduce trust in other claims. But distrust is not positive evidence for a specific alternative mechanism. The State Department’s concealment of the signal makes later reassurance less credible socially. It does not make a microwave-induced leukemia mechanism more physically likely by itself.

The distinction is essential in intelligence-adjacent topics where secrecy is real and extraordinary claims cluster around it.

“Government cover-up” is often not one proposition.

Did the government conceal the existence of the signal? Yes, from embassy personnel for years.

Did it conceal its uncertainty about possible health effects? To a meaningful extent, yes.

Did it conceal a proven Soviet behavior-control weapon? Publicly available evidence does not establish that.

Did it suppress conclusive epidemiological proof of widespread microwave disease? The record reviewed here does not establish that either.

Once those propositions are separated, the history becomes less cinematic and more serious.

Congressional oversight did something else important: it forced the executive branch to preserve and organize a story that had been distributed across agencies. Intelligence programs are often documented vertically. The people measuring a signal keep one file. Medical officers keep another. ARPA keeps research reports. State keeps cables. The White House keeps diplomatic memoranda. No one folder contains “what happened.”

Oversight creates horizontal history.

That horizontal view exposed contradictions that each agency could tolerate internally.

Why were employees told there was no established health problem while senior officials warned Moscow of a possible hazard?

Why were blood samples collected under a cover explanation?

Why had a signal known for years become urgent only after it changed?

Why did early animal findings differ from later ones?

Why were U.S. and Soviet safety standards so different?

Why had the purpose of the signal never been resolved?

These questions did not prove a weapon. They proved that the official narrative was more complicated than the public had been told.

That is a legitimate outcome of oversight.

There is a modern lesson in the committee’s wording. It did not need to choose between “nothing happened” and “we were attacked.” It could say the health evidence was not convincing and the disclosure process was unacceptable.

Contemporary institutions often fail to preserve that middle because political systems punish uncertainty.

If an agency says it has not established a causal mechanism, victims hear dismissal.

If it says a mechanism is plausible, the public hears attribution.

If intelligence analysts say a foreign actor is unlikely to be responsible for most incidents, some readers hear that patients are not sick.

These are category errors.

The Senate report’s structure offers a better model: separate care, process, exposure, causation, and attribution.

Care asks what should be done for people reporting illness.

Process asks whether employees were informed and protected appropriately.

Exposure asks what physical field reached them.

Causation asks whether that field produced the illness.

Attribution asks who generated the field and for what purpose.

A government can fail process while causation remains unproven.

A patient can deserve care while attribution remains unknown.

An adversary can create an exposure for espionage without intending biological harm.

A biological effect can occur without an attack.

The Moscow case becomes manageable only after these columns are separated.

Congress did not always maintain the distinctions perfectly. Political rhetoric pulled toward accusation. Some members cited dramatic claims about Soviet intentions or microwave illness that exceeded what the strongest evidence could establish. Others leaned heavily on official reassurances.

That is what legislative records are: evidence of oversight and evidence of politics.

They should be read proposition by proposition, not treated as scientific consensus.

The 1979 review’s most durable contribution may therefore be negative.

It refused to manufacture closure.

No convincing causal health evidence had been established.

Employees should have been told.

Long-term questions deserved continued attention.

The signal’s purpose remained unresolved.

Those four statements can coexist without contradiction.

In an era addicted to verdicts, coexistence sounds weak.

It is actually the strongest part of the record.
