# Diplomacy by Dosimeter

Diplomacy usually pretends that measurement comes first.

A state detects an intrusion, determines what happened, decides how serious it is, and then protests. The Moscow Signal reversed the order. The United States could measure enough to know that directed microwave energy was reaching its embassy, but not enough to say with confidence what the Soviet Union intended. It had to conduct diplomacy against an unknown function.

The first high-level exchanges are revealing for what they do not claim.

At the Glassboro summit in June 1967, Secretary of State Dean Rusk raised the issue with Soviet foreign minister Andrei Gromyko. According to the declassified memorandum of conversation, Rusk described an electromagnetic signal directed at the chancery and said the United States did not know its purpose. He wanted it investigated and stopped. Gromyko was skeptical and suggested that the Americans conducted similar activity against Soviet facilities in the United States.

This is a mature diplomatic formulation of uncertainty.

Rusk did not need to prove a health effect to object. A foreign government was deliberately directing a technical signal at an embassy. The receiving state did not understand the function. That alone was enough to make the activity unacceptable.

The distinction is useful because later discussion often assumes the United States needed a biological case before it could complain. It did not.

Embassies are protected spaces in international relations even though every serious intelligence service tries to penetrate them. Technical surveillance is common; official tolerance is not the same thing as consent. The United States could plausibly be conducting aggressive collection around Soviet diplomatic facilities and still object when the Soviets penetrated its own. Espionage lives inside that hypocrisy.

The Soviet counteraccusation therefore has two possible functions at once. It may have been deflection. It may also have reflected genuine American electromagnetic collection or interference near Soviet facilities. The public record should not force us to choose without evidence.

What matters is that the conversation remained technically unresolved after it became diplomatically explicit.

The signal continued.

That persistence changed the meaning of each later measurement. A one-time unexplained emission might be interference. A recurring transmission after senior protest looks more deliberate. Deliberate still does not tell us the purpose. It tells us someone valued the activity enough to continue it despite the risk of political friction.

By 1975, the problem sharpened. A newer signal, stronger or more persistent in relevant respects than the older pattern, drew attention from Ambassador Walter Stoessel. The embassy wanted better technical data and considered another high-level protest. Medical experts were less willing to give absolute reassurance because duration and signal characteristics had changed.

At this point, measurement became negotiating material.

Power density was no longer simply a scientific variable. It was a diplomatic fact.

If the Americans could show that the signal exceeded Soviet safety limits, they could confront Moscow using the Soviet Union’s own regulatory language. That was politically attractive even if American standards were more permissive. It turned a vague complaint about an unknown beam into a charge of exposing foreign diplomats to levels the Soviet system itself regarded as unacceptable.

The numerical comparison was stronger rhetorically than scientifically.

Different standards do not necessarily encode the same risk model. An eight-hour occupational limit, a public-exposure guideline, a maximum local value, and a long-duration residential exposure cannot be swapped casually. But diplomats are not writing dosimetry papers. They need propositions that can survive a meeting.

The Soviets had another advantage: plausible ambiguity.

Industrial radio sources exist in cities. Radar exists. Communications links exist. If the signal could be attributed to ordinary technical activity, Moscow could deny targeting even while the Americans saw directional evidence. When U.S. and Soviet technicians conducted measurements together in the 1970s, disagreements over interpretation did not disappear.

Joint measurement is not joint meaning.

This is a recurring theme in arms control and technical diplomacy. Two states can agree on an instrument reading and disagree completely about what behavior produced it. Verification regimes are designed to reduce that space. The Moscow Signal had no treaty-defined inspection mechanism. The Americans could measure from inside their own embassy; the Soviets controlled the source.

The asymmetry left diplomacy dependent on inference.

Henry Kissinger’s conversations with Soviet ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin in late 1975 make the problem almost comic. Kissinger pressed Dobrynin about the beam and worried about public disclosure. The exchanges included jokes about radiation treatment, but the humor sat on top of a real political problem. Too many people knew. If the administration appeared to have tolerated an unexplained Soviet transmission into the embassy without protecting its personnel, it would face domestic consequences.

This is where the history changes from “What are the Soviets doing?” to “What can Washington survive being seen to have done?”

Those are different questions.

Once public accountability enters, risk assessment changes. Officials must consider not only the expected harm from the exposure but the harm from having withheld information if the exposure later proves significant. This is a classic low-probability governance trap. If the risk is genuinely tiny, disclosure can seem disproportionate. If the event later looks worse, the same nondisclosure looks reckless.

The Moscow case was especially unforgiving because the exposure was adversarial.

People tolerate uncertain risks differently depending on who imposed them. A low-level electromagnetic field from a household device, an employer, a medical treatment, and a hostile intelligence service may be physically identical at the body but politically incomparable. Consent and intent alter perceived risk.

A diplomat who learns that the Soviet Union has deliberately irradiated his office for years will not be reassured simply by being told the level is comparable to some regulated civilian exposure. The relevant comparison is not a microwave oven leakage limit. It is why a foreign government is doing it.

This helps explain why the health issue grew even without convincing evidence of injury.

The unknown intent amplified the meaning of every symptom.

Headache is common. In an unexplained adversarial field, it becomes a possible effect. Fatigue is common. It becomes data. Cancer occurs. It becomes a clue. Once the signal is known, ordinary medical events enter a new causal story.

This is not irrational. Causal inference begins by considering exposures. The danger is retrospective selection. People remember the illnesses that fit the story and forget the healthy years. A rare disease in a visible individual, such as Ambassador Stoessel’s later leukemia, can dominate perception even when the cohort data do not support a causal association.

Officials understood this psychology and feared it.

Their fear contributed to withholding.

The withholding then made the psychology worse.

There is a cruel feedback loop here. Secrecy is chosen partly to prevent alarm-related symptoms and political overreaction. When secrecy breaks, the fact of concealment increases alarm and decreases trust, making symptom attribution harder.

A more transparent policy may have produced more short-term noise and better long-term evidence.

The diplomatic record also reveals how technical uncertainty can be strategically useful to both sides.

For the Soviet Union, ambiguity allowed continued operation without admitting the purpose. For the United States, uncertainty allowed complaints without having to disclose what American intelligence thought the signal might be interrogating. If U.S. officials suspected a link to particular eavesdropping devices or to American collection systems on the roof, explaining too much in a protest could reveal technical insight.

Both sides had reasons not to close the interpretive gap.

This does not mean the uncertainty was manufactured. It means genuine uncertainty can acquire utility.

The same phenomenon appears in modern electronic warfare. A state may deliberately operate just below the threshold at which attribution or response becomes easy. GPS interference, cyber operations, laser illumination, acoustic harassment, and drone incursions can all exploit ambiguity. The target knows something happened but lacks enough evidence to choose the most consequential interpretation.

The Moscow Signal is an early case of that strategic gray zone in the electromagnetic domain.

Whether the Soviets intended the health anxiety is unknown.

They produced it anyway.

This is an important distinction. Effects do not have to be intended to be strategically consequential. If a surveillance system causes the target government to spend millions on research, alter embassy operations, worry about employee health, confront senior Soviet officials, and eventually endure a public scandal, then the system has imposed costs beyond intelligence collection.

An adversary observing those costs might later decide to exploit them deliberately. That is speculative synthesis, not a claim about Soviet intent in the 1960s. It points to a general mechanism: once a technical operation is known to create uncertainty, the uncertainty itself becomes a potential instrument.

The American response may have taught the Soviet side as much as the signal taught the Americans.

Every protest reveals sensitivity. Every shielding change reveals what the target thinks the signal is doing. Every measurement campaign reveals where the target can detect. Every medical evacuation reveals what level of concern has been reached.

Countermeasures are also information.

This makes embassy security a game of recursive observation. The Soviets transmit. Americans measure. Soviets observe the measurement effort. Americans shield windows. Soviets infer that the windows matter. Americans protest a particular change in signal. Soviets learn that the change was detected. Each side updates.

The human beings inside the building are not players in that technical game. They are the board.

This is why the occupational-health obligation should be treated separately from the counterintelligence problem. The government may need to conceal exactly how it detects a signal while still telling employees that an unusual exposure is being evaluated. Conflating the two lets operational secrecy swallow personal risk information.

Moscow did that for too long.

By 1976, the government’s position had become internally awkward. It had enough concern to press the Soviets, dispatch experts, monitor changing signals, install or consider shielding, and prepare employee briefings. Yet it also wanted to reassure personnel that no causal relationship between the transmissions and health problems had been established.

Both positions could be true.

To employees, they sounded inconsistent because the government had not brought them along through the uncertainty.

Risk communication works badly when the first message contains the whole history at once.

Imagine being told: the signal has existed for years; we did secret animal research; we collected biological samples under another explanation; we complained to Moscow; the signal recently changed; medical experts do not believe there is evidence of harm; please do not panic.

The institution is asking for trust at the exact moment it reveals why trust is damaged.

Diplomacy by dosimeter had reached its limit.

A number could help Washington argue with Gromyko.

It could not tell an employee whether to believe Washington.
