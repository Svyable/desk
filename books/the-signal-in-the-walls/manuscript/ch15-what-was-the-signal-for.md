# What Was the Signal For?

A mystery becomes dangerous when every explanation can use the same fact.

The Moscow microwave signal was directional.

Espionage can use that.

Jamming can use that.

Device interrogation can use that.

A biological attack can use that.

Harassment can use that.

The signal persisted after diplomatic complaints.

Every hypothesis can use that too.

This is why the hardest question in the book is not whether the signal existed. It is what evidence would distinguish purposes.

The strongest mundane hypothesis is technical intelligence.

The Soviet Union had already demonstrated a passive radio-frequency listening device in the Great Seal. Technical security officers later found microphones and other penetrations in the embassy. U.S. officials repeatedly considered the possibility that microwave energy was being used to activate, interrogate, or otherwise support eavesdropping devices in the building. This hypothesis has a direct historical precedent, fits Soviet intelligence priorities, and does not require a novel biological mechanism.

Its weakness is not plausibility.

Its weakness is closure.

The public record does not provide a single recovered device tied conclusively to the measured TUMS or MUTS waveform, with a documented operational chain from transmitter to hidden transducer to Soviet receiver. There may be classified or lost records that narrow this. What is publicly available supports a strong inference, not the artifact-level proof supplied by the Great Seal bug itself.

A related hypothesis is electronic interference.

The American embassy used communications and intelligence equipment, including rooftop systems. Soviet transmissions could have been intended to jam, desensitize, confuse, or characterize those systems. This is technologically ordinary. Electronic warfare and counter-surveillance operate through exactly such interactions.

Again, the problem is specificity.

A jammer is designed against a receiver. To establish this purpose, one wants correlation between the Soviet signal and degradation in American equipment, or technical analysis showing the waveform was well suited to interfere with the relevant systems. Some U.S. records considered this seriously. The surviving public material does not make the case so decisively that all other hypotheses disappear.

A third possibility is intelligence illumination in a broader sense.

A directed radio-frequency field can probe an environment. Reflections change with objects, structures, and electronic states. Modern radar and remote-sensing concepts make this intuitive, but one should not project modern capability backward without evidence. The question is not what a current laboratory could extract from a field. It is what the Soviet system of that period was designed to extract.

This is where historical technical analysis matters more than analogy.

Recovered Soviet equipment, engineering manuals, intelligence archives, patent trails, design bureau histories, and transmitter-site records could move the question. So could declassified U.S. technical studies that remain obscure. Until then, “some form of technical collection” remains a family of hypotheses.

Then there is the biological hypothesis.

It comes in several versions that should not be conflated.

One: the Soviets intended to injure embassy personnel physically.

Two: they intended to degrade cognition or work performance without obvious injury.

Three: they intended to create discomfort, stress, or fear.

Four: they were experimentally exposing Americans to study long-term effects.

Five: the signal had another purpose, but Soviet operators knew it might produce biological effects and accepted them.

These are different claims requiring different evidence.

The first two are the versions most often meant by “microwave weapon.” They have the highest evidentiary burden because they require a demonstrated effect at the measured parameters and evidence of intent.

Project PANDORA investigated whether relevant microwave exposures could alter biology and behavior. The corrected-level primate work did not validate overt performance degradation. The later epidemiology did not establish a convincing pattern of illness attributable to the signal. Those findings weaken a simple high-confidence biological-attack theory.

They do not make it logically impossible.

A weapon can be ineffective.

A program can be based on bad science.

An adversary can believe a literature that does not replicate.

Intent is a mental and institutional state, not a guarantee of efficacy.

This point is important because biological-attack proponents sometimes respond to negative health evidence by saying the Soviets could still have intended harm. True. But that move changes the thesis. It shifts from “the signal caused illness” to “the Soviets tried to cause illness.” The latter still requires documentary or intelligence evidence of intent.

The Soviet scientific literature alone is not enough.

A country researching microwave bioeffects is not proof that every state microwave transmission is a weapon. The United States researched the same field partly because of Moscow. Research establishes interest.

Operational intent requires a bridge.

What might that bridge look like?

A Soviet directive describing effects sought at the U.S. embassy.

A program file connecting the transmitter to biomedical research.

Testimony from operators with corroborating technical detail.

Signal parameters deliberately chosen to match a known biological effect rather than an electronic function.

Changes in exposure correlated with political or behavioral objectives instead of surveillance needs.

None of these can be replaced by the word secret.

The harassment hypothesis sits between biology and politics.

Perhaps the signal did not need to produce a specific physiological effect. Perhaps Soviet operators understood that once detected, persistent irradiation would unsettle Americans, consume counterintelligence resources, and create fear. This is strategically plausible but chronologically tricky. For harassment through fear to work, the target has to know or suspect the exposure. For many years, most embassy employees did not.

The U.S. government knew.

That raises the possibility of harassment aimed at the institution rather than the individual. A technically unexplained signal can impose costs on security services even if ordinary staff are unaware. It forces measurement, shielding, investigation, and diplomatic attention.

Still, no public evidence establishes that this cost-imposition was the Soviet objective rather than a side effect.

A fourth family of explanations is more mundane still: leakage or ordinary industrial emissions.

Soviet officials sometimes invoked benign or industrial explanations. Directional measurements and the persistence of U.S. complaints make a purely accidental account difficult to sustain for the core signals described in American records, but “industrial background” should not be dismissed solely because Moscow said it. A city contains many sources. The correct technical question is whether measured direction, timing, frequency stability, and spatial pattern are compatible with an accidental source.

American officials evidently did not think the major signals were adequately explained that way.

That judgment is evidence.

It is not the same as access to the Soviet transmitter.

There may also have been more than one answer.

This possibility deserves more attention than it gets.

The Moscow Signal story covers decades. Equipment changed. Soviet intelligence requirements changed. American countermeasures changed. New signals appeared. One transmitter might have supported passive listening devices in one period; another might have interfered with electronics; a later system might have served a different collection function. American code names can make a series of emissions feel like one continuous project.

Historical continuity should be demonstrated, not assumed.

This is where the title of the book becomes literal. “The signal in the walls” is partly a mistake because there may never have been one signal.

There was an electromagnetic environment shaped by adversarial technical operations.

The desire for one purpose reflects storytelling more than engineering.

Complex intelligence systems often accrete. A technique proves useful, is modified, replaced, or layered with another. Different Soviet organizations could have operated systems near the embassy for different purposes. One service might care about audio intelligence, another about jamming, another about technical research. Institutional plurality on the Soviet side is as plausible as it was on the American side.

This matters because motive arguments usually imagine a single mind called “the Soviets.”

States are not minds.

They are organizations with overlapping authorities, rivalries, secrecy compartments, and changing leadership. A biological-research institute publishing low-level microwave findings does not mean the intelligence unit operating a transmitter at the embassy designed its system around those findings. A KGB technical directorate can use radio energy without consulting a military biomedical program.

Convergence requires evidence of connection.

One of the most seductive arguments for the biological theory is the Soviet safety standard itself. Why would Moscow permit a signal near or above its own strict limit if it did not expect an effect?

There are several answers that do not require a weapon.

Operators may have believed the limit conservative.

The standard may have applied differently to occupational, public, or controlled exposures.

The field at the transmitter site or in Soviet-occupied spaces may have been lower.

Intelligence priorities may have overridden safety rules.

The purpose may have been technical enough to justify an exception.

Or Soviet officials may indeed have accepted a biological risk to Americans.

The standard does not choose among them.

The strongest evidence for purpose would come from Soviet archives.

That archive has not yielded a public, authoritative program record equivalent to an engineering manual labeled “Moscow embassy biological irradiation.” Absence of public evidence is not proof such a document never existed. It limits what can responsibly be claimed now.

This is where suppressed-knowledge narratives usually make their leap. If the decisive archive is inaccessible, secrecy itself becomes evidence that the most dramatic hypothesis is true.

That is epistemically fatal.

A proposition protected from disconfirmation by the absence of records can expand forever.

The correct treatment is to increase uncertainty, not certainty.

The signal’s unresolved purpose is not a flaw in the book.

It is the book.

We have an unusually well-documented case in which a superpower directed electromagnetic energy at another superpower’s embassy for years, the target measured it, studied possible biological effects, complained diplomatically, and never publicly established the sender’s purpose with finality.

That alone is remarkable.

The pressure to add “therefore mind weapon” comes from discomfort with unresolved history.

History is full of systems whose operators died without leaving us the diagram we want.

The honest ending to the purpose question may be ranked hypotheses rather than revelation.

Technical espionage or electronic countermeasures fit the demonstrated environment and require the fewest unsupported assumptions.

Biological effect was sufficiently plausible to American officials to justify research but was not demonstrated at the relevant levels in a way that establishes an operational weapon.

Deliberate biological intent remains unproven.

Harassment as a secondary effect is plausible but undocumented as purpose.

Purely accidental background does not fit the American technical and diplomatic record well for the principal signals.

Multiple systems over time may explain why the record resists one answer.

This hierarchy can change if new primary evidence appears.

That final condition matters.

A good historical thesis should be vulnerable to a file.

If tomorrow a Soviet technical manual emerges with transmitter specifications and operational instructions for activating passive bugs in the chancery, the espionage hypothesis jumps. If a biomedical directive orders exposure of embassy personnel to a modulation chosen to impair cognition, the biological-intent hypothesis jumps. If logs show the emissions correlate precisely with radar interference tests, the jamming account strengthens.

Until then, we should not reward the hypothesis that requires the most imagination simply because it makes the best documentary.

The Soviet transmitter did something.

The archive still does not tell us exactly what.
