# Fifty Feet Wide

A fifty-foot beam is a wonderfully precise image until you ask what, exactly, was fifty feet wide.

Maclyn Musser, a State Department technical security officer, was among the people who turned the Moscow Signal from an unease into a measurable object. In accounts assembled from declassified records, he identified microwave radiation directed at the American embassy and in 1963 described a beam roughly fifty feet across. The figure survives because it gives the invisible thing a body. A reader can put fifty feet against a building facade. One can imagine windows inside the footprint, desks behind the windows, people spending workdays in those rooms.

But the image can outrun the measurement.

Electromagnetic fields do not generally behave like a theatrical spotlight with a knife-edge boundary. Antennas produce patterns. Buildings reflect. Windows, walls, structural steel, nearby objects, and frequency all affect propagation. Measuring a field at several points inside a large masonry structure is not the same as photographing the transmitter’s full radiation pattern. “Beam” is a useful word if it means a directed emission. It becomes misleading if it encourages us to imagine every person within an architectural circle receiving the same exposure.

The physical history of the Moscow Signal is full of this problem: numbers that are real enough to matter but too incomplete to bear the stories later hung from them.

Frequency is one example. Later reviews describe signals in the microwave range, including reported frequencies roughly between 2.5 and 4 gigahertz for major periods of exposure. Power density is another. The values changed over time, and different documents refer to different signals, measurement locations, maxima, averages, and exposure durations. In the mid-1960s, ARPA’s biological work was initially informed by an estimate that proved too high. By 1967, improved measurements placed the older signal at no greater than about 0.05 milliwatts per square centimeter in the terms used by the declassified progress report—fifty microwatts per square centimeter. Other summaries describe still lower typical values. Later, in 1975, a newer signal appeared with intensities reported in State Department material up to about thirteen microwatts per square centimeter and with long daily operating periods.

There is no single “Moscow Signal dose” that can be carried across twenty years like a physical constant.

That sounds obvious. It has not stopped generations of argument from treating the affair as if there were.

A serious exposure reconstruction would want more than a headline maximum. It would need transmitter location or at least reliable direction-finding; carrier frequency; modulation; polarization; duty cycle; time of day; changes across years; field strength at different exterior surfaces; attenuation through windows and walls; multipath reflections; floor plans; office assignments; residence patterns; time spent at desks; and, for any proposed biological mechanism, the quantity actually relevant to that mechanism. For thermal effects, absorbed energy and resulting temperature rise matter. For a hypothesized modulation-specific nonthermal effect, the carrier alone is not enough. For an implanted-device theory, the important quantity may be the field at a concealed resonator and the character of the returned signal.

The historical record provides fragments of this matrix, not the matrix itself.

This is why the story deserves engineering before epidemiology. If exposure cannot be classified with reasonable accuracy, a health study inherits noise before the first medical chart is opened. Two diplomats both listed as having served in Moscow may have had very different electromagnetic environments. One may have worked behind a window in a high-field region during a period of long signal duration. Another may have worked on a different floor during an earlier low-level period. Family members may have lived in parts of the building exposed differently from the chancery offices. People rotated. Signals changed. Shielding was eventually installed.

Retrospective studies can handle imperfect exposure classification, but imperfection has consequences. If many people are placed in the same “exposed” category despite materially different doses, a real effect can be diluted. If assignment patterns differ between Moscow and comparison posts for reasons related to health, age, job, or family status, confounding enters. If dozens of health outcomes are tested, some differences will appear by chance. If investigators later focus only on the significant ones, the literature can harden around noise.

The physical uncertainty therefore does not merely frustrate engineers. It propagates into medicine.

The early American measurements also shaped the animal work directly. Project Pandora and the related BIZARRE experiments attempted to expose animals to microwave conditions thought relevant to Moscow. This sounds like the correct move: if one cannot experiment on embassy personnel, reproduce the signal in a controlled laboratory and see what happens. But the quality of such an experiment depends on whether “reproduce the signal” means anything operationally precise.

Richard Cesaro’s September 1967 progress report is a remarkable document because it records the collapse of an assumption in bureaucratic prose. New ARPA measurements in Moscow had produced a lower estimate of the signal’s power density than the value used for earlier concern. When primate behavior was tested at the corrected level, Cesaro reported no overt performance degradation. A CIA scientist associated with the work concluded that people exposed at the reported Moscow levels were not at risk of injury.

One can read this as a reassuring result. It was.

One can also read it as an example of how intelligence uncertainty enters experimental science. The first question was not simply whether microwaves altered behavior. It was whether microwaves with the relevant Moscow parameters altered behavior. If the exposure estimate changes by an order of magnitude or more, the experiment is a different experiment.

The phrase “relevant Moscow parameters” hides another difficulty: which parameter is relevant?

Suppose the true mechanism, if any, were simple heating. Then average absorbed power and local thermal response would dominate. Suppose instead the mechanism depended on peak power or pulse structure. Then average power density might conceal important differences. Suppose the field was not intended to interact biologically at all but to illuminate or interfere with electronics. Then reproducing the biological exposure while ignoring the target device could tell investigators nothing about Soviet purpose.

The national-security problem therefore had at least three experimental objects occupying the same signal: a human exposure, a communications or surveillance phenomenon, and an adversary action.

Those are not interchangeable.

This distinction becomes more important in the 1970s because U.S. officials began to care not only about the signal’s strength but about the fact that it had changed. In June 1975, Ambassador Walter Stoessel reported a microwave radio-frequency interference signal that appeared distinct from the older TUMS pattern. The newer emission, later associated with MUTS or Signal 3A, became persistent. State Department descriptions said it could operate for many hours each day. Technical teams sought better measurements. Medical experts revisited the health question. The signal’s changed characteristics turned a problem some officials thought had been studied into a live uncertainty again.

This is a recurring failure mode in risk communication. Institutions want a sentence that survives time: “the exposure is below the safety standard” or “the studies found no hazard.” But the sentence has hidden variables. Below which standard? For which signal? Measured where? Averaged over what period? Under what mechanism assumption? If the exposure changes, does the reassurance automatically carry forward?

The embassy did not have the luxury of waiting for perfect characterization.

People were living and working inside the measurement problem. The State Department had to decide whether to tell them. The White House had to decide whether to confront Moscow. Technical officers had to decide whether shielding would help. Medical personnel had to decide whether symptoms required evacuation or testing. Intelligence officers had to decide how much detail could be shared without exposing collection methods. Every decision had to be made with incomplete information and asymmetric costs.

False reassurance could leave employees in a harmful exposure.

False alarm could trigger panic, damage careers, disrupt a sensitive post, compromise intelligence capabilities, create a diplomatic crisis, and poison major negotiations with the Soviet Union.

The existence of those costs does not justify secrecy automatically. It explains why secrecy can become attractive.

There is a line in the history where technical uncertainty becomes political convenience. It is rarely marked with a date. Officials can sincerely believe more measurement is needed before disclosure. They can also notice that every additional month of measurement postpones an unpleasant conversation. The Moscow Signal was present for years while the government accumulated reasons not to tell the people under it.

The measurement problem helped sustain that delay because no single number forced a decision.

At low microwave power densities, the established hazard framework of the era focused heavily on thermal effects. American permissible exposure levels were much higher than the stricter Soviet limits. If one used the American standard, measured embassy levels could look comfortably low. If one used Soviet standards or took Soviet reports of nonthermal bioeffects seriously, the same values looked less comfortable. The signal therefore sat not only between two countries but between two scientific-regulatory cultures.

That difference became rhetorically explosive.

It is easy to imagine how an embassy employee would hear it. The government tells you the beam is below the American safety limit. Then you learn that the government sending the beam uses a limit far lower than ours. You ask whether the Soviets know something we do not. Officials answer that their standard may simply be excessively conservative or based on weak science. You then learn that the Pentagon funded secret animal experiments specifically because it wanted to know whether low-level modulated microwaves could change behavior.

At that point, reassurance becomes a trust problem regardless of what the biology eventually shows.

The stricter Soviet standards have often been treated as indirect evidence of Soviet knowledge about nonthermal effects. That inference needs discipline. A regulation can embody precaution, different interpretations of uncertain literature, workplace policy, institutional conservatism, or a desire for a larger safety margin. It is not a classified confession. Soviet researchers did publish claims about biological effects at exposures below Western thermal thresholds, and those claims deserved replication. Some did not replicate cleanly. The difference in standards is evidence of different regulatory judgments, not proof that the Soviet state possessed a working microwave weapon.

The American side was capable of its own overconfidence. A standard designed to prevent established thermal injury can be perfectly useful while remaining silent about a proposed mechanism nobody has validated. Saying an exposure is below that standard is not the same as saying every conceivable effect has been excluded. Good risk communication names the scope of the claim.

Moscow produced the opposite incentive. Every caveat sounded alarming.

Consider the sentence: “At the measured power density, no established harmful effect is expected, but long-term low-level modulated exposure has not been characterized well enough to exclude every proposed effect.” Scientifically, this can be responsible. To employees who have just learned they were exposed in secret, it sounds like an admission that nobody knows whether they have been harmed. To a diplomat negotiating with Moscow, it sounds like a public crisis waiting for a headline. To a researcher, it sounds like a grant proposal.

The same uncertainty feeds different institutions differently.

That is one reason Project Pandora expanded beyond a single direct replication of the embassy signal. Once the question becomes “what can microwaves do biologically?” rather than “what did this specific Moscow field do to these specific people?” the research space becomes enormous. Frequencies, modulation patterns, pulse structures, species, endpoints, exposure durations, and power densities multiply. A finding in one corner can then drift back toward the embassy case even when the physical parameters differ.

This drift is common in frontier science. An experiment shows an effect under condition A. A real-world mystery exists under poorly characterized condition B. Because A and B share a noun—microwave, plasma, magnetic field, ultrasound, radiation—the effect from A becomes a candidate explanation for B. Sometimes that is how discovery begins. Sometimes it is how categories become haunted.

The cure is parameter discipline.

Every claim in this book has to carry its physical conditions with it. A thermal cataract experiment at hundreds of milliwatts per square centimeter does not establish an effect at microwatts per square centimeter. A pulsed auditory phenomenon at one set of peak powers and pulse widths does not prove a chronic neurological syndrome at another. A behavioral change in a primate exposed under laboratory conditions does not establish that Soviet operators intended behavior modification. An epidemiological association among embassy staff does not identify the transmitter’s purpose.

This can feel like pedantry until one realizes that the entire history turns on orders of magnitude.

The corrected Moscow measurement in 1967 changed the interpretation of the primate work. The 1975 signal changes altered the State Department’s medical posture. Comparisons with Soviet and American exposure standards changed the diplomatic argument. Later debates over Havana would again hinge on whether hypothesized radio-frequency mechanisms could deliver sufficient energy under plausible geometries without leaving clearer physical traces.

Invisible phenomena invite narrative because the reader cannot see the instrument panel.

A photograph of a damaged aircraft wing forces some engineering honesty. A beam does not. It can be described as faint or powerful, narrow or diffuse, continuous or pulsed, targeted or ambient, depending on which feature the storyteller needs. The Moscow record is valuable because enough measurements survive to resist total mythologizing, while enough gaps survive to prevent closure.

One especially important gap is the transmitter side.

American measurements characterize what arrived at or inside the embassy. They do not, by themselves, reveal how much power left the Soviet antenna, how the system was steered, what receiver or target it interacted with, or what operational feedback the Soviet side monitored. A system designed to interrogate passive devices could use low incident power at the room compared with a system designed to heat tissue. A jammer would be evaluated against the electronics it disrupted, not the people standing nearby. A harassment system might optimize annoyance, uncertainty, or political effect rather than injury.

Intent changes the engineering requirements.

That is why “could microwaves hurt someone?” is not the same question as “was this microwave signal a weapon?” The first is biomedical. The second requires evidence of purpose or at least a technical architecture whose function points strongly toward biological targeting.

The public record gives us the first question in abundance and the second in fragments.

The fifty-foot beam is the beginning of that asymmetry.

It was concrete enough to measure, strange enough to investigate, and ambiguous enough to support several futures at once. In one future it is part of a sophisticated eavesdropping system. In another it is counterintelligence interference. In another it is an experiment in chronic biological exposure. In still another, the Soviet operators care nothing about the people inside; the health controversy is an unintended by-product of a technical operation aimed at equipment.

The mistake is choosing the future before the evidence does.

By the time Project Pandora began, American officials had not solved the signal. They had done something more dangerous and more interesting: they had translated a field measurement into a biological question.

The beam was fifty feet wide.

The uncertainty was much larger.
