# The Thing in the Seal

The wooden seal looked ceremonial because that was the point.

In August 1945, children from a Soviet youth organization presented W. Averell Harriman, the American ambassador in Moscow, with a carved Great Seal of the United States. The war in Europe was over. Hiroshima was days away. The United States and Soviet Union were still technically allies, although the future had already begun to harden around them. The carving went into the ambassador’s residence at Spaso House, where it could do what ceremonial objects do best: become invisible through familiarity.

Inside was a listening device.

The National Security Agency’s museum now explains the trick with the bluntness that only decades can provide. The device was passive. It did not need a conventional internal power supply broadcasting continuously. When Soviet operators illuminated it from outside with radio-frequency energy, the resonant cavity responded to sound in the room. The returning signal carried the conversation. When the external illumination stopped, the device became much harder to detect because it was not sitting there obligingly transmitting on its own.

The Americans discovered it in 1952.

There is a reason this object belongs near the beginning of a book about the Moscow Signal. The Great Seal bug is often invoked too aggressively, as if it proves that every later microwave transmission aimed at an American diplomatic building must have been activating a hidden microphone. It proves no such thing. Different frequencies, geometries, devices, buildings, and decades require their own evidence.

What it does prove is that a Soviet intelligence service had operationalized a concept that sounds, when described badly, almost occult: invisible energy projected from outside a building could make a passive object inside reveal information. The energy was not itself carrying the room’s conversation inward. It was illuminating a transducer. The conversation changed the device; the device changed the reflected or reradiated signal; somebody outside listened to the difference.

That distinction is everything.

A person unfamiliar with radio engineering can hear “microwaves aimed at the embassy” and imagine the beam as the payload. In the Great Seal system, the radio-frequency illumination was closer to the flashlight than the photograph. It supplied energy to a hidden mechanism and created a return that could be interpreted. The interesting thing was the interaction between the field and the object in the room.

Once that possibility has existed in the same city, aimed at the same diplomatic target, the later Moscow microwave problem enters a different evidentiary landscape. A security officer who has physically removed a passive resonator from an ambassador’s office does not need a theory of mind control to take unexplained radio-frequency illumination seriously.

The seal also teaches something about technological surprise. Surprise is rarely a clean matter of one country possessing a law of physics unknown to another. The cavity-resonator principle was not supernatural and, in broad terms, not beyond Western science. The surprise came from implementation: a known physical principle, an elegant device, a diplomatic gift, a trusted location, a power architecture that defeated ordinary assumptions about how a bug should behave, and an adversary patient enough to exploit the gap.

This is the kind of surprise intelligence organizations fear because it is not cured by saying, afterward, that the underlying science was available in textbooks.

The discovery changed American technical security. It also distorted it. Once you have found one object that violates your detection assumptions, the rational response is to search for others. But the search itself can widen faster than the evidence. Every unexplained field becomes a possible activator. Every metal surface becomes a candidate resonator. Every strange signal acquires an espionage story. The job is to be suspicious without becoming superstitious.

Moscow made that difficult.

The old embassy building was not a clean American bubble planted inside Soviet territory. Local workers had built parts of it. Soviet citizens worked around it. Technical penetrations accumulated. State Department histories and surviving artifacts document microphones hidden in structural material, including a bug installed through a brick. Jerome Wiesner’s 1964 review came after a major discovery of embedded listening devices. He did not trust the walls because there was evidence not to trust them.

A wall in such a building is not merely a boundary. It is a possible component.

That phrase sounds dramatic until one thinks like an engineer. Building materials conduct vibration. Metalwork can act as an antenna or reflector. Utility lines cross spaces. Electrical systems create unintended paths. A microphone concealed in masonry can use the structure to collect sound. A passive device can be energized from outside. Even an innocent object can reradiate a field in ways that reveal something about its environment. Technical surveillance is full of transformations: sound to motion, motion to capacitance, capacitance to radio response, light to current, current to data.

The Americans knew enough of this by 1964 to recommend electromagnetic screening and continuous signal monitoring. What they did not know was which mechanism, if any, explained the persistent microwave energy directed at the chancery.

There is a methodological lesson here that becomes important later when we reach Project Pandora. A demonstrated mechanism should raise the prior probability of related explanations, but it should not annex them.

The Great Seal system makes an espionage purpose for later directed radio-frequency energy more plausible than it would otherwise be. It does not tell us that TUMS was the illumination channel for a hidden device. To establish that, one would want technical correlation: signal frequency and modulation compatible with known or recovered devices; spatial geometry matching likely targets; changes in signal when rooms, equipment, or diplomatic activity changed; intelligence reporting from Soviet sources; or the recovery of hardware that depended on the observed field.

Publicly available records do not provide a complete chain of that kind.

Instead, they preserve an argument.

By the 1960s and 1970s, American officials considered the possibility that the signal was connected to eavesdropping. Some records also entertained interference with American electronic collection equipment on the embassy roof. Others explored biological effects. It is tempting to rank these as mutually exclusive purposes. They need not have been. A signal could serve a technical intelligence function and still expose occupants. Different signals at different times could have different purposes. Soviet organizations did not have to maintain one unchanging objective across two decades merely because American paperwork later grouped the transmissions under a common label.

The labels themselves encourage false continuity.

“TUMS” and “MUTS” sound like objects. They are administrative containers around observations. A code name can make a changing technical environment feel more coherent than it was. If one frequency disappears and another appears, if direction changes, if power rises, if duty cycle expands, the historian has to decide whether this is evolution of one system, replacement by another, or several unrelated emissions sharing a political target. The people living in the building, meanwhile, experience none of those distinctions. They experience the fact that somebody outside is directing energy at their workplace.

The Great Seal bug had another property that matters: its purpose was obvious once the device was opened.

There was a cavity. There was a diaphragm. There was a physical object placed where it could capture conversation. One could take it to a laboratory, illuminate it, speak in front of it, and reconstruct the collection mechanism. The Moscow Signal offered no equivalent moment of closure. The Americans could measure the energy at the receiving end, but the transmitter’s full architecture and operational purpose remained on the other side of Soviet control.

This asymmetry changed the burden of inference.

When you possess the device, engineering can settle arguments. When you possess only the field, the field has to be interpreted. Direction finding can suggest an origin. Spectrum analysis can reveal frequency and modulation. Power measurements can estimate exposure. Timing can hint at operational patterns. But purpose is not a physical unit. It has to be inferred from correlation, intelligence, recovered equipment, doctrine, or admission.

The biological interpretation was especially vulnerable to this gap because it could grow in the absence of hardware. A hidden eavesdropping device ought, in principle, to be discoverable. A hypothesized biological effect can remain invisible while investigators argue about mechanism. That makes the latter hypothesis unusually durable. Every failure to find a bug can be reinterpreted as evidence that the target was the person rather than the wall.

A good investigation has to resist that one-way ratchet.

The same caution applies in reverse. Once the Great Seal bug is known, it becomes easy to say the microwave signal was “obviously” for surveillance. But that too can become an evidentiary shortcut. If the technical record lacks a recovered device or definitive Soviet documentation connecting the specific signal to a specific collection system, “espionage” remains a strong hypothesis, not a solved case.

Cold War technical security lived inside this tension.

The Americans were not passive. They developed countermeasures, inspected buildings, tested for emissions, shielded rooms, used sound masking, and built their own collection systems. The Soviet complaint, raised in diplomatic exchanges, that American facilities directed signals toward Soviet premises should not be dismissed out of hand merely because it was useful as a debating response. Both superpowers operated dense electronic-intelligence environments around diplomatic missions. The question is not whether only one side used electromagnetic energy for espionage. The question is what a particular signal was doing.

That specificity gets lost in moral theater.

The Great Seal device also complicates the popular language of “radiation.” Radio-frequency electromagnetic radiation sounds, to a lay reader, adjacent to ionizing radiation because the same word is used. The physics is different. Microwaves do not carry enough energy per photon to ionize atoms in the way X-rays and gamma rays do. Their established interactions with tissue at sufficiently high exposures are dominated by heating. The dispute that animated Project Pandora concerned whether low-intensity exposures, including modulation patterns, could produce biologically meaningful effects below levels explained by bulk heating.

The passive resonator does not need any of that biology. Its operation belongs to ordinary electromagnetism.

This matters because one can tell the whole Great Seal story without invoking an exotic effect. The Soviets did something extraordinarily clever with conventional physics. That should make us more cautious, not less, when later stories leap immediately to exotic mechanisms. Intelligence history is full of technologies that seem magical at the target end because the missing apparatus sits elsewhere.

The Great Seal also gives us a useful model for what strong evidence looks like in this domain.

There is an artifact. The artifact has provenance. Government museums preserve it and describe its recovery. Its operation can be physically explained. Its strategic use is consistent with where it was placed. Multiple institutional histories converge on the basic story. There is no need to rely on one anonymous source recalling a program decades later. The uncertainty is in details—exact duration of useful collection, exact information compromised—not in whether the device existed.

That evidentiary profile is rare in stories about secret weapons.

Later in this book we will encounter claims supported by classified interest, by suggestive laboratory results, by epidemiological associations, by participant recollection, by congressional suspicion, or by technical plausibility. None of those is worthless. None should be allowed to masquerade as a Great Seal bug sitting on the table.

One reason anomalous technology stories become unstable is that people unconsciously equalize evidence. A declassified memo noting a possibility, a peer-reviewed animal experiment, a secondhand allegation about a secret program, and a recovered device are placed in the same paragraph as if each adds one unit to the scale. They do not. Their error structures are different.

An artifact can be misidentified. A memo can record a rumor. A witness can be sincere and wrong. An experiment can detect an effect that fails replication. An epidemiological association can arise from confounding or chance. A classified program can spend money on an idea that never works. The discipline is not cynicism. It is refusing to forget which kind of thing one is looking at.

The Great Seal is evidence class one.

The later hypothesis that the Moscow microwave signal interrogated hidden listening devices is more complicated. It is consistent with demonstrated Soviet capability and with the bug-saturated environment of the embassy. It appears in serious official analysis. It may well be correct. Publicly available evidence, however, does not let us close the loop with the same confidence.

That difference is precisely what makes the Moscow Signal interesting.

If the beam had been followed immediately to a receiver and a hidden resonator had been dug from the wall, Project Pandora might never have existed. The mystery would have belonged to technical security. Engineers would have built countermeasures. Diplomats would have protested. The health question might still have been studied, but the most unsettling hypothesis—that the human being was the intended receiver—would have had less room to grow.

Instead, purpose remained open.

Open purpose is a dangerous thing inside a security bureaucracy. It creates competing stories, each attached to different expertise and institutional incentives. Technical-security officers see collection. Communications specialists see interference. Physicians see exposure. behavioral scientists see a possible stimulus. intelligence analysts see adversary capability. diplomats see a bilateral problem that can contaminate negotiations. employees see a government that knows something about their workplace and will not tell them what.

All of them can be acting rationally from where they sit.

The phrase “weaponization of uncertainty” can sound like an accusation that somebody deliberately manufactured confusion. Sometimes that happens. It is not required here. Uncertainty can weaponize itself when institutions have incompatible responsibilities. The security office wants secrecy. The medical office wants disclosure. The embassy wants the transmission stopped. Washington wants to avoid a public crisis. ARPA wants to know whether the biological hypothesis is real. Intelligence collectors want to protect methods. Scientists want accurate exposure parameters. Nobody owns the whole problem.

The Great Seal device had been elegant because every part served a single function.

The American response to the later signal would be the opposite: many parts, many purposes, no shared circuit diagram.

This is where the story shifts from an artifact hidden in wood to an experiment performed on uncertainty itself.

The Americans had learned that radio energy could make a silent object in an embassy speak.

Now they had to decide whether radio energy could make the people inside the embassy change.
