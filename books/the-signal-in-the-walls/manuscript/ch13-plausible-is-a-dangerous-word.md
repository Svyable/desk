# Plausible Is a Dangerous Word

Plausible is the word scientists use when they are trying not to lie.

It is also the word everyone else hears as probably true.

A mechanism is plausible when it does not violate known constraints and fits some relevant observations. That is a low but meaningful bar. It is much higher than fantasy and much lower than event attribution.

The microwave auditory effect makes directed RF exposure plausible as an explanation for some unusual auditory sensations. The National Academies and the Intelligence Community expert panel both found versions of pulsed RF plausibility worth taking seriously in the AHI context. But a plausible mechanism still needs a source capable of generating the exposure, a propagation path, a target geometry, a dose, a temporal match, and preferably an independent measurement.

This ladder is where public argument collapses.

Suppose a report describes a person who hears a sharp sound and experiences vertigo. Step one: can pulsed RF produce an auditory sensation? Yes. Step two: can RF at some parameters produce other biological effects? The literature reports many effects, with varying quality and reproducibility. Step three: can a portable or concealed source deliver those parameters at the reported range? Maybe, depending on the parameters. Step four: did such a source exist at the location? Unknown. Step five: did an adversary operate it? Unknown. Step six: did it cause this person's symptoms? Unknown.

The word plausible belongs mostly between steps one and three.

By step six it has become a smuggler.

Skeptics make a mirror-image error. If a proposed weapon seems operationally cumbersome, they may dismiss the underlying effect. But the physics of microwave hearing does not depend on whether somebody built a useful weapon. A phenomenon can be real and strategically irrelevant. Project Pluto's reactor could work while the full weapon remained unattractive. Remote viewing experiments can produce statistical arguments while operational intelligence value remains poor. The recurring lesson is that subsystem reality and system utility are separate burdens.

Directed-energy claims need the same discipline.

A real bioeffect does not imply an effective weapon.

A technically feasible emitter does not imply deployment.

A deployment does not imply a particular injury.

An injury does not imply a particular adversary.

The reason to preserve those separations is not rhetorical moderation. It is search efficiency. Each layer points investigators toward different evidence. Mechanism demands laboratory replication. Device feasibility demands engineering. Deployment demands logistics and collection. Injury causation demands medicine and exposure data. Attribution demands intelligence.

Plausibility is useful only if it tells you what to measure next.
