# Hearing Radio

There is a category of fact so strange that people use it badly in both directions.

The microwave auditory effect is one of them.

Beginning with work by Allan Frey in the early 1960s, researchers demonstrated that pulsed radiofrequency energy could produce auditory sensations in humans under specific exposure conditions. Subjects described clicks, buzzing, knocking, or related sounds. The effect did not require ordinary acoustic sound to travel from the transmitter to the ear. Later experiments and physical analysis established a thermoacoustic mechanism: a sufficiently brief pulse deposits a small amount of energy in tissue, causing rapid thermal expansion and launching a pressure wave that can stimulate the auditory system.

This is not fringe physics.

That sentence is important because the phenomenon has been repeatedly dragged into arguments where its reality is confused with the reality of whatever story follows it.

The effect proves that electromagnetic energy can, under particular pulse conditions, create an internally perceived sound. It does not prove remote speech injection. It does not prove brain damage. It does not prove the cause of any reported AHI. It does not prove a Soviet weapon. It does not prove that a person who heard a click was irradiated.

The fact is narrower and more interesting.

In 1962 Frey reported perceived sounds at carrier frequencies including 425 and 1,310 MHz, with peak power density far above the average because the energy arrived in pulses. In 1974 Kenneth Foster and Edward Finch published evidence that pulsed microwaves could generate acoustic transients in water at levels sufficient to account for the clicks heard by exposed people. Later work by Arthur Guy, C. K. Chou, James Lin, and others deepened the physical and biological description. Reviews now describe the thermoelastic theory as the accepted mechanism for microwave hearing.

The mechanism creates a useful forensic signature. If a reported sound is produced through microwave hearing, there should be exposure parameters consistent with the effect. There is an energy-per-pulse threshold, frequency-dependent absorption, a relationship between pulse width and generated pressure, and constraints imposed by antenna physics. One can ask whether a hypothesized source at a given distance could deliver the required pulses without obvious collateral signatures. One can ask what detector bandwidth would have been necessary to see it. One can ask whether the event description—click, buzz, spatial localization, persistence—matches the known phenomenon or merely resembles it metaphorically.

This is where the modern debate often becomes unsatisfying. The National Academies' 2020 review of illnesses reported by U.S. personnel judged directed, pulsed radiofrequency energy a plausible mechanism for important features of the early cases. The Intelligence Community's 2022 expert panel also concluded that pulsed electromagnetic energy could plausibly explain the core characteristics of a subset of AHIs, while emphasizing information gaps.

Plausible is not empty. A panel of specialists saying a mechanism fits observed features deserves weight.

Plausible is also not a detection.

The missing object remains the field measurement taken during an incident.

Moscow had one.

That is why it keeps haunting this subject.
