# The Lilienfeld Problem

By the time Abraham Lilienfeld’s team entered the story, the experiment had already happened in the worst possible way.

Nobody had randomized exposure. Nobody had assigned dose. Nobody had built a control building. The Soviet Union had directed microwaves at the American embassy for years, personnel had rotated through, technical characteristics had changed, medical monitoring had been partly secret, and the population learned about the exposure only after much of it was over.

Epidemiology had to reverse-engineer an experiment from bureaucracy.

Lilienfeld, a major figure in twentieth-century epidemiology at Johns Hopkins, led the Foreign Service Health Status Study commissioned by the State Department. The basic design was retrospective: identify people who had served in Moscow, compare their health experience with Foreign Service personnel at other Eastern European posts, and look for differences in mortality and illness that might be associated with the microwave exposure.

This was the right kind of study to attempt.

It was also almost guaranteed to be argued about forever.

The official conclusion was broadly reassuring. The study did not find convincing evidence that service in Moscow was associated with a pattern of illness attributable to the microwave signal. The 1979 Senate staff review reached a similar cautious position: no convincing evidence directly implicated the microwave exposure in adverse health effects as of the time of analysis, while long-term mortality follow-up remained limited by the passage of time.

That conclusion has been attacked from two directions ever since.

One side treats the study as proof that the Moscow Signal was harmless.

The other treats scattered statistically significant findings and design weaknesses as evidence that a real syndrome was hidden or dismissed.

Both positions ask more from the data than the data comfortably provide.

The first problem is exposure classification.

“Moscow” is a location, not a dose.

Personnel assigned to the embassy did not all work in the same room, during the same years, under the same signal conditions. Some were present during earlier weaker transmissions. Others were present after signal characteristics changed. Office location mattered. Residential location may have mattered. Shielding changed. Daily duration varied. Individual time spent in the building varied.

The comparison group had the opposite problem. Personnel at other Eastern European posts were treated as less exposed to the specific Moscow signal, but they were not a random sample of identical people transported to a different building. Assignments reflected job, rank, family status, language, career path, health clearance, and institutional needs.

Retrospective cohort studies routinely manage such imperfections. The question is how much they matter relative to the effect size being sought.

If Moscow exposure produced a large increase in a specific rare disease, even crude classification might detect it.

If it produced subtle symptoms, a small risk increase, or an effect confined to those sitting in a high-field zone during a particular period, location-level classification could easily dilute it.

This is not special pleading. It is ordinary measurement error.

The second problem is outcome multiplicity.

A broad health study examines many diseases and symptoms. Some differences will reach conventional statistical significance by chance. The more outcomes examined, the more likely at least one unusual result appears. A responsible interpretation looks for coherent patterns: dose-response, related diagnoses, replication, biological plausibility, consistency across sexes and age groups, and persistence in follow-up.

Critics of the official interpretation have pointed to particular elevated outcomes and argued that they fit a microwave or radiofrequency sickness picture. A 1998 review by researcher John Goldsmith, for example, revisited statistically significant findings and argued that they warranted further inquiry. Later authors have similarly reexamined the cohort.

The existence of these reanalyses means the case cannot be reduced to “the study found nothing.”

The stronger statement is that the study did not produce a convincing, coherent causal pattern that resolved the exposure question.

J. Mark Elwood’s 2012 review is useful because it returns the argument to epidemiological structure. He examined the original study and its interpretation, emphasizing limitations but finding that the evidence did not support a clear adverse health effect from the Moscow exposure. A 2019 review by José Martínez again revisited the case, arguing that loose ends remained and that the historical study should not be treated as the final word.

These later papers disagree in emphasis partly because the underlying data cannot be made more randomized by analysis.

The argument is over how much uncertainty remains after an imperfect natural experiment.

This is a common pattern in environmental epidemiology. The exposure is involuntary and historically interesting precisely because it was not controlled. The people who later demand a definitive answer are asking observational data to behave like a trial.

It cannot.

One response is to retreat into agnosticism. That is too easy.

Imperfect studies still move probabilities.

If thousands of person-years of exposure fail to produce a strong, consistent disease pattern, hypotheses predicting large common effects weaken. If a proposed syndrome should dramatically increase mortality or a specific cancer across the exposed population, the absence of that pattern matters. The data do not need to be perfect to reject extravagant claims.

At the same time, absence of a large population signal does not exclude every small, rare, delayed, or highly exposure-specific effect.

That is the correct asymmetry.

The Lilienfeld study therefore narrows the plausible harm profile.

It does not certify zero risk.

It also does not answer Soviet intent.

This point is worth repeating because health outcomes have repeatedly been used as a proxy for purpose. If the epidemiology is negative, some writers conclude the signal must have been for espionage. If an association appears, they conclude the signal was a weapon. Neither follows.

A surveillance system could cause an unintended health effect.

A biological harassment program could fail to cause a measurable effect.

Purpose requires purpose evidence.

The cohort has another interpretive problem: disclosure itself.

People who learned they had been exposed may have reported symptoms differently afterward. Medical surveillance could increase diagnosis. Employees concerned about microwaves might seek care more frequently. Conversely, classified culture could suppress reporting before the story became public. These effects can influence morbidity data in opposite directions.

Mortality is less vulnerable to reporting bias but less sensitive to many neurological or functional outcomes.

This is why no single endpoint could settle the case.

The health study also arrived in an atmosphere where trust had already collapsed. Employees knew the government had withheld information. Some knew of secret medical monitoring. Official reassurance therefore looked self-interested.

A study commissioned by the same department that had delayed disclosure faced a credibility problem independent of methods.

This is not a scientific reason to reject the results.

It is a governance reason the results failed to close the controversy.

Independence matters because people do not evaluate evidence in a vacuum. If the institution under scrutiny funds the study, controls exposure records, and communicates the conclusion, critics will ask whether unfavorable findings were suppressed. The best answer is transparency: release protocols, data definitions, analytic methods, correspondence, and follow-up so independent researchers can reproduce the analysis.

The Moscow record became more open over decades, but not fast enough to prevent mythology.

The same problem appears in modern disputes over environmental exposures, military burn pits, contaminated water, occupational toxins, and anomalous health incidents. Agencies often begin from a narrow question—can we prove causation?—while exposed people ask a different one—can you prove this did not hurt me?

Neither side can usually meet the other’s burden.

Causal inference operates between those impossible demands.

One useful approach is to state what the data would have looked like under competing hypotheses.

If the Moscow signal caused a strong generalized illness syndrome in most exposed personnel, one would expect broad differences between the Moscow cohort and comparison posts, perhaps with exposure-duration gradients. The study did not reveal such a decisive pattern.

If the signal caused a small effect in a susceptible minority, the cohort might show little, especially with coarse exposure classification.

If specific modulation patterns during certain years mattered, averaging service across decades could obscure the relevant subgroup.

If anxiety after disclosure drove many subjective symptoms, objective mortality differences might remain absent while symptom reports rose.

If the signal caused no health effect, a broad study would still produce some statistically unusual outcomes by chance.

The actual data are compatible with more than one of these weaker hypotheses.

That is why mechanism matters.

A validated mechanism would tell investigators which outcomes and exposure metrics deserve emphasis. Without one, retrospective epidemiology becomes a wide search.

The microwave-biology literature never supplied a single mechanism specific enough to turn Moscow service into a clean causal model.

This is where advocates sometimes reach for the term “microwave syndrome.” Historical Soviet and Eastern European literature described clusters involving fatigue, headaches, sleep disturbance, cardiovascular changes, and other symptoms among workers exposed to radio-frequency fields. Western researchers disputed whether the syndrome represented a distinct exposure-caused entity, nonspecific symptoms, thermal effects, stress, or methodological artifact.

A syndrome built from common symptoms is epidemiologically difficult because the background rate is high.

Headaches are common in people who have never worked in a microwave field.

So are insomnia, fatigue, dizziness, and concentration problems.

This does not mean the symptoms are imaginary. It means causal attribution requires strong exposure-response structure.

The Lilienfeld study could not supply that at individual dosimetry resolution.

One can see why later analysts continue reopening it. The study is large enough to seem decisive and flawed enough to resist decisiveness.

This is the Lilienfeld problem.

It is not that the epidemiology was bad.

It is that the event was badly designed for epidemiology.

The distinction matters for how responsibility is assigned. Scientists did not choose an embassy as their laboratory. Diplomats did not choose the signal. Employees did not consent to exposure. Intelligence secrecy degraded the information environment. By the time a capable epidemiologist arrived, the causal structure had already been scrambled.

There is a temptation to demand that later science undo earlier governance.

It cannot.

What it can do is bound claims.

The Moscow cohort does not support saying that a large epidemic of microwave-caused disease was demonstrated among embassy personnel.

It does not support saying every reported illness was unrelated.

It does not establish a Soviet biological attack.

It does justify continued attention to the quality of exposure reconstruction, long-term follow-up, and the possibility that subtle or subgroup effects could have been missed.

Those are less dramatic conclusions than either acquittal or conviction.

They are more durable.

The best evidence in controversial science often has this shape. It reduces the space in which extraordinary claims can live without erasing uncertainty entirely.

That is not failure.

That is what evidence is for.
