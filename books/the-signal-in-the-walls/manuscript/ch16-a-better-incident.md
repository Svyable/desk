# A Better Incident

If the next anomalous health incident happens tomorrow, the first hour matters more than the next congressional hearing.

The goal should be to make the incident scientifically better.

That sounds grotesque when a person may be injured. It means something practical: collect enough contemporaneous data that investigators do not spend the next decade arguing from analogy.

A modern diplomatic facility can maintain passive wideband RF sensors covering frequencies selected for likely threat mechanisms and local communications environments. The system should preserve short high-resolution buffers, because unusual pulsed signals can disappear inside long averaging windows. Acoustic and ultrasonic sensors should be synchronized. Magnetic-field channels may help reject or identify other sources. Environmental data—temperature, pressure, HVAC state, power anomalies—should share a clock.

None of this requires living in a Faraday cage.

The challenge is false positives. Cities are electromagnetic jungles. Wi-Fi, radar, satellite links, cellular systems, security scanners, broadcast transmitters, microwave backhaul, switching supplies, and malfunctioning electronics all generate structure. A detector that alarms at novelty will become a machine for manufacturing incidents.

So the system needs a baseline before it needs a siren.

The same is true medically. Personnel in higher-risk posts can volunteer for standardized vestibular, ocular, auditory, cognitive, and neurological baselines. The State Department has already moved in this direction. A post-event exam is much more informative when the clinician knows what the patient looked like six months earlier.

The response protocol should then separate care from attribution. Treat symptoms. Preserve specimens. Capture exact location. Record device state. Interview witnesses independently before narratives converge. Pull building RF logs. Preserve negative sensor channels. Do not tell the patient that a weapon was used before evidence supports it. Do not tell the patient nothing happened because the first detector was quiet.

The instrument itself must be audited. A broadband meter designed for occupational safety may report a harmless time-averaged field while missing nanosecond or microsecond peaks. A spectrum analyzer may sweep past an intermittent emitter. A phone is not a calibrated RF dosimeter. Detector failure modes belong in the incident file.

Then comes the step government investigations often perform late: adversarial testing of the favored hypothesis.

If pulsed RF is suspected, bring in engineers who think the RF story is wrong. Ask them to design signals the detector would miss and mundane sources that could imitate the observation. If psychogenic or functional mechanisms are considered, involve clinicians who treat patients with structural neurological injury. If a foreign service is suspected, have regional analysts build the best non-attack explanation for the same collection.

A better incident is not one with a more dramatic conclusion.

It is one with fewer places for belief to hide.
