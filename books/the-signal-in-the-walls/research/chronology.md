# Chronology — The Signal in the Walls

- **1945** — Soviet schoolchildren present Ambassador W. Averell Harriman with the carved Great Seal of the United States containing a passive resonant-cavity listening device.
- **1952** — U.S. personnel discover the Great Seal device at Spaso House.
- **Early 1950s** — U.S. technical personnel begin detecting unusual radio-frequency energy associated with the Moscow embassy environment; later records describe a continuous microwave signal.
- **1963** — State Department technical security officer Maclyn Musser identifies microwave radiation and describes a directed beam roughly fifty feet across.
- **June 1964** — Seventeen Soviet listening devices are discovered in the walls of the U.S. Embassy. Jerome Wiesner conducts a security review and urges officials to understand or stop the microwave signal.
- **Mid-1960s** — ARPA/DARPA and associated agencies organize PANDORA/BIZARRE work on possible microwave biological/behavioral effects and Soviet research claims.
- **1967** — Improved ARPA measurements in Moscow revise the power-density estimate downward. Richard Cesaro reports no overt primate performance degradation at the corrected level and discusses possible future controlled human testing.
- **June 1967** — Secretary of State Dean Rusk raises the microwave transmissions with Soviet foreign minister Andrei Gromyko at Glassboro; the U.S. says it does not know the signal’s purpose and wants it stopped.
- **Late 1960s–early 1970s** — U.S. research includes animal work, occupational comparisons, and medical/cytogenetic investigation of Moscow personnel. Personnel are not fully informed of the underlying exposure concern.
- **1975** — A newer or changed microwave signal becomes a renewed technical and medical concern; U.S. officials seek more precise measurements and discuss further protest.
- **Late 1975** — Senior U.S. officials, including Henry Kissinger, press Soviet ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin about stopping the transmissions while worrying about domestic disclosure.
- **Early 1976** — Ambassador Walter Stoessel presses for personnel briefing as rumors and concern grow. A planned briefing is cancelled and then revived as the situation becomes untenable.
- **1976** — Embassy personnel are formally informed; the microwave issue becomes public. State Department materials stress that no causal relationship between transmissions and health problems has been established.
- **1977** — ARPA official George Heilmeier tells Congress that DARPA does not foresee developing microwave weapons to alter nervous-system function or behavior and is unaware of U.S. or adversary forces developing such weapons.
- **1978** — The Foreign Service Health Status / Lilienfeld work is completed/reported, finding no apparent coherent health pattern establishing microwave causation while leaving methodological and latency questions.
- **1979** — Senate Commerce Committee staff review criticizes the failure to inform employees and finds no convincing evidence then directly implicating microwave exposure in adverse health effects.
- **1980s onward** — FOIA releases and historical research gradually reconstruct PANDORA and the microwave diplomacy; significant intelligence-community records about nonbiological testing and purpose remain unavailable publicly.
- **2012** — J. Mark Elwood publishes a peer-reviewed reassessment of the Moscow embassy epidemiology.
- **2016** — U.S. personnel in Havana begin reporting unusual sensory and neurological symptoms later grouped under “anomalous health incidents.”
- **2020** — A National Academies committee concludes directed pulsed radio-frequency energy is a plausible mechanism for a distinctive subset of reported illnesses, with important limitations and no attribution conclusion.
- **March 1, 2023** — U.S. Intelligence Community assessment judges foreign-adversary responsibility for anomalous health incidents very unlikely overall.
- **July 29, 2024** — GAO reports gaps in AHI patient communication, care coordination, and registry implementation.
- **January 10, 2025** — Updated IC assessment retains the overall “very unlikely” foreign-adversary judgment while recording minority components with greater uncertainty about a small subset and possible relevant RF capability.

## Chronology cautions

The phrase “Moscow Signal” spans changing emissions over decades. Do not imply continuity of transmitter, waveform, organization, or purpose without a document that establishes it. Date every safety claim, measurement, and medical judgment against the signal characteristics known at that time.
