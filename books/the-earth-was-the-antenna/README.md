Working promise: reconstruct Project Sanguine, Seafarer, and the operational U.S. Navy ELF system closely enough to show what it physically costs to make an electromagnetic signal penetrate seawater and reach a deeply submerged submarine. The machine is useful precisely because it was enormous, inefficient, politically visible, and constrained by bandwidth. It supplies a physical control for later claims about weak fields, hidden transmitters, remote biological influence, and globe-spanning electromagnetic effects.

The strongest competing explanation is deliberately built in: Sanguine's gigantism reflected a particular Cold War communications requirement—reliable one-way strategic submarine messaging with 1960s–1980s engineering—not a universal lower bound on every ELF source or biological interaction. The book will not turn one engineered system into a proof that all weak-field effects are impossible. It will ask a narrower question: when a claim requires a specified field at a specified target, where are the source, coupling path, power, geometry, modulation, noise budget, and measured dose?
