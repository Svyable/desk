*How Algorithms Learned to Want Things for You*

The working thesis is that recommendation systems did not invent human suggestibility, but they industrialized it: they made the sorting of choice cheap, continuous, personalized, and mostly invisible, tuned to optimize engagement, purchase, or retention rather than whatever the chooser would have said they wanted if asked directly. Agentic AI — software empowered to search, decide, and pay on a person's behalf — completes that shift by removing the moment of comparison itself.

The book moves from the 2000 jam-tasting experiment that explains why curation exists at all, through the patents and papers that built collaborative filtering (Amazon's "customers who bought this also bought," Spotify's Discover Weekly, YouTube's watch-time engine), into the empirical fight over filter bubbles and algorithmic radicalization, through personalized pricing, slot-machine-style engagement design, and dating-app matching, and out into the agentic checkout stack now shipping — ChatGPT's Instant Checkout, Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol, the Agent Payments Protocol — asking what remains of a human "taste" that a machine is increasingly authorized to act on without asking first.
