# Research trail — Call Someone Else

This directory is the publication research trail for *Call Someone Else: The Hidden Architecture of Escalation* by Sven Hardy Benson.

The manuscript is narrative nonfiction. Institutional examples are used to examine a design question: what changes when a problem crosses the knowledge, authority, capacity, independence, presence, or legitimacy of the person currently holding it? A case is not treated as proof that its design generalizes everywhere.

## Source hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. Primary rules, procedures, standards, official investigations, and institutional documentation.
2. Peer-reviewed or otherwise methodologically transparent research.
3. High-quality secondary reporting for context that primary material cannot supply.
4. Commentary only when the fact being established is the commentator’s own view.

Historical dialogue and inner thoughts are not reconstructed unless a checkable record supports them.

## Chapter map

### 1 — Call Someone Else

Conceptual synthesis. Check every concrete institutional example against the chapter-specific sources below. Falsification question: when does escalation merely displace responsibility rather than add capability?

### 2 — The Call Taker

Primary source family: U.S. National 911 Program / 911.gov materials on Next Generation 911, routing, transfer, interoperability, and continuity. Falsification question: when does additional routing create delay or context loss rather than resilience?

### 3 — Failure to Rescue

Primary/research source family: AHRQ Patient Safety Network materials and systematic evidence on rapid-response systems. Preserve the mixed evidence: rapid-response systems should not be described as a universal mortality solution. Falsification question: can extra calls create alarm burden, defensive escalation, or unnecessary intervention?

### 4 — Unable

Primary source family: FAA Aeronautical Information Manual and applicable operating rules concerning pilot-in-command authority, amended clearances, and inability to comply safely. Falsification question: how does a system prevent refusal authority from becoming arbitrary noncompliance?

### 5 — The Pager

Primary source family: Google Site Reliability Engineering materials on on-call work, incident response, paging, and escalation policy. Falsification question: at what point does escalation noise consume the expert capacity the system is trying to reach?

### 6 — Transfer of Command

Primary source family: FEMA / U.S. Fire Administration Incident Command System materials on transfer of command and briefing content. Falsification question: when is continuity under the current commander safer than transfer?

### 7 — The Appeal

Primary source family: Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts public guidance on courts of appeals, appellate review, records, and the distinction between review and retrial. Falsification question: how do finality, cost, and standards of review prevent infinite escalation?

### 8 — Stop the Line

Primary source family: Toyota Production System materials on jidoka and andon. Falsification question: when can stop authority be overused, ritualized, or socially suppressed despite formal permission?

### 9 — Flight

Primary source family: Columbia Accident Investigation Board report and NASA materials on Mission Control, technical authority, organizational silence, and lessons from Columbia. Falsification question: does adding an independent authority actually create independence if incentives and information remain shared?

### 10 — The Back Channel

Primary source family: U.S. Department of State Office of the Historian material on the 1963 U.S.–Soviet direct communications link following the Cuban Missile Crisis. Falsification question: when can privileged direct channels undermine ordinary institutions or create unequal access?

### 11 — The Specialist

Research source family: studies of specialist referral completion, communication between primary and specialty care, and closing the referral loop. Falsification question: when does referral increase fragmentation without adding usable expertise?

### 12 — May I Speak to Your Supervisor?

Primary source family: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau materials describing the consumer complaint process and company response pathway. Falsification question: when does supervisor escalation reward aggression or privilege customers with more time, confidence, or status?

### 13 — When Speaking Up Costs You

Primary/research source family: NASA safety materials on organizational silence and normalization of deviance, supplemented by organizational research on employee voice and speaking up. Falsification question: can a formally protected route still be unusable because informal retaliation remains cheap?

### 14 — The Machine That Knows It Does Not Know

Primary/research source family: NIST AI Risk Management Framework materials on roles, human oversight, governance, and communication; research literature on classification with a reject option / selective prediction. Falsification question: when does human review merely launder an automated decision because the human lacks time, context, independence, or authority?

### 15 — Escalation Debt

Conceptual synthesis grounded in the recurring patterns established in earlier chapters. Treat “escalation debt” as the book’s term, not an established scientific construct. Falsification question: when is repeated escalation evidence of healthy specialization rather than badly placed capability?

### 16 — The Handoff Tax

Conceptual synthesis supported by incident-command, clinical-referral, and operations examples. Treat “handoff tax” as descriptive language, not a measured universal constant. Falsification question: when is overlap waste rather than useful redundancy?

### 17 — The Wrong Person at the Top

Conceptual synthesis using Mission Control, incident command, specialist referral, and appellate review. Falsification question: when is hierarchy itself the relevant capability because legitimate authority—not expertise—is what the case lacks?

### 18 — Build the Second Person

Synthesis chapter. Every proposed design principle should be traceable to at least two materially different domains or explicitly labeled as an inference. Falsification question: which principles fail when expertise is extremely scarce or decisions must be made immediately?

### 19 — The Dead Man’s Switch

Research before publication must distinguish colloquial “dead man’s switch” language from specific vigilance, presence, watchdog, fail-safe, timeout, and acknowledgment mechanisms. Use authoritative technical sources for any concrete implementation. Falsification question: when does monitoring absence become surveillance, bureaucracy, or a dangerous automatic response?

### 20 — Two Keys

Research before publication must distinguish dual control, two-person control, separation of duties, independent verification, and domain-specific authorization procedures. Use authoritative domain sources for concrete examples. Falsification question: when do two people share the same error, incentive, source, or hierarchy so completely that the second signature adds ceremony but no independence?

## Publication rule

A chapter is not source-complete because this file names a source family. Before publication, every material factual claim that is not common knowledge must be checked against the actual source and entered in `source-ledger.csv`. Counterevidence should be retained in the prose where it changes the reader’s conclusion, not hidden here.
