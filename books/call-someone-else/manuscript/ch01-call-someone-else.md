# Call Someone Else

The first person is usually an accident.

Not an accident in the sense that the person is unqualified or should not be there. An accident in the simpler sense that complicated problems rarely arrange themselves to appear first in front of the ideal expert.

A customer notices a charge and calls the number on the back of a card. A parent hears a strange noise from the furnace and reaches whoever answers the service line. A patient becomes short of breath while the bedside nurse happens to be in the room. A production service begins returning errors while one engineer has the pager. A driver finds a downed tree and calls 911. An employee sees a machine behaving oddly and tells the nearest supervisor. A junior lawyer opens a document and discovers a fact that changes the case.

The first person matters enormously. But the first person is almost never a guarantee that the problem has arrived where it belongs.

This is so obvious that we stop seeing it. We design institutions around specialization, then build myths around individual competence. The myths say: handle what lands on your desk. Own it. Solve it. Be resourceful. Do not become the person who needs constant rescue.

The institution, meanwhile, quietly says something more realistic. If it is electrical, call facilities. If it is a security incident, call security. If the patient deteriorates, activate the clinical response. If the service is outside your ownership, page the owning team. If the legal issue exceeds your authority, send it to counsel. If the customer threatens litigation, stop improvising. If the aircraft cannot safely comply, say so. If you are the incident commander and the incident has outgrown you, transfer command.

The contradiction is not trivial. It produces a peculiar form of organizational danger: people are formally given escalation paths while socially rewarded for not using them.

A new employee learns this faster from facial expressions than from policy. There is the manager who says, “You can always ask,” then sighs when asked. The senior engineer who publishes office hours and answers the first basic question with enough contempt to end future traffic. The doctor who wants nurses to raise concerns but becomes visibly irritated when a concern turns out to be benign. The executive who says, “Wake me up for anything important,” without defining important, which means the person holding the phone at 2:00 a.m. must calculate not only the operational risk but the interpersonal one.

Every escalation system therefore has two versions.

There is the version drawn on paper.

Then there is the version people believe.

The distance between them can be small. In a good system, the person facing a boundary can identify the next person, understand the threshold for involving them, transfer useful context, and continue to participate until ownership is clear. The next person does not punish the escalation merely because the problem turns out to be manageable. False alarms are examined for whether the threshold was sensible, not treated as a character test.

In a bad system, the official map still looks fine. The names and phone numbers exist. The boxes connect. Yet the actual map contains invisible tollbooths: embarrassment, delay, status, blame, fear of waking someone, fear of being labeled dramatic, fear of admitting ignorance, fear that the recipient will simply bounce the problem back.

The difference explains why “just escalate it” is often useless advice. If the path were real, the person probably would.

The most useful way I have found to think about escalation is to ask what the current layer lacks.

Sometimes it lacks knowledge. The person cannot reliably interpret what is happening. A generalist needs a specialist. A customer-service representative needs someone who understands a particular account restriction. An engineer needs the person who knows the database internals. A primary-care clinician needs a specialist's view. Knowledge escalation is the easiest kind to praise because asking an expert sounds sensible.

Sometimes the shortage is authority. The current person may know exactly what should happen but lack permission to make it happen. A representative can see that a refund is justified but has a dollar limit. A nurse can see that a patient's condition is worsening but cannot independently order every intervention. A technician can identify a safety problem but cannot unilaterally redesign the product. An employee can recognize a conflict of interest but cannot adjudicate it. Authority escalation is more political because it forces the organization to reveal where decision rights actually live.

Sometimes the shortage is capacity. The person may know what to do and be allowed to do it, but cannot do it quickly enough alone. A fire department requests more units. An incident response team brings in additional engineers. A surgeon calls for another specialist. A dispatcher coordinates multiple agencies. Capacity escalation is a reminder that competence is not the same thing as infinite bandwidth.

And sometimes the shortage is independence. This one is easy to miss. The current person may know enough, have authority, and have capacity, yet the decision should still move because the system needs a perspective not captured by the original chain. Courts of appeal exist partly for this reason. Safety organizations create independent technical review for this reason. Auditors, ombuds offices, inspectors general, peer reviewers, and external boards exist because certain judgments become less trustworthy when the same person or hierarchy is asked to investigate itself.

These shortages overlap. A problem can need more knowledge and more authority. A team can have the right expertise but no time. An executive can have authority but be too invested in the original decision to provide meaningful review. The point is not to sort every escalation into a neat box. It is to notice that “someone else” is not a generic resource. The value of the second person comes from what is different about them.

That difference is what separates escalation from passing the buck.

Passing the buck moves discomfort.

Escalation moves the problem toward something it lacks.

The distinction becomes visible in the handoff. Suppose an engineer discovers that a production service is failing because a database is saturated. The engineer can page the database team with one sentence: “Database is broken, please fix.” That technically moves the problem, but it discards the work already done. The receiving team must begin by discovering what the first person already knew.

Or the engineer can transfer the relevant context: when the failure began, which service is affected, what changed, what has been ruled out, what mitigation is in place, which dashboards matter, how severe the user impact appears, and why the database team is now needed. The first person may remain on the incident while the specialist takes a specific part of the problem.

The second version is not merely more polite. It preserves information.

Escalation fails surprisingly often because people treat ownership like a package that can be dropped at another desk. They forget that the package has a history.

Emergency systems have learned this the hard way. A caller does not simply possess an “emergency.” The call contains location, hazards, number of people involved, symptoms, uncertainty, background noise, fragments of sequence, and sometimes contradictions. The act of routing is valuable only if enough of that context survives to help the next person act.

The same is true in medicine. A patient's deterioration is not a red icon that can travel without a story. What changed? Over what period? What are the vital signs? What medications were given? What is unusual relative to this patient's baseline? What concerns the bedside clinician even if no single number crosses a clean threshold?

The same is true in law. An appellate court does not receive a vague complaint that the lower court felt wrong. It receives a record, briefs, identified grounds for review, and a constrained question about whether the proceedings or legal conclusions require correction.

The same is true in organizations that are not dealing with life, liberty, or infrastructure. A manager who forwards an email with “Thoughts?” is creating an escalation while outsourcing the work of reconstructing the problem. A better handoff tells the recipient why they are being involved and what decision, expertise, or authority is needed.

This sounds ordinary because the best pieces of institutional design often do.

The difficulty appears when there is uncertainty about whether the threshold has been crossed.

If a building is visibly on fire, most people understand that the problem has moved beyond personal troubleshooting. The interesting cases happen earlier.

A patient looks a little worse but not dramatically worse. A server is behaving strangely but still serving traffic. A supplier says a shipment may be late. A pilot receives a clearance that is legal on its face but uncomfortable in the conditions. A line worker notices a defect that may be isolated. A junior employee sees a senior colleague making a decision that feels wrong but can imagine an innocent explanation.

These are the moments when delay can masquerade as prudence.

People tell themselves they will gather one more fact. Try one more fix. Wait for one more measurement. See whether it resolves. Avoid overreacting.

Sometimes that is exactly right. Escalation has costs. It interrupts other work. It consumes scarce attention. It can cause expensive stoppages. It can embarrass people unnecessarily. A hospital cannot summon every specialist for every mild abnormality. A software organization cannot page ten teams for each brief spike in latency. A court system cannot offer infinite levels of review. A factory that stops constantly for harmless variation cannot produce reliably.

Good systems therefore do not remove judgment. They change what the judgment is about.

Instead of asking, “Am I certain something is wrong?” the system may ask whether the problem has crossed the point at which another set of eyes is cheaper than continuing alone.

That is a different standard.

It accepts that escalation can be correct even when the feared outcome never arrives.

This matters because hindsight corrupts our evaluation of alarms. If the senior engineer joins an incident and fixes it in six minutes, the person who paged may look as if they overreacted. But the speed of the resolution may be evidence that the escalation worked. If a rapid-response team examines a patient and decides no transfer is needed, the call was not necessarily wasteful. The purpose of review is partly to discover that the situation is less dangerous than feared.

A fire alarm is not judged only by whether the building burns down.

Organizations often understand this principle for machines and forget it for people. They install smoke detectors with expected false alarms, redundant sensors, circuit breakers, pressure relief valves, and automated failovers. They accept that safety infrastructure will sometimes activate when catastrophe was not inevitable.

Then they tell the employee holding an ambiguous human signal to be very sure before bothering anyone.

The asymmetry reveals how much escalation is governed by status rather than risk.

The higher the next person sits, the more the first person is asked to justify the interruption. That is reasonable up to a point. Senior attention is scarce. But the organization can accidentally make status itself the gating variable. The question stops being whether the problem needs different authority or expertise and becomes whether the junior person feels confident enough to invade the senior person's time.

The result is predictable. Confident people escalate. Less confident people wait. People with strong informal relationships escalate. Outsiders do not. Veterans know which phone number bypasses the official queue. Newcomers follow the published route and discover that it leads to voicemail.

The actual system becomes a network of social privilege.

One reason emergency organizations use named roles and formal procedures is to reduce this dependence on personal access. You do not need to be friends with the incident commander to know the role exists. A 911 caller does not need a private relationship with the fire chief. A litigant does not need to know a trial judge's boss. A line worker should not need to be socially powerful to signal an abnormality.

Formalization can go too far. Bureaucracies can turn a simple request for help into a ticket category, a severity code, three approvals, and a wait. But the underlying achievement is easy to underestimate: a formal escalation path converts personal favor into institutional capability.

It says that the second person is part of the system, not a rescue you must earn.

This is especially important in organizations that celebrate ownership. Ownership is useful language when it means staying with a problem, caring about the outcome, and refusing to let ambiguity become somebody else's hidden burden.

It becomes dangerous when ownership is interpreted as solitary possession.

A person can own the problem and still escalate it.

In fact, some of the strongest ownership is visible in the quality of the escalation. The person does not simply announce that the issue is no longer theirs. They stabilize what they can. They assemble context. They identify what is missing. They reach the correct next role. They stay available. They confirm that the receiver actually accepts responsibility. They update the people affected. They return later to learn whether the boundary should be moved next time.

The opposite of ownership is not asking for help.

The opposite is abandoning the chain.

This is why escalation cannot be understood purely as upward movement. The image of climbing a hierarchy is too narrow. Many useful escalations go sideways. A security engineer calls legal. A primary-care doctor refers to cardiology. A software team calls a vendor. A local emergency command requests state resources. A manager asks an independent reviewer. A pilot coordinates with a dispatcher. A diplomat opens a direct channel to the other government. The destination is not “higher” in a simple sense. It is different in the dimension the problem now requires.

Sometimes the correct escalation goes downward. A senior executive confronting an operational question may need the technician who knows the system, not another executive. A general officer may need the person at the edge who can see the terrain. A hospital administrator may need the bedside nurse's account. A chief executive may discover that three layers of management have polished the bad news until the only useful information remains with the person who first noticed it.

The true map of escalation is therefore not an org chart. It is a map of capability.

Who knows?

Who may decide?

Who has capacity?

Who can review independently?

Who can stop the process?

Who can see what the current decision-maker cannot?

Organizations become more resilient when these answers are known before the problem becomes urgent.

They become brittle when every exception requires an improvised search for a human being powerful enough to make it disappear.

There is a cultural cost to acknowledging this. The heroic model of competence is attractive because it is easy to tell. One person faces the problem and solves it. The story has a protagonist.

Real systems are less flattering and more interesting.

The pilot relies on dispatch, maintenance, air traffic control, weather information, other crew members, and procedures that assign overlapping responsibility. The emergency department relies on nurses, physicians, lab staff, imaging, pharmacy, consultants, rapid-response pathways, and transfer agreements. The software service relies on ownership maps, observability, incident commanders, on-call rotations, vendor contacts, and people who wrote code years earlier. The court relies on a record made below it. The factory relies on workers who can expose abnormality quickly enough for somebody else to help resolve it.

Competence is distributed because reality is larger than a job description.

There is a useful counterexample in the other direction. Escalation can become a substitute for competence.

A new employee is afraid to make any decision and asks the manager about everything. A physician refers every uncertain presentation rather than maintaining a reasonable generalist scope. An engineer pages a senior colleague before reading the runbook. A manager sends every disagreement to the vice president because taking a position feels risky. A public official creates a committee for choices already delegated to the office.

The organization can respond by shaming people for escalation, which pushes the problem back toward silence.

Or it can ask what is missing below the boundary.

Maybe the first person needs training.

Maybe the role lacks clear authority.

Maybe the policy is ambiguous.

Maybe the organization's punishment for an ordinary mistake is so severe that rational employees seek cover before acting.

Maybe the escalation itself is cheap enough that people use it as insurance.

A healthy route teaches the lower layer over time. The senior engineer answers the page and improves the runbook. The supervisor explains why the exception was granted and delegates a clearer rule. The specialist returns a useful note. The incident review identifies which decisions can safely remain local next time.

Escalation should not merely move problems. It should sometimes move capability back.

This is the difference between an organization that gets more dependent on experts and one that gets smarter through contact with them.

The first person also needs permission to remain uncertain after escalating.

This sounds odd. We often expect the person raising a concern to become the advocate for one conclusion. The nurse who calls must prove the patient is deteriorating. The engineer who blocks the launch must prove the release would fail. The employee who raises a risk must prove the feared outcome.

But the point of escalation is often that the first person cannot resolve the uncertainty alone.

A cleaner signal is sometimes: I do not know whether this is serious, and that uncertainty has crossed the threshold where another capability should look.

The receiving layer can then evaluate the condition without turning the conversation into a debate over whether the escalator deserves vindication.

That preserves a crucial distinction between signal and verdict.

The person who notices does not always need to be the person who diagnoses.

The person who stops does not always need to be the person who redesigns.

The person who appeals does not always need to prove the entire case before gaining review.

The person who pages does not always need to know the root cause.

If the system requires diagnosis before escalation, it has placed the second person's job onto the first person as an entry fee.

This is one reason the best thresholds often combine explicit criteria with a judgment route. Numbers can make escalation legitimate without status negotiation. Judgment can catch the case the numbers miss.

Neither should be treated as infallible.

There is another reason to design escalation before it is needed: people under pressure become less inventive in exactly the way institutions imagine they will become more inventive.

The emergency arrives and the organization says, “Use common sense.”

But common sense depends on knowing who is reachable, which decisions are reversible, which rules can bend, which facts matter, and how much risk the next delay creates. Those are institutional facts. The person at the edge cannot conjure them from character.

Prepared routes reduce the cognitive load of improvisation.

The 911 caller does not identify the correct fire battalion. The call taker routes.

The nurse does not need to assemble an intensive-care team socially. The response pathway routes.

The on-call engineer does not search the employee directory for whoever might know the database. The ownership system routes.

The pilot does not negotiate the existence of final operational responsibility from first principles. The role is already defined.

Preparation does not eliminate judgment. It leaves more judgment available for the part that could not be prepared.

This book is not an argument against expertise or self-reliance. Most problems should be solved at the lowest sensible level. A system in which nobody can act without permission from above is not resilient. It is paralyzed.

The argument is narrower and more demanding.

Every role has a boundary.

If the boundary is invisible, people discover it by failing past it.

If the boundary is shameful, people conceal it.

If the boundary has no route, people improvise under pressure.

If the route exists but using it is punished, the route is fictional.

If the route is used constantly for the same ordinary case, the boundary itself may be wrong.

And if the second person is merely a more senior copy of the first, the system has changed status without changing capability.

The first person remains essential. Somebody must notice. Somebody must care. Somebody must begin.

But the first person should not have to be the last person simply because the problem found them first.
