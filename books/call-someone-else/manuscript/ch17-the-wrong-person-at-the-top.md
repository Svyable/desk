# The Wrong Person at the Top

The higher a problem travels, the less likely the next person is to have touched it.

This is not an argument against hierarchy.

It is a property of scale.

A chief executive cannot know the product at the level of every engineer, the customers at the level of every support representative, the books at the level of every accountant, and the contracts at the level of every lawyer. The executive's job is broad because everyone else's can be narrow.

Yet organizations often escalate as if broad authority implies broad expertise.

The issue rises.

The room becomes more senior.

The people who know the most become quieter.

Eventually the decision lands with the person who can decide anything and directly knows the least about this particular thing.

Sometimes that is exactly right.

The decision may require a tradeoff only the senior leader is authorized to make. Should the company shut down a product line? Accept a major legal risk? Spend emergency capital? Delay a launch that affects the entire business? The executive's comparative advantage is not technical depth. It is authority across competing interests.

The problem begins when escalation fails to preserve the expertise underneath that authority.

The senior person becomes a substitute for the specialist rather than an integrator of specialists.

Mission Control gives us a better model. Flight makes the real-time tradeoff, but the console experts are expected to bring deep system knowledge into the decision. The flight director's authority does not erase their responsibility to speak.

Toyota's production system gives us another. The person at the line can surface an abnormality before senior management knows the line has a problem.

Clinical care gives us another. A specialist may know more about one condition while the bedside clinician knows more about how this patient has changed over the last six hours.

The information needed for a good decision can be located below, beside, and outside the person with final authority.

This sounds obvious enough that organizations routinely claim to believe it.

Then meetings reveal the truth.

An executive asks a technical question.

The vice president answers.

The director who knows more remains silent because the vice president is speaking.

The engineer who knows the exact fact is not in the room.

Someone offers to “circle back.”

The decision is made before the circle closes.

Hierarchy has turned an information problem into etiquette.

This is one reason skip-level communication can be useful when handled carefully. A senior leader sometimes needs uncompressed information from the edge. Site visits, listening sessions, technical reviews, customer calls, audits, frontline shadowing, and direct incident participation can all reopen information that hierarchy has compressed.

They can also create chaos.

A chief executive who routinely bypasses managers to issue instructions to individual contributors destroys local authority. Employees learn that the real path is personal access to the top. Managers become messengers. Priorities change based on whichever edge case the executive encountered most recently.

The answer is not flatness.

It is separating information access from command authority.

A leader can listen directly without managing directly.

A board can hear from an internal auditor without running the finance department.

A safety authority can inspect engineering evidence without owning the schedule.

A specialist can brief an executive without becoming the project's manager.

A frontline worker can describe the abnormality without deciding company strategy.

Good escalation preserves these distinctions.

This is the chapter where “go higher” finally breaks apart.

A problem can escalate downward.

A senior leader may need the person closest to the work.

It can escalate sideways.

Engineering may need legal. Legal may need security. Security may need a vendor. A hospital may need another specialty. A local government may need a utility.

It can escalate outward.

The company may need a regulator, insurer, auditor, or outside expert.

It can escalate inward.

A broad organizational question may need to be decomposed until one team can answer the technical fact hidden inside it.

The useful direction is the direction of missing capability.

Org charts are weak maps for this because they encode reporting relationships, not knowledge.

A capability map looks different.

Who can make the decision?

Who knows the system?

Who knows the customer?

Who can independently review the claim?

Who can interrupt the process?

Who can see the risk the current group is incentivized to minimize?

Who has capacity right now?

Who has the external relationship?

The answers form a network.

This is why experienced people often appear to “know everyone.” They have built an internal capability graph in memory. They know which lawyer actually understands the regulation, which engineer knows the old service, which finance person can explain the model, which operations leader can make the warehouse exception, which executive assistant can reach someone during travel.

That social graph is useful and dangerous.

Useful because organizations are too complex to document perfectly.

Dangerous because access becomes a property of tenure and relationships.

The person who has been at the company for ten years can route a problem in five minutes. The new employee follows the formal diagram and reaches three dead ends.

This is institutional inequality in miniature.

The same issue appears for customers and citizens. The person who knows the system's hidden language reaches the right office. The person who does not becomes trapped in the general queue.

A mature organization captures enough of the capability graph to make ordinary escalation less dependent on social privilege.

Not every expert's name needs to be public. Roles, intake points, rotations, response expectations, and rerouting responsibilities can be.

The goal is not to eliminate relationships.

It is to prevent relationships from being the only functioning infrastructure.

The person at the top also faces an information incentive problem.

Bad news becomes more polished as it rises.

This is partly self-protection. Nobody enjoys telling the chief executive that the project is failing.

It is partly professional compression. Senior leaders need concise summaries.

It is partly selection. Managers who can frame uncertainty confidently often rise faster than people who present every caveat.

The organization gradually develops an upward dialect in which risk is “manageable,” delays are “timing adjustments,” missed targets are “headwinds,” and unresolved technical questions are “workstreams in progress.”

The language is not always deceptive.

It can simply be too smooth for escalation.

A problem that feels severe at the edge can become moderate in the slide deck.

This is where senior leaders need reverse escalation channels: ways to pull raw information closer when the stakes justify it.

Ask the person who observed it.

Read the incident timeline.

Listen to the customer call.

Inspect the failed part.

Bring the dissenting engineer into the review.

Ask what would make the optimistic forecast false.

Do not do this for everything. The whole point of management is that leaders cannot inspect every raw input. Use the route where consequence, uncertainty, or conflicting incentives make compression dangerous.

The top has another weakness: availability.

The more senior the person, the more demand on their attention. Escalation to the top can create a queue with no formal queueing system. People send messages. Assistants triage. Meetings are scheduled. The leader becomes a scarce shared dependency across the organization.

This is the human equivalent of a single service called by every other service.

The architecture is fragile.

Delegation is the obvious remedy, yet delegation often fails because leaders transfer tasks without transferring decision rights.

The director prepares the recommendation.

The executive still approves.

The manager negotiates the contract.

The executive still agrees to every exception.

The team owns the roadmap.

The founder still changes priorities in direct messages.

The lower layer performs the work while the upper layer remains the hidden final step.

The organization claims decentralization and operates centralization.

Escalation patterns expose this quickly.

If routine decisions repeatedly return to the same senior person, the actual authority boundary is higher than the org chart says.

That may be deliberate.

If so, own the choice and staff around it.

If not, move the authority down with the responsibility.

This is escalation architecture working in reverse.

The better the lower layer becomes, the fewer ordinary cases need to travel upward.

The top becomes available for the decisions that are truly cross-system.

There is a status loss in this for some leaders.

Being needed feels like being important.

An executive who can solve every exception receives constant proof of relevance. A senior engineer who knows every hidden system becomes indispensable. A manager who resolves every cross-team dispute becomes the organization's diplomat.

Removing escalation debt can feel like removing identity.

This is rarely discussed because the official language is empowerment. Everyone says they want capable teams.

The emotional reality can be more complicated.

A leader may need to learn that good architecture makes them less necessary in ordinary moments.

That is not a demotion.

It is evidence that the institution can survive the leader being unavailable.

The wrong person at the top is not wrong because they are incompetent.

They are wrong when the system sends them a problem that needs a kind of difference they do not provide.

More rank is not more medicine.

More rank is not more database knowledge.

More rank is not more evidence.

More rank is not more independence when the senior leader owns the incentive causing the concern.

Authority is one capability among several.

A good escalation route knows when authority is the missing one.

And when it is not, it has the confidence to call someone else.
