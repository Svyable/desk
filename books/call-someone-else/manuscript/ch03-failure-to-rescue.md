# Failure to Rescue

Hospitals contain an odd distribution of knowledge.

The person who knows a patient best at 3:17 in the afternoon may not be the person with the most authority to change the treatment plan.

A bedside nurse may have watched the patient for hours. A family member may know what the patient looks like on a normal day. A respiratory therapist may notice a change in work of breathing. A junior physician may see a pattern developing before the attending physician has returned to the floor. A monitor may record a trend that is not yet dramatic enough to look like an emergency when reduced to one number.

The knowledge is local.

The authority and specialized capability are distributed elsewhere.

This is not a flaw unique to hospitals. It is what specialization produces. No modern hospital could function if every clinician carried every skill, permission, and resource at every bedside. The problem is what happens when the patient begins crossing from the level of care where they currently are into one that demands a different response.

Medicine has a name that captures the consequence with uncomfortable precision: failure to rescue.

The phrase is used in patient-safety literature for situations in which a patient develops a complication or deterioration and the system fails to recognize or respond effectively enough. The exact definitions used in research can vary, but the phrase is powerful because it changes the moral frame. The original problem may not have been preventable. A patient can worsen despite competent care. The institutional question becomes whether the worsening was noticed and whether help arrived.

That is an escalation question.

Rapid-response systems were developed around a simple idea: when a patient on a general ward shows signs of serious deterioration, staff should have a direct way to summon a team with additional critical-care capability before the event becomes a cardiac arrest or another catastrophe.

The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality describes the concept in similarly direct terms. A rapid-response system allows providers to summon help when a patient shows signs of imminent clinical deterioration. The team comes to the bedside to assess and treat, with the aim of preventing worse outcomes or an emergency transfer.

The simplicity is deceptive.

The technology required to make a phone ring is trivial compared with the social design required to make a nurse use it.

Who is allowed to call?

Does the nurse have to ask a physician first?

What counts as enough deterioration?

What if the vital signs are technically inside a threshold but the patient looks wrong?

What happens if the rapid-response team arrives and concludes that the concern was unfounded?

Does the caller feel supported or embarrassed?

Does the primary team experience the call as help or as a challenge to its authority?

Does the institution track repeated calls as useful signals or evidence that a unit is “overcalling”?

A pathway can be clinically sensible and culturally blocked.

AHRQ's patient-safety review of rapid-response systems points to precisely this difficulty. The evidence is not the clean success story that a management book might prefer. Rapid-response teams are widely used, and available evidence suggests they can reduce unexpected cardiac arrests modestly, but effects on overall in-hospital mortality have been inconsistent. One reason discussed in the literature is underuse caused by local practice and culture.

That caveat belongs in the center of the story, not in a footnote.

An escalation system does not become effective merely because it exists.

This is where organizations outside medicine often misunderstand safety mechanisms. They count the mechanism. We have a hotline. We have a manager-on-call. We have a risk committee. We have an appeals process. We have a “raise a concern” button. We have an open-door policy.

The count is administratively comforting.

The relevant measure is whether a person uses the route at the ambiguous moment when it matters.

Hospitals offer a particularly clear version of that ambiguity because deterioration rarely waits for certainty. A patient can move from stable to unstable through a sequence of small changes. Each change is interpretable. The heart rate is higher, but pain could explain it. The blood pressure is lower, but perhaps the patient is dehydrated. The patient seems more confused, but medications may be involved. Breathing is faster, but anxiety is possible. The individual observations can each support waiting.

The pattern may support calling.

Pattern recognition is one reason the bedside matters. The person who has been near the patient can notice deviation from baseline before a threshold captures it cleanly. That local knowledge is valuable only if the system gives it a route to additional authority and expertise.

The escalation threshold therefore has to accommodate two different kinds of signal.

One is explicit: a measurement crosses a defined boundary.

The other is judgment: something is wrong.

Organizations love the first kind because it can be audited. Numbers look fair. They reduce the burden on the junior person who otherwise has to justify why they interrupted someone senior. A heart rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, score, alert, or other criterion can make the call legitimate without requiring a social negotiation.

But no threshold captures every meaningful change. A patient is not a dashboard. The second signal survives because human beings notice combinations and context that rules may not encode.

This produces a design tension that appears in every escalation system: if the criteria are too rigid, early concerns fail to qualify. If the criteria are too loose, scarce response capacity can be flooded.

There is no universal formula that resolves this. The answer depends on the system, the stakes, the prevalence of false alarms, available capacity, and what the first-line person can reasonably evaluate.

What matters is acknowledging the tradeoff instead of pretending that a perfect trigger exists.

A hospital also reveals something important about hierarchy.

Hierarchy is often criticized as the enemy of speaking up. Sometimes it is. A steep authority gradient can make junior staff reluctant to challenge a senior clinician. But hierarchy is also how responsibility is organized. The problem is not simply that some people have more authority. The problem is what happens when the path to that authority requires permission from the very person whose judgment may need a second look.

A meaningful rapid-response pathway creates another route.

This is an architectural move. It does not abolish the primary team. It introduces a mechanism for crossing the hierarchy when the patient's condition justifies it.

The same principle appears in aviation when crew members are trained to challenge unsafe actions and the pilot in command retains final authority. It appears in engineering when an independent safety organization can review a program's technical judgment. It appears in courts when a lower court's decision can be reviewed without pretending that the lower court lacked legitimate authority to decide in the first place.

Good systems can contain authority and review at the same time.

The existence of review does not insult the first decision-maker.

It acknowledges that the consequences of error sometimes justify another route.

This is especially important because the first team can become attached to its own story. Once a diagnosis, plan, or interpretation has taken hold, new information may be seen through it. That is ordinary human cognition, not a unique defect of clinicians. The people closest to a problem gain valuable context and can also become prisoners of it.

The second team brings different expertise, but it may also bring the simple benefit of entering the story later.

Fresh eyes are not magical. They can be wrong too. They may lack local context. They can duplicate work, create conflict, or overreact. This is why escalation cannot be reduced to “more people equals safer.”

The value comes from difference plus integration.

The new team needs enough context to understand what has happened. The original team needs to remain engaged. The roles need to be clear enough that the bedside does not become a contest between competing command structures. The escalation should add capability without erasing history.

This is the handoff problem hiding inside the escalation problem.

The handoff begins before anybody leaves.

A nurse who calls a rapid-response team must communicate what changed and why the change matters. The response team must decide what to do with information that includes both measurements and judgment. The primary team may need to be contacted or involved. If the patient transfers to a higher-acuity unit, still more information must move.

Every transfer is an opportunity for detail to disappear.

Hospitals have spent years developing structured handoff practices partly because ordinary conversation is unreliable under pressure. The Joint Commission has repeatedly emphasized the risk of inadequate handoff communication. The central problem is not that clinicians do not care. It is that the sender and receiver can have different expectations about what information matters.

That phrase—different expectations—is worth carrying into every organization.

The person escalating thinks the recipient will infer what is important.

The recipient thinks the person escalating would have said it if it mattered.

Between those assumptions, context vanishes.

A technical team says, “The service is degraded.” The database team assumes the caller has already ruled out the application layer. The caller assumes the database team will check everything. A customer-service representative says, “Customer wants a refund.” The supervisor does not hear that the customer was charged after cancellation and has already provided documentation twice. A project manager says, “Legal is blocking us.” Legal receives none of the facts that make the deadline unusual.

The transfer creates a new version of the problem.

Medicine cannot tolerate unlimited distortion because the object at the center is a human body. Other organizations can often tolerate it longer, which is why the design problem remains hidden.

Another hospital lesson is the danger of punishing false positives.

Suppose a rapid-response team is called and the patient does not require intensive intervention. An institution interested only in efficiency may see wasted capacity. The caller may be told to use better judgment next time.

That feedback can improve thresholds if handled carefully. It can also train the floor to wait.

This is the same problem faced by security teams, fraud systems, aviation reporting programs, and software on-call operations. If every alarm that does not reveal disaster is treated as a mistake, people learn to escalate only once the disaster is visible.

At that point, the system is no longer doing early detection. It is documenting failure.

There is a better question than “Was the alarm correct?”

Was the decision to escalate reasonable given what the caller knew at the time?

That standard preserves uncertainty. It evaluates judgment rather than outcome.

It also protects organizations from a common hindsight error. After the fact, everyone knows which subtle signs mattered. Before the fact, the same signs competed with benign explanations. A patient who recovers makes the concern look smaller. A patient who deteriorates makes the same concern look prophetic.

The caller did not have either ending available.

This is why good incident reviews reconstruct the information environment at the time rather than judging every choice from the final outcome. Hospitals, aviation investigators, software postmortems, and accident boards all benefit from this discipline.

Escalation decisions are choices under uncertainty. They should be reviewed as such.

The hospital also forces us to confront capacity.

A rapid-response team is not an abstract concept. It is people with other responsibilities. A specialist referral consumes an appointment. An intensive care bed is scarce. A physician interrupted repeatedly has less attention for other work. The more aggressively a system escalates, the more it must invest in the receiving layer.

This is often omitted from leadership advice. “Encourage people to speak up” is cheap to say. The advice becomes operational only when somebody is staffed to listen.

The same is true in companies that tell frontline employees to escalate difficult customers but maintain one supervisor for an impossible queue. It is true in technical organizations that add more alerts without adding on-call capacity. It is true in public agencies that create appeals rights without enough people to process appeals.

A route without receiving capacity becomes a waiting room.

Waiting can be appropriate. Scarce resources must be allocated. The dangerous fiction is calling the route available when its real response time makes it irrelevant to the underlying risk.

Hospitals therefore help separate three pieces that are frequently collapsed.

There is detection: somebody notices a possible problem.

There is escalation: the signal reaches a different level of capability or authority.

There is response: the receiving system actually acts.

An organization can succeed at the first and fail at the second. It can succeed at the first two and fail at the third.

A dashboard can detect. A nurse can call. A pager can fire. A ticket can be opened. None of those facts guarantees rescue.

This is why the phrase “failure to rescue” is larger than medicine.

A company can notice fraud and fail to act. An engineering team can identify a safety risk and fail to get independent review. A software service can page the correct team and still sit degraded because nobody has authority to make the necessary tradeoff. A government can create a complaint channel that records harm without changing the decision.

The presence of a signal is not the same thing as the presence of a system.

A functioning escalation path closes the distance between noticing and action.

It also makes the first person less alone.

That matters more than policy writers usually admit. The bedside nurse deciding whether to call is not making an abstract systems choice. The nurse is making a social bet: this concern is important enough to interrupt other people and expose my judgment to theirs.

If the institution has done its work, the bet is smaller.

The route is known. The criteria are intelligible. Judgment is permitted. The receiving team expects the call. A reasonable false alarm is not treated as incompetence. The caller remains part of the response rather than becoming a nuisance who has handed the problem away.

That is what architecture can do for culture. It can make the difficult sentence less personal.

I need someone else.

In a hospital, spoken early enough, it can be a form of care.
