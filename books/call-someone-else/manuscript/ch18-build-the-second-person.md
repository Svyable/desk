# Build the Second Person

The second person should exist before the first person needs them.

That is the whole book reduced to one design requirement.

A call taker should know where the emergency can go.

A nurse should know how to summon another clinical layer.

A pilot should have language for a clearance that cannot be accepted.

An engineer with the pager should know the next team.

An incident commander should know that command can transfer.

A litigant should have a route for review.

A worker should be able to surface an abnormality before the line multiplies it.

A mission-control specialist should be able to put technical judgment into a room where the whole mission is being integrated.

Governments in crisis should not have to invent the communication channel while interpreting the threat.

A generalist should be able to reach a specialist and receive the answer back.

A customer should not need exceptional persistence to obtain the level of authority their case actually requires.

An employee should not need exceptional bravery to tell the institution something the institution says it wants to know.

A machine that should not decide a case alone should have somewhere meaningful to send it.

None of this requires worship of escalation.

Most work should remain where it starts.

A system that escalates ordinary judgment constantly is not mature. It is anxious, undertrained, overcentralized, or badly designed. The first line matters. The person closest to the work should have enough knowledge, authority, and tools to solve normal problems without seeking permission for every move.

The architecture begins where normal stops.

That boundary will never be perfectly clean.

Rules help.

Measurements help.

Training helps.

Experience helps.

None removes uncertainty.

The person at the boundary often has to act before knowing whether the escalation will prove necessary.

That is why culture cannot be separated from design.

A beautiful escalation chart can be killed by one senior person who makes callers regret using it.

A mediocre chart can work surprisingly well when the receiving people respond with seriousness, redirect mistakes without humiliation, and treat the route as shared infrastructure.

The strongest systems make the desired behavior easier than the socially risky alternative.

They do not tell the junior person to “use judgment” while keeping every threshold hidden in senior people's heads.

They do not call a hotline confidential and then allow the whole office to infer who used it.

They do not give a stop button and measure supervisors only on uninterrupted throughput.

They do not add a human reviewer after an AI system and call the problem solved when the reviewer has no time, evidence, or authority to disagree.

They align the route with the behavior they claim to want.

The first question is ordinary: what is the person allowed to solve without escalation?

This is where many designs should start, because a route cannot be understood until the first boundary is understood.

If the frontline worker has no meaningful authority, everything becomes escalation.

If the authority is unlimited, nothing does.

Neither works.

A customer-service representative might resolve standard refunds and escalate exceptions beyond a defined amount or category.

An on-call engineer might mitigate common failures and escalate cross-service or prolonged incidents.

A nurse might manage expected changes and activate additional response when defined criteria or clinical concern appear.

The exact boundaries are domain-specific. The principle is to make ordinary autonomy compatible with abnormal escalation.

Then the organization needs a destination.

“Tell someone” is not a destination.

“Raise it with management” is not a destination.

“Contact the appropriate team” is not a destination.

A real destination is a role, service, queue, rotation, committee, office, specialist, external institution, or other receiving mechanism that is expected to accept a known kind of signal.

The destination should exist as a function rather than only as a particular helpful person.

People matter enormously. Roles survive vacation, resignation, illness, promotion, and time zones better than names alone.

This does not mean eliminating named relationships. The fastest route in a real emergency may still be to call the person you know.

The institution should not depend entirely on that luck.

Then comes the reason for the transfer.

The second person should differ.

More knowledge.

More authority.

More capacity.

More independence.

A broader integrating view.

A different jurisdiction.

A different clock.

If nothing meaningful changes, the escalation is likely theater.

This test is especially useful for supervisory layers. A manager receiving a case should not be merely a more expensive reader of the same script. The manager might possess exception authority, cross-team visibility, personnel responsibility, or the ability to change priority. If none applies, the case probably belongs somewhere else.

The same test protects experts. Do not wake the database specialist because the specialist is senior. Wake the specialist because the problem now requires database expertise.

The escalation also needs a payload.

The reader has seen enough handoffs by now to know the problem.

The payload should say what happened, what is known, what has been tried, what remains uncertain, why the next person is needed, and what action is requested—adjusted to the domain and urgency.

The emergency call cannot become a five-page intake form.

The appellate brief cannot become a one-line text message.

Tempo determines compression.

The payload should preserve the information the receiver cannot cheaply rediscover.

Then somebody needs to know the signal arrived.

Acknowledgment is one of the most underrated pieces of escalation infrastructure.

Without it, the sender lives in limbo.

Should I wait?

Should I try again?

Should I use another channel?

Did anyone hear me?

Unacknowledged escalation produces duplicate pages, copied executives, repeated complaints, and eventually shadow channels.

A simple “received” can change behavior because it converts uncertainty into state.

Received is not resolved.

That distinction should remain visible.

The next question is ownership.

Did responsibility transfer?

Did only decision authority transfer?

Is the second person advising while the first person remains owner?

Is the original person expected to remain present until the handoff completes?

Who communicates with the affected customer, patient, employee, operator, or public?

Who closes the loop?

If those verbs are unclear, adding more people can reduce responsibility rather than increase it.

Escalation should produce a clearer owner at every stage, not a larger audience.

Then comes time.

How long can the problem wait?

An emergency response, a security incident, a routine appeal, a specialist consultation, a procurement exception, and a policy review belong to different clocks.

The receiving layer needs capacity appropriate to the clock.

This sounds like staffing, and often it is.

Organizations love escalation rights that cost nothing. They create committees without time, hotlines without investigators, review queues without reviewers, on-call rotations without enough people, human oversight without enough humans.

The route exists in policy and collapses in latency.

A serious system budgets attention for the second person.

Scarcity also requires protection against noise.

The threshold should not become a moral test, but it should exist.

Repeated false alarms can exhaust responders and teach people to ignore the route. The answer is not necessarily a higher threshold. It may be better training, better detection, another intermediate layer, clearer categories, or fixing the underlying condition that keeps generating alarms.

The route itself needs review.

Was this escalation reasonable given what the sender knew?

Did it reach the right destination?

Did the receiver get enough context?

Did the recipient possess the difference the case needed?

Was the response timely?

Did ownership become clear?

Did the result return?

Does recurrence suggest the boundary should move?

These questions are more useful than praising low escalation counts.

The goal is not fewer escalations in the abstract.

The goal is better placement of problems.

That leads to escalation debt.

A healthy system should be changed by some of the signals traveling through it.

If every second-level decision looks like one the first level could safely make with better authority or training, move capability downward.

If every specialist referral arrives missing the same information, improve the first handoff.

If every incident pages the same person for the same failure, fix the failure or distribute the knowledge.

If every customer escalates the same policy, review the policy.

If every employee concern dies at the same manager, create another path.

Escalation is not only a safety valve.

It is a sensor for organizational design.

Then there is the hardest piece: the social price.

No diagram can eliminate status.

The junior person knows the senior person is busy.

The frontline worker knows the supervisor controls schedules.

The engineer knows the executive wants the launch.

The nurse knows the physician may disagree.

The customer knows the representative can end the call.

The employee knows the complaint involves someone who influences promotion.

The system must counter those realities through repeated behavior.

Thank reasonable escalations.

Redirect mistaken ones without ridicule.

Do not make “false alarm” synonymous with “bad judgment.”

Separate the abnormality from the person who found it.

Make retaliation visible as damage to the reporting system, not merely misconduct against one employee.

Let senior people be interrupted under the conditions where the organization has said interruption is appropriate.

The first few uses teach more than the policy.

Leaders should pay particular attention to the first escalation after a new route is created.

People are watching.

If the first caller is punished, the system can remain quiet for years.

If the first caller is treated seriously even when the concern is ultimately benign, the institution has demonstrated that the route is real.

This is how architecture becomes culture.

The design should also contemplate failure of the escalation system itself.

What if the first recipient does not respond?

What if the recipient is the subject of the concern?

What if the specialist is unavailable?

What if the on-call phone is dead?

What if the government office lacks jurisdiction?

What if the model defers to a human queue that is full?

What if the second decision is wrong?

A route needs a route.

This recursive property is not absurd. It is how resilient systems avoid single points of human failure.

The call center has a supervisor and an external complaint route.

The trial court has an appellate court and limited further review.

The incident commander can transfer command.

The on-call engineer can page another team.

The specialist can refer again.

The pilot can declare an emergency when ordinary coordination no longer fits.

The second person is also a first person at the next boundary.

No escalation tree reaches omniscience.

That fact should make the system calmer, not less ambitious.

The purpose is not to find the person who can never be wrong.

It is to prevent one person's boundary from becoming the system's boundary by accident.

This is also why the phrase “single throat to choke” has always contained a design mistake. Accountability matters. A named owner matters. But complex systems are not made safe by pretending one person can contain every dependency. The stronger goal is a named owner with explicit routes to the capabilities the owner does not possess.

Ownership should keep the problem from disappearing.

Escalation should keep ownership from becoming isolation.

There will always be people who call too early.

There will always be people who call too late.

There will always be managers who want fewer interruptions, experts who want cleaner questions, operators who resent paperwork, customers who demand exceptions, and systems that do not fit the categories built for them.

No design removes human judgment.

The best designs make judgment less lonely.

Somewhere in a functioning institution, there is a second person.

They may be holding a pager.

They may be sitting behind another console.

They may be working in another department, another hospital, another court, another country, another time zone.

They may be a role no one notices on an ordinary day.

That is fine.

Infrastructure is often quiet when it works.

The important thing is that when the first person reaches the edge of what they know, what they may do, what they can carry, or what they can independently judge, the edge is not a cliff.

There is a route.
