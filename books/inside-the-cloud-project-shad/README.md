## Thesis

Project SHAD was a real Cold War effort to learn whether American warships could survive chemical and biological attack. Its most consequential experiment, however, may have been institutional: thousands of servicemen could participate in classified vulnerability tests, some involving live chemical or biological agents, while the knowledge required to understand their own exposure remained compartmented for decades. The later health record does not establish a broad SHAD-caused disease epidemic. That negative result matters. It makes the harder story about consent, secrecy, record architecture, retrospective epidemiology, and the asymmetry between what an institution can know operationally and what a participant can know personally.

## Reader promise

This book follows the tests from Deseret Test Center planning through shipboard releases, detection and decontamination problems, secrecy, veteran discovery, declassification, notification, and two generations of epidemiological study. It refuses both easy endings: neither “nothing happened because later studies found no overall excess” nor “the tests prove a hidden mass poisoning.” The documentary record is stranger and more useful than either.

## Evidence discipline

1. **Verified/documented:** Project 112/SHAD existed; DoD conducted land- and sea-based chemical/biological vulnerability tests; active agents, simulants, tracers, and decontaminants were used; roughly 5,900–6,000 servicemembers are reported as participants; declassification and VA notification accelerated around 2000–2002.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** test-specific exposure intensity, individual dose, protective status, and the completeness of reconstructed participant rosters and records.
3. **Disputed/not established:** a broad long-term disease syndrome caused by SHAD, deliberate intent to injure servicemembers, or claims that every participant received a hazardous dose.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** secrecy as an exposure multiplier after the physical test—because it can delay recognition, clinical context, cohort assembly, and the participant’s ability to reconstruct what happened.
