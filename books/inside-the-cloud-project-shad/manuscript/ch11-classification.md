# Classification

Classification solved a Cold War problem. It also created a future one.

A vulnerability report can reveal exactly how to defeat a ship. Release methods, detector weaknesses, protective failures and operational procedures are obvious candidates for secrecy. The fact that SHAD remained classified therefore cannot, by itself, prove a cover-up of injury.

But classification is not passive storage. It determines who can connect facts. A medical system cannot act on an exposure it does not know occurred. A veteran cannot supply history he was never given. An epidemiologist cannot build a cohort until names and tests can be joined.

The crucial chronology is not simply “secret, then declassified.” DoD began a difficult reconstruction around 2000 after VA requests, with fact sheets released in 2002 and 2003 and veteran notification following. Records were dispersed; Deseret Test Center had long since closed. Some details remained classified even after broad program disclosure.

The ethical question is therefore temporal. At what point did the operational value of secrecy become smaller than the medical and personal value of disclosure? That question can be investigated without pretending the answer was obvious in 1963—or that forty years of delay was consequence-free.
