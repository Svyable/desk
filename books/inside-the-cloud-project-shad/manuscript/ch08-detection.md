# Detection

A detector changes the meaning of an invisible threat. Before the alarm, contamination is an inference. After it, somebody has to decide whether the instrument is right, whether the crew has time, and what action costs less than waiting.

SHAD’s shipboard logic was inseparable from this timing problem. A defensive system that identifies an aerosol after it has penetrated occupied compartments may be scientifically sensitive and operationally useless. A detector that alarms constantly creates another failure: the crew learns to discount it.

The full chapter will reconstruct the detection chain in specific tests rather than treating “defense” as one machine. Sampling equipment, alarms, protective posture and command decisions belong in the same sequence. The point is not gadget history. It is that the program’s ethical and technical questions meet at the clock. How long between release, detection, warning and protection? Who knew which clock had started?

That final question survives declassification. Participants could be essential to realism precisely because they did not possess the planner’s full picture. Whether that was necessary, routine, avoidable or ethically unacceptable has to be answered from the records, not from the drama of secrecy itself.
