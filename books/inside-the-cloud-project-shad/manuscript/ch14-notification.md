# Notification

In May 2002 VA announced that it had begun contacting veterans identified as Project SHAD participants. The initial notification involved 622 men out of roughly 4,300 then identified. More names would follow as reconstruction continued.

A notification letter is a peculiar form of delayed consent. It cannot change what happened. It can tell a person that an event he participated in had a name, that materials were involved, and that health care or benefits questions may now be asked with different information.

Notification also changes behavior. The later National Academies analysis observed increased VA health-care use among SHAD veterans beginning around 2002, when notification began. That does not prove new disease appeared. It demonstrates something almost as important for interpretation: disclosure itself can alter ascertainment.

Once people learn they belong to an exposed cohort, they may seek care, report symptoms differently, file claims, compare experiences and become visible to researchers. Any post-disclosure health dataset has to reckon with that.

The letter therefore sits at the junction of ethics and statistics. Telling people what happened is morally relevant. It also changes the system that will later try to measure what happened to them.
