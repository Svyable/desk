# SHAD I

The first large attempts to measure long-term health did not produce a clean ending.

Studies reported in the 2007–2009 period yielded mixed signals. The earlier Institute of Medicine work did not find a difference in all-cause mortality between SHAD veterans and its comparison group, while survey responses suggested poorer overall physical and mental health among participants. A VA analysis reported increased all-cause mortality, driven primarily by heart disease, with the excess appearing among veterans associated with simulant exposures rather than active-agent groups.

That pattern is exactly the sort of result that should slow a writer down. It is neither “proof of poisoning” nor “nothing to see.” Multiple outcomes, subgroup analyses, exposure uncertainty and different data sources create opportunities for both real signals and statistical noise.

The proper response was more follow-up, not a slogan. Congress eventually requested another Institute of Medicine study with additional years of observation and access to Medicare and VA diagnostic data.

SHAD I matters because it shows how a classified exposure history becomes ordinary epidemiology: denominators, comparison groups, confidence intervals, missing dose and arguments over what a signal means. Secrecy makes the beginning unusual. It does not exempt the ending from statistics.
