# Sailors Inside the Experiment

Approximately six thousand servicemembers are now associated with Project 112/SHAD. “Participant” is the official word and a useful one, but it contains several different relationships to the experiment. Some men operated test equipment. Some crewed challenged ships. Some may have had specialized knowledge; others may have known little beyond the immediate task.

The book cannot flatten those positions into a single consent story. Military service already changes the ordinary civilian relationship between choice and risk. Orders are real. So are duties. That makes informed participation more complicated, not less important.

The strongest evidence will come from matching individual accounts to test documentation. Memory decades later is evidence of experience, not a substitute for an exposure log. Conversely, a technical report can describe a release perfectly and tell us almost nothing about what a sailor was told.

Those two archives—operational paper and remembered life—need each other. Where they agree, the story sharpens. Where they conflict, the conflict itself deserves preservation. A book about classified testing should be suspicious of any method that makes inconvenient uncertainty disappear.
