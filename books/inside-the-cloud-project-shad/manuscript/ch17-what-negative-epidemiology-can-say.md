# What Negative Epidemiology Can Say

People often ask a negative study to do impossible work. One side wants it to prove nobody was harmed. The other treats any limitation as permission to ignore the result.

SHAD offers a cleaner lesson. If a reasonably constructed participant cohort does not show broad excess mortality or morbidity against an appropriate comparison group, hypotheses predicting a large common effect lose credibility. That is real knowledge.

But the resolution of the instrument matters. Rare outcomes, heterogeneous exposures, uncertain individual doses and small test-specific groups may remain difficult to evaluate. Multiple comparisons can also manufacture apparently interesting subgroup results by chance. The more outcomes and subgroups inspected, the more disciplined the analysis has to become.

The book’s evidence matrix therefore separates population findings from individual claims. A veteran’s illness is real without automatically being attributable to SHAD. A cohort-level null result is informative without proving every exposure harmless.

This may be the least cinematic part of the story, which is why it is essential. Classified programs attract certainty after the fact. Epidemiology, when it is doing its job, often gives us something better: a narrower range of defensible uncertainty.
