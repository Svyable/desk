# Knowledge After Exposure

The physical experiment ends when the release is over, the samples are collected and the ship is cleaned. The informational experiment can last for decades.

SHAD’s public afterlife required veterans to surface concerns, agencies to locate old records, DoD to declassify material, VA to identify and notify participants, researchers to construct comparison groups, and the National Academies to ask what the available health data could support. None of that changes the Cold War test. It changes what society can know about it.

The strongest claim this book can make is therefore narrower than the most sensational one. Project SHAD does not currently provide clear evidence of a broad long-term disease burden among participants. It does provide unusually good evidence of what happens when operational secrecy and personal exposure history are allowed to live on different clocks.

A state may have legitimate secrets. Bodies are bad filing cabinets. They do not preserve classification markings, test numbers or the distinction between a material released for national defense and one encountered by accident. When a physician eventually asks what a patient was exposed to, the answer has to come from somewhere else.

The final research pass should end where the program began: aboard a ship, with air crossing a boundary somebody hoped would hold. SHAD discovered that boundaries leak. The archive did too.
