# A Ship Becomes a Laboratory

A warship is already an experiment in controlled atmosphere. Steel compartments, ventilation trunks, hatches, filters, washdown systems, crowded berthing, machinery spaces: a floating city designed around the assumption that the outside world must sometimes be kept outside. Chemical and biological warfare made that assumption measurable. Could an aerosol find the ship anyway? Where would it enter? How quickly would detectors notice? Could a crew keep fighting while contaminated?

Project Shipboard Hazard and Defense—SHAD—grew inside that Cold War question. The later public record describes tests carried out mainly in the 1960s as part of Project 112, a broader Department of Defense program. The objective was defensive in the military sense: learn the vulnerability of ships and forces to chemical and biological attack. That purpose should not be blurred into a claim that the Navy set out to poison its own people. It also does not settle what the people aboard were entitled to know.

That distinction is the book’s starting pressure. A commander can require secrecy about a detector, a dispersal system, a defensive weakness. A physician looking at a former sailor thirty years later has a different problem. So does the sailor. The operational secret has become medical history, but only if somebody allows the categories to touch.

The National Academies eventually worked with a SHAD cohort of 5,868 participants and a comparison population of 6,753 veterans. Those numbers make the program large enough to study and small enough to disappear inside the armed forces that produced it. They also tempt us into retrospective neatness. The cohort did not exist in 1965 as a future epidemiological dataset. It had to be reconstructed from a classified program whose records were dispersed and whose test center had closed.

A ship became a laboratory first. Decades later, the archive had to become one.
