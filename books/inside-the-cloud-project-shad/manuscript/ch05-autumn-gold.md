# Autumn Gold

Autumn Gold ran near Oahu in May 1963. In the later Defense Department accounting it sits among the shipboard tests involving BG, the bacterial simulant used repeatedly in the era. On paper this looks almost tidy: date, place, material, investigation status. The neatness is a product of hindsight.

A ship at sea does not experience a table row. Aerosol behavior depends on wind, release geometry, ventilation, ship motion and the stubborn complexity of openings that are closed in doctrine and open in practice. The military reason for doing the experiment rather than solving it on paper was that the vessel was too complicated to trust to assumption.

That is also why the later health question cannot be solved from a list of agents. Exposure is geometry plus time. Two sailors assigned to the same ship can occupy different microenvironments. A man on deck, a machinist below, a sampler working the test and somebody sleeping after watch are not interchangeable units merely because a roster places them under one code name.

The temptation, decades later, is to make the surviving metadata carry more certainty than it can. Autumn Gold establishes that a test occurred and what the released material was reported to be. It does not by itself produce an individual dose history. The gap is not evidence that records were falsified. It is the ordinary, consequential difference between an operational record and a medical one.

The project was built to answer whether ships were vulnerable. Veterans later asked whether they had been vulnerable. Those questions overlap, but the archive was built around the first.
