# Eager Bell

The public fact-sheet chronology begins to make SHAD legible by names that sound almost deliberately unserious: Eager Bell, Autumn Gold, Flower Drum, Shady Grove. Code names do useful work. They compress a test into a label that can travel through schedules and security systems without carrying its whole meaning with it.

Eager Bell and its related 1963 work in the Pacific used Bacillus globigii, then employed as a biological simulant. The test belongs to the part of SHAD that can be discussed without pretending every release was a live warfare agent. That is precisely why it is useful. A simulant test exposes the logic of the program cleanly: generate an aerosol, challenge a ship, sample where the material goes, and learn what the vessel’s defensive boundaries actually mean.

But “simulant” is not the same word as “nothing.” It is a technical claim about what a material stands in for and what risks were accepted in using it. The safety assumptions around historical simulants deserve their own audit rather than retroactive reassurance or panic.

Eager Bell also introduces the archive problem. A fact sheet produced decades later is not the test. It is a reconstruction intended to answer questions the original classification system did not anticipate. Dates, participating units, substances and broad purposes can be recovered. Individual dose is much harder. The difference between those two levels of knowledge will haunt every health study that follows.

A code name can be declassified in a day. Reconstructing what happened to a particular body under it can take the rest of a career.
