# The Core

In 1966, drilling at Camp Century reached through roughly 1.4 kilometers of ice and into material below it.

The core became one of the foundational archives of Greenland climate history. This is where the military base acquires a second life so unlikely that fiction would overplay it: an installation built partly to test the military use of apparently stable ice helped recover evidence that the ice sheet itself had not been stable across deep time.

Ice cores turn atmosphere into stratigraphy. Snow traps chemistry, dust and isotopes. Layer by layer, a vertical cylinder becomes a chronology of climate. Camp Century's core contributed to the emerging realization that abrupt climate shifts were recorded in Greenland ice.

But the most disruptive material was not the ice.

It was dirt at the bottom.

Subglacial sediment is difficult to obtain because the Greenland Ice Sheet is not a convenient laboratory floor. Camp Century's drilling had accidentally created a sample of landscape history from beneath one of the planet's major ice masses.

The sample would later be misplaced, rediscovered and reanalyzed with techniques unavailable to the original teams.

There is a lesson in that sequence that institutions usually learn too late: the scientific value of a sample can increase after the instrument that collected it becomes obsolete.

A missile concept died. A core persisted.

The military project had been built around the question, Can we use the ice?

The sediment asked a more dangerous one: How permanent is the ice itself?
