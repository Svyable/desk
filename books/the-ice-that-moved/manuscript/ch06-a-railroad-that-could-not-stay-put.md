# A Railroad That Could Not Stay Put

Railways assume coordinates.

The rails may expand, bridges may settle, frost may heave the bed, but the enterprise begins with confidence that two points can be connected by geometry durable enough to schedule a train.

Inside an ice sheet that confidence becomes conditional.

Camp Century included experiments with subsurface transport because any large buried system would require movement of people, supplies and, in the Iceworm concept, missiles. A railway under snow had obvious attraction. The surface could remain visually empty while logistics moved beneath it.

But tunnels in ice narrow. They warp. Their roofs and walls respond to stress. The exact rate depends on geometry, temperature, density and local conditions, which means infrastructure is always racing its own medium.

This is where Camp Century stops being merely a Cold War story and becomes a book about model error. Engineers did not fail to know that ice moves. They measured deformation. The deeper mistake was one of operational tolerance: whether known movement could be maintained within acceptable bounds for the intended system.

That distinction matters because it is how most serious engineering errors happen. Reality is rarely unknown in the cinematic sense. More often a known effect is assigned the wrong magnitude, timescale or consequence.

For a camp, deformation was work. For Iceworm, deformation was strategy.

A launch network depends on more than empty volume. Rails must remain aligned. Doors must open. utilities must survive strain. Shafts must remain serviceable. Positioning must remain known. The architecture must be inspected continuously across a huge footprint while still deriving military advantage from concealment.

The supposed hiding medium had become a maintenance adversary.

No Soviet missile was needed to expose the weakness. The base's own walls were conducting the test.
