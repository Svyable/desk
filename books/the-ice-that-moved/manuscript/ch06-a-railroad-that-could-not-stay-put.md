# A Railroad That Could Not Stay Put

In August 1962, the Army put a short experimental railroad under the Greenland ice sheet. The test was successful enough that later accounts of Project Iceworm could plausibly begin with rails rather than fantasy: if a train could run beneath the snow, perhaps missiles could move there too.

The trouble was not that the Army had forgotten ice moves. Camp Century existed partly to find out how much it moved and what an engineer could do about it.

Clark's 1965 technical history is striking on this point because it is not a postmortem written to prove the idea foolish. The report concluded that subsurface ice-cap camps were feasible and practicable. It also recorded the maintenance bill imposed by the medium itself. Camp Century's trenches deformed vertically and horizontally under the pressure of the surrounding firn and ice; warmer and wider spaces performed worse. Keeping the camp near its intended dimensions meant cutting material back from walls and roofs. Clark estimated that maintaining even Century's tunnel system would require removing roughly 15,000 cubic metres of snow and ice each year.

That number changes the railroad story. A tunnel is not merely something you excavate. In moving ice it is a geometry you keep purchasing.

For a research camp, the purchase could make sense. Crews were already present. A wall could be shaved, a roof supported, a troublesome section watched more closely. Camp Century was an experiment, and maintenance was partly the experiment's product. The fact that engineers had to fight deformation did not by itself prove that construction in ice was a failure.

That is the strongest case for Iceworm, too. Civil engineering routinely accepts maintenance. Railways need tamping, tunnels need lining, ships need dry docks. A strategic system does not become impossible merely because the environment sends it an annual invoice.

Iceworm changed the arithmetic by changing the denominator. The proposed missile field was not one camp with a short test railway. Historical reconstruction places the concept at a subsurface network spread across roughly 130,000 square kilometres of the Greenland ice sheet, supporting about 600 ballistic missiles. Its military value depended partly on mobility and uncertainty: an adversary should not know which concealed position held which missile at a given time.

Now maintenance is no longer housekeeping. It enters the weapon system.

Rails must remain aligned closely enough to carry vehicles. Junctions and doors have to work. Utilities have to survive strain. Access routes have to remain open. Surveyors have to know where a route has gone after the medium around it has moved. Every inspection crew, trimming machine, repair cache and regularly reopened section is also activity imposed on an architecture whose attraction was concealment.

The important distinction is therefore not moving ice versus stable ground. Engineers already knew the ice moved. It is local maintainability versus network-scale maintainability. A deformation rate that is tolerable across a few kilometres of occupied trenches can become strategically expensive when multiplied across a dispersed missile field, especially when the field must stay ready rather than merely habitable.

This also keeps the historical judgment honest. Camp Century did not run a full Iceworm network to failure. The short railroad test cannot tell us the exact maintenance burden of a missile system that was never built, and later cancellation had strategic and political causes as well as engineering ones. It would be too neat to say that one sagging tunnel killed the idea.

What Century supplied was more useful than that. It converted a hidden assumption into a measured recurring cost. The ice did not need to make a railroad impossible. It only had to make every kilometre temporary.
