# The Danish Problem

Greenland was not an empty white board owned by the planners drawing on it.

The United States operated there through agreement with Denmark, while Greenlanders lived with decisions made inside a larger colonial and alliance structure. Camp Century sits inside that political geometry as awkwardly as it sits inside the ice.

Historical scholarship by Nikolaj Petersen and others shows that Danish and American interests overlapped without becoming identical. Denmark wanted the alliance and American defense commitment. It also had sovereignty to protect and domestic political constraints, including sensitivity around nuclear weapons.

This is where secrecy stops being simply a curtain around technology and becomes a mechanism for distributing consent.

The general existence of Camp Century was known. Danish liaisons participated in aspects of research and environmental work. The harder question is how completely Danish authorities understood the military possibilities being studied, especially Iceworm's nuclear-missile implications.

The evidence does not justify cartoon claims that Denmark knew nothing or knew everything. Government is not one mind. Information can be available to a small group while remaining politically unusable elsewhere. A partner can consent to a category of research without consenting to every future architecture inferred from it.

Camp Century therefore belongs to the history of compartments between allies as much as within intelligence agencies.

That matters again decades later when responsibility for abandoned waste becomes a question. Who owns yesterday's secrecy when the physical leftovers survive into a different political era? The United States built the infrastructure. Denmark was the sovereign power. Greenland's contemporary government and population inherit the environmental consequences without having authored the Cold War bargain.

A secret can expire while its liability remains classified by nobody.
