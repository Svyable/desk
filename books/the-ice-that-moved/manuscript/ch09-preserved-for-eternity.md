# Preserved for Eternity

Few phrases age as badly as 'for eternity' in an engineering report.

Camp Century was abandoned with much of its physical infrastructure and waste left within the ice. The assumption, reconstructed in later scientific work from contemporary design thinking, was that continued snow accumulation would carry the remains deeper and keep them sequestered.

At first glance this seems reasonable. In a cold accumulation zone, snow falls, compacts and becomes ice. Buried material descends relative to the surface. A structure that is impractical to remove can appear to have acquired a natural tomb.

But sequestration is not a property of burial alone. It is a forecast about the future state of the burial medium.

In 2016, William Colgan and colleagues revisited Camp Century under modern climate projections. They inventoried abandoned physical, chemical, biological and radiological wastes and asked what happens if surface mass balance changes enough that the site eventually enters net ablation. Their answer was not that contamination is presently pouring out of the ice. It was that the old assumption of indefinite burial can fail under a warming climate, opening pathways for future remobilization.

The distinction between present hazard and future pathway matters. Alarm can distort this story as easily as secrecy can.

What Camp Century gives us is a precise example of intergenerational model debt. Engineers made a disposal decision using an environmental regime they expected to persist. Later generations inherited both the material and a different probability distribution.

The waste did not become political because somebody discovered it had been secret.

It became political because time changed the boundary conditions.
