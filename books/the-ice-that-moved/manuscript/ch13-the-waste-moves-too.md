# The Waste Moves Too

The abandoned base did not vanish. It changed coordinates.

Ice accumulated above it. Firn compacted. The tunnel network collapsed. Solid material moved downward relative to the surface while the whole ice sheet continued its slow flow toward the coast.

Colgan and colleagues estimated thousands of tonnes of physical material remained, along with diesel fuel, wastewater, PCBs and radiological waste associated with reactor operations. The exact inventory carries uncertainty because historical records are incomplete and some quantities must be reconstructed.

What is established is enough to make the problem real without making it apocalyptic.

The old base is a distributed waste site inside a moving cryosphere.

The 2016 paper's most important move was to reject a static picture. A buried contaminant is not merely at depth X. Its future depends on accumulation, ablation, meltwater pathways, firn permeability, ice flow and chemistry. A warming climate can change those relationships long before a piece of steel appears at the surface.

This is where military history collides with hydrogeology.

The political question follows immediately. Which generation owns a waste-management decision whose physical assumptions expire after the officials are dead? Which government pays when the sovereign arrangement has changed? What duty is created by a hazard that is plausible but not imminent?

Camp Century does not answer those questions.

It demonstrates that the ice has kept them open.
