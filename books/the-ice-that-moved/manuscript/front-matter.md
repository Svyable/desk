# The Ice That Moved

Camp Century is usually introduced as a Cold War oddity: tunnels under Greenland, a portable nuclear reactor, and a secret plan to hide hundreds of ballistic missiles in the ice. That version is true enough to be dangerous because it makes the story feel finished.

The stranger story begins after the military project fails.

The Army went to Greenland because ice looked like terrain that could be engineered. It cut trenches, roofed them, installed power and rail, and learned in a few years that the medium itself flowed. Then, while trying to understand the ice well enough to occupy it, Camp Century's scientists drilled almost a mile downward and recovered a core that outlived the military theory which paid for it. Decades later, forgotten sediment from the bottom of that core would help show that northwestern Greenland had once been ice-free and vegetated.

The base was built inside one mistaken timescale and accidentally recovered evidence of another.

This book keeps four kinds of claims separate. Declassified and official records establish Camp Century, the PM-2A reactor, the engineering work, and the Army's Iceworm concept. Historical scholarship fills in diplomacy and intent where the operational record is incomplete. Later scientific work establishes what the ice core and abandoned wastes can tell us. Speculation begins only after those things have been named for what they are.

No secret missile city became operational under Greenland. No present hazard is proved merely because waste remains buried. No old military assumption becomes foolish simply because later science improved. The interesting part is harder: institutions build against models of the world, and the world keeps changing after the appropriations end.
