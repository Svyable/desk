# What the Radars Found

Buried infrastructure can become geology to the eye and remain architecture to radar.

Airborne ice-penetrating radar surveys have recorded anomalous reflections near the inferred position and depth of Camp Century's remains. The signatures are useful because they provide an independent way to test historical models of burial and movement without excavating the site.

But radar is not a magical inventory machine. A bright return does not identify every buried object or chemical. Resolution, geometry, dielectric contrast and interpretation matter. The correct use of the data is narrower: map structure, constrain depth, compare expected and observed positions, and design later investigation.

Camp Century therefore becomes an unusually good case study in chain-of-evidence thinking. Documents say what was built. Engineering models estimate how it should deform and be buried. Radar tests whether subsurface anomalies exist where those models predict. Climate models address future surface conditions. Chemistry and hydrology govern what any remobilized contaminant might do.

No single stream is enough.

This is the opposite of how classified-adjacent mysteries are usually consumed, where one suggestive document or sensor trace is asked to carry origin, capability and intent at once.

At Camp Century, the interesting conclusion emerges because the evidence streams are allowed to remain different.
