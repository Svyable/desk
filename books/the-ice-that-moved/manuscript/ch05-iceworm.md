# Iceworm

Project Iceworm was what happened when a camp became a diagram.

By the early 1960s the Army had enough confidence in its Greenland work to propose something far larger: a dispersed network of ballistic missiles moving through tunnels beneath the ice sheet. Later historical reconstruction places the concept at roughly 600 missiles across an immense area, connected by subglacial routes.

The logic was strategic geometry. A fixed silo can be surveyed, counted and targeted. A weapon moving among many concealed positions forces an adversary to solve a harder problem. Greenland's scale could become part of the defense.

The proposal also reflected a bureaucratic moment. The services were competing over the future of strategic deterrence. Missile technology, hardened basing, mobility and submarine launch were all evolving. Iceworm gave the Army an answer that was distinctly terrestrial and distinctly its own.

What is documented and what is inferred must remain separate. Camp Century existed. Its experimental infrastructure existed. The Army studied larger ice-cap military systems. Historical scholarship has reconstructed the Iceworm missile concept from declassified records. But no operational 600-missile city was built beneath Greenland, and Camp Century should not be retroactively described as if every corridor were secretly a launch tunnel.

The more interesting point is how scaling hides inside language. If a 100-meter experimental rail segment behaves tolerably, the word railway appears. If a buried camp remains habitable, the phrase subsurface facilities appears. Somewhere between those phrases and a continental missile network, engineering reality must be multiplied by several orders of magnitude.

That multiplication was the danger.

A small camp can accept constant maintenance. A strategic basing system cannot rely on crews perpetually trimming every tunnel faster than the ice deforms it. A research station may tolerate local failure. A deterrent architecture must retain alignment, access, communications, launch readiness and known geometry across an enormous network.

Iceworm was not defeated by one spectacular flaw. It was defeated by scale acting on ordinary flaws.

The ice did not have to crush the whole idea at once. It only had to keep moving.
