# A City Under Snow

The most useful photograph of Camp Century is not the reactor. It is a trench.

A machine has cut a long wound into Greenland's firn. Men stand where a street will be. Steel arches will go overhead. Snow will be pushed back on top. Once covered, the thing will look less like a base than a rumor.

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers began building Camp Century in 1959 on the northwestern Greenland Ice Sheet, roughly two hundred kilometers east of Thule Air Base. The engineering method was brutally literal: cut-and-cover construction in snow. Excavate. Install a prefabricated arch. Put the snow back. Repeat until a grid of buried passageways exists where a few seasons earlier there had been a white horizon.

Official histories describe housing, laboratories, communications, medical space, recreation, workshops, food service and power. Depending on the period, roughly scores to a couple hundred people could live there. It was not a bunker in rock. It was a town temporarily persuaded into the upper layers of an ice sheet.

The distinction matters because rock and ice have different attitudes toward architecture. Rock may fracture. Ice creeps.

Camp Century was public enough to be filmed and described as a triumph of polar engineering. That public story was not wholly false. The Army genuinely wanted to know whether large installations could operate inside the Greenland Ice Sheet. It genuinely studied construction, logistics, sanitation, power and snow mechanics. Scientists genuinely used the site.

But a second question sat behind the first. Could the ice sheet support a much larger military system?

That question eventually acquired the name Project Iceworm.

The habit when looking backward is to let the secret purpose swallow the visible work. That is a mistake. A cover story that contains real laboratories is not the same thing as a fictional laboratory. Camp Century was useful precisely because several intentions overlapped there: Arctic engineering, military research, nuclear logistics, glaciology, strategic planning and alliance politics.

The base became stranger because all of those purposes depended on one material assumption. The ice had to be stable enough, long enough, for buried infrastructure to remain infrastructure.

The photograph of the trench already contains the problem. The walls look solid because a photograph freezes them.
