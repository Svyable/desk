# Lost Dirt

Scientific archives have their own archaeology.

Material from the bottom of the Camp Century core passed through institutions and decades before researchers recognized how much information survived in it. What looked like frozen sediment became, under modern analysis, a repository of plant remains, biomolecules and exposure history.

The episode is a useful antidote to the clean mythology of discovery. Important evidence is not always waiting under dramatic lighting. Sometimes it is in a freezer because somebody labeled a box well enough for another person to become curious forty years later.

Andrew Christ and colleagues eventually analyzed Camp Century subglacial sediment using multiple methods, including plant macrofossils, biomarkers, stable isotopes and cosmogenic nuclides. Their 2021 PNAS paper concluded that northwestern Greenland experienced at least two episodes of ice-free, vegetated conditions in the Pleistocene and that the ice sheet melted and re-formed at least once within roughly the last 1.1 million years.

That conclusion did not fall from one spectacular fossil. It emerged from converging measurements with different failure modes.

The evidence is especially powerful because Camp Century lies far inland under thick ice. An ice-free episode there implies a Greenland Ice Sheet much smaller than the modern one.

The dirt survived a secret military program, abandonment, institutional transfers and the long period during which nobody knew what later instruments would be able to ask of it.

The most valuable artifact at Camp Century may have been the thing nobody built.
