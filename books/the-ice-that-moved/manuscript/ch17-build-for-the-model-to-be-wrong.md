# Build for the Model to Be Wrong

A serious postmortem does not ask why earlier engineers failed to possess later knowledge. It asks which assumptions were allowed to become irreversible commitments.

Camp Century offers several.

Tunnel deformation was measurable. The uncertainty concerned scale and maintenance. Climate permanence was assumed over disposal timescales far beyond operational planning. Alliance consent depended on political compartments that could outlive the officials who created them. Waste was left in a medium treated as a sink. Scientific samples were preserved without knowing their future value.

Some of those decisions aged badly. One aged magnificently.

The core survived because somebody preserved material whose eventual questions were unknown.

That suggests a better rule than humility slogans: design reversibility where uncertainty is structural, and preserve evidence where future measurement is likely to improve.

For infrastructure in changing environments this means monitoring pathways before they are needed, maintaining records in durable public formats where security permits, separating removal cost from abandonment assumptions, and periodically reopening models whose boundary conditions are moving.

For classified programs it means recognizing that secrecy itself can prevent technical criticism from reaching the assumptions most in need of it.

A closed program may hide from adversaries and from corrective expertise at the same time.

Camp Century did not need perfect foresight.

It needed a plan for the possibility that Greenland would refuse to stay inside the forecast.
