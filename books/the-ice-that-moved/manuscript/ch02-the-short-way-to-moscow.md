# The Short Way to Moscow

Greenland became strategically enormous because maps lie politely.

On a Mercator projection it sits high and peripheral. In the geometry of the early Cold War, it was on the short route between North America and the Soviet Union. Aircraft, radar, weather, communications and later missiles made the Arctic less a margin than a front porch.

The 1951 Defense of Greenland Agreement between the United States and Denmark formalized an American military presence that included Thule. What followed was not simply base construction. The Arctic became an experimental domain. Machines had to start in cold that changed lubricants. Foundations sat on permafrost. Men, fuel and food had to move across terrain without roads. Radio behaved differently. Snow buried things and also revealed them.

The Cold War rewarded any geography that could shorten reaction time while complicating enemy targeting. Greenland offered both. It was close to strategic routes and physically difficult. Difficulty could be a burden or a shield depending on which side of the engineering equation one occupied.

The Army's interest in the ice sheet belongs in that logic. If deep inland ice could hold installations, then the white interior might become military depth: infrastructure dispersed over an enormous area, concealed beneath a surface that offered few familiar landmarks.

This was before submarine-launched ballistic missiles had matured into the form that would eventually make some land-based Arctic concepts less attractive. Strategic systems were still competing with one another not only for performance but for bureaucratic territory. The Army had reasons to imagine a nuclear role of its own.

Project Iceworm emerged from that institutional environment. Its ambition was not merely to hide a few missiles. Historical accounts describe a mobile system spread through a vast subglacial tunnel network, large enough to complicate Soviet targeting and move weapons between firing positions.

It sounds science-fictional now because we know what happened to the tunnels at Camp Century. At the time, the absurdity was not self-evident. The United States had already built cities where cities did not belong, reactors small enough to ship, aircraft that operated at the edge of material tolerance, and radar lines across the Arctic.

The question was not whether Greenland was hostile.

The question was whether hostility itself could be industrialized.
