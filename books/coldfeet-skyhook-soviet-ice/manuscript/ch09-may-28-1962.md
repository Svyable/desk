# May 28, 1962

On May 28, 1962, Major James F. Smith and Lieutenant (junior grade) Leonard A. LeSchack left the aircraft over NP-8 and entered the operation the simplest way available: by parachute.

The date survives in CIA's later account, along with the identity of the two-man team and the B-17 that brought them over the station. The insertion is almost anticlimactic compared with the extraction. It should not be.

Parachuting onto drifting Arctic sea ice turns every later task into a logistics problem. The men had what they carried and what had been staged for them. They were not arriving at a functioning base. They were entering a recently abandoned one whose structural condition, remaining supplies, and local ice stability could not be fully known from the air.

Their job was not simply to look around. They photographed the station, examined equipment, selected material, and prepared what could be carried out. That required judgment under a constraint intelligence collection rarely admits in its polished summaries: weight.

An abandoned station may contain thousands of objects. An aircraft pickup does not. Every document, component, sample, or instrument selected for removal displaced something else. The team had to decide what deserved scarce extraction capacity while still on the ice.

This is where subject expertise again mattered more than cinematic nerve. A spectacular-looking box could be useless. A dull notebook could contain a year's observations. A wiring modification might reveal more than a finished instrument. A calibration sheet might explain whether a measurement program was serious or improvised.

The later CIA account says the team gathered about 150 pounds of documents, samples, and equipment. That number has become one of the fixed facts in the story. It is also a reminder of how selective the haul necessarily was.

COLDFEET did not recover NP-8. It sampled NP-8.

Any later claim about what the station was, what the Soviets knew, or what their military intentions were has to pass through that sampling problem. The Americans saw what remained after abandonment and carried away what two men judged worth taking under severe constraints.

The mission was intelligence collection. It was also curation under pressure.
