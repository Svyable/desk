# Learning to Pick Up a Human

A technology can be mechanically successful and still be operationally useless if nobody is willing to occupy the failure mode.

Fulton's pickup system had to cross that boundary. Packages were not enough. Dummies were not enough. The system was intended to recover people, which meant somebody eventually had to become the payload.

CIA's later histories describe the progression from test objects to a pig and then to Staff Sergeant Levi Woods in 1958. The details are memorable because they make the engineering physical. A person on the ground was attached to a line lifted by a balloon. An aircraft caught the line at speed. The pickup converted horizontal aircraft motion into vertical acceleration and then into a suspended tow beneath the airplane until the winch could bring the person aboard.

The engineering problem was not simply strength. It was impulse, oscillation, body position, line behavior, visibility, and crew coordination. A system safe in still air could become different in wind. A pickup that worked on a prepared range could behave differently on rough sea ice. Human orientation before the catch mattered. So did the time spent suspended behind the aircraft.

This is a useful place to resist the word *gadget*. Gadgets are self-contained. Skyhook was a procedure distributed across a person, balloon, line, aircraft, winch, pilot, pickup crew, and weather envelope. The hardware mattered because the choreography worked.

That distinction carries forward into the mission itself. COLDFEET is sometimes told as though Fulton invented a miraculous hook and the B-17 simply used it. In reality, operational competence had to be built through repeated testing, specialized crews, and judgment about when not to attempt a pickup.

The later Arctic extraction would include dragging and difficult body orientation in strong wind, according to CIA's museum history. That does not mean the system failed. It means the operational envelope was real.

By 1962, the United States possessed something stranger than a new airplane: a practiced method for turning an aircraft into a temporary vertical escape route. NP-8 gave that method a mission worth risking it for.
