# Leonard LeSchack Sees an Opening

Operations often become inevitable in retrospect. COLDFEET was not.

Someone had to look at an abandoned Soviet ice station and see something other than a wrecked camp. Leonard A. LeSchack, a Navy officer associated with the Office of Naval Research, was central to turning the opportunity into a plan. Later CIA histories credit the project to an ONR-originated idea that survived changes in target, funding, and institutional ownership before becoming an Agency operation.

That bureaucratic path is important because it breaks the familiar image of intelligence as a single organization deciding what it wants and then executing. COLDFEET moved through a network: naval research, military intelligence, Air Force expertise, CIA aviation, contract pilots, and polar navigation. The target itself was hybrid, so the team became hybrid too.

The first abandoned Soviet station considered for exploitation was not ultimately the one entered. Planning continued long enough for circumstances to change. NP-8 became the practical target after its abandonment in 1962. By then ONR funding had run out, according to CIA's later account, and the Agency took over.

That handoff reveals something about how unusual operations happen. The decisive resource is not always a large budget or formal program. Sometimes it is institutional persistence—the ability of an idea to migrate until it finds an organization with the aircraft, authorities, people, and appetite to attempt it.

LeSchack's value was not simply that he wanted to visit the station. He understood what might be worth looking for. An intelligence exploitation team without subject knowledge can collect spectacular junk. Scientific intelligence depends on knowing which notebook, instrument modification, wiring arrangement, calibration sheet, or discarded component deserves weight.

The operation therefore joined two kinds of expertise that are often separated in popular accounts. One group had to know how to survive and retrieve people from a floating ice platform. Another had to know what the platform's scientific remains might mean.

COLDFEET did not become possible because the Arctic suddenly became easy. It became possible because a narrow technical idea—airborne pickup without landing—met a narrow intelligence opportunity—an abandoned station with material still on it.

LeSchack saw the opening. Robert Fulton had spent years building the mechanism that could make the opening usable.
