# Intermountain Aviation

The airplane that made COLDFEET possible belonged to the gray machinery of Cold War aviation: a company, an aircraft, a crew, and an institutional relationship designed to do things government organizations did not always do under their own names.

CIA histories identify Intermountain Aviation as the proprietary that supplied the B-17 and experienced pickup crew. The company was headed by Garfield Thorsrud, and contract pilots Connie Seigrist and Douglas Price appear repeatedly in the surviving accounts of the operation.

This matters because covert capability often lives in infrastructure that looks ordinary from a distance. A B-17 was no longer cutting-edge by 1962. The novelty was in what had been done to it and who knew how to use it. The aircraft carried the Fulton pickup installation, a winch crew, navigation expertise, fuel planning, and people who had rehearsed the geometry of recovering a suspended load at low altitude.

There is a tendency to explain secret operations by invoking secret technology. COLDFEET suggests a less glamorous rule. Mature technology plus specialized practice can be more useful than a prototype. The B-17 was old. The mission profile was not.

The operation also required polar navigation. The target was moving. There was no conventional destination in the sense of a paved airport with a fixed coordinate. The floe had drifted. Weather could erase visual references. The aircraft had to find a small human presence on an enormous white surface and then fly an extraction profile close enough to the ice to catch a line.

None of this proves institutional omnipotence. The mission was delayed by weather. Funding had already migrated between organizations. The operation cost more than its initial project figure, according to William Leary's historical account, in part because bad weather increased flying time.

That is closer to the texture of real technical intelligence work: improvised, expensive in unexpected places, dependent on people who know a machine unusually well, and vulnerable to weather in a way no organizational chart can solve.

The secret was not the existence of the airplane. It was the combination of aircraft, modification, crew, mission, and timing.
