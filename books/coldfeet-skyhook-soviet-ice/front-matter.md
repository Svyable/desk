# Coldfeet

*Skyhook, Soviet Ice, and the Intelligence Left on the Floe*

Sven Hardy Benson

There are operations that become famous because they changed history, and operations that become famous because they look like they should have.

Project COLDFEET belongs to the second category unless we are careful.

Two Americans parachuted onto an abandoned Soviet station drifting in the Arctic Ocean. They moved through buildings the Soviet crew had left behind, photographed what they found, opened equipment, collected documents, and waited on breaking ice for an aircraft that could not land. When the airplane finally returned, it did not land. A line rose into the air beneath a balloon. A B-17 came in low. Steel horns on its nose caught the line. A winch hauled first cargo and then men into the aircraft.

That is enough plot for a book. It is not yet enough meaning.

The station itself matters more than the stunt. Soviet drifting stations were not movie sets built for spies to raid. They were working scientific platforms, part of a decades-long effort to measure an ocean hidden beneath moving ice. Their observers recorded weather, sea ice, ocean structure, currents, and other properties difficult to know from shore. By the early 1960s the same ocean had also become navigable to nuclear submarines. Science and strategy occupied the same cold geometry.

This book follows the operation without pretending every scientific instrument was a weapon or every classified conclusion was correct. It asks what an intelligence service can learn from the machinery, paperwork, habits, and unfinished work of another country's scientists. It also asks what gets distorted when a mission's extraction method becomes more memorable than the thing extracted.

The answer, if there is one, begins with a runway cracking apart.
