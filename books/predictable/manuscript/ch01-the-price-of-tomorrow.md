# The Price of Tomorrow

On August 26, 2026, the future of artificial intelligence had a price.

Kalshi, a U.S. prediction market designated by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, was running an event called “When will OpenAI achieve AGI?” Traders had put about $910,000 through the market. The screen showed 15 percent before 2027, 36.8 percent before 2028, and 48.1 percent before 2030.

A dispute large enough to occupy laboratories, boardrooms, venture funds, philosophy departments, and half the internet had been reduced to a row of percentages.

The numbers were useful. They were also answering a narrower question than they appeared to be.

The 2026 contract did not ask whether a panel of neuroscientists believed a machine had reached human-level general intelligence. It did not point to a benchmark, a productivity threshold, a Turing test, or a consensus among researchers. It would resolve yes if OpenAI announced that it had attained AGI by the deadline, with the outcome verified from OpenAI.

That is a perfectly sensible way to write a contract. A market cannot settle on a philosophical argument. Somebody has to define the event, pick a date, name a source, and decide what counts as being right.

But the rule changes the thing being forecast.

A trader buying the 2026 contract was not simply forecasting technical progress. The trade also depended on corporate language, timing, disclosure, and whatever OpenAI itself would be willing to call AGI. A system might become broadly capable without the company using that label. The company might use the label while researchers continued to argue. Either outcome could make the market settle in a way that surprised somebody who had only read the headline.

The contract would not have malfunctioned. The reader would have misunderstood the contract.

This is one of the useful disciplines of prediction markets. Conversation can drift forever. A trade cannot. The question has to acquire edges.

It is also the first reason to be careful with the number on the screen.

A displayed probability is not a vial of distilled truth. It is a summary of a market with bids, offers, spreads, fees, position limits, available liquidity, and people arriving with different information, incentives, wealth, and tolerance for risk. The trader does not get to buy “15 percent.” The trader gets whatever price is actually available for the position he wants to take.

That sounds like a technical distinction until the number begins to travel.

“I think AGI may arrive soon” sounds like an opinion. “The market says 36.8 percent” sounds measured. The decimal place helps. So does the presence of money. So does the knowledge that somebody can profit from disagreeing.

Those are real advantages over a television panel or an unsupported prediction. They are not the same thing as certainty.

Prediction markets work because disagreement becomes costly enough to be informative. A binary contract pays according to a future outcome. On Kalshi, a correct contract settles at one dollar and an incorrect one at zero. If a participant thinks the market is too pessimistic, there is a reason to buy. If another thinks the crowd has become carried away, there is a reason to take the other side. Information that might otherwise remain private can become a trade.

The mechanism deserves respect because it has earned some.

In a widely cited review, economists Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz found that prediction markets were often quite accurate and could outperform moderately sophisticated forecasting alternatives. The Iowa Electronic Markets offered a more concrete test. Joyce Berg, Forrest Nelson, and Thomas Rietz compared their presidential-election forecasts with 964 national polls across five U.S. elections from 1988 through 2004. The market was closer to the eventual two-party vote split 74 percent of the time, with a particular advantage at longer horizons.

That is strong evidence against dismissing prediction markets as decorative gambling.

It is not evidence that every market is wiser than every alternative.

The conditions matter. A market with clear rules, enough liquidity, meaningful stakes, and participants who possess genuinely different information is a very different instrument from a thin market in which the same small group of people is reading the same news and trading around the same rumor.

Even the familiar shortcut from price to probability has limits.

A binary contract trading around forty cents invites us to say the market assigns roughly a forty percent chance to the event. Wolfers and Zitzewitz later examined when that interpretation is reasonable and when prices can be biased. Charles Manski pushed the objection further: an equilibrium market price does not reveal a single objective probability, nor can it tell us the full distribution of beliefs among traders.

The intuition is easier than the mathematics.

People do not arrive with identical bankrolls. They do not share the same appetite for risk. Some are hedging. Some are speculating. Some know more than others. Some cannot participate at all. A person with a strong view and little money does not move the market like a person with a weaker view and a large balance sheet. Correlated information can also masquerade as independent agreement when hundreds of people have simply consumed the same source.

A price is the outcome of a structure.

That sentence matters far beyond prediction markets.

Modern finance is full of numbers that look like direct statements about reality but are actually compressed statements about assumptions. A company’s valuation is not a referendum on whether its technology matters. It is a claim about future cash flows, competition, margins, capital requirements, dilution, discount rates, and how long all of those things take to arrive. A credit spread is not a pure measure of whether a borrower will fail. An options price is not a prophecy about where a stock will finish.

Every useful financial number has a contract hiding inside it.

Prediction markets make the hidden structure unusually easy to inspect. One dollar or zero. Yes or no. Deadline. Settlement source. Dispute process.

The clarity is refreshing. It can also be intoxicating.

On August 26, the AGI market showed 48.1 percent before 2030. It was tempting to read that number as though humanity had measured the distance to general intelligence and discovered that the answer was almost exactly a coin flip.

Nothing like that had happened.

Traders had exchanged contracts whose settlement depended on whether OpenAI would make a qualifying announcement before a deadline. To price those contracts they had to form views about technical progress, corporate behavior, language, and the market rule. The resulting number may have contained useful information about all four.

It still was not a physical constant.

The seduction becomes stronger when the price moves.

A forecast in a report can become stale quietly. A market advertises its revisions. A model release, a research result, an executive comment, a regulation, or a rumor can move the price before the day is over. The chart becomes a public diary of belief: yesterday the future cost this much; today it costs something else.

Good forecasters are supposed to update, and markets do it without embarrassment. They do not defend last quarter’s memo or explain away the old strategic plan. They trade again.

There is genuine intelligence in that habit.

There is also a trap. Responsiveness is not the same as understanding. A market can react quickly to information that later proves irrelevant. It can overreact and correct. It can inherit the blind spots of the people inside it. Liquidity and incentives improve the machinery of judgment; they do not lift judgment out of the human condition.

The market is not outside the crowd. It is one of the better machines the crowd has built for arguing with itself.

Artificial intelligence makes this especially interesting because the entire industry is already organized around forecasts.

Researchers forecast scaling. Companies forecast demand. Semiconductor makers forecast accelerator shipments. Utilities forecast data-center load. Cloud providers forecast utilization. Investors forecast margins. Governments forecast productivity and employment effects. Workers forecast which parts of their jobs will remain valuable. Prediction markets simply put some of those expectations behind a bid and an ask.

The AI age is not short of predictions. It is saturated with them.

The harder question is what happens when the forecasts begin to confirm one another.

An AI company expects demand to rise, so it secures more compute. A cloud provider sees customers securing compute, so it orders more chips and data-center capacity. Utilities see the interconnection requests and lift their load forecasts. Investors see the buildout and conclude that demand must be enormous. Suppliers expand because customers are expanding. Analysts point to supplier growth as evidence that adoption is accelerating.

Each step can be rational on its own.

The loop can still overshoot.

That is the uncomfortable feature of a capital boom. The evidence used to justify the next investment often includes the investments already being made by everyone else.

Nothing in that process requires artificial intelligence to be a fraud.

The technology can be extraordinary. The demand can be real. The infrastructure can be necessary. The error, if one develops, can sit somewhere else: in the conversion from “this will matter” to “this amount of capital will earn an adequate return on this timetable.”

History is full of technologies that mattered more than their early investors could sensibly price.

Railroads did shrink distance. Electricity did reorganize industry. The internet did become basic infrastructure for modern life. None of those truths guaranteed that every railroad bond, utility promotion, radio stock, fiber network, or dot-com security issued during the excitement would earn a good return.

This is what makes technological bubbles so difficult to recognize from the inside. The strongest argument for caution is often built on top of a true story.

A fraud can be exposed. A real revolution is harder. It keeps producing evidence for the optimists even while the economics become more demanding.

That is why the AGI contract belongs at the beginning of this book.

It forces a broad future into a narrow settlement rule. It can aggregate information without escaping definitions, incentives, or uncertainty. Most importantly, it reminds us that being right about what happens is not the same as being right about what it was worth to pay for the path there.

Suppose the market is directionally right and some form of artificial general intelligence arrives within the next several years. That would settle an important argument about technology. It would not settle the investment case.

By then, trillions of dollars may have been committed to accelerators, data centers, transmission, generation, cooling, models, startups, and software. Some of that infrastructure may prove indispensable. Some may be obsolete before it is fully depreciated. Some companies may earn extraordinary returns. Others may discover that the defining technology of their generation has become a commodity, a loss leader, or a feature inside somebody else’s product.

Customers may capture much of the value. Competitors may drive prices down. New hardware may turn scarcity into abundance. The social return can be enormous while the financial return to a particular owner is disappointing.

The future event and the investment return are different contracts, even when no exchange writes them that way.

That distinction is the spine of this book.

The central mistake in a technology boom is not always believing too much in the technology. Sometimes the technology exceeds the hype. The more expensive mistake is believing that seeing the future early is the same as pricing it correctly.

Prediction markets are useful because they make one layer of forecasting explicit. They force us to define the question, choose a deadline, accept a settlement rule, and put money behind uncertainty. Used well, that discipline can make thinking more honest.

But the number is the beginning of the argument, not the end.

Before trusting a probability, ask what contract produced it. Before trusting a valuation, ask what future has already been paid for. And before congratulating ourselves for recognizing the next revolution, ask the question that tends to arrive only after the excitement has passed:

What did we pay for it?
