# The Price of Tomorrow

On August 26, 2026, the future of artificial intelligence had a price.

On Kalshi, a regulated prediction market in the United States, traders could buy a contract tied to a question that had occupied laboratories, boardrooms, investment committees, and online arguments for years: when would OpenAI achieve artificial general intelligence?

The screen made the uncertainty look almost domesticated. Before 2027: 15 percent. Before 2028: 36.8 percent. Before 2030: 48.1 percent. More than nine hundred thousand dollars of volume had traded across the event. A question large enough to support religions, careers, fortunes, and existential fears had been compressed into a row of percentages.

The compression was useful. It was also easy to misunderstand.

The contract for 2026 did not resolve according to whether a committee of neuroscientists concluded that machines had matched the general capacities of human beings. It did not depend on a benchmark score, an economic productivity threshold, a Turing test, or a vote among artificial-intelligence researchers. The market rule was narrower. It would resolve yes if OpenAI announced that it had attained AGI by the end of the year, with the outcome verified from OpenAI.

That is a different event.

A trader buying the contract was not merely answering a technological question. The trader was forecasting a public statement by a particular company, made before a particular deadline, under a particular set of market rules. The underlying technical reality mattered, but so did language. So did corporate judgment. So did timing. So did whatever OpenAI itself would be willing to call AGI.

The distinction is small enough to miss when the number is sitting there on the screen.

This is one of the strange accomplishments of prediction markets. They force uncertainty into a contract. A question that can wander indefinitely in conversation has to acquire an expiration date, a source, a settlement mechanism, and a definition of what counts as being right. The future becomes less philosophical because someone eventually has to pay.

That discipline is part of their appeal. It is also where their apparent objectivity begins to fray.

Kalshi’s displayed probability for AGI before 2027 was 15 percent that day, but the market around it contained more texture than the headline number suggested. The page showed a yes price of 17.8 cents and a no price of 84.9 cents. Those two offers added to more than a dollar. A trader could not step into the market at an abstract 15 percent. The trader had to face an order book, a spread, fees, position size, counterparties, and whatever liquidity was available at the moment of execution.

The percentage was not false. It was a summary.

That difference matters because summaries acquire authority quickly. We have learned to treat numbers as the opposite of stories. A person who says, “I think AGI might arrive soon,” sounds like a pundit. A market showing 36.8 percent for an announcement before 2028 appears to have done something more rigorous. The decimal place implies measurement. The price implies money at risk. The market implies competing views. All three are real advantages over an unsupported opinion.

But none of them turns the number into a fact about the future.

A prediction market begins with a simple mechanism. A contract pays according to a future outcome. On Kalshi, a correct binary contract settles at one dollar and an incorrect one at zero. Traders who think the event is more likely than the market price can buy one side; traders who think it is less likely can take the other. Their disagreement becomes a price.

That price can be remarkably informative.

The strongest case for prediction markets is not mystical and does not require believing that crowds are always wise. Markets create an incentive to act on information rather than merely possess it. Someone who knows something that other participants do not know has a reason to trade. Someone who notices that the market is inconsistent has a reason to trade. Someone who thinks an emotional consensus has gone too far has a reason to take the other side. In a liquid market with clear rules, diverse participants, and meaningful stakes, the mechanism can pull private judgments into a common number faster than a committee can collect them in a meeting.

There is a long empirical case for taking that process seriously. In their review of prediction markets, economists Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz found that market-generated forecasts were typically fairly accurate and often outperformed moderately sophisticated alternatives. Work on the Iowa Electronic Markets later compared market forecasts with 964 national polls across five United States presidential elections from 1988 through 2004. The market forecast was closer to the eventual two-party vote split 74 percent of the time and performed especially well at longer horizons.

That does not mean every market is better than every poll. It means the basic proposition survived contact with data: under some conditions, prices can aggregate dispersed information in ways that are difficult to reproduce by asking people what they think and averaging the answers.

This should make anyone writing about prediction markets cautious about easy mockery. Calling them casinos avoids the hard part. Wagers can contain information. Financial incentives can improve forecasting. A market price can be more disciplined than a television panel, a social-media consensus, or an executive’s intuition.

The deeper problem is not that market prices are meaningless. It is that their meaning is conditional.

Even the apparently simple step from price to probability has qualifications. A binary contract paying one dollar if an event occurs invites a natural interpretation: if the contract trades at forty cents, perhaps the event has a forty percent chance of happening. Wolfers and Zitzewitz later examined the theory behind that interpretation and concluded that prediction-market prices can often provide useful estimates of average beliefs, though sometimes biased ones. Charles Manski had pressed the underlying objection more sharply: an equilibrium price need not reveal a single objective probability, and the distribution of trader beliefs cannot be reconstructed from the price alone.

The argument may sound technical, but the intuition is ordinary. People do not arrive at a market with identical wealth, identical risk tolerance, identical information, or identical conviction. Some cannot participate. Some can trade only small amounts. Some may be hedging rather than maximizing expected profit from a pure forecast. Some beliefs are correlated because many traders read the same sources. A quoted price is the outcome of those conditions, not a poll of all informed minds on Earth.

And then there is the contract itself.

The AGI market is unusually revealing because the word at its center is unusually unstable. “Artificial general intelligence” has been used to describe everything from broadly capable machine reasoning to systems that outperform humans across economically important work. Researchers disagree over definitions. Companies may use the term differently from academics. A market cannot settle on disagreement. It needs a rule.

Kalshi solved that problem by choosing an observable event: an OpenAI announcement.

The choice makes the contract tradable. It also changes the question.

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that an AI system released in December 2026 could perform almost every cognitive task an ordinary office worker could perform, but OpenAI declined to label it AGI. Under the market rule, the contract could still settle no. Suppose instead that OpenAI publicly announced that it had attained AGI under its own definition while many researchers remained unconvinced. The contract could settle yes.

The market would not have malfunctioned in either case. It would have done exactly what the contract asked.

This is the first rule of reading a prediction market: the wording is part of the forecast.

A market price does not answer the question you wish had been asked. It answers the contract that can actually be settled.

The same principle becomes important far beyond prediction markets. Modern finance is full of numbers that look like direct statements about reality but are actually statements about definitions, assumptions, and time horizons. A company’s valuation is not simply a verdict on whether its technology matters. It is a compressed claim about future cash flows, competition, margins, capital requirements, discount rates, dilution, and the time it takes for all of those things to arrive. A bond spread is not a pure measure of whether a borrower will fail. An options price is not a prophecy about where a stock will finish. Each number is produced by a structure.

Prediction markets make that structure easier to see because the payoff is so stark. One dollar or zero. Yes or no. Date certain. Source specified.

Yet even here, precision can outrun knowledge.

On the day the AGI market showed 48.1 percent before 2030, it was tempting to read the number as though humanity had measured the distance to general intelligence and found itself almost exactly at even odds. Nothing of the sort had happened. Traders had exchanged contracts whose settlement depended on whether OpenAI would make a qualifying announcement before a deadline. Their trades condensed whatever they believed about model progress, corporate behavior, public language, and the market rule into a price.

That is still interesting. It may be very interesting. It is simply not the same thing as measuring a physical constant.

The distinction becomes harder to hold onto when prices move.

A forecast written in a report can become stale quietly. A market advertises its revisions. A new model release, a research result, an executive comment, a regulatory change, or even a rumor can move the price within minutes. The chart creates a visible history of belief. Yesterday the future cost this much. Today it costs something else.

That motion is intellectually seductive because updating is one of the habits good forecasters are supposed to cultivate. A person should change a probability when the evidence changes. A market appears to do this continuously and without embarrassment. It does not have to defend an old column or explain why last quarter’s strategic plan was wrong. It simply trades.

There is genuine wisdom in that.

There is also a danger. Because the number changes continuously, we may begin to confuse responsiveness with understanding. A market can react quickly to information that later proves irrelevant. It can move on a rumor. It can overreact and correct. It can be thin. It can inherit the blind spots of the people trading it. Liquidity and incentives can improve the quality of a forecast without abolishing the ordinary limits of human judgment.

The market is not outside the crowd. It is one of the machines through which the crowd expresses itself.

This becomes especially important in artificial intelligence because so much of the field is already organized around forecasts. Researchers forecast scaling. Companies forecast demand. Investors forecast margins and market size. Utilities forecast electricity load. Semiconductor companies forecast accelerator shipments. Governments forecast productivity and labor-market effects. Employees forecast which skills will matter. Prediction markets merely make the habit explicit. They give some of these beliefs a bid and an ask.

The AI age is therefore not suffering from a shortage of predictions. It is suffering from a surplus of them.

The interesting question is what happens when forecasts begin to reinforce one another.

An AI company expects demand to rise, so it secures more compute. A cloud provider sees customers securing compute, so it builds more data centers. A utility sees data-center requests and raises its load forecast. Investors see the buildout and infer that demand must be enormous. Suppliers see investor confidence and expand capacity. Analysts take the spending as evidence of adoption. The existence of large commitments becomes part of the case for making still larger commitments.

None of the individual steps has to be irrational. The technology can be real. The demand can be real. The infrastructure can be necessary. The mistake, if there is one, can live in the conversion from “this will matter” to “this amount of capital will earn an adequate return on this timetable.”

That conversion is where bubbles become difficult to recognize from inside them.

A technological bubble is rarely built entirely on a lie. Lies do not usually require enough rail, fiber, factories, power, concrete, chips, and debt to reshape an economy. The more dangerous manias begin with something true. Railroads really did shrink distance. Electricity really did reorganize industry. The internet really did become infrastructure for modern life. The fact that these technologies mattered did not guarantee that every security issued to finance them was fairly priced.

This is why the AGI contract is such an appropriate place to begin.

It asks a narrow question about the arrival of a broad future. Traders can be right or wrong according to a rule. The price can be useful without being omniscient. The market can aggregate information without escaping definitions, incentives, and uncertainty. Most importantly, a correct forecast about what happens does not tell us what it was worth to pay for the path there.

Imagine that the market is directionally right and some form of artificial general intelligence arrives within the next several years. That outcome would settle an argument about technology. It would not settle the investment case.

By then, trillions of dollars may have been committed to chips, data centers, power systems, models, startups, and software. Some of that infrastructure may be essential. Some may be obsolete before it is fully depreciated. Some companies may earn extraordinary returns. Others may discover that the most important technology of their generation is also a commodity, a loss leader, or a feature inside somebody else’s product. Customers may capture much of the value. Competitors may drive prices down. New entrants may turn scarcity into abundance.

The future event and the financial return are different contracts, even when no exchange writes them that way.

That is the distinction this book will follow.

The central error of a technology boom is not necessarily believing too much in the technology. Sometimes the technology exceeds the hype. The error can be subtler: believing that recognizing the future early is the same as pricing it correctly.

Prediction markets are valuable because they make one layer of that problem visible. They force us to say what we mean, choose a deadline, accept a settlement rule, and attach a number to uncertainty. Used well, they can make thinking more honest.

But the number is the beginning of the argument, not the end.

Before trusting a probability, we have to ask what contract produced it. Before trusting a valuation, we have to ask what future it already contains. And before congratulating ourselves for seeing a revolution coming, we have to ask the question that history keeps asking after the excitement has passed:

What did we pay for it?
