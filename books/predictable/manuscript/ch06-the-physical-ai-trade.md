# The Physical AI Trade

A model can be copied in seconds. A substation cannot.

That difference sits underneath much of the current artificial-intelligence boom.

On a screen, AI appears to belong to software. A prompt goes in. Tokens come out. The product feels closer to search than to steelmaking. Yet the newest systems are forcing some of the largest technology companies in the world to negotiate for electricity, transformers, land, cooling equipment, transmission capacity, construction crews, and permits on timelines measured in years.

The abstraction ends at the wall socket.

By June 2026, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated that U.S. data centers could account for 11.8 percent of national electricity consumption by 2030 in its reference case. Its scenario range ran from 9.5 to 15.3 percent. The estimate was not a tally of announced campuses. It was built from expected shipments of computing equipment, per-device electricity use, cooling-system performance, facility types, and locations.

The range is almost as important as the central estimate.

AI demand is uncertain. Hardware efficiency is uncertain. The pace at which new facilities can connect to the grid is uncertain. A forecast that places data centers somewhere between roughly one-tenth and one-sixth of U.S. electricity use by the end of the decade is not a precise destination. It is a statement that planners are now dealing with loads large enough to matter at the scale of the power system.

The global picture is similar. The International Energy Agency estimated data-center electricity consumption at roughly 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, around 1.5 percent of world electricity demand. Its base case reaches about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030. In that scenario, accelerated servers associated mainly with AI account for almost half of the increase.

These forecasts may prove too high.

The IEA has a high-efficiency case in which stronger gains in hardware, software, and infrastructure materially reduce energy requirements. It also has a headwinds case in which slower AI adoption and physical bottlenecks restrain deployment. The point is not that one forecast has finally measured AI's appetite for power. The point is that the same institution can produce credible futures separated by hundreds of terawatt-hours because the physical system and the computing system are changing at the same time.

That uncertainty is not stopping construction.

A modern AI data center begins with the obvious machinery: processors, memory, networking, storage. But most of those machines are useless until a much slower set of systems arrives around them.

Electricity has to be generated somewhere. It has to move through transmission lines. Voltage has to be transformed. A site needs switchgear, protection systems, backup power, and cooling. High-density racks produce heat that has to leave the building continuously. Fiber has to connect the campus to the rest of the network. Roads, concrete, steel, pipes, pumps, and labor turn a site plan into an operating facility.

Every layer has its own supply curve.

This is where the AI trade stops looking like a software cycle and starts looking like an infrastructure cycle.

The power problem is the easiest to see because electricity cannot be stored and shipped around the world as conveniently as processors. A cloud provider can redirect hardware purchases between regions. It cannot move a gigawatt of available grid capacity from Iowa to northern Virginia by changing a procurement spreadsheet.

The location matters.

So does the hour.

Data centers generally want continuous power. Their economics improve when expensive processors stay busy. Reliability matters because a cluster that loses power does not merely turn off office lights; it interrupts workloads spread across thousands of machines. Utilities therefore have to plan not only enough annual energy but enough capacity, transmission, and local equipment to serve concentrated loads when they appear.

The scale is unusual for an industry whose end product is digital.

The IEA uses 100 megawatts as a representative hyperscale data-center load and notes that the largest facilities under construction can be far larger. One hundred megawatts is not a national problem. It can be a very large local one. Several such projects arriving in the same utility territory can move faster than the planning assumptions that determined which substations, lines, and generators were built years earlier.

That is why the bottleneck increasingly appears before the building is finished.

In June 2026, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission ordered all six regional grid operators under its jurisdiction to justify or reform the rules governing how data centers and other large electricity users connect to the grid. The order was not an abstract statement about future AI demand. It was a response to large loads arriving quickly enough to expose gaps in tariffs and planning procedures built for a different pattern of growth.

The regulatory question itself reveals the physical problem.

Who pays for the transmission upgrade if a giant customer requests service and the grid has to be reinforced before that customer appears? What happens if the customer later cancels? How much firm capacity should a utility build against a load forecast dominated by projects that may compete for the same tenants? Should a data center be allowed to co-locate beside an existing power plant and consume much of its output directly? What obligations remain to the wider grid?

These are not questions a model benchmark can answer.

They are allocation problems created by scarcity.

FERC's 2026 actions included a push for cost-recovery agreements intended to prevent ordinary customers from being left with infrastructure costs when a large planned load does not materialize. That detail matters for the capital cycle. A utility can overbuild just as a cloud provider can. The difference is that regulated infrastructure has to decide in advance who bears the cost of being wrong.

Generation is another constraint, but it is not simply a story of too few proposed power plants.

At the end of 2025, Berkeley Lab counted more than 2,060 gigawatts of generation and storage actively seeking transmission interconnection in the United States. That number was enormous relative to the existing power system. It also overstated what would actually be built.

Most projects entering interconnection queues eventually withdraw.

The queue is therefore a map of interest, not a map of future supply.

This distinction mirrors the data-center buildout itself. An announced project is not a completed project. A requested grid connection is not an energized campus. A generator in a queue is not electricity available to a rack.

Physical infrastructure accumulates layers of optionality long before it becomes productive capacity.

Berkeley Lab's 2026 queue data also offered a useful counterpoint to the idea that grid bottlenecks only get worse. Active generation and storage capacity in U.S. queues fell about 10 percent from the prior year. More interconnection agreements were being processed in several regions. The backlog remained large and timelines remained long, but the system was responding.

That response is the most important thing to understand about a bottleneck.

A bottleneck is not a permanent feature of nature.

It is a price signal with engineering consequences.

Long waits for grid connections encourage data-center developers to consider different regions. Scarce grid power encourages on-site generation and storage. Expensive electricity increases the value of more efficient chips and software. Transmission congestion can make a site with existing electrical infrastructure more valuable than a site with cheaper land. Slow permitting can redirect projects toward jurisdictions willing to accelerate approvals.

The same logic reaches down to components that most AI users will never see.

Transformers are a good example.

They are old technology, manufactured by an industry that spent most of the last decade far from the center of technology investing. Yet a large electrical load cannot simply be plugged into the grid at whatever voltage happens to be passing nearby. Transformers are needed throughout the system to change voltage levels and move power safely from generation to transmission, distribution, and end users.

The U.S. Department of Energy reports that distribution-transformer lead times rose from roughly three to six months in 2019 to 12 to 30 months in 2023, the latest comparable data in its supply-chain analysis. The causes were broader than AI: pandemic-era disruptions, materials constraints, labor, and fragmented specifications all mattered. But data-center construction has entered a market that was already tight.

This is what physical bottlenecks do to a technology boom. They recruit unrelated industries into it.

Copper producers, electrical-equipment manufacturers, utilities, engineering firms, cooling specialists, gas-turbine suppliers, construction contractors, and landowners can all become participants in the AI trade without building a model or selling a token.

Scarcity rents migrate.

A chip supplier may have pricing power because accelerators are scarce. A utility territory may gain leverage because megawatts are scarce. A site developer may gain leverage because an energized parcel is scarce. A transformer manufacturer may gain leverage because delivery slots are scarce.

Investors then make a familiar inference: if an input is scarce and valuable now, capacity that produces more of it must also be valuable.

Sometimes that inference is correct.

Sometimes it contains the seed of the next excess.

The transformer example again helps. DOE's response to long lead times has not been to accept them as a permanent tax on electrification. The agency has been convening manufacturers and utilities to reduce the proliferation of specifications, improve interoperability, and shorten production times. In 2026 it was still describing the supply chain as constrained, but it was also describing specific ways to make it less constrained.

A shortage attracts investment and standardization.

The same process is happening at a much larger scale in electricity markets. Grid operators are revising interconnection procedures. Developers are proposing generation. Utilities are changing forecasts. Technology companies are signing long-term power agreements and pursuing nuclear, gas, renewables, storage, and geothermal options. Data-center operators are experimenting with load flexibility and on-site power.

None of these responses guarantees that power will become abundant quickly.

Transmission projects can take many years. Large transformers and turbines are not software. Permits can outlast product generations. New nuclear projects have long development timelines. Natural-gas infrastructure faces its own equipment and pipeline constraints. A site can have land and fiber while waiting years for an electrical upgrade.

But the direction of causality matters.

The shortage causes the investment intended to end the shortage.

Cooling has the same dual character.

The processors doing AI work consume electricity and turn nearly all of it into heat. Higher-density racks concentrate that heat into smaller spaces. Traditional air cooling becomes less attractive as rack densities rise, pushing operators toward liquid cooling and other designs that move heat more efficiently.

The IEA estimates that cooling can account for about 7 percent of electricity use in highly efficient hyperscale facilities and more than 30 percent in less efficient enterprise data centers. The wide range is a reminder that a megawatt of IT load does not imply one fixed amount of overhead.

Engineering choices matter.

So does climate. So does utilization. So does facility age. So does the temperature at which equipment can operate safely.

Water adds another local constraint.

Some cooling systems consume water directly. Electricity generation can consume water indirectly. Berkeley Lab research has found enormous variation in the water footprint of individual computing workloads depending on server efficiency, grid mix, utilization, cooling technology, climate, and location. A 2025 review found workload-level water use varying by more than four orders of magnitude across plausible conditions.

That makes the phrase "AI uses water" both true and incomplete.

A workload run on an efficient server in a cool climate using a low-water electricity source can have a very different footprint from the same broad class of workload run elsewhere. Moving computation changes the physical consequences.

This creates another form of arbitrage.

AI workloads are more geographically flexible than steel mills or apartment buildings. Some training jobs can be scheduled where power is cheaper or cleaner. Some inference has latency requirements that keep it close to users, but not all of it does. A company facing an impossible grid queue in one region may be able to build in another.

That flexibility is strong counterevidence to the claim that today's bottlenecks necessarily impose a hard ceiling on AI growth.

The computing industry has repeatedly learned to route around constraints.

When processors become expensive, engineers use fewer bits, better scheduling, sparsity, caching, and specialized silicon. When cooling becomes difficult, they redesign the thermal system. When one region lacks power, they search for another. When the grid is slow, they investigate co-location, dedicated generation, and storage.

The physical system is slower than software, but it is not static.

This is why the most dramatic project announcements should be read carefully.

A proposed five-gigawatt campus is not five gigawatts of operating load. It is a claim on a future combination of land, equipment, financing, customers, and power. The project may be built in stages. It may be redesigned. It may secure less power than requested. It may never reach its headline scale.

The same caution applies to power forecasts.

Berkeley Lab's 2030 reference case of 649 terawatt-hours of U.S. data-center electricity use is a model built from the best available information about hardware shipments and facility performance. Its scenario range is deliberately wide. Treating the central estimate as a committed load would repeat the mistake this book keeps returning to: turning a forecast into an asset before the uncertain steps between them have occurred.

Yet dismissing the forecast because it is uncertain would be another mistake.

Utilities cannot wait until every server is installed before deciding whether to build a substation. Transformer manufacturers cannot expand capacity after every order is already late. Power plants cannot be permitted after demand arrives. Physical systems require decisions before certainty.

That is what makes the AI buildout financially interesting.

Capital has to move before the evidence is complete.

A data-center developer may reserve electrical capacity years before tenants fully commit. A utility may reinforce a network based on a forecast. A manufacturer may build a transformer plant because current lead times imply years of demand. A power developer may enter a queue because data-center growth appears durable.

Each decision can be rational on its own.

Together they create the future supply response.

The risk is not only that demand disappoints.

The risk is that solutions arrive from several directions at once.

A faster accelerator reduces the electricity required for a given amount of useful work. Better models can need fewer tokens. Liquid cooling can raise power density without requiring the same building footprint. More standardized transformers can shorten delivery times. Interconnection reform can bring generation online faster. New generation can relieve local scarcity. Flexible workloads can migrate away from constrained regions.

A site that looks uniquely valuable because it has access to 200 megawatts in 2026 may face a different competitive environment after utilities, developers, and engineers spend four years trying to create more sites like it.

This does not mean today's scarcity rents are imaginary.

They are real enough to change federal regulation.

They are real enough to push transformer delivery times into years.

They are real enough for technology companies to structure power agreements and data-center investments on a scale once associated with heavy industry.

The more difficult question is who owns the bottleneck after everyone tries to solve it.

That question separates physical scarcity from durable market power.

A transformer shortage can enrich transformer manufacturers until factories expand or specifications standardize. A grid-constrained parcel can command a premium until nearby transmission is reinforced or compute moves elsewhere. A power producer can earn attractive returns until new generation arrives. A cooling technology can command high margins until it becomes standard equipment.

Some constraints will prove harder to remove than others.

Transmission rights-of-way, permitting, and local opposition may remain stubborn. Existing nuclear plants near large fiber networks may become unusually valuable. Certain regions may preserve advantages in electricity cost, tax policy, water availability, and network connectivity. Some equipment supply chains may remain concentrated enough to earn durable returns.

The chapter's countercase is therefore not that bottlenecks disappear.

It is that bottlenecks can be genuine and temporary at the same time.

This was true of compute in the previous chapter. It is even more important in the physical layer because the response takes longer and creates assets with much longer lives.

A GPU purchased during scarcity may become second-tier hardware within a few years. A transmission line built because of that scarcity may remain in service for decades. A substation can outlive several generations of processors. A water agreement, gas pipeline, or power plant can shape local economics long after the original model architecture has been forgotten.

That mismatch in asset life is where the physical AI trade becomes a financial problem.

The technology companies are making commitments across several clocks at once.

Models improve in months. Accelerators turn over in years. Data-center shells live longer. Transmission and generation can live for decades.

A coherent investment case has to make those clocks fit together.

If AI demand compounds for twenty years, today's grid investments may look prescient. If computation becomes dramatically more efficient, some planned generation may arrive into a softer market. If training concentrates in a handful of giant campuses while inference disperses, different assets will win. If regulations make large loads pay more of their connection costs, project economics will change. If utilities socialize too much of those costs, political resistance may become a constraint of its own.

There is no single physical AI trade.

There is a chain of assets whose values depend on one another.

The processor needs the rack. The rack needs cooling. The cooling needs electricity and often water. The electricity needs generation, transmission, transformation, and regulation. The building needs land, permits, financing, and customers. A failure or delay at one layer can strand capital at another.

That chain is why the physical bottlenecks deserve attention even from someone who never intends to buy a utility stock or a transformer manufacturer.

They determine how quickly AI capacity can actually arrive.

They also determine where profits can migrate while the buildout is underway.

The AI boom began in public imagination with models.

It is now visible in the less glamorous objects that make models possible: switchyards, cooling loops, transformer factories, generation queues, transmission plans, concrete pads, and parcels of land with enough power behind them.

The physical layer makes the boom more real.

It does not make the investment case simpler.

Every bottleneck has a price. Every high price invites a response. Every response takes time. And because the assets being built have different lives, the accounting can remain favorable long after the original assumptions have started to change—or deteriorate while the technology itself keeps improving.

The next step is to bring those physical commitments back into the financial statements.

A data center may stand for decades. The processors inside it may not. The depreciation schedule decides how quickly that difference reaches the income statement.

Utilization decides whether the assets earn their keep before then.

Pricing decides who captures the savings from better hardware.

The physical AI trade ends, eventually, in a spreadsheet.
