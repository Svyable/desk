# Chapter 8 — prediction-market self-trading enforcement

## Claim under test

Chapter 8 uses a political candidate who traded on his own candidacy as evidence that a regulated prediction market can detect and punish prohibited self-interested trading. The example is useful, but the manuscript should distinguish an exchange disciplinary action from broader proof that prediction markets reliably prevent insider trading or manipulation.

## Primary-source verification

On February 25, 2026, the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission's Division of Enforcement issued a prediction-markets advisory describing two public cases involving misuse of nonpublic information and fraud on KalshiEX, a CFTC-designated contract market. One case involved a political candidate who traded on his own candidacy in May 2025. The CFTC said Kalshi contacted the candidate the same day; the trader acknowledged that the trades were improper and violated exchange rules prohibiting trading in a contract whose outcome the trader can influence.

Source: U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission, “CFTC Enforcement Division Issues Prediction Markets Advisory,” February 25, 2026.
https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9185-26

The CFTC advisory reports a $2,246.36 financial penalty: $246.36 in disgorgement tied to the improper activity plus a $2,000 penalty, along with a five-year suspension from direct or indirect access to Kalshi.

Kalshi's own disciplinary notice identifies the trader as Kyle Langford, then a candidate for governor of California. It says he placed two trades on May 24, 2025 after he had been added as a market option for the 2026 California gubernatorial election, then promoted the trades on social media. The notice cites Kalshi Rule 5.17(z), which bars a trader from entering a market when the trader is a decision maker or otherwise has direct or indirect influence over the underlying event.

Source: Kalshi Exchange, “Notice of Disciplinary Action — Kyle Langford,” File No. KDA-2026-0002, effective February 25, 2026.
https://kalshi-public-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/regulatory/notices/Notice%20of%20Disciplinary%20Action%20%282.25.2026%29%20%282%29.pdf

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

This case should not be used to imply that regulated prediction markets make insider trading easy to eliminate. The conduct was conspicuous: the candidate traded on an event he directly influenced and then promoted the activity publicly. Detection in such a case says little about quieter misuse of private information, coordinated manipulation, beneficial ownership hidden behind intermediaries, or thin-market distortions that do not trigger obvious surveillance alerts.

It also matters that the sanction described here was imposed through Kalshi's exchange disciplinary process. The CFTC publicized the case and used it to remind exchanges of their monitoring and enforcement obligations, but the exchange penalty itself is not the same thing as a federal adjudication finding broader market abuse.

## Editorial implication

The existing Chapter 8 paragraph is factually sound. The strongest formulation is narrow: regulated markets can create auditable rules, surveillance, and sanctions that make some forms of self-interested trading easier to identify and punish. That is a governance advantage, not evidence that market integrity problems have been solved.
