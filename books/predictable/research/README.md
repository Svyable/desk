# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

Predictable asks why societies can be broadly right about a technological revolution and still be disastrously wrong about its financial value.

The strong version of the argument — that AI is simply a bubble and will crash — is too brittle to be useful. The version worth testing is more durable:

> Transformative technologies often attract capital faster than their economics can mature. Investors may correctly identify the technology that changes the world while mispricing the timetable, the amount of profitable capacity, and the companies that will capture the eventual returns.

AI is the contemporary test case. Prediction markets supply a second test: they make beliefs about future events observable as prices, but a price is still produced by incentives, liquidity, information, narrative, market structure, and human judgment. Better mechanisms for expressing forecasts do not automatically eliminate overconfidence.

The book should be able to survive either outcome. If AI investment earns extraordinary returns without a major bust, the evidence should force the argument to change. If a large capital destruction occurs, the book should explain the mechanism without pretending that the technological revolution was therefore false.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Technology versus security returns:** How often have technologies that transformed productivity produced poor returns for the investors who financed their first great buildout?
2. **Capital-cycle timing:** What happens when capacity, financing, and competition expand faster than end demand or margins?
3. **AI economics:** How rapidly are AI-related revenues, productivity gains, and willingness to pay growing relative to spending on chips, data centers, cloud infrastructure, and power?
4. **Physical constraints:** Which parts of the AI trade are limited by semiconductor supply, grid interconnection, generation, transformers, cooling, land, permitting, and construction lead times?
5. **Prediction-market accuracy:** Under what conditions do prediction markets outperform polls, experts, or simple base rates, and where do liquidity, contract design, resolution rules, or correlated beliefs weaken the signal?
6. **False precision:** When does translating uncertainty into a quoted probability improve reasoning, and when does it disguise fragile assumptions behind a precise number?
7. **Narrative transmission:** How do stories about inevitable dominance move from research labs and venture capital into earnings calls, public markets, social media, and prediction exchanges?
8. **After the bust:** When speculative capital is destroyed, which infrastructure, capabilities, standards, and surviving firms continue to compound value afterward?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should look for evidence against the central argument.

- A large AI buildout may be economically rational if demand, margins, and productivity gains rise fast enough to absorb it.
- High capital intensity does not by itself imply a bubble. Railways, power grids, telecom networks, and semiconductor fabs require enormous upfront spending even when they create lasting value.
- Valuation can remain elevated for long periods when winner-take-most economics are real rather than imagined.
- Prediction markets can be well calibrated in domains with liquid trading, clear resolution criteria, diverse information, and meaningful incentives.
- Public-market prices may incorporate bubble risk more efficiently than retrospective narratives admit.
- A technology boom can contain pockets of excess without producing a system-wide crash.
- A collapse in some securities does not prove that the underlying technology was overhyped; it may instead show that competition transferred value from producers to customers.

The manuscript should distinguish these possibilities rather than forcing every fact into a crash narrative.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Technology bubbles and overbuilding

Build comparative case studies around railways, canals where useful, the telegraph and telephone, electrification, radio, automobiles, fiber-optic networks, and the dot-com era. Separate technological adoption from investor returns. Pay attention to the assets that became socially valuable only after their original owners were wiped out or restructured.

### II. The capital cycle

Trace the recurring sequence of scarcity, high returns, financing, new entrants, capacity expansion, price competition, margin compression, consolidation, and eventual normalization. Test where AI infrastructure currently sits in that sequence rather than assuming the answer.

### III. The AI income statement

Track the relationship among hyperscaler capital expenditure, accelerator purchases, depreciation schedules, data-center commitments, AI-related revenue, gross margins, enterprise adoption, model prices, inference costs, and measured productivity. Preserve the distinction between announced capacity, committed spending, installed capacity, and utilized capacity.

### IV. The physical buildout

Follow the AI trade beyond software into semiconductors, advanced packaging, networking, substations, transformers, generation, cooling, water, land, construction, and financing. The key question is whether bottlenecks create durable scarcity rents or merely invite the next wave of overcapacity.

### V. Prediction markets

Study the intellectual lineage from forecasting tournaments and information aggregation to modern event-contract markets. Compare market probabilities with polls, expert forecasts, base rates, and realized outcomes. Treat contract wording, market depth, participant composition, fees, limits, manipulation incentives, and resolution procedures as part of the forecast itself.

### VI. Forecasting psychology and false precision

Use calibration research, base-rate neglect, overconfidence, motivated reasoning, herding, reflexivity, and narrative economics to examine why a probability can feel more objective than the reasoning beneath it deserves. The goal is not to argue against quantification; it is to distinguish measured uncertainty from decorated confidence.

### VII. Narrative contagion

Trace how a technological claim changes as it passes through research papers, product launches, venture financing, sell-side research, earnings calls, media, social platforms, and tradable markets. Record where conditional statements become inevitabilities and where time horizons silently shorten.

## Planned figures

1. **Real technology, bad investment** — selected historical technology buildouts plotted against investor returns and subsequent adoption.
2. **The capital-cycle clock** — investment, capacity, pricing, margins, and consolidation across one or more historical infrastructure booms.
3. **The AI buildout** — hyperscaler capital expenditure and data-center investment over time.
4. **Revenue versus infrastructure** — AI-linked revenue or monetization measures compared with capital committed to compute.
5. **The physical bottlenecks** — chips, power, grid connection, transformers, construction, and cooling lead times.
6. **The price of a prediction** — selected prediction-market probabilities against eventual outcomes and competing forecasts.
7. **Calibration, not confidence** — forecast probability buckets compared with realized frequencies.
8. **After the mania** — adoption and infrastructure growth continuing after the market value of the first-wave companies collapses or consolidates.

## Source discipline

- Prefer company filings, earnings transcripts, regulatory filings, exchange or platform data, government datasets, original academic papers, and contemporaneous historical records.
- Separate realized figures from guidance, announced projects, forecasts, scenarios, and market-implied probabilities.
- Record the vintage of every dataset and the exact resolution criteria of every prediction-market contract used as evidence.
- Do not treat market price as objective truth. Record liquidity, volume, participant restrictions, fees, and rule changes where they matter.
- Distinguish technological adoption, consumer surplus, corporate profit, and security returns; they are not interchangeable outcomes.
- Avoid selecting only bubbles that fit the thesis. Include important capital-intensive technologies whose early investors did well.
- Preserve uncertainty around AI revenue attribution and productivity measurement.
- Never quote a historical figure, executive, researcher, or trader without verifying the original source.
- For every major claim, keep a note titled “What would make this false?”
