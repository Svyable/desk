# Chapter 8 research note: Kalshi election-contract litigation

## Claim under review

Chapter 8 opens by compressing the 2024 arrival of federally regulated U.S. election contracts into October. The legal chronology is more specific and worth preserving because the distinction between a court victory, a temporary trading window, and a final merits ruling affects what the episode actually demonstrates.

## Primary source

**U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, _KalshiEX LLC v. Commodity Futures Trading Commission_, No. 24-5205, decided October 2, 2024.**

Official opinion: https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2024/10/24-5205-2077790.pdf

The opinion records that the district court vacated the CFTC's prohibition of Kalshi's Congressional Control Contracts. After the district court denied a stay pending appeal on September 12, 2024, Kalshi listed those contracts and they traded for roughly eight hours before the D.C. Circuit imposed an administrative stay while considering the CFTC's emergency motion. On October 2, the D.C. Circuit denied the CFTC's motion for a stay pending appeal and dissolved its administrative stay.

That chronology supports a narrower formulation than "election markets became legal in October." The October 2 order allowed trading to continue while the appeal proceeded; it did not finally decide every legal or policy question surrounding election contracts.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The appellate court did **not** hold that the CFTC's concerns about manipulation, election integrity, or foreign participation were baseless. It held that the agency had not shown the irreparable harm required for the extraordinary remedy of a stay. The opinion explicitly left the door open for the CFTC to renew its request if concrete evidence of harm emerged.

There is now concrete post-launch evidence that regulated prediction markets can face misconduct rather than merely hypothetical manipulation concerns. On February 25, 2026, the CFTC Division of Enforcement disclosed two Kalshi-related enforcement matters involving misuse of nonpublic information and fraud. In one, a political candidate traded on his own candidacy in May 2025; Kalshi's compliance team contacted him the same day, imposed disgorgement plus a $2,000 penalty, and suspended him for five years. The episode cuts both ways: it demonstrates that the conflict-of-interest risk is real, and that exchange surveillance and enforcement can detect and punish at least some misconduct.

**Commodity Futures Trading Commission, “CFTC Enforcement Division Issues Prediction Markets Advisory,” Release No. 9185-26, February 25, 2026.**

Official release: https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9185-26

That matters for the chapter's larger argument. Kalshi's 2024 launch is evidence that a regulated market can rapidly turn political expectations into tradable prices. It is not evidence that the market's existence settled the normative question of whether election contracts improve public decision-making, nor that regulatory concerns about manipulation were disproved. The later enforcement record suggests a better formulation: regulation can make misconduct more observable and punishable without making the underlying incentives disappear.

## Editorial implication

If Chapter 8's opening is revised later, preserve the narrative momentum but distinguish three events: the September 12 district-court trading window, the temporary appellate stay, and the October 2 order that allowed trading to resume while the appeal continued. Avoid describing the October order as a final judicial endorsement of election markets.

When revising the chapter's manipulation section, avoid either extreme. The 2026 enforcement evidence is stronger than a hypothetical rich-partisan thought experiment because it shows actual prohibited self-interested trading; the same case also supplies the counterevidence that a regulated venue can identify, penalize, and suspend a participant. The durable question is therefore not whether bad motives exist, but how reliably market design, surveillance, position limits, disclosure, and enforcement keep them from becoming durable price distortion.
