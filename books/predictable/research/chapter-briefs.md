# Chapter Briefs

These are research and argument briefs, not prose outlines. Each chapter should still discover its own narrative shape from the evidence available when it is written.

## Chapter 1 — The Price of Tomorrow

**Job:** Introduce the book through a live, tradable probability about AI or another consequential future event and show what changes when belief becomes a market price.

**Questions to answer:** What exactly does a quoted probability mean? Who can trade it? What are the resolution rules? How much liquidity is behind it? Why does a number feel more authoritative than a forecast stated in words?

**Evidence to seek:** First-party prediction-market rules and data; calibration or forecasting literature; a concrete contract with clear historical price movement; comparison with a poll, expert forecast, or base rate when appropriate.

**Counterevidence:** Prediction markets can aggregate dispersed information remarkably well under the right conditions. Do not begin by assuming that market probability is merely gambling or noise.

**Avoid:** A generic explainer of prediction markets. The chapter should make the reader feel the intellectual seduction of seeing the future quoted to the cent.

**Handoff:** End with the deeper distinction that drives the book: being right that a future arrives is different from pricing the path to it correctly.

## Chapter 2 — The Revolution Is Real

**Job:** Establish that the book is not an argument that AI is fake, trivial, or destined to fail.

**Questions to answer:** What forms of AI capability and adoption are already observable? Where is economic value real today? Why can a technology create large social or consumer value while investors in its providers earn poor returns?

**Evidence to seek:** Measured model capability where meaningful; enterprise adoption; usage and revenue disclosures; price/performance changes; historical examples of consumer surplus diverging from producer profit.

**Counterevidence:** Some AI firms or infrastructure owners may earn durable rents because of scale, scarce inputs, network effects, distribution, or proprietary data.

**Avoid:** Benchmark tourism and breathless model-release chronology. The point is the distinction between technological consequence and investment outcome.

**Handoff:** Once the revolution is granted, ask what history says about financing revolutions.

## Chapter 3 — Railroads, Radios, Routers

**Job:** Build the historical pattern carefully: technologies can transform society while first-wave capital is overbuilt, restructured, or destroyed.

**Questions to answer:** Which historical booms genuinely fit the pattern? Which do not? When did infrastructure become socially useful relative to when its original investors earned or lost money?

**Evidence to seek:** Railway mileage, financing, bankruptcies, and freight/passenger adoption; radio or early communications economics where evidence is strong; fiber-optic and dot-com capacity, telecom bankruptcies, bandwidth prices, and later internet use.

**Counterevidence:** Avoid cherry-picking only failed financiers. Include a case where early capital earned attractive returns or where apparent excess was rational under uncertainty.

**Avoid:** A montage of bubbles. Use two or three cases deeply enough to show mechanism, timing, and survivorship.

**Handoff:** Historical anecdotes become useful only when the recurring financial mechanism is made explicit.

## Chapter 4 — The Capital Cycle

**Job:** Explain the economic machinery that turns scarcity and high returns into investment, competition, capacity, falling prices, and compressed returns.

**Questions to answer:** What signals attract capital? Why does individually rational investment become collectively excessive? How do long lead times, leverage, fixed costs, and expectations worsen the cycle?

**Evidence to seek:** Capital-cycle literature; industry cases with measurable capacity, pricing, margins, and entry; financing conditions; evidence on consolidation after overbuilds.

**Counterevidence:** Capacity expansion is not automatically excess. Show how persistent demand growth or real scarcity can justify sustained investment.

**Avoid:** Treating the capital cycle as a universal law or timing tool.

**Handoff:** Apply the mechanism to the most visible scarce input in AI: compute.

## Chapter 5 — The Compute Gold Rush

**Job:** Quantify how much capital is pursuing frontier compute and what assumptions must hold for that buildout to earn acceptable returns.

**Questions to answer:** How fast are hyperscaler and data-center capital expenditures growing? What is being bought? What is the expected economic life of the assets? How quickly are model and inference prices changing?

**Evidence to seek:** Company filings and earnings calls; capex disclosures; accelerator and networking supply data; data-center project announcements separated from committed or completed capacity; cloud and model pricing histories.

**Counterevidence:** High capex can be rational when demand is compounding, assets are multipurpose, and scarcity rents are durable. Distinguish replacement capex from expansion capex when possible.

**Avoid:** Summing every announced project as though all will be built. Do not turn vendor revenue into proof of end-user economics.

**Handoff:** Compute is not a disembodied asset. It sits inside a physical system.

## Chapter 6 — The Physical AI Trade

**Job:** Move outside the chatbot window into chips, packaging, networking, substations, transformers, generation, cooling, water, land, construction, and permitting.

**Questions to answer:** Which constraints are real today? Which are temporary? Where can scarcity rents persist, and where does scarcity itself trigger overinvestment?

**Evidence to seek:** Semiconductor supply-chain disclosures; grid interconnection data; utility filings; transformer lead times; data-center power requirements; construction and permitting data; credible cooling/water evidence.

**Counterevidence:** Bottlenecks can be solved faster than expected through engineering, substitution, geographic relocation, efficiency, or policy changes.

**Avoid:** Presenting every bottleneck as permanent. A shortage is often the first act of a capital cycle, not its conclusion.

**Handoff:** Physical assets become financial assumptions through accounting and valuation.

## Chapter 7 — The Trillion-Dollar Spreadsheet

**Job:** Reconstruct the assumptions hidden inside the AI investment case without pretending there is one universal spreadsheet.

**Questions to answer:** What utilization, depreciation, pricing, demand growth, gross margin, and cost-of-capital assumptions are required? How sensitive are returns to shorter hardware lives or lower inference prices?

**Evidence to seek:** Depreciation policies in filings; useful-life changes; cloud gross margins where disclosed; utilization commentary; model/API pricing; analyst or company investment-return frameworks only when their assumptions can be inspected.

**Counterevidence:** Falling unit prices can stimulate enough demand to raise total revenue. Faster depreciation can coexist with high returns if assets generate cash quickly enough.

**Avoid:** False precision. Use scenarios to expose assumptions, not to manufacture a definitive fair value.

**Handoff:** The same problem appears in miniature when markets place a single number on uncertain future events.

## Chapter 8 — The Market for Tomorrow

**Job:** Give prediction markets their strongest case before examining their limits.

**Questions to answer:** When do markets aggregate information better than polls or experts? What role do incentives, participation, liquidity, arbitrage, and clear settlement play?

**Evidence to seek:** Forecasting and prediction-market studies; platform rules; historical markets with measurable calibration or comparative performance; examples where prices moved before conventional consensus.

**Counterevidence:** Markets can be thin, restricted, reflexive, manipulated, or badly specified. But do not smuggle Chapter 9's critique in too early.

**Avoid:** Treating all prediction platforms or contracts as equivalent.

**Handoff:** A functioning market can still produce a precise number whose meaning is more conditional than it looks.

## Chapter 9 — A Number Is Not Knowledge

**Job:** Examine false precision: when quantified uncertainty improves thinking and when it launders weak assumptions into a persuasive number.

**Questions to answer:** What does calibration require? How do contract wording and resolution criteria alter the object being predicted? How do base rates, correlated beliefs, and motivated reasoning survive quantification?

**Evidence to seek:** Calibration research; forecasting tournaments; platform disputes or edge cases with documented resolution issues; market microstructure and liquidity evidence; psychological work on overconfidence and base-rate neglect.

**Counterevidence:** Quantification often forces clarity. A numerical forecast can be more falsifiable and improvable than vague punditry.

**Avoid:** An anti-math argument. The target is decorative precision, not probability itself.

**Handoff:** Ask how the assumptions underneath numbers are formed socially before they become prices.

## Chapter 10 — Narrative Becomes Price

**Job:** Trace how conditional technical claims become institutional stories and eventually financial prices.

**Questions to answer:** How does a research claim change as it moves through product launches, venture decks, earnings calls, analyst models, media, social networks, and markets? Where do time horizons compress or caveats disappear?

**Evidence to seek:** A small number of traceable claim chains using original research, company communications, earnings transcripts, analyst/public commentary, and subsequent market reactions.

**Counterevidence:** Narratives can transmit genuine information and coordinate investment into productive new capacity. Not every shared story is a delusion.

**Avoid:** Vague claims about “hype.” Show the mutation of a specific idea through institutions.

**Handoff:** By this point the reader should be able to recognize the observable signs of excess without pretending to know the crash date.

## Chapter 11 — The Bubble Everyone Can See

**Job:** Build a diagnostic chapter rather than a crash forecast.

**Questions to answer:** Which indicators would distinguish healthy industrial expansion from capital-cycle excess? What would count as evidence that the thesis is wrong?

**Evidence to seek:** Capacity growth versus utilization; capex versus attributable revenue; pricing trends; financing terms; project cancellations; depreciation assumptions; return on invested capital; credit conditions; resale values; power and land commitments; prediction-market odds where relevant.

**Counterevidence:** Strong demand, rising utilization, stable pricing, disciplined financing, and improving returns could validate the buildout.

**Avoid:** A countdown clock. No single metric should be called “the” bubble indicator.

**Handoff:** The final question is not whether a boom ends, but what remains useful after capital is repriced.

## Chapter 12 — After the Mania

**Job:** Resolve the book by separating financial destruction from technological aftermath.

**Questions to answer:** What infrastructure, standards, skills, networks, and firms survived earlier busts? Who captured value after first-wave investors lost it? What would an analogous AI aftermath look like under several possible scenarios?

**Evidence to seek:** Post-bust adoption and price data from historical infrastructure cases; restructuring and consolidation histories; surviving assets and standards; current AI scenarios framed explicitly as scenarios rather than predictions.

**Counterevidence:** Some booms do not require a spectacular bust, and some early leaders remain leaders. The book should allow for gradual normalization as well as collapse.

**Avoid:** Declaring vindication. The conclusion should remain useful whether AI equities crash, plateau, or compound for years.

**Final note:** Better tools for forecasting do not abolish uncertainty. They can make assumptions visible, tradable, and contestable. The durable skill is learning to separate a true story about the future from a price that already assumes too much of it.
