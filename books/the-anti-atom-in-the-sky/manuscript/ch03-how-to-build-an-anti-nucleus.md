# 3 — How to Build an Anti-Nucleus

An antiproton can be born alone enough for our purposes. An antihelium nucleus cannot.

To get antihelium-3, a collision must create at least two antiprotons and an antineutron in the right neighborhood of phase space. For antihelium-4, add another antineutron. They must emerge close enough in momentum that the strong nuclear force can bind them before the expanding debris carries them apart. The production threshold is high. The combinatorics are cruel. The phase-space coincidence is worse.

Collider physicists know this because they make antinuclei. Heavy-ion collisions are especially prolific environments: dense, hot systems create many baryons and antibaryons. ALICE's observation of antihyperhelium shows how far the laboratory can push composite antimatter. But a collider is not the interstellar medium. A cosmic proton striking a hydrogen atom in diffuse gas is a different production environment from a lead-lead collision producing a quark-gluon plasma.

This distinction is the beginning of the coalescence problem, but before the model comes the physical picture. Imagine a hadronic collision producing a spray of particles. Somewhere in that spray are several antinucleons. If their relative momenta are too large, they separate. If their emission points are too distant, they never form a bound state. If their spin and quantum numbers do not cooperate, the nucleus does not appear. What the detector eventually calls antihelium is the surviving corner of a much larger many-body distribution.

The simplest phenomenological models encode this with a coalescence momentum: antinucleons within a sphere of relative momentum are allowed to bind. It is a useful approximation, but the parameter is not a universal constant of nature handed down on a stone tablet. It can depend on collision system, energy, hadronization environment and implementation. Quantum-mechanical Wigner-function treatments try to do better by combining phase-space distributions with a bound-state wavefunction. In 2026, comparative work using ALICE and ALEPH data argues that physically motivated coalescence prescriptions can describe multiple production environments more consistently than older ad hoc scaling.

That is progress. It is not permission to erase uncertainty.

The reason this matters for cosmic antihelium is that the difference between `impossible background` and `small but not absurd background` can be orders of magnitude. A few years of theoretical work have already shown how supposedly negligible channels can be resurrected. Anti-Lambda-b decays, for example, can create correlated antinucleons in a way that enhances antinucleus formation in some dark-matter scenarios. Strongly coupled dark sectors can produce high-multiplicity soft hadronic states that make coalescence more efficient. Fireball scenarios deliberately construct an environment where many antibaryons thermalize before expansion.

Each mechanism is a warning against slogan physics. `Antihelium is impossible astrophysically` is too strong. `Antihelium proves antimatter stars` is much too strong. The right statement is conditional: under standard secondary cosmic-ray production models, especially at low energies, antihelium is expected to be extremely rare; some exotic mechanisms can raise the flux, and some conventional uncertainties remain in coalescence and production cross sections.

There is a deeper point. Composite particles are not merely heavier versions of elementary ones. They remember something about the local phase-space density at birth. An antihelium nucleus is therefore a kind of fossil of hadronization. Its abundance relative to antiprotons and antideuterons encodes whether its parent process was sparse, jet-like, thermal, high multiplicity, heavy-flavor-rich, or something stranger.

That is why isotope ratios matter. Antihelium-4 is particularly hard to produce by ordinary secondary collisions. A verified population with an unexpectedly large antihelium-4 fraction would be much more disruptive than a few antihelium-3 events at energies where coalescence uncertainties are broad. The reported candidate ratio has inspired theory precisely because it looks difficult under ordinary expectations—but the ratio itself inherits all the uncertainty of candidate classification.

The nucleus is the message only after the track is real.
