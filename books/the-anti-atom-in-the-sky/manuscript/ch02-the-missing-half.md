# 2 — The Missing Half

The easiest version of the matter-antimatter problem is the one taught with a cartoon: the Big Bang makes matter and antimatter, they annihilate, yet we are here. Somewhere, the cartoon says, nature cheated in favor of matter.

The real problem is less cinematic and more embarrassing. The Standard Model contains CP violation. We have measured it in mesons, and in 2025 LHCb reported observation of CP violation in baryon decays. That is an important extension of where the asymmetry is experimentally visible. It is not, by itself, an explanation of the cosmic baryon asymmetry. The known Standard Model sources are insufficient to generate the observed imbalance under conventional cosmological assumptions.

CERN describes the leftover asymmetry with a memorable order of magnitude: roughly one extra matter particle per billion matter-antimatter pairs. After the pairs annihilated, the tiny excess remained. That residue is us.

The phrase `the universe is made of matter` is nevertheless more empirical than metaphysical. We infer it from the absence of annihilation signatures, from Big Bang nucleosynthesis, the cosmic microwave background, gamma-ray observations, cosmic rays, and the fact that nearby stars, gas, planets and dust behave as matter. Large adjacent domains of matter and antimatter would not politely ignore their boundaries. Gas mixing across interfaces would annihilate and radiate. The sky is not glowing with the required signal.

This does not make every conceivable pocket of primordial antimatter impossible. Scale, distribution and environment matter. Compact antimatter objects in sparse regions can evade constraints that exclude vast matter-antimatter domains. An antistar in the Galactic halo would not sit behind a bright neon sign reading `wrong baryon number`. Its surface interaction with ordinary interstellar gas could produce gamma rays, but the flux would depend on accretion rate, velocity, local density and stellar properties.

That is why antihelium is so interesting. It can turn an otherwise indirect question into a transported sample. A charged anti-nucleus carries its baryon sign to the detector. It has been scrambled by magnetic fields, so reconstructing its birthplace is difficult, but its existence is not a spectral inference in the same way a gamma-ray annihilation feature is.

Still, one must resist a common leap: antihelium does not equal primordial antimatter. High-energy physics produces antimatter routinely. ALICE has created heavy antinuclei and antihypernuclei in collisions. Cosmic-ray interactions can do smaller versions of the same thing. Dark-matter annihilation or decay, if it exists, could produce hadronic showers containing antinucleons. Exotic astrophysical or beyond-Standard-Model events could create dense antibaryonic fireballs. The origin question begins after detection, not before it.

The book's governing tension is therefore asymmetrical. A robust non-detection of antihelium improves constraints but leaves many baryogenesis models alive. A robust detection at a surprisingly high flux could destroy a much larger region of complacency. The theoretical consequence is not automatically antistars. It is that at least one of our priors about production, propagation, detector backgrounds, dark sectors, or cosmic antimatter inventory was wrong.

That is a good kind of experiment. It does not need a preferred miracle. It needs hypotheses that are forced to pay different bills.

The Standard Model can create antihelium in energetic collisions. Let it calculate the flux. A dark-matter model can boost antinucleus production. Let it pay the antiproton and gamma-ray constraints. An antistar model can inject antihelium through ordinary stellar processes or explosive events. Let it pay the Fermi-LAT annihilation limits and Galactic population constraints. A primordial antimatter-domain model can preserve pockets from the early universe. Let it pay the cosmological boundary conditions.

A detector artefact model has a bill too. It must reproduce the candidate distributions across rigidity, velocity, charge, tracker quality and isotope mass, and then explain why independent classifiers select the same rare events.

Physics becomes interesting when all explanations are made expensive.
