# 15 — Antihelium-4 Is the Hard One

Antihelium-3 is strange. Antihelium-4 is rude.

Adding one antineutron sounds incremental. In production probability it can be catastrophic. Four antinucleons must emerge with the right correlations to form a tightly bound alpha-particle analogue. Standard secondary cosmic-ray production generally predicts a steep suppression with mass number. That is why publicly discussed AMS events compatible with antihelium-4 attracted such disproportionate attention.

The isotope assignment therefore deserves almost obsessive scrutiny.

AMS infers mass from rigidity and velocity. Near the boundary between isotopes, detector resolution matters. A small number of events can move the apparent antihelium-4 fraction substantially. If one of three putative antihelium-4 events is reclassified, the ratio changes dramatically. Theoretical papers that reproduce a reported 2:1 or 3:1 antihelium-3 to antihelium-4 candidate ratio are solving a target that may itself still move.

Yet the target is physically meaningful. If a collaboration-level analysis eventually confirms several antihelium-4 nuclei at a flux comparable within an order of magnitude to antihelium-3, ordinary secondary coalescence would face a severe challenge. Dense-source mechanisms would become much more compelling. Antistellar nucleosynthesis could also produce antihelium-4 naturally because ordinary stars overwhelmingly make helium-4; an antistar's winds would carry the anti-isotope.

That contrast is potentially extraordinary. A high antihelium-4 fraction might favor an astrophysical reservoir of bulk antimatter over sparse collision production. But propagation, source acceleration and injection physics complicate the comparison. Antihelium from antistars would need to be accelerated into the cosmic-ray population. Stellar winds alone have characteristic energies far below the relativistic candidates discussed from AMS unless shocks or explosive processes intervene.

So even the apparently cleanest signature has a chain.

There is also a laboratory opportunity. Collider measurements of antihelium-4 production, including in heavy-ion systems, constrain coalescence under dense conditions. Better event-generator modeling can test whether rare heavy-flavor topologies produce more anti-alpha particles than previously expected. The antihelium-4 background may be tiny, but the uncertainty on a tiny number can still matter if the observed sample is tiny too.

This chapter therefore refuses the phrase `smoking gun` unless the speaker names what is smoking. Antihelium-4 could be a smoking gun for *an unexpected source of composite antimatter*. It would not by itself distinguish dark matter, antistars, primordial pockets or exotic fireballs.

The fourth antinucleon raises the stakes. It does not finish the inference.
