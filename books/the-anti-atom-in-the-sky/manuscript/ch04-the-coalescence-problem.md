# 4 — The Coalescence Problem

Every frontier anomaly eventually finds the denominator it deserves.

For antihelium, one denominator is the expected secondary flux. That number sounds like a prediction. In practice it is a stack: primary cosmic-ray spectra, interstellar gas composition, nuclear collision cross sections, antinucleon production, antinucleus coalescence, Galactic diffusion, reacceleration, convection, annihilation losses, heliospheric modulation and detector exposure. Some layers are tightly measured. Others are models stitched to sparse accelerator data.

This does not make the prediction arbitrary. It makes it auditable.

The coalescence layer has historically carried an awkward amount of leverage. Early calculations could differ sharply because they used different prescriptions for deciding when antiprotons and antineutrons bind. The underlying issue is intuitive. A deuteron is a large, weakly bound object by nuclear standards. Treating its formation as a hard sphere in momentum space is expedient, but real production depends on both momentum and spatial correlations. Antihelium adds another body and magnifies the sensitivity.

Collider programs have therefore become part of astroparticle physics. ALICE measurements of antideuterons and antihelium calibrate how often nuclei emerge from high-energy collisions. ALEPH data from hadronic Z decays provide a very different environment. A 2026 Physical Review D analysis tested coalescence prescriptions across these regimes and found that a common physically motivated treatment can reproduce both within uncertainties. That is exactly the kind of work the field needs because a cosmic anomaly should not be compared against a background model whose most important parameter is retuned whenever it is challenged.

But collider universality is not guaranteed. A model that works in proton-proton and Z decays may still misdescribe nuclear-target effects in cosmic-ray collisions on interstellar hydrogen and helium. The energy range differs. The angular and multiplicity distributions differ. Heavy-flavor contributions can differ. Propagation then smears the production spectrum before it reaches Earth.

The clean way through is not to choose one coalescence model and declare victory. It is to propagate the model family into observable predictions and ask which measurements collapse the uncertainty fastest.

AMBER at CERN is relevant here for a seemingly humbler reason: it aims to measure antiproton production in proton-hydrogen and proton-helium collisions with high precision. Better antiproton source terms improve the transport model against which antinucleus excesses are judged. Antideuteron measurements constrain the next coalescence step. Antihelium then sits at the top of a ladder rather than floating as an isolated wonder.

This hierarchy offers an important diagnostic. If a new source makes antihelium, it should usually make antiprotons and antideuterons too. Not always in the same ratios—indeed, exotic hadronization can change the ratios dramatically—but the multi-species spectrum becomes a fingerprint. Any model proposed to explain antihelium candidates must survive the precision antiproton data already collected by AMS and the low-energy antideuteron search now being performed by GAPS.

The same logic kills one of the laziest forms of dark-matter storytelling. A model cannot be rewarded for matching a handful of rare antihelium events if it overshoots antiprotons by an order of magnitude. Conversely, a mechanism that naturally increases composite antinuclei relative to single antiprotons deserves attention even if it sounds baroque, because the rarity structure is exactly what it is trying to explain.

This is what `background` should mean in modern physics: not a sacred curve, but a constrained generative model whose weak joints are identified before an anomaly appears.

If antihelium is eventually discovered, the coalescence problem will not have been an obstacle on the way to the discovery. It will have been half of the discovery.
