# 9 — The Balloon Over Antarctica

On December 16, 2025, GAPS left Antarctica under a NASA scientific balloon. It rose to roughly thirty-five kilometers and spent twenty-five days circling the continent twice. On January 10, 2026, the flight ended and the instrument came down on the Ross Ice Shelf about a hundred kilometers southeast of McMurdo.

The timing could hardly be better.

GAPS exists to hunt low-energy cosmic antinuclei, especially antideuterons, using a method that looks almost perversely different from AMS. There is no large magnetic spectrometer deciding the sign of curvature. A slow antiparticle enters the detector and stops in silicon. It replaces an electron in an atom, forming an exotic atom. The exotic atom de-excites, emitting characteristic X-rays, and then the antiparticle annihilates with the nucleus, producing a star of secondary particles. Time-of-flight information measures velocity and helps reconstruct the event.

The signature is therefore compound: stopping depth, timing, ionization, X-ray energies and annihilation topology. Different antiparticles produce different exotic-atom cascades and annihilation products.

Orthogonality is the point. If AMS's limiting background is charge-sign confusion in a magnetic tracker, GAPS does not share that failure mode. GAPS has other backgrounds and other systematics—atmospheric secondaries, detector response, exotic-atom modeling, reconstruction of annihilation products—but an independent signal would not be a duplicate vote from the same measurement architecture.

As of September 2026, the first flight has completed and data were acquired. That is a documented fact. A published cosmic antinucleus result from the flight is not yet something this book can claim. The proper tense is prospective.

The low-energy range is particularly valuable because secondary astrophysical backgrounds are expected to be suppressed there for antideuterons. Dark-matter models can produce relatively enhanced low-energy fluxes after Galactic propagation. Antihelium is rarer, but the same logic applies: the energy spectrum matters as much as the particle label.

There is a beautiful experimental symmetry here. AMS has enormous exposure time and a magnet in orbit. GAPS has shorter balloon flights but a large acceptance optimized for slow antiparticles and a qualitatively different identification mechanism. If both instruments eventually report compatible antinucleus fluxes, detector-specific explanations collapse quickly. If AMS candidates persist while GAPS sees nothing in an overlapping expected region, source spectra and acceptance will have to be examined before declaring contradiction, but the model space will tighten.

This is how anomaly science should mature: not by asking the first instrument to become infinitely persuasive, but by designing a second instrument that can be wrong differently.

The first GAPS flight also changes the sociology of the antihelium story. For years, theoretical papers could only wait for AMS to publish. Now there is another data stream. The field may soon have antiproton and antideuteron measurements at energies AMS does not dominate, plus potential antihelium sensitivity. A multi-species, multi-instrument picture can replace candidate mythology.

A balloon above Antarctica sounds modest next to a space station. In epistemology, it may be the heavier instrument.
