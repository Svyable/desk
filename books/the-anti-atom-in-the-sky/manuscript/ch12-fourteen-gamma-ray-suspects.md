# 12 — Fourteen Gamma-Ray Suspects

In 2021, researchers used ten years of Fermi Large Area Telescope data to ask a gloriously literal question: if antistars are moving through ordinary interstellar gas, where is the annihilation light?

They searched the gamma-ray source catalog for objects with spectra compatible with proton-antiproton annihilation and no association with known gamma-ray source classes. Fourteen candidates survived their selection.

Fourteen antistar candidates is not fourteen antistars.

The paper's more durable result was a constraint. Under an assumption that antistars are distributed like ordinary disk stars, the analysis placed an upper limit of roughly one antistar per 300,000 normal stars in the solar neighborhood. Halo populations could be less constrained because of lower ambient gas densities and different spatial distributions.

This is a perfect companion to the AMS story because the instruments make opposite mistakes.

AMS can, in principle, identify the baryon sign of an incoming nucleus directly. It largely loses the source direction because charged cosmic rays wander through Galactic magnetic fields. Fermi sees photons that point back toward sources, but a gamma-ray spectrum compatible with annihilation is not unique. Pulsars and other high-energy sources can occupy confusing regions of spectral space.

Combine them and the hypothesis becomes testable in a richer way. An antistar population large enough to supply a measured antihelium flux should not be allowed to hide arbitrarily from gamma-ray constraints. Population synthesis can connect stellar distribution, gas density, accretion physics and antihelium ejection. Conversely, a genuine annihilation-like Fermi source with unusual stellar properties could motivate targeted multiwavelength observations.

The strongest version would add proper motion, optical spectra, X-rays and local gas maps. An isolated star whose ordinary-looking optical spectrum sits on top of a persistent annihilation-like gamma-ray source would be interesting. Still not proof. A compact object, pulsar or unrelated coincidence could mimic the geometry.

The fourteen candidates therefore belong in the disputed/incomplete category, not because the paper is weak but because candidate classification is the paper's starting point. The authors themselves treated them as unassociated sources with compatible spectra, not confirmed antimatter stars.

This is another recurring lesson in anomaly science: candidate lists are instruments for follow-up, not baskets of discoveries.

If the AMS antihelium candidates vanish, the Fermi antistar search remains valuable as an independent population constraint. If AMS confirms antihelium, the fourteen sources become more interesting overnight—but they do not retroactively become antistars. Their posterior probability changes because the prior on Galactic antimatter changes.

That is Bayesian language for a simple idea: one strange measurement should make us look harder at another without allowing either to borrow certainty it has not earned.
