# 16 — Antimatter Falls Down

For decades, antimatter attracted one of physics's favorite loophole fantasies: perhaps it falls up.

In 2023 ALPHA-g directly observed the effect of Earth's gravity on trapped antihydrogen. The result was consistent with ordinary downward gravitational acceleration and excluded the cartoon version of repulsive antigravity. The measured acceleration had large uncertainty compared with precision equivalence-principle tests on matter, but the sign question was answered directly.

Why include this in a book about cosmic antihelium?

Because antimatter research has a long history of allowing the unknown to become a storage locker for unrelated hopes. Antigravity, free energy, cosmic antimatter domains and baryogenesis are often rhetorically bundled because they share the word `antimatter`. They are experimentally different questions.

ALPHA-g is a model of how to collapse one of those questions. Build neutral antihydrogen, trap it, release it in a vertical apparatus, reconstruct where annihilations occur, compare with simulations under different gravitational accelerations. The result does not solve baryogenesis. It does not tell us whether antistars exist. It removes a large class of naive gravitational speculation.

CERN's antimatter program has similarly pushed CPT comparisons of hydrogen and antihydrogen, proton and antiproton charge-to-mass ratios, magnetic moments and spectroscopy. So far the broad story is symmetry: matter and antimatter obey the same local physics to extraordinary precision, aside from the CP-violating processes already embedded in particle interactions.

This makes the cosmic asymmetry more, not less, interesting. The missing antimatter is not easily explained by antimatter having bizarre everyday properties. If an antistar existed, it could be spectroscopically almost indistinguishable from a star until matter accretion or ejected antinuclei revealed its sign.

The lesson for antihelium is methodological. Do not ask a rare cosmic-ray event to solve every antimatter mystery. First establish the particle. Then establish the flux. Then distinguish production mechanisms. Then ask what those mechanisms imply for baryogenesis.

One clean experiment can make a hundred pages of speculation obsolete. ALPHA-g did that to antigravity. GAPS and a full AMS antihelium analysis may soon do something similar to several cosmic-matter stories.

That is progress even when the romantic answer dies.
