# 14 — Fireballs and Other Escapes

When an anomaly is hard to produce, theorists have two options: reduce the anomaly or increase the density of the thing being produced.

The fireball proposal chooses density.

A 2024 Physical Review D paper considered catastrophic injections of large quantities of energetic antiquarks in the Galaxy. The antiquarks thermalize into a rapidly expanding plasma rich in antibaryons. In that temporary furnace, antinucleosynthesis can proceed far more efficiently than in an ordinary sparse cosmic-ray collision. The authors showed that such fireballs could, for some parameters, produce both antihelium-3 and antihelium-4 with ratios resembling those tentatively discussed from AMS, while respecting certain antiproton and antideuteron constraints.

The mechanism is deliberately exotic. That is not a flaw if the data eventually demand exotic production. The flaw would be treating the existence of a mathematically viable source as evidence that nature uses it.

What creates the fireball? The paper considers beyond-Standard-Model possibilities capable of injecting the required antiquarks. Those parent mechanisms would need their own evidence. One can imagine dark-sector events, decays or collisions that are rare locally but energetic enough to create dense antimatter plasmas.

The idea is useful because it reveals what the isotope ratio is really measuring: not just total antibaryon production, but density and correlation at hadronization. Antihelium-4 requires four antinucleons to find one another. A dense thermal source can radically alter that probability.

There are other escape routes. Primordial black holes evaporating by Hawking radiation can inject particles that ultimately form antinuclei, although antiproton constraints heavily limit the available parameter space. Strongly coupled hidden sectors produce soft unclustered energy patterns with large multiplicities. Heavy baryon decays provide correlated antinucleons. Each proposal attacks a different bottleneck.

A good experimental paper should eventually make these models uncomfortable by publishing the full energy and isotope information. A good theory paper should publish not merely the antihelium curve but its correlated predictions for antiprotons, antideuterons, gamma rays and perhaps neutrinos.

The danger is epicycles. If every new limit can be escaped by adding another dark particle, another mediator and another tuned branching fraction, the model has stopped predicting. The antihelium field should therefore value minimal discriminants over maximal parameter spaces.

What would make a fireball-like source genuinely persuasive? A spectrum or isotope composition that standard secondary coalescence cannot reproduce but dense antinucleosynthesis predicts naturally; correlated rare antideuteron behavior; perhaps temporal or spatial clustering if sources are catastrophic and nearby; and no violation of precision antiproton data.

This is a harder standard than `can produce ten events`. It is also more interesting.

An anomaly earns new physics when the new physics knows something the anomaly did not tell it.
