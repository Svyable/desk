# 8 — Twelve Is Not a Discovery

There is a useful brutality to the number twelve.

Twelve antihelium candidates sounds like twelve pieces of antimatter carried across the Galaxy. In a newspaper headline it is nearly a discovery already. In the 2026 machine-learning thesis, twelve is a maximum surviving count under certain classifier working points. The corresponding excess over the simulated background is only mild.

This difference is the whole chapter.

Particle physics inherited five sigma as a cultural threshold for discovery partly because large datasets generate seductive fluctuations. The threshold is not sacred, and it does not repair bad systematics. But it captures a real asymmetry: when the consequence of a claim is enormous, a bump that can plausibly occur by chance should not be promoted by narrative force.

The antihelium case adds another layer because the uncertainty is not only Poisson counting. The background itself is inferred. If charge-confused helium in the signal mass region cannot be isolated in a direct sideband, Monte Carlo modeling becomes central. A significance of one to two sigma therefore lives on top of assumptions about how well simulation reproduces rare reconstruction failures.

This is why exclusion limits can be more honest than a best-fit signal. The thesis reports CLs limits on antihelium-to-helium ratios rather than claiming observation. Limits are not glamorous, but they preserve information: here is how much flux can be ruled out under stated assumptions, even though a small cluster of candidates remains.

There is also a look-elsewhere issue in spirit, even when not expressed as a formal trials factor. Multiple neural networks, working points, isotope windows and quality selections can create many views of the same sparse sample. If the most exciting cut is emphasized after looking at the data, nominal significance can overstate surprise. A robust analysis should freeze selections on simulation or control data, then reveal the blinded signal region.

The same concern applies to theorists. Ten candidate events can generate ten papers. Each paper may be mathematically impeccable, but the literature count is not independent evidence for the experimental signal. All ten theories can be conditioned on the same uncertain handful of tracks.

This distinction sounds obvious until citation networks blur it. A later review cites several peer-reviewed papers discussing `the AMS antihelium events`. A newcomer sees a literature and assumes there is a published event sample. There may not be. The epistemic provenance has to remain visible.

One could overcorrect and refuse to discuss the candidates until collaboration publication. That would also be a mistake. Frontier theory often advances by exploring implications of provisional results. The proper contract is conditional language plus explicit provenance.

`If the reported candidate antihelium-4 events are confirmed, mechanism X would...`

That sentence is scientifically useful.

`AMS has detected antihelium-4, therefore mechanism X...`

That sentence spends evidence the field does not yet possess.

The 2026 analysis improves the situation because it gives the public a more explicit statistical frame. Its modest significance is not disappointing. It is clarifying. The universe has not yet paid the extraordinary claim. The detector has handed us a set of events and a difficult background problem.

That is enough to design the next experiment.
