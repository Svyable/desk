# 7 — The Candidates

The antihelium story entered physics sideways.

There was no collaboration paper titled `Discovery of Cosmic Antihelium`. Instead there were conference talks, plots, references in theoretical papers and progressively more detailed public discussion. By 2024, a Physical Review D paper on fireball antinucleosynthesis summarized publicly available information for roughly ten candidate events and noted that detailed basic parameters had been shown for only a subset.

This is an odd evidentiary architecture. The candidate events are influential enough to motivate serious peer-reviewed theory, but the canonical experimental analysis remains unpublished. Theoretical papers therefore cite conference materials and reconstruct event properties from slides. That is not how anyone would design scientific communication from scratch.

It does, however, create a useful test of discipline. A candidate can be scientifically important before it is confirmed. The right verb is `motivate`, not `demonstrate`.

Several features drew attention. Publicly discussed candidates appeared compatible with both antihelium-3 and antihelium-4. The possible antihelium-4 events were especially provocative because conventional secondary production falls rapidly with nuclear mass. Some presentations suggested an unexpectedly high antihelium-4 to antihelium-3 ratio relative to naive production expectations. Theorists responded by constructing mechanisms that enhance high-multiplicity antinucleus formation.

Notice the direction of inference. The events did not prove those mechanisms. The events, if real, created a target that ordinary models struggled to hit. That is exactly what anomalies are for.

But a target inferred from an unstable candidate list is dangerous. If later reconstruction moves an event from antihelium-4 to antihelium-3, a model tuned to the isotope ratio can lose its motivation. If charge confusion accounts for half the sample, the inferred flux changes. If acceptance estimates shift, the energy spectrum changes. Theoretical creativity can outrun the experimental object it is trying to explain.

The 2026 thesis is therefore more consequential than its modest significance might suggest. It is a bridge from public candidate lore toward a reproducible analysis framework. It does not validate all earlier public event claims one-to-one. Instead it applies explicit DNN-based selections and reports the surviving counts and limits. Up to twelve candidates can appear under some working points, but the background-only tension remains in the 1–2 sigma range.

That number is the antidote to mythology.

A one-to-two-sigma excess is ordinary in a field with many analyses. It can disappear with more data, better simulation, or revised selection. Yet the events may still be physically real antihelium; low significance is not proof of background. The proper state is suspension with pressure toward independent discrimination.

The strongest next evidence will not be another slide with another candidate count. It will be one of four things: a full AMS collaboration paper with event-level methodology; a statistically growing AMS excess whose significance scales as expected with exposure; a distinctive spectral or isotope pattern difficult for charge confusion to mimic; or a compatible signal from GAPS or another independent architecture.

Candidate events are not scientific embarrassment. They are what detectors produce before nature has agreed to be generous.
