# 10 — Antideuterium First

The antihelium question may be answered first by a particle with only two antinucleons.

Antideuterium—or more commonly antideuteron when referring to the bare nucleus—is the intermediate rung between antiprotons and antihelium. Its production is rare enough that low-energy secondary backgrounds are strongly suppressed, but common enough that several dark-matter scenarios predict detectable fluxes. GAPS was designed around this window.

That makes antideuterons a calibration of the entire exotic-source story.

Suppose GAPS observes a convincing low-energy antideuteron population above secondary expectations. The result would not identify dark matter automatically. Primordial black-hole evaporation, exotic astrophysical sources and uncertainties in production could enter. But any antihelium model would suddenly face a measured neighboring species. Coalescence parameters could no longer wander freely.

Suppose GAPS instead places strong limits with no antideuterons. Some dark-matter explanations for antihelium candidates would become strained because they predict both. Other models might survive precisely because they enhance heavier antinuclei through unusual high-multiplicity dynamics. The null would therefore be discriminatory, not merely disappointing.

This is why the antinucleus ladder should be analyzed jointly. Antiprotons are abundant enough for precision spectra. Antideuterons test two-body coalescence and exotic low-energy components. Antihelium-3 adds a third antinucleon. Antihelium-4 is the severe test. A source model produces a vector of fluxes, not a single miracle.

The latest theory is moving in this direction. A September 3, 2026 accepted Physical Review D paper revisits galactic dark-matter predictions for antiprotons, antideuterons and antihelium together, explicitly motivated by precision AMS antiproton data and GAPS's completed first Antarctic flight. That date matters. This book is being written while the denominator is still changing.

There is a temptation to regard theoretical uncertainty as a reason to wait. The opposite is often true. When uncertainty is dominated by a few identifiable processes, new measurements can be targeted directly at them. Collider coalescence studies constrain formation. AMBER constrains antiproton production. GAPS constrains low-energy antinuclei. AMS constrains high-statistics cosmic-ray propagation and rare candidates.

A good anomaly program does not ask one dataset to bear all explanatory weight. It builds a network whose edges are physical dependencies.

Antideuterium is therefore not a side chapter in a book about antihelium. It is the control experiment nature has conveniently made one nucleon cheaper.
