# 13 — Dark Matter Learns Nuclear Chemistry

Dark matter is often asked to explain whatever particle excess happens to be fashionable. The result is a graveyard of models that fit one spectrum and die against another.

Antihelium makes this harder, which is good.

A dark-matter particle that annihilates or decays into quarks can produce hadronic showers. Those showers contain antiprotons and antineutrons. If enough emerge nearby in phase space, they coalesce into antideuterons and antihelium. The process is not conceptually mysterious. The quantitative rate is the problem.

Precision antiproton measurements already constrain how much hadronic dark-matter activity can be hiding in the Galaxy. A model that simply raises the entire antibaryon yield until antihelium becomes detectable usually produces too many antiprotons. The interesting models are therefore those that alter the *composition* of the antimatter debris.

One example uses heavy-flavor decays. A 2021 Physical Review Letters paper showed that anti-Lambda-b baryons can decay in topologies where antinucleons are unusually correlated, enhancing antihelium formation relative to older estimates. The effect can raise predicted dark-matter-induced antihelium rates substantially in appropriate models.

Another route is a strongly coupled dark sector. If dark-matter annihilation produces many soft dark mesons that decay into Standard Model quarks, the resulting event can have enormous hadronic multiplicity. More antinucleons in a dense phase-space cloud make coalescence easier. A 2023 Physical Review D paper found scenarios with dramatic antihelium enhancement, including antihelium-4.

These papers are sometimes described as ways to `explain the AMS events`. Better language is that they show the candidate events are not uniquely diagnostic of primordial antimatter. Particle physics can manufacture composite antimatter more efficiently than old back-of-envelope expectations suggested.

The models pay elsewhere. Dark-sector masses and annihilation rates are constrained by gamma rays, the cosmic microwave background, antiprotons and collider searches. Heavy-flavor hadronization uncertainties enter. Coalescence parameters enter. Galactic propagation enters. A viable model must thread all of these.

That is what makes antihelium so valuable. It drags dark-matter phenomenology into nuclear chemistry. The signal is not a featureless energy deposit. It is a bound state whose formation remembers the microphysics of the shower.

There is a subtle inversion here. Usually composite structure is nuisance detail in searches for fundamental particles. Here the composite structure may be the amplifier. A dark sector that produces many soft hadrons can be nearly invisible in one messenger while spectacular in a rare antinucleus channel.

This is the kind of neglected collision that deserves a book: cosmology asks where the dark mass is; collider physics asks how quarks hadronize; nuclear physics asks when nucleons bind; cosmic-ray physics asks how the composite survives propagation. Antihelium forces all four questions into one event.

If the candidates disappear, these theoretical connections remain useful because GAPS and future instruments still search the same parameter space. If the candidates strengthen, the models will have to stop being imaginative explanations and become predictive ones.
