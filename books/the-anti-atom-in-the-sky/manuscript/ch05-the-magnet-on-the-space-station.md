# 5 — The Magnet on the Space Station

AMS-02 is an improbable place to conduct cosmology. It is an eight-and-a-half-ton particle detector bolted to the International Space Station, exposed to vacuum, radiation, thermal cycling and the indignities of a platform never designed as a quiet underground laboratory. It has been operating since May 2011. By September 10, 2026, its public counter had passed roughly 268 billion cosmic-ray events.

The instrument turns the orbital environment into a spectrometer. A permanent magnet bends charged particles. Silicon tracker layers measure trajectories. Time-of-flight scintillators provide timing and charge information. A transition-radiation detector helps separate leptons from hadrons. A ring-imaging Cherenkov detector measures velocity and charge. An electromagnetic calorimeter characterizes showers. None of these subsystems alone says `antihelium`. The identification emerges from consistency among them.

Rigidity is momentum divided by charge. The magnetic field supplies the sign. Positive helium curves one way; negative antihelium curves the other. Mass comes from combining rigidity with velocity. Charge magnitude comes from ionization and other detector responses. A clean candidate should therefore occupy a very constrained region of detector space: |Z|=2, negative rigidity, a mass compatible with an antihelium isotope, sensible velocity, and a track topology inconsistent with interaction or reconstruction failure.

The engineering achievement can obscure the statistical problem. Helium is abundant. Antihelium, if present, is fantastically rare. A tiny fraction of misreconstructed helium can swamp the signal region. This is the generic rare-event curse: the detector may be 99.9999 percent correct and still fail if the signal fraction is one part in a billion.

AMS has spent years publishing precision spectra for ordinary cosmic-ray species. That body of work matters to the antihelium search because it calibrates the instrument in the regime where statistics are enormous. The collaboration understands rigidity resolution, charge measurement, acceptance, unfolding and many detector systematics through repeated cross-checks on protons, helium, carbon, oxygen, electrons, positrons and antiprotons.

But rare tails are their own country. A distribution that is beautifully modeled over six orders of magnitude can still hide a pathological tail produced by tracker scattering, secondary interactions, alignment errors or reconstruction ambiguities. Antihelium is not found by extending a Gaussian one more decimal place and trusting it.

That explains some of the long silence around the candidates. Outsiders can interpret delay as secrecy, embarrassment or institutional caution. Any of those stories is easier to tell than the boring technical possibility: a collaboration confronted with a handful of events whose background lives in a difficult tail may need years to build a result it is willing to publish.

There is another institutional complication. Public talks by collaboration leaders can precede journal papers. Theoretical physicists then cite the talks because the possible implication is enormous. Those papers become part of the scientific literature even though the underlying candidate sample is not fully public. The anomaly acquires an ecosystem before it acquires a canonical dataset.

That is not misconduct. It is a fragile information state.

The correct response is neither to pretend the candidates do not exist nor to promote them into detections. They belong in the second evidence class: credible but incomplete. The instrument is real, the presentations are real, the candidate discussions are real. The missing piece is a peer-reviewed collaboration analysis with event-level selection, background model, efficiencies, systematic uncertainties and statistical interpretation sufficient for independent scrutiny.

GAPS matters because it changes that dependency. It cannot reproduce AMS's exact detector history. It can ask a more valuable question: does the sky contain low-energy antinuclei when measured by a device that does not infer charge sign from magnetic curvature at all?
