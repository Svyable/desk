# 1. The Wrong Telescope

The most famous machine in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence is not a machine built by extraterrestrials. It is ours: a radio telescope pointed outward.

That choice solved a practical problem. Radio crosses interstellar distance. A transmitter can be deliberate, energetic, narrowband, unmistakably technological. Cocconi and Morrison's 1959 proposal and Drake's Project Ozma in 1960 gave SETI a clean experimental grammar: choose stars, choose frequencies, listen, reject interference, quantify the search. The method matured. The silence became data rather than theology.

But it also trained the imagination around simultaneity.

A radio search samples a thin slice of another civilization's history. Their transmitter has to exist, operate, point or leak, and intersect our observing window. We may be early. They may be late. They may have transmitted for ten thousand years and stopped a million years before radio evolved on Earth. A signal is powerful evidence when received; its weakness as a search object is that it can be temporally brief.

Hardware has the opposite profile. A probe may never transmit. A machine may fail. A panel may fracture. A casing may be struck by micrometeoroids, irradiated, sputtered, buried, gardened into regolith, darkened, reddened, abraded until its original purpose is unreadable. Yet pieces can remain.

James Benford made this asymmetry explicit in a 2021 artifact version of the Drake equation: the comparative advantage of searching for artifacts is not that probes are known to exist but that an artifact's residence time near Earth might exceed the lifetime of a detectable transmission. The equation is a framework, not a frequency estimate. It moves the dispute to variables we can argue about: how often probes would be sent, how long they survive, which orbits retain them, what fraction of the relevant volume we have actually searched.

The crucial phrase is *actually searched*.

People say we have mapped the Moon. We have, magnificently, for the purposes we had. That is not the same as having performed an artifact-complete survey down to a chosen scale. People say we track space. We do, intensively, for collision avoidance, defense, astronomy, and operations. That is not identical to a survey optimized for inactive, dark, small, noncooperative objects in dynamical niches selected for longevity. People say a foreign object would be obvious. Human space history is an excellent answer to that confidence. We misplace our own hardware.

The opposite error is worse: treating every gap as a hiding place.

An unsearched region does not become evidence. The fact that a ten-centimeter object could evade a survey says only that the survey cannot exclude ten-centimeter objects. Search completeness is a denominator, not a revelation.

This is the first discipline of Solar-System archaeology: name the telescope you need before declaring the object mysterious. If the prediction is a polished surface, ask whether the instrument measures phase-dependent specular reflection. If the prediction is engineered material, ask whether a sample can preserve microstructure under radiation and impacts. If the prediction is a dormant object, ask what orbital lifetime and size distribution follow from the hypothesis.

The best artifact search may not look like SETI at all. It may look like planetary geology, debris tracking, meteorite curation, hyperspectral mineralogy, archival astronomy, or forensic materials science.

A different question changes the instrument.
