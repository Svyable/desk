# The Silent Hardware

## An Archaeology of the Solar System

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a superstition hiding inside modern SETI. It is a respectable superstition because it comes with antennas.

We imagine intelligence as something that must be caught in the act.

A narrowband carrier. A laser pulse. A modulation pattern. An atmosphere with chemistry so far from equilibrium that somebody must be maintaining it. The technological civilization is alive somewhere, now enough for its behavior to overlap ours. We listen. We wait. We calculate the fraction of the sky we have not listened to very well.

But technology has another property. It makes junk.

It leaves surfaces where geology would not make them, alloys where equilibrium chemistry would not put them, isotopic recipes that have no obvious natural factory, cavities, shards, films, dust, wreckage, dead electronics, exhaust products, excavations, altered regolith, abandoned orbits. Most of these traces decay. Some do not decay quickly. Some become harder to interpret precisely because they survive long enough to lose their context.

That is the subject of this book.

Not the claim that someone has been here. Not a literary exercise in hiding a probe behind every asteroid. The question is narrower and, to me, stranger: **if technology had entered the Solar System at any point during a long interval before human technological history, what classes of material evidence could still be here, and what would we have to measure to know what we were looking at?**

The question becomes newly practical when astronomy stops being only astronomy. Planetary cameras now revisit surfaces at useful resolution. Survey telescopes repeatedly image near-Earth space. Machine vision can trawl planetary archives. Sample-return missions bring grams of other worlds into laboratories that can see structures smaller than a bacterium. On September 8, 2026, SETI Institute researchers publicized a preprint proposing a quantitative search for microscopic engineered particles in lunar soil. They reported no artifact. The important thing is not the absence of a discovery. It is the change in posture: a formerly comic question can now be framed as a sampling problem with transport, survival and detection terms.

That does not make it likely. It makes it testable.

Four evidence classes are used throughout. **Class 1** is documented fact: measured orbital populations, known human spacecraft, returned lunar material, established space-weathering processes, published searches, actual sensor capabilities. **Class 2** is credible but incomplete evidence: candidate anomalies, uncertain classifications, plausible but under-tested detection methods. **Class 3** is allegation or interpretation without independent confirmation, including claims that specific Solar-System objects are artificial. **Class 4** is synthesis: what might follow if we designed a search around preservation and provenance rather than around stories.

The classes do not upgrade themselves because a subject is exciting.

A strange glint is not a probe. A non-gravitational acceleration is not engineering. A metallic grain is not manufacture. A stable orbit is not evidence it was chosen. A pre-Sputnik flash on a photographic plate is not automatically a satellite. A chain of custody that begins with “someone found this” is not a chain.

The field will become interesting when it becomes boring enough to survive those sentences.
