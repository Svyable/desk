# 5. The Co-Orbital Closet

Earth does not move alone.

The neighborhood around one astronomical unit contains asteroids in resonances, horseshoe trajectories and temporary co-orbital relationships. These objects are interesting entirely without SETI. Their dynamics preserve information about transport through near-Earth space, and they can be difficult to discover because observing geometry is often bad.

Artifact proposals entered this niche because a probe intended to observe Earth might plausibly use orbits that remain nearby without repeatedly fighting Earth's gravity well. James Benford called such hypothetical objects “lurkers” and argued that Earth co-orbitals deserved deliberate examination.

The word is too good. That is a problem.

Language can smuggle purpose into mechanics. A co-orbital object is not lurking. It is following a trajectory. Stability does not imply intention. A point in phase space that engineers might choose is also a point natural bodies can occupy.

The scientific form of the lurker idea is therefore not “search suspicious orbits.” It is to compute a prior-independent observing program for populations whose dynamical residence times are long enough to matter and whose discovery completeness is poor enough that additional surveys buy real information.

Then inspect what is found without changing the rules.

What could make a co-orbital object technologically interesting? Not the orbit by itself. A combination might: radar albedo inconsistent with expected composition; a phase curve suggestive of planar specular surfaces; unusual polarization; thermal inertia not fitting size and rotation; a density estimate that does not match plausible rubble piles; active station-keeping; resolved geometry. Each has natural alternatives and measurement failure modes.

Even station-keeping requires care. Outgassing can perturb comets and asteroids. Radiation pressure changes trajectories of high area-to-mass-ratio objects. Poorly modeled non-gravitational forces can look deliberate when we begin with deliberate in mind.

The co-orbital search is valuable because it is cheap in the epistemic sense. We do not need to believe in probes to justify better completeness of near-Earth object populations. Planetary defense wants the same telescopes. Dynamical astronomy wants the same orbits. Space situational awareness wants related tracking.

This is a recurring theme. The best technosignature experiment often has a second reason to exist.

If every result is natural, we still learn the architecture of our own neighborhood.
