# 6. The Lagrange-Point Myth

People who like hidden probes like Lagrange points.

The appeal is intuitive. There are locations in rotating gravitational systems where the geometry permits relatively stable configurations. Space missions use neighborhoods around Lagrange points because the thermal, lighting, communication and dynamical conditions can be useful. JWST works near Sun-Earth L2. SOHO observes from Sun-Earth L1. The engineering is real.

Then folklore performs a small theft: useful becomes stable, stable becomes permanent, permanent becomes ideal hiding place.

The actual dynamics are less cinematic.

L1, L2 and L3 are not little gravitational shelves. Orbits around the collinear points require careful dynamics and, for spacecraft, station-keeping. L4 and L5 can host stable tadpole populations under the right mass ratios, but stability depends on perturbations, inclinations, eccentricities, resonances and timescale. Dust behaves differently from boulders. Solar radiation pressure matters for small objects. Planetary perturbations matter over long intervals.

If a hypothetical ancient probe were placed near Earth millions of years ago, “check the Lagrange points” is not a search plan. One must propagate survival distributions.

Where would fragments go after propulsion ceased? What area-to-mass ratios survive? Which dynamical families retain objects for the interval being tested? Which regions have already been surveyed to what absolute magnitude? What solar elongations make optical discovery difficult? Could radar reach them? What thermal backgrounds dominate infrared searches?

This is where orbital mechanics becomes archaeology.

An archaeologist does not ask where a person *might* have dropped a tool. She asks how activity, deposition and later disturbance distribute objects. In space, disturbance is celestial mechanics.

There is also a negative lesson. If a proposed artifact requires active station-keeping for geological time, the hypothesis has quietly changed. We are no longer searching for durable dead hardware. We are proposing either extreme reliability, self-repair, replenishment, or continuing activity. Those are stronger assumptions and should pay a stronger evidentiary price.

A dead artifact search should prefer places where dead things can actually remain.

The Lagrange-point myth is useful because it catches us anthropomorphizing before we have data. Engineers choose Lagrange-region orbits now because our missions have particular constraints. An unrelated technology may choose differently. Natural dynamics, meanwhile, do not care what looks convenient on a mission-design slide.

The search should follow residence time, not science-fiction real estate.
