# 17. Build the Archaeology Before the Discovery

The serious search does not begin with a target.

It begins with infrastructure.

First, build a Solar-System search-completeness map by scale and modality. For the Moon and Mars, map what surface area has imagery at what effective resolution and illumination. For near-Earth space, map discovery efficiency as a function of size, albedo, orbit and solar elongation. For co-orbitals, propagate dynamical residence and survey bias. For sample archives, catalog how much returned material has been screened at micron and submicron scales using techniques capable of identifying engineered structure.

Second, build the human contamination atlas. Spacecraft material manifests, launch debris, curation hardware, laboratory consumables, terrestrial micrometeorite contamination, satellite fragmentation products. This is not glamorous. It may save the field.

Third, create degradation libraries. Expose known manufactured materials to radiation, thermal cycling and hypervelocity impacts. Analyze old space hardware. Model burial and comminution on the Moon. Produce a time series from machine to ambiguous matter.

Fourth, define anomaly metrics that are not synonyms for surprise. Morphological regularity, compositional purification, isotopic structure, multilayer periodicity, coherent thermal behavior, dynamic control. Each metric needs natural comparison sets.

Fifth, make independent confirmation physically easy. Archive raw data. Keep untouched sample reserves. Use open formats. Design missions with calibration targets and witness plates.

Sixth, exploit surveys already funded for other reasons. Planetary defense discovers co-orbitals. Lunar science returns regolith. Space-debris programs model artificial fragments. Astronomical time-domain surveys find transients. Every one of these can carry a technosignature sidecar at modest marginal cost if the criteria are defined in advance.

Seventh, practice on ourselves. Can the system find a known Apollo fragment in lunar data without being told where it is? Can it distinguish aged spacecraft alloy from meteoritic metal? Can an algorithm recover known artificial satellites in an intentionally incomplete catalog while holding false positives low?

Only then should anyone brag about being ready for a discovery.

The field has spent decades discussing what an extraterrestrial artifact might look like. The more urgent task is to make sure we know what our own instruments look like when they are wrong.
