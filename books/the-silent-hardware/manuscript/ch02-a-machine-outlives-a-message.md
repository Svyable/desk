# 2. A Machine Outlives a Message

The Voyager Golden Records are already teaching the central lesson, although we made them ourselves.

Their communicative content is deliberate. Their existence as hardware is incidental to anyone who does not know the code. In enough time, a record is not a message. It is a manufactured object moving through space.

Suppose its label erodes, its cover pits, the spacecraft loses recognizable geometry, the assembly is shattered and one alloy fragment continues alone. How far down the ladder of context can an intelligent observer descend before manufacture is no longer obvious?

That is not science fiction. It is a materials problem.

Technological objects have several clocks. There is the operational lifetime, usually short. There is structural survival, longer. There is chemical and isotopic legibility, which can sometimes survive after geometry is gone. There is orbital residence, which may be shorter or longer than physical survival depending on the environment. And there is discoverability, which is not a property of the object alone. It depends on what somebody later decides to measure.

The clocks rarely expire together.

On Earth, archaeology lives inside this mismatch. Organic handles vanish while stone blades remain. Workshops disappear while slag survives. Written context burns while composition persists in a shard. In space the biases change. Vacuum preserves some interfaces and destroys others. Ultraviolet photons, solar wind, cosmic rays and thermal cycling alter surfaces. Micrometeoroids turn objects into populations of smaller objects. Regolith gardening buries and exhumes grains. Orbits diffuse under perturbations. A dead machine can become a geological sample without ever touching a planet.

This gives artifact SETI its strongest argument and its most dangerous temptation.

The strong argument is that technological evidence can in principle integrate over a much longer time than a directed communication campaign. If a technological species sent hardware through interstellar space a million years ago, the probability of hearing its transmitter now may be zero while some material residue remains nonzero.

The temptation is to let longevity substitute for prior probability. “It could survive” is not “it exists.” A durable class of evidence is still empty until sampled.

The right quantity is therefore not artifact lifetime by itself but **detectable artifact lifetime**. A titanium plate can survive as atoms for an absurd interval and become scientifically useless as an artifact long before that. What matters is how long at least one diagnostic feature remains above the natural-background and contamination thresholds of a real measurement.

Geometry is one such feature, but perhaps an overrated one. Perfect rectangles are technologically suggestive because macroscopic natural systems rarely make thin flat plates with drilled holes and repeating fasteners. But impact fragmentation removes geometry first. Composition can last longer. Grain boundaries, multilayers, dopant patterns, isotopic purification, nanoscale periodic structures, semiconductor junction remnants, or manufacturing textures could outlive recognizable shape.

This reverses the cinematic search. We should not expect a black monolith standing politely upright. A sufficiently old technological residue may look like mineralogy that refuses to make geological sense.

That is harder science. It is also better science, because geology already knows how to ask why a material is where it is.
