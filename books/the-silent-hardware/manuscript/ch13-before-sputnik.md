# 13. Before Sputnik

There is a seductive archive in astronomy: the sky before we filled near-Earth space with machines.

Photographic plates taken before October 1957 provide a human-technological baseline unlike anything available today. There were aircraft, balloons, meteors, plate defects, variable stars and optical ghosts. There were not thousands of artificial satellites in orbit.

That makes pre-Sputnik imagery interesting for searches for short optical transients or specular reflections that might otherwise be dismissed as space debris.

Recent work has proposed using historical plates and modern transient surveys to search for such signatures. A 2025 preprint by Beatriz Villarroel and colleagues outlined several Solar-System probe search methods, including pre-Sputnik flashes, space-based searches, reflectance spectra and the use of Earth's shadow as a filter for near-Earth luminous objects.

The idea is clever. The archive is also treacherous.

Photographic emulsions have defects. Dust can sit on plates. Cosmic rays and radioactive contamination can create marks. Internal reflections can make ghosts. Asteroids trail. Meteors flash. A one-plate event without an independent exposure is weak evidence because the detector itself is part of the hypothesis space.

The pre-Sputnik boundary also does not eliminate human causes entirely. Rockets existed. High-altitude experiments existed. Classified aerospace activities existed. A date before the first successful satellite launch is not proof that nothing human could appear above ordinary aircraft altitudes.

This is where conspiracy instincts should be replaced by archival discipline. Observatory logs matter. Plate series matter. Telescope configuration matters. Emulsion batches matter. Comparison stars matter. Independent observatories matter.

If the same transient appears at consistent celestial geometry on separated plates from independent instruments, the evidentiary class changes. If it appears once on one emulsion, it remains a candidate anomaly.

The attraction of the archive is real: it lets us ask what near-Earth optical transients looked like before the space-junk explosion.

Its danger is equally real: old data have old failure modes, and mystery grows in the missing metadata.
