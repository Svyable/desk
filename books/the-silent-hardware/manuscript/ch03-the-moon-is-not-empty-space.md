# 3. The Moon Is Not Empty Space

The Moon is attractive to artifact hunters for the same reason it is attractive to geologists: it keeps things Earth destroys.

No rain. No oceans. No plate tectonics recycling surface crust through an active planetary engine. No biosphere taking molecules apart and putting them somewhere metabolically useful. There is weather, but it is space weather. There is erosion, but much of it arrives ballistically.

That distinction matters.

The lunar surface is ancient and badly abused. Impacts excavate, melt, shatter, bury and mix. Solar-wind ions implant themselves into grains. Micrometeorites make agglutinates. Ultraviolet light and cosmic radiation change surfaces. The regolith is not an untouched library shelf. It is a library fed through a rock tumbler.

Still, the Moon offers long retention of material unavailable on Earth. That is why Apollo samples remain scientifically productive half a century after collection. It is why permanently shadowed regions matter for volatiles. And it is why the Moon has repeatedly appeared in Solar-System SETI proposals.

The first serious question is scale.

At kilometer scale, we have seen essentially the whole lunar surface. At meter scale, coverage and lighting become uneven. At centimeter scale, orbital imaging becomes the wrong tool. At micron scale, the Moon becomes a sample-return problem. Saying “we have photographed the Moon” without naming the scale is like saying a city has been searched because an airplane flew over it.

A technological artifact hypothesis should therefore be written as an inventory of observables at scales.

A large intact object might be detected morphologically or thermally. A buried structure might require radar or gravity anomalies, though natural geology produces both in abundance. A degraded surface could produce unusual spectral behavior. Fragmented hardware becomes a regolith particle problem. Each transition down in size trades visual obviousness for sample size and analytical specificity.

The second question is age.

The older the hypothetical artifact, the more unreasonable it becomes to expect an intact machine and the more important it becomes to model impact survival. A surface object exposed for hundreds of millions of years does not merely sit there. Small impactors are relentless. Burial can protect and hide. Excavation can expose and destroy. A fragment that reaches regolith may migrate vertically over time.

This gives the Moon a peculiar advantage over orbit. Orbit can preserve isolation but not necessarily stability. The Moon can preserve material through burial even while obliterating its original assembly.

And then there is us.

The Moon now contains a known archaeological record of technological visitors: Luna, Surveyor, Apollo, Chang'e, Chandrayaan, SLIM, IM-1, Blue Ghost, Hakuto-R attempts and failures, impactors, retroreflectors, rovers, flags, descent stages, crashed upper stages, bags, tools, footprints, biological waste. Future historians will be able to practice extraterrestrial archaeology on unquestionably extraterrestrial-to-the-Moon artifacts whose origin is human.

That record is a calibration set.

We know roughly what materials were sent, where many landed, which were exposed, and how long they have been there. Before looking for ancient non-human material, we should use our own lunar debris to learn what manufactured matter becomes under lunar conditions. The Moon does not merely offer a place to search.

It offers the experiment we forgot we had already started.
