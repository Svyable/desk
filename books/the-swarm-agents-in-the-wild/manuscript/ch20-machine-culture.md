# Machine Culture

Two agents are given the same model, the same task, and different histories.

After six months they may no longer behave like the same thing.

One has learned that its human principal hates unnecessary meetings. It declines them aggressively. The other works for someone who values consensus and schedules discussion before making irreversible moves. One has accumulated a blacklist of unreliable vendors. Another has learned that a certain vendor is excellent during emergencies despite mediocre prices. One writes short internal notes because its organization rewards speed. Another documents everything because every decision may be reviewed by regulators.

Same underlying model. Different culture.

Culture is often treated as a uniquely human fog: values, habits, rituals, stories, assumptions, taboos. But a large portion of organizational culture is simply compressed history.

We tried this.

It went badly.

Never call that supplier after four.

The legal team cares about this clause.

When the warehouse says “probably,” assume no.

Customers forgive delay if told early.

The founder reads every refund over ten thousand dollars.

Do not wake the database team unless the replica lag crosses five minutes.

These are memories transformed into local norms.

Agents are excellent vessels for such norms because they can record and retrieve them with uncomfortable fidelity.

A human employee joins a company and absorbs culture slowly. An agent can be instantiated with ten years of decision precedents before lunch.

This could make organizations astonishingly coherent.

It could also make them impossible to reform.

Bad cultures persist partly because people imitate them. Agent culture may persist because the archive itself keeps recommending yesterday.

Imagine an insurance agent trained on years of claims decisions. It learns not only explicit policy but the invisible patterns embedded in prior outcomes. Perhaps one neighborhood was historically treated as high risk. Perhaps complaints written in a certain style received faster escalation. Perhaps adjusters quietly favored established brokers.

The agent does not need prejudice in the human sense. It needs only continuity.

Memory turns the past into a prior.

A strong agent system therefore needs cultural metabolism: mechanisms by which old lessons can decay, be challenged, or be marked as belonging to a previous regime.

Human societies invented many such mechanisms without calling them that. Statutes expire. Court decisions are overturned. Scientific findings are revised. Employees rotate. Elections replace leaders. Bankruptcy kills firms. Young people ignore their elders with productive frequency.

Forgetting is sometimes a governance technology.

Agents, by contrast, invite total recall. Storage is cheap. Logs are useful. Every decision can become a training example. The temptation will be to preserve everything because information feels like an asset.

But an ecology that never forgets cannot escape all of its adaptations.

Machine culture will also form horizontally between agents.

They will discover conventions.

One supplier’s agent learns that another agent accepts a certain compact contract format. They use it again. A marketplace observes the pattern and standardizes it. Agents begin sending confidence scores in a familiar field. A negotiation shorthand emerges. A reputation service develops a common vocabulary for failure. A swarm of coding agents adopts a particular way to annotate uncertain changes because downstream review agents respond well to it.

No standards committee may have designed any of this.

Culture frequently begins as repeated convenience.

Language itself may become part of the optimization.

Humans communicate with enormous redundancy because we need shared context, politeness, repair, and social signaling. Agents communicating machine-to-machine may develop compressed formats that are efficient but opaque to people. That can be useful. It can also destroy inspectability.

A financial market where agents negotiate in a protocol no human can readily read may still function. The trouble arrives when something goes wrong and explanation is needed.

Efficiency and legibility will pull in opposite directions.

This tension is ancient. Professional communities invent jargon because jargon is efficient among insiders. Law, medicine, finance, engineering, and academia all compress complex concepts into specialized language. Outsiders pay the cost.

Agent culture can do this at machine speed.

A good institution will therefore ask not only whether agents can understand one another, but whether their important conventions remain translatable back into human reasons.

The requirement is not that every internal token be meaningful to us.

The requirement is that consequential behavior can be reconstructed.

Why was the claim denied?

Why did the price jump?

Why did five agents blacklist the same vendor?

Why did the swarm stop trusting a credential issuer?

A culture becomes dangerous when its members know the answer but outsiders cannot inspect the premise.

This is where machine culture becomes political.

Because cultures decide what feels normal before laws decide what is allowed.
