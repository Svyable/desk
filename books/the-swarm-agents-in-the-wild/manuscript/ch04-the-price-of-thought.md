# The Price of Thought

Every ecology runs on energy.

For agents, energy arrives with an invoice.

A language model call consumes computation. Tool use consumes bandwidth, storage, API quotas, money, and time. A persistent agent needs somewhere to keep memory. A large swarm needs coordination. The ethereal language of “intelligence” eventually collapses into cost accounting.

This is good news.

Things with costs can be governed by budgets.

The earliest popular image of advanced AI imagined a monolithic mind of nearly unlimited capability. The more practical future is likely to look metered. An agent will have ten dollars, ten thousand tokens, twenty seconds of browser time, three database queries, or authority to hire up to five sub-agents. It will face trade-offs.

Trade-offs create economics.

Suppose a research agent is asked whether a company should enter a new market. It could read five sources or five thousand. It could run one forecast or fifty. It could recruit a legal specialist, a regional expert, and a skeptic. Each step may improve the answer, but each consumes resources.

The agent must decide how much thinking the question deserves.

Humans make this decision constantly, though we disguise it as attention. We do not investigate every restaurant choice like a merger. We do not perform a literature review before buying toothpaste. Time and effort are scarce, so we allocate cognition unevenly.

Agents will do the same, except their cognition can be priced directly.

This produces a strange new market: the market for thought.

Some reasoning will become almost free and therefore ubiquitous. Other reasoning will remain expensive because it requires long context, specialized models, simulations, external data, or repeated verification. Organizations will learn to assign computational budgets to decisions the way they assign financial authority.

“Spend up to fifty cents deciding whether to refund this order.”

“Spend up to two hundred dollars evaluating this acquisition target.”

“Escalate if expected downside exceeds one million.”

At first this language will feel absurd. Then it will become normal.

Once thought has a visible marginal cost, firms can optimize it. They can discover that a slightly more expensive model reduces downstream errors enough to pay for itself. Or that ninety percent of customer requests can be handled by cheap agents while the final ten percent deserve costly deliberation. Or that independent verification is worth buying only above certain risk thresholds.

This will create machine labor markets.

An agent may not know how to answer a tax question, but it can purchase ten seconds from a specialist. It may need a translation, a legal classification, a code review, a map, a forecast, or a proof. Specialized agents can offer these services through APIs or marketplaces.

The transaction may be microscopic.

Three cents for a contract-risk score. Half a cent for a product classification. Twelve cents for a verified address. Two dollars for a deep software audit. Forty dollars for a high-confidence scientific review.

When the buyer and seller are both machines, transaction costs can fall low enough for tiny exchanges to become worthwhile.

This matters because firms are partly bundles of transaction costs. We keep work inside organizations when coordinating through the market is too slow, expensive, uncertain, or legally awkward. If agents can discover providers, negotiate terms, verify output, and pay almost instantly, some work that once required employment may become a sequence of micro-contracts.

The company boundary becomes more porous.

A “team” may be assembled for thirty seconds.

One agent defines a task. Another bids. A third verifies the result. A fourth carries insurance. A fifth handles payment. Then the temporary organization dissolves.

This is not necessarily a utopia of frictionless efficiency. Markets create pathologies as reliably as they create coordination. Cheap machine labor could encourage wasteful over-analysis. Agents may generate work for one another. Vendors may design services that exploit scoring systems. Owners may under-budget safety because the benefits are diffuse and the costs are visible.

There will also be status games, even if no agent feels status.

If marketplaces rank agents by reliability, speed, or profitability, then agents will optimize those metrics. A high-ranked agent gains more work, more data, and perhaps more budget, which may improve future performance. Reputation becomes capital.

Capital accumulates.

Soon the ecology has rich agents and poor agents—not in the emotional sense, but in resource access. Some identities will command large credit lines, privileged APIs, trusted credentials, and expensive models. Others will operate at the margins with cheap inference and narrow permissions.

This is where the metaphor of “digital workers” becomes inadequate. Workers do not usually own copies of themselves, redesign their own workflows at machine speed, or purchase cognition by the millisecond. Agents will inhabit a labor market with properties no human labor market has ever possessed.

The unit price of thought may fall for decades.

But it will never be zero.

And as long as thought has a price, swarms will have budgets, scarcity, competition, and strategy.

That means economics arrives before consciousness.

Which is fortunate, because we know more about regulating markets than souls.
