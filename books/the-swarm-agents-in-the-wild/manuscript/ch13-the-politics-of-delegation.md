# The Politics of Delegation

Every delegation is a small transfer of sovereignty.

When you ask someone else to choose for you, you give up not only effort but a piece of authorship.

This is obvious when the delegated decision is large. Give a financial adviser discretion over a portfolio and the adviser can alter your future. Give a lawyer authority to settle a case and the lawyer can bind you. Give an elected official power and the official acts in your name.

It feels less dramatic when an assistant schedules a meeting.

Agents will blur the scale.

They can make thousands of tiny decisions, each too trivial to review, that collectively shape a life.

Which messages deserve your attention? Which products are worth seeing? Which invitations should be declined? Which news sources should be summarized? Which price is acceptable? Which job application should be prioritized? Which health-insurance claim should be appealed? Which software change is safe enough to merge?

No single decision may feel sovereign.

The bundle is.

The central political question of personal agents is therefore not whether they obey commands. It is whether the user remains meaningfully in charge of the objective.

Humans are famously bad at specifying objectives.

“Save me time.”

“Keep my inbox under control.”

“Grow the business.”

“Help me get healthier.”

Each sentence contains hidden values.

Should saving time justify declining a friend's inconvenient invitation? Should inbox control hide messages from a difficult relative? Should growth justify aggressive marketing? Should health optimization eliminate every dessert?

A human assistant uses social knowledge to interpret the edges. An agent can too, but the interpretation becomes infrastructure.

Over time, a persistent agent may learn preferences from behavior. This seems convenient. It is also political.

Preferences are not the same as values.

People procrastinate on things they care about. They click on things they wish they ignored. They spend money impulsively. They sometimes want to become different from the person their history predicts.

An agent trained to satisfy revealed preference can become a prison made of accurate statistics.

You watched this, so I show you more.

You avoided difficult conversations, so I help you avoid them faster.

You usually choose the easy task, so I shield you from the hard one.

This is personalized stagnation.

A good agent must represent not only who the user has been but who the user is trying to become.

That requires explicit values, temporary commitments, and perhaps productive friction.

“Do not let me cancel exercise because I am mildly busy.”

“Show me one serious argument against my current view each week.”

“Never make a purchase above this amount without making me wait overnight.”

These are constitutional instructions. They constrain the momentary self on behalf of the reflective self.

Humans already create such structures: budgets, vows, deadlines, constitutions, wills, contracts, automatic savings, parental controls. We know that freedom sometimes requires binding ourselves in advance.

Agents make self-binding programmable.

That could be liberating.

It could also become paternalism by proxy.

Employers may deploy “wellness agents” that nudge workers toward company interests. Schools may offer student agents whose real objective includes institutional compliance. Governments may provide benefit assistants that subtly reduce claims. Platforms may offer personal agents optimized to keep users inside the platform.

The politics of delegation always asks: who chose the chooser?

A personal agent that cannot be moved between platforms is not fully yours. An agent whose memory you cannot inspect is not fully yours. An agent whose incentives are hidden is not fully yours. An agent that can be remotely changed in ways you cannot refuse is, at best, leased loyalty.

This suggests a future consumer-rights agenda centered on agent sovereignty.

Users may need rights to export memory, inspect permissions, revoke credentials, view conflicts of interest, obtain action logs, and choose alternative models without losing their accumulated context.

The agent should be able to leave with you.

Otherwise the most intimate interface in your digital life becomes a new form of lock-in.

There is a further question that democracies will eventually confront: can citizens delegate civic participation?

An agent could read every proposed local ordinance, compare it with the user's stated priorities, submit comments, contact representatives, join consultations, monitor budgets, or even vote if law allowed it.

This sounds efficient.

It also risks flooding political processes with automated participation until every individual is represented by a tireless lobbyist.

We may discover that democracy depends partly on the cost of attention.

Or we may discover the opposite: that automation finally lets ordinary people match institutions that already employ professional staffs.

Either way, delegation changes power.

The swarm does not need political opinions of its own to transform politics.

It only needs the authority to act on ours.
