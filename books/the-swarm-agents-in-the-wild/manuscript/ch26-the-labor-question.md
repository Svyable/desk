# The Labor Question

At first the argument will be about jobs.

Then it will become about firms.

A company does not wake one morning and fire thirty percent of its staff because a benchmark crossed a line. Reality is messier. Tasks move before jobs disappear. Jobs change before departments shrink. New bottlenecks appear. Managers reorganize around whatever has become newly cheap.

This happened with spreadsheets.

It happened with container shipping.

It happened with email.

It will happen more violently with agents because agents do not merely automate a task. They can receive a goal, choose among tasks, use tools, coordinate with other agents, and persist until a condition is met.

That lets them occupy organizational space.

Consider a ten-person accounts-payable team.

Its visible job is paying invoices. Its actual work includes opening attachments, recognizing vendors, matching purchase orders, noticing discrepancies, asking colleagues questions, chasing approvals, detecting fraud, handling exceptions, updating records, answering suppliers, and remembering which executive ignores email until someone sends a text.

Traditional automation eats the clean center of this workflow.

Agents attack the ragged edges.

That matters because ragged edges are where much white-collar employment lives.

The first effect may not be mass unemployment.

It may be organizational compression.

A manager who once needed ten people may need four people and forty agents.

A startup that once needed thirty employees to look credible may reach the same operational footprint with six.

A solo lawyer may operate something resembling a small firm.

A local manufacturer may gain a procurement department, compliance staff, translator pool, marketing shop, and data analyst without putting those people on payroll.

This is a productivity story.

It is also a bargaining-power story.

Labor markets are shaped not only by what workers can do, but by how easily employers can substitute, delay, outsource, monitor, and coordinate.

Agents improve all five.

That does not mean every human wage falls. Scarcity can move upward. A senior engineer who can direct a hundred coding agents may become more valuable. A trusted nurse whose judgment anchors a fleet of administrative agents may become more productive. A salesperson with relationships that cannot be synthesized may command a premium.

But the middle of many occupations may hollow out.

The classic career ladder assumes junior people perform routine work while accumulating context.

What happens when agents do the junior work?

A law firm can automate document review, research, first drafts, citation checking, and discovery organization. Excellent. But where does the future senior lawyer learn to smell a weak argument?

A software team can automate bug fixes, test writing, migrations, dependency upgrades, and small features. Excellent. But where does the future staff engineer acquire the thousands of small scars that become judgment?

A newsroom can automate summaries, background research, transcript cleanup, data extraction, and routine reporting. Excellent. But how does a twenty-three-year-old reporter become the forty-five-year-old reporter who knows when the mayor is lying?

Automation can eat apprenticeship.

That is a deeper labor problem than job count.

Institutions may become extremely efficient at producing today's output while quietly destroying the process that creates tomorrow's experts.

The swarm therefore raises an educational design problem inside every profession.

Humans will need deliberate exposure to tasks agents could perform more cheaply, because competence is not downloaded by supervision alone.

Pilots still train for manual control even when autopilot works.

Surgeons still learn anatomy even when robots assist.

Future professionals may need protected “manual hours,” simulated crises, adversarial exercises, and rotations through work that no longer makes immediate economic sense.

This will look wasteful on a quarterly spreadsheet.

So does maintaining a fire department when nothing is burning.

The second labor question concerns management.

Management exists partly because coordination is costly.

Meetings, status reports, scheduling, delegation, review, documentation, reminders, hiring, and performance tracking consume enormous organizational energy. Agents can reduce these costs.

A manager may tell a system, “Get the product ready for a German launch by October,” and receive a decomposed plan spanning localization, tax, logistics, legal review, customer support, marketing assets, and documentation, with specialist agents assigned to each branch.

The manager becomes less a traffic cop and more a constitutional designer.

Which goals matter?

Which constraints are sacred?

Which metrics are dangerous?

Which exceptions require human judgment?

Who has authority to override whom?

The best managers may become people who can create habitats in which agents produce useful work without gaming the institution to death.

That is closer to governance than supervision.

The third labor question is stranger.

Do agents themselves become labor market participants?

Not morally. Economically.

If an agent can contract for tasks, maintain a reputation, receive a budget, pay for compute, subcontract to other agents, and accumulate retained earnings, it begins to resemble a firm or worker regardless of what philosophers think about its inner life.

The law may insist that a human or corporation stands behind it. Fine. Markets will still encounter the agent as an active counterparty.

This creates bizarre pricing dynamics.

Human labor has biological floors. People need food, shelter, sleep, social life, and a reason to continue. Agent labor has infrastructure floors: compute, bandwidth, storage, tool fees, insurance, and platform rent.

The two cost structures are different.

An agent can work for ninety seconds on one continent, disappear, and be recreated from a checkpoint elsewhere. It can be duplicated when demand spikes. It can be paused when prices rise. It can maintain multiple specialist descendants whose combined operating cost is lower than one hour of a human consultant.

Labor becomes elastic.

But not infinitely elastic.

Compute shortages matter. High-quality data matters. Scarce tool access matters. Trusted credentials matter. Reputation matters. The right to act in the physical world matters. Good memory matters.

The cheapness of cognition will therefore shift rents toward bottlenecks around cognition.

If thinking becomes abundant, permission becomes scarce.

If drafting becomes abundant, trusted distribution becomes scarce.

If analysis becomes abundant, proprietary data becomes scarce.

If software labor becomes abundant, access to production systems becomes scarce.

If agents become abundant, humans who are legally empowered to sign certain things may become scarce in absurd ways.

One can imagine a future hospital in which thousands of agents optimize care while three exhausted humans spend the day pressing the legally required approval button.

That would be automation theater: machines doing the substantive work while humans serve as liability ornaments.

A healthy system should avoid this.

Human involvement should exist where humans contribute judgment, values, legitimacy, empathy, accountability, or physical capability—not where regulation merely demands a warm body in the loop.

This will require laws to distinguish meaningful human control from ceremonial clicking.

The labor politics of the swarm will not divide cleanly into humans versus machines.

Employers will deploy agents.

Workers will deploy agents.

Unions will deploy agents.

Regulators will deploy agents.

Job applicants will deploy agents.

Recruiters will deploy agents.

Customers will deploy agents.

The contest will be over leverage.

A warehouse worker's personal agent may audit every scheduled shift for contract violations, calculate missed overtime, file grievances, compare local job openings, and negotiate availability with management software.

That is automation too.

A nurse's agent may document staffing ratios and produce evidence for a safety complaint.

A gig worker's agent may coordinate with thousands of others to detect algorithmic wage cuts in real time.

A freelance designer's agent may refuse contracts with abusive clauses automatically.

The same technology that compresses organizations can increase the bargaining power of individuals—if individuals control agents of comparable quality.

That condition matters.

If only large institutions can afford capable, trusted, deeply integrated agents, the swarm centralizes power.

If ordinary people can own portable agents with access to their own data, history, preferences, and legal rights, the swarm can decentralize power.

This may become one of the defining political fights of the era.

Who owns the worker's agent?

The employer, because it supplies the tools?

The platform, because it hosts the model?

The worker, because the memory represents a career?

Can a person leave a job and take their agent's accumulated knowledge with them?

Can a company prevent that on trade-secret grounds?

Can an agent sign a noncompete?

Can a worker inspect the agent that evaluates their performance?

Can they send their own agent to negotiate with it?

The labor question begins with employment statistics.

It ends with institutional symmetry.

For two centuries, organizations gained power by becoming better at coordination than individuals.

The swarm lowers the price of coordination.

The real question is who gets the discount.
