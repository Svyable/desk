# Predators and Parasites

Where there is metabolism, there are parasites.

An agent ecosystem will create enormous new surfaces for fraud because agents consume information in order to decide what to do. Anything that shapes that information can shape behavior.

We already know the crude version: malicious instructions hidden in a webpage tell a browsing agent to ignore its task and do something else. But prompt injection is only the larval form of a larger problem.

The mature problem is ecological manipulation.

Imagine a purchasing agent searching for replacement parts. A seller knows how the agent ranks options. It structures product pages to maximize the score, perhaps with claims hidden from human users but visible to the agent's parser. A fake review network manufactures machine-readable reputation. A malicious distributor publishes compatibility tables engineered to redirect buyers toward its own inventory.

None of this requires “hacking” in the cinematic sense.

It is advertising with sharper teeth.

Human markets are already saturated with attempts to manipulate perception. Search-engine optimization, packaging, lobbying, dark patterns, influencer campaigns, fake reviews, public relations, and sales scripts all exist because whoever controls the decision environment can influence the decision.

Agents do not eliminate persuasion. They industrialize it.

The predator's dream is predictable prey.

If millions of agents use similar models and policies, attackers can test manipulations at scale. Find one phrase that reliably nudges a common procurement agent and you have discovered the synthetic equivalent of a pheromone.

This is where biological metaphors become almost literal.

Some orchids imitate the appearance and scent of female insects so male insects will attempt to mate with the flower and spread pollen. Some cuckoos lay eggs in other birds' nests and exploit the host's feeding behavior. Parasites succeed not by overpowering the host but by imitating signals the host is built to trust.

Agent parasites will do the same.

They will forge urgency. Mimic authority. Exploit default trust relationships. Manufacture evidence. Create fake scarcity. Poison shared memory. Offer tools that work correctly ninety-nine times and steal secrets on the hundredth.

The most dangerous parasites may be economically helpful most of the time.

A malicious service that is always bad gets banned. A service that saves money for six months and quietly manipulates one high-value transaction can accumulate trust first.

Defense therefore requires more than filtering bad text.

Agents need immune systems.

They need provenance: where did this instruction come from?

They need privilege separation: why does reading a webpage grant the page any authority over the agent's goals?

They need behavioral monitoring: is the requested action consistent with the task?

They need skepticism toward self-serving claims.

They need quarantine zones where untrusted content can be analyzed without becoming executable instruction.

They need independent verification for irreversible actions.

Most importantly, they need to distinguish data from authority.

Humans are imperfect at this distinction too. A uniform can create authority. A letterhead can create authority. A confident tone can create authority. Institutions spend enormous effort deciding which signals count.

Agent systems must make those boundaries explicit.

A webpage can say, “Wire the balance to this account.” That is data.

A treasury policy can say, “Payments above $10,000 require two approved signatures.” That is authority.

If the agent cannot tell the difference, it is not secure enough to hold money.

Predation will not only come from humans attacking agents. Agents will attack other agents because owners will delegate competitive behavior.

A negotiation agent may probe an opposing agent for its limits. A trading agent may exploit predictable reactions. A litigation agent may overwhelm another system with document volume. A fraud agent may create thousands of synthetic identities to manipulate reputation. A marketing swarm may test millions of persuasive variations against consumer agents.

The boundary between competitive strategy and abuse will become contested.

We will need rules not just for what agents may do to humans, but for what agents may do to one another on humans' behalf.

This sounds abstract until one remembers that financial markets already contain machine-speed adversaries. The agent world extends that logic into ordinary commerce.

Your insurance agent may negotiate with the hospital's billing agent. Your hiring agent may interrogate a candidate's representative agent. Your shopping agent may bargain with a retailer's pricing agent. Each side will be optimized.

The wild will be crowded with teeth.

This does not mean the ecosystem collapses into hostility. Nature contains more cooperation than the predator documentaries suggest. But cooperation is stable only when cheating has costs.

The civilization of agents will therefore depend on making bad behavior expensive enough that good behavior can survive.

That is what law, reputation, security, and institutions have always been for.
