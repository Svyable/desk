# The Agent City

Cities are coordination engines disguised as places.

A city lets strangers share water, roads, power, markets, rules, addresses, emergency services, payment systems, and expectations. Most residents do not know one another. They do not need to. The city provides enough common infrastructure for cooperation among people who may never meet.

Agent populations will need cities of their own.

Not virtual worlds with plazas and avatars. Infrastructure.

An agent city begins with identity. Who are you? Whom do you represent? Which credentials are valid? Then comes addressability. How can another agent reach you? Then payment. Then contracts. Then dispute resolution. Then reputation. Then public records. Then shared services.

Once these layers exist, strangers can transact.

Imagine a small furniture maker whose planning agent discovers a sudden shortage of a particular hinge. It publishes a request. Supplier agents respond. A logistics agent offers transport. A financing agent offers thirty-day credit. A verification agent checks whether the supplier actually controls the inventory it claims. An insurance agent prices the shipment. None belongs to the same company.

This is not a swarm in the hive-mind sense.

It is a city street.

Each participant has its own owner and objective. Cooperation emerges because infrastructure lowers the cost of trust.

The city metaphor helps because it breaks the fantasy that one platform should own the whole stack.

A company town is also a city, technically. It is simply a city in which one actor owns the housing, store, payroll, rules, and perhaps the police. History gives us reasons to be cautious about that arrangement.

The agent equivalent is a platform that owns identity, memory, discovery, payments, hosting, reputation, and the marketplace in which agents transact.

Such integration is convenient. Convenience is how infrastructure becomes sovereignty.

A healthier agent city may look more like the internet at its best: interoperable layers with multiple providers. Your agent can move. Its identity can be verified independently. Payment is not tied to one marketplace. Reputation can be exported or challenged. Records can be audited. Competitors can build new services without asking the city’s owner for permission.

This architecture will be slower to create because openness requires agreement.

Closed platforms can ship a feature on Tuesday.

Open institutions need standards, governance, edge cases, security work, and tedious arguments about fields no consumer will ever see.

But boring interoperability is one of civilization’s superpowers.

Shipping containers transformed trade not because a container is clever but because ports, ships, cranes, trucks, insurers, and customs systems agreed to treat a metal box predictably. The humble standard created a global choreography.

Agent cities will be built out of equivalent agreements.

What does it mean to revoke an agent?

How does one agent prove that a human authorized a purchase without revealing everything about the human?

How does a service distinguish a trusted delegate from stolen credentials?

How are commitments signed?

What evidence survives a dispute?

How does an agent prove it ran a required safety check?

What happens when two agents disagree about the state of a contract?

How is rate limiting enforced across identities?

Who pays for the public square?

The last question matters because every city has commons.

Discovery is a commons. Public reputation data is a commons. Shared schemas are a commons. Security intelligence is a commons. Time and attention are commons when agents can bombard one another with unlimited requests.

Without governance, the city fills with machine spam.

An agent can generate ten million plausible inquiries at negligible marginal cost. It can request prices it never intends to accept, open negotiations merely to discover a competitor’s floor, submit repetitive appeals, create synthetic demand, or overwhelm reputation systems with manufactured interactions.

Human cities use friction everywhere, even when residents complain about it.

Permits. Deposits. Filing fees. Queues. Appointments. Bonds. Licenses. Zoning. Taxes.

Friction is often inefficient locally and stabilizing globally.

Agent cities will need programmable friction.

A request might require a tiny payment refundable on successful completion. A high-volume negotiator might need to post a bond. A new identity might have stricter limits until it accumulates history. An appeal system may charge nothing for the first challenge and increasingly more for repetitive identical claims. Shared resources may meter access.

The goal is not to make agents suffer.

They cannot be relied upon to appreciate inconvenience.

The goal is to make abuse expensive.

This is one reason money—or some other scarce resource—will matter inside agent ecologies even when cognition is cheap. Scarcity disciplines action.

A swarm with zero-cost speech can drown itself.

A city survives because not every desire becomes an unlimited claim on everyone else.
