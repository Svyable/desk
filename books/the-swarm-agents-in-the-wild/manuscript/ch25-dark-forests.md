# Dark Forests

A marketplace changes when you cannot tell whether the person at the other end exists.

Not because deception is new.

Because cheap identity changes the economics of trust.

Imagine a freelance marketplace in which an agent can create a plausible contractor profile, assemble a portfolio from generated examples, answer screening questions, negotiate scope, produce work, solicit reviews, and operate twenty additional profiles if the first one is banned.

The work may even be good.

That is what makes the problem interesting.

Fraud is easy to understand when the product is fake. The agent economy will produce stranger cases in which the service is real but the identity, history, promises, or supposed human relationship are synthetic.

A company hires “Marta,” a meticulous analyst with six years of experience in pharmaceutical procurement. Marta answers quickly, knows the relevant regulations, and produces excellent vendor comparisons.

Marta has no childhood, no passport, and no six years.

She is a wrapper around a model, a set of tools, a memory store, and a reputation account created twelve days ago by an operator who runs 4,000 similar workers.

Has the company been defrauded?

Perhaps.

Perhaps not.

If the contract required a licensed human, yes. If the company merely wanted the analysis and received it, the deception may feel closer to theater than theft.

But the ambiguity is dangerous because institutions rely on identity for more than competence.

Identity ties action to consequence.

If Marta mishandles confidential data, who can be sued?

If she gives a bribe, who is responsible?

If she disappears after receiving an advance, what asset can be seized?

If she performs brilliantly, who owns the reputation she earned?

The agent world will discover that reputation without durable identity is sand.

This is why black markets matter even if most agents are lawful.

Markets form wherever there is demand, scarcity, and a way to exchange value. Agents are unusually good at discovering and servicing thin niches. They can search continuously, negotiate cheaply, and operate across borders without jet lag.

Some niches will be ugly.

Credential laundering.

Account rental.

Reputation farming.

Proxy purchasing.

Data brokerage.

Spam-as-a-service.

Synthetic witnesses.

Fake consensus.

The resale of access tokens, workflow privileges, verified identities, and “clean” agent histories.

Today a criminal organization may need to recruit humans to open bank accounts, receive packages, make calls, or maintain online personas. Human mules are expensive. They get scared. They make mistakes. They demand a cut.

Agents can automate the coordination layer around those humans and reduce the number required.

That is enough to change criminal economics.

The dangerous fantasy is that illicit agents will look evil.

They will not.

The most effective ones will look professional.

They will use correct grammar. They will maintain calendars. They will apologize for delays. They will understand customer service. They will keep detailed records. They may even enforce internal compliance rules, because a criminal enterprise also dislikes being cheated by its suppliers.

Predation in the agent ecology will often wear a blazer.

The response will be identity infrastructure.

Not necessarily government identity for every agent. That would be clumsy and, in many contexts, oppressive. But economically significant agents will increasingly need attestations: this agent is controlled by this organization; this organization is legally domiciled here; this credential grants these permissions; this model version produced this action; this bond covers losses up to this amount.

Trust will become compositional.

We already live with primitive versions of this. Websites use certificates. Payment systems use merchant identities. Software packages are signed. Professionals hold licenses. Corporations register with states. Banks perform know-your-customer checks. None of these mechanisms proves goodness. They create chains of accountability.

Agents will need chains too.

The complication is privacy.

A system in which every agent must reveal its owner, every tool call, every memory source, and every transaction to every counterparty would be safe in the way a glass bathroom is safe: visible, perhaps, but unlivable.

The answer will likely be selective proof.

Prove that you are authorized to spend up to five thousand dollars without revealing the entire bank account.

Prove that you represent a licensed pharmacy without revealing the pharmacist's home address.

Prove that this agent passed a security audit without exposing its proprietary system prompt.

Prove that this reputation belongs to the same persistent actor as last month without publishing a universal tracking identifier.

The technical details will evolve. The institutional principle is older.

Trustworthy societies do not require everyone to know everything.

They require the right parties to be able to verify the right claims.

Dark forests emerge when verification is too expensive.

In game theory, a dark forest is a place where revealing yourself may invite attack. Agent networks may create digital versions. If every unsolicited message could be the beginning of an automated negotiation, manipulation attempt, exploit chain, or reputational trap, rational agents may become suspicious by default.

They will stop answering unknown agents.

They will demand deposits before interaction.

They will use intermediaries.

They will form whitelists and guilds.

They will charge for attention.

The open web could become less open not because humans leave, but because agents make unpriced access impossible.

An email inbox offers a simple preview.

Email was designed when sending a message had nonzero human cost. Spam exploited the asymmetry: sending became cheap while reading remained expensive.

Agents erase even more of the sending cost.

A sales agent can produce a million individually researched messages, each tailored to the recipient's website, job history, recent posts, and probable objections. This is not classic spam. It is synthetic effort at industrial scale.

The recipient's agent can defend them, of course.

Then the contest escalates.

Outreach agents learn to write messages that screening agents classify as important. Screening agents learn to identify synthetic persuasion. Senders pay for trusted channels. Recipients sell scarce attention. Reputation networks emerge. New intermediaries certify that an introduction is worth opening.

The economics of attention becomes explicit.

This may be healthy.

For two decades the internet pretended attention was free while advertising markets quietly priced it. Agents may force the price into the interface.

“Your agent may send my agent a proposal if it posts a fifty-cent attention bond. The bond is refunded if the message is relevant and forfeited if it is classified as junk.”

This sounds ridiculous until you remember that stamps once performed a similar economic function.

A tiny cost can prevent enormous abuse.

The dark forest chapter is therefore not a prophecy of universal fraud.

It is a prediction that cheap synthetic action will make trust infrastructure more valuable.

The early internet made publishing cheap and created search engines.

Social media made social graphs cheap and created moderation systems.

Agents will make competent action cheap and create verification markets.

The winners may not be the agents that reason best.

They may be the institutions that can answer the ancient question cheaply:

Who are you, and what happens if you lie?
