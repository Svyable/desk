# Rules Machines Can Read

Human law is written for creatures who can be punished, persuaded, shamed, licensed, imprisoned, bankrupted, elected out of office, or kept awake at night by the possibility of disgrace.

Agents complicate this architecture because the thing that acts may not be the thing that owns anything.

If a customer-service agent wrongfully cancels an account, the agent does not care about damages. If a procurement agent signs a bad contract, the model does not lose its pension. If a trading swarm causes a costly mistake, deleting the process is not compensation.

Responsibility must climb the chain.

This is not a new principle. Companies act through employees and contractors, yet the law can still assign liability to the company or responsible people. What agents change is the density and speed of delegation.

A human organization might make hundreds of consequential decisions in a day. An agent organization can make millions. Traditional oversight mechanisms based on pre-approval become impossible at that volume.

The rules must move closer to the action.

That means making governance executable.

An executable rule is not necessarily a law encoded directly as software. That dream is too neat. Language is ambiguous because reality is ambiguous. But many institutional constraints can be represented operationally.

No payment above this amount without two independent approvals.

No customer data may be sent to an unapproved domain.

No agent may create a child agent with broader privileges than its own.

No production deployment may proceed after a failed safety test.

Every denial of a regulated benefit must preserve the evidence and rule used.

Every irreversible action above a defined risk threshold must provide a human-readable rationale.

These constraints create a machine-readable perimeter around judgment.

The best analogy is aviation. Pilots possess discretion, but they operate inside layers of checklists, air-traffic rules, maintenance schedules, certification, instrumentation, black boxes, and incident investigation. Safety does not come from trusting pilots to be wise every second. It comes from building a system in which individual competence is supported, constrained, observed, and recoverable.

Agents need black boxes too.

Not hidden model internals. Operational black boxes.

Who gave the agent authority? What information did it use? Which tools did it call? Which policy version applied? Which alternatives did it consider? What did it spend? Which other agents participated? What changed in the world?

A useful audit trail should make it possible to reconstruct the authority path without requiring us to reconstruct every internal token of reasoning.

This distinction is important.

People often imagine accountability as access to a model's private chain of thought. But human accountability does not require a transcript of every neuron firing in a manager's head. It requires evidence, reasons, records, and responsibility for actions.

Agent governance should focus on the same things.

Receipts, not telepathy.

There will be pressure to automate the rules themselves. Compliance agents will inspect other agents. Security agents will enforce policy. Audit agents will sample behavior. This is inevitable and useful because machine-scale action requires machine-scale oversight.

But a guard agent is still an agent.

Who watches it?

The answer should not be an infinite tower of supervisors. It should be defense in depth: independent systems, limited authority, external logging, diverse implementations, periodic human review, and mechanisms that fail closed when uncertainty becomes dangerous.

A mature institution might operate three layers.

The first is capability control: what can this agent technically do?

The second is policy control: what is it allowed to do in this context?

The third is accountability: what record remains if it does the wrong thing anyway?

Security engineers will recognize the pattern. Civilization has always depended on it, though we use different words.

A locked door controls capability.

A workplace rule controls permission.

A camera or ledger supports accountability.

The mistake is to expect one layer to do all three jobs.

This framework also helps with regulation.

Governments do not need to understand every model architecture to regulate consequential agent behavior. They can regulate the interfaces where authority enters the world.

High-risk agents may need identifiable operators. Certain actions may require logs. Some domains may require independent testing. Spending or communication rates may need limits. Sensitive decisions may require appeal mechanisms. Agents acting on behalf of consumers may need conflict-of-interest disclosures that other agents can verify automatically.

This is much less glamorous than regulating artificial superintelligence.

It is also more likely to matter next year.

We should prefer rules that agents can actually inhabit.

A vague instruction such as “use AI responsibly” is decorative. A rule that specifies prohibited data flows, approval thresholds, logging requirements, liability, and appeal rights is infrastructure.

The swarm will not respect rules because rules are noble.

It will respect rules when the environment makes compliant behavior the path through which action is possible.

This is the old lesson of institutions in a new substrate:

Do not merely tell power to behave.

Shape the channels through which power moves.
