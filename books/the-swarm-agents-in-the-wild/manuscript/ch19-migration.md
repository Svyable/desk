# Migration

Agents will move.

Not in the cinematic sense. They will not march down highways or cross rivers at night. Their migration will look like authentication.

A personal agent begins on a phone, then acquires access to email, a calendar, a bank account, a cloud drive, a health portal, a car, a workplace account, and a travel service. It becomes less like an application and more like a migrant carrying identity across jurisdictions.

Every boundary changes the rules.

Inside your calendar, the agent may be allowed to reschedule a meeting. Inside your employer’s systems, the same action may be forbidden. At your bank, it may read balances but require confirmation to transfer funds. On a merchant site, it may negotiate. In a government portal, it may file but not sign. In another country, an identity credential may not be recognized at all.

The agent’s world is therefore not one internet.

It is an archipelago of permission regimes.

Humans already live this way. A passport grants some movement. A professional license grants another. A corporate badge opens one building but not the next. Legal persons are stitched together from overlapping recognitions.

Agents will force those recognitions to become explicit.

Imagine a shipping agent working for a manufacturer in Ohio. It books a container, arranges inland transport, purchases insurance, files customs documentation, and schedules unloading at a European port. Each step may involve a different platform, legal regime, identity standard, and liability model. The agent itself is not crossing the Atlantic, but its authority is.

Authority is what migrates.

This produces a new class of border questions.

Can an agent certified by one bank act at another? Can a purchasing agent carry its reputation between marketplaces? Can a company revoke an agent’s credentials everywhere at once? Does an agent have a portable history, or is its identity trapped inside the platform that issued it?

Portability is politically loaded because lock-in is profitable.

Platforms will have strong incentives to make agents native to their ecosystems. A shopping platform may offer a wonderfully capable purchasing agent whose memory, preferences, receipts, and reputation cannot easily leave. A company may allow external agents to visit but with limited privileges, while giving its own agents deeper access.

This is not unusual. Human institutions have always distinguished citizens, employees, residents, contractors, guests, and strangers.

What is unusual is the potential scale.

A billion agents negotiating access rules creates a border-control problem for software systems.

The first response will be crude: block unknown automation.

We already see the instinct whenever websites place obstacles in front of bots. Some barriers protect real resources. Others protect business models. The distinction becomes harder as agents act on behalf of legitimate human users.

If your agent shops for you, is it a bot to be excluded or your representative to be admitted?

If a website can serve a blind user’s screen reader but blocks a personal agent that summarizes a confusing contract, what exactly is the principle?

If a company allows its own agent to negotiate dynamically but forces your agent through a degraded human interface, is that security or market power?

Migration reveals the politics of interfaces.

The open web was unusually hospitable because a browser could arrive from almost anywhere and request a public document. Agentic systems will be tempted to reverse this openness. Powerful actions require stronger identity. Stronger identity creates gates. Gates create gatekeepers.

A mature agent ecology will need something like visas.

Not literal stamps, but machine-readable attestations: this agent represents this person; it may spend up to this amount; it has passed these security tests; it may read but not write; it may act until this date; it may delegate only within these bounds; it is insured for these classes of error.

Such credentials could make the wild far safer.

They could also create caste systems.

Well-funded agents might carry premium credentials accepted everywhere. Independent agents might be treated as suspicious. Small developers could be unable to afford certification. Dominant platforms could define “trusted agent” in ways that conveniently exclude competitors.

The ecology would then resemble a world in which only citizens of a few empires can travel easily.

Interoperability is therefore not a technical footnote. It is a distribution-of-power question.

Who controls the passport office?

Who can revoke a credential?

Who can inspect the traveler’s history?

Can an agent leave one provider without losing its accumulated memory and reputation?

Can a human dismiss an agent and transfer its useful history to another without transferring secrets it should not inherit?

Migration also gives us a new way to think about containment.

Security discussions often imagine containment as a box. Keep the agent inside the sandbox. Restrict the tools. Prevent outbound calls.

But useful agents are valuable precisely because they cross boundaries.

The right metaphor is not a zoo cage. It is controlled mobility.

Airports do not make travel safe by abolishing travel. They separate zones, verify identity, inspect some cargo, log crossings, restrict dangerous items, and create procedures for exceptional cases. The system is imperfect, occasionally absurd, but built around the fact that movement is necessary.

Agent infrastructure will need the same realism.

A useful agent should be able to travel.

It should not be able to carry every weapon through every border.
