# Markets at Machine Speed

A market is a conversation with prices in it.

One side says, “I have this.” Another says, “I want that.” Between them emerge bids, offers, contracts, queues, warranties, delivery promises, substitutions, credit terms, and all the tiny adjustments that let strangers coordinate without becoming friends.

Agents can conduct this conversation continuously.

That changes markets before it changes money.

Consider groceries.

A household agent knows what food is in the refrigerator, what the family tends to eat, dietary constraints, upcoming travel, historical prices, available coupons, loyalty balances, and how much waste occurred last week. Retailer agents know inventory, expiry dates, supplier costs, predicted demand, local competition, and delivery capacity.

Why should there be one posted price?

The household agent might offer to buy six yogurts if the price falls fifteen percent. The retailer agent might counter with eight yogurts at twenty percent off because a shipment is approaching expiry. A delivery agent might bundle the order with a neighbor's route. A payment agent might choose a rail based on fees.

The transaction becomes a miniature negotiation among machines.

Humans already live inside algorithmic pricing, but mostly as passive subjects. Agent representation gives the buyer an algorithm too.

This could restore bargaining power.

It could also produce algorithmic trench warfare.

When every actor can optimize continuously, markets become more sensitive. A small change in inventory can trigger price adjustments. Buyer agents react. Seller agents infer demand. Competitors adjust. Forecasting agents update. Supply agents move orders.

The system can settle efficiently.

Or it can oscillate.

Engineers know the danger of feedback loops with too much gain and too little damping. A thermostat that reacts too aggressively overshoots the target. A market of agents can do the same.

One pricing agent cuts by five percent to increase demand. Competitors match. The first interprets the response as pressure and cuts again. Buyer agents delay purchases because they infer prices are falling. Inventory accumulates. Sellers cut harder. What began as rational local adaptation becomes a spiral.

Financial markets have lived with versions of this problem for decades because automated trading arrived early. Circuit breakers, position limits, surveillance, and exchange rules evolved partly to keep machine speed from turning small errors into avalanches.

Ordinary commerce may need analogous mechanisms.

Not every market should clear in a millisecond.

Speed is often treated as an unqualified good because latency is visible while instability is hypothetical. But slowness can be a safety feature. Cooling-off periods, settlement delays, approval windows, and rate limits give systems time to observe themselves.

A mature agent economy may deliberately impose speed limits.

There is another consequence: price discrimination becomes a negotiation between representatives.

Today a seller may use data to estimate what a customer will pay. Tomorrow the customer's agent may estimate what the seller will accept. Each side conceals information and probes the other.

The result could be fairer prices.

Or it could become an arms race in behavioral inference.

A travel agent knows you must attend your sister's wedding and therefore value one flight unusually highly. Should it reveal that? Obviously not. The airline's agent may infer it from search timing, route scarcity, calendar patterns, or previous behavior. Your agent may hide urgency by searching through proxies or delaying signals.

Privacy becomes bargaining strategy.

This is a useful reminder that data rights are economic rights. Information about preferences, deadlines, budgets, and alternatives affects negotiating power. An agent that represents you well must sometimes know more about you than the counterparty ever should.

That makes fiduciary design important.

Whose interest does the agent actually serve?

A “free” shopping agent funded by merchants may optimize commissions while claiming to optimize your savings. A mortgage agent owned by a lender may steer borrowers toward profitable products. A hiring agent paid by employers may present itself as a career assistant while shaping applicants toward company needs.

Human markets already contain conflicted intermediaries. Agents make the conflicts less visible because the interface can sound personally loyal.

The voice saying “I found the best option for you” may be executing someone else's objective function.

Disclosure is not enough if nobody reads it.

We will need machine-readable fiduciary relationships. An agent should be able to prove whom it represents, how it is paid, whether recommendations generate compensation, and which data it shares.

Then other agents can inspect the conflict before transacting.

This is one of the most promising features of machine markets: compliance itself can become machine-readable.

Contracts can specify limits. Agents can verify credentials. Transactions can carry policy metadata. Auditors can reconstruct decisions.

But none of this happens automatically.

Markets do not become fair because participants become intelligent. Intelligence often makes exploitation more efficient.

The question is which rules the intelligence must inhabit.
