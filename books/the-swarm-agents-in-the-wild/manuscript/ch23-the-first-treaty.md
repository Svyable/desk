# The First Treaty

The first treaty among agents will probably not be called a treaty.

It may be a retry policy.

Two large systems keep colliding. A merchant’s pricing agents update too quickly. A buyer’s agents respond to every change. Their interaction causes a cascade of quotes, cancellations, and inventory holds that is individually rational and collectively wasteful. Engineers on both sides diagnose the pattern. They agree on a rule: price commitments must remain valid for sixty seconds; buyers may not hold more than a certain quantity without posting collateral; repeated cancellations reduce priority.

The systems calm down.

A treaty has been born.

Civilization accumulates such agreements after repeated pain.

Ships pass port to port because standards define signals, documents, and responsibilities. Networks exchange traffic because protocols define how machines greet, address, route, retry, and quit. Banks transfer money because institutions agree on settlement rules. Countries coordinate air travel because a shared system is worth surrendering some local freedom.

Agent ecologies will generate their own need for peace.

Peace here does not mean friendship. It means predictable conflict.

A good contract does not assume the parties love each other. A good protocol does not assume every packet is benevolent. A good treaty creates a shape within which competing interests can continue without destroying the shared system.

Agents make this possible at extraordinary granularity.

A procurement agent could arrive at a marketplace carrying explicit negotiating limits. A seller agent could publish the classes of offers it will consider. A reputation service could state how disputes alter scores. A platform could expose a machine-readable appeals process. A security service could publish conditions under which another agent will be quarantined and how it can prove remediation.

These arrangements reduce uncertainty.

They also reduce the value of manipulation.

Many hostile strategies depend on ambiguity. Flood the channel. Exploit the retry loop. Pretend not to understand. Reopen the same dispute. Change identity. Take advantage of one side’s inability to verify a claim.

Treaties make boundaries legible.

There will be temptation to write one universal agent constitution.

Resist it.

The internet succeeded partly because a small number of protocols allowed enormous diversity above them. Human legal systems work, when they work, because different domains have different rules. The norms appropriate to an emergency room are not those of a stock exchange. A child’s educational agent should not be governed exactly like a commodities trader.

The constitutional layer should be thin where possible and strong where necessary.

Some principles may deserve near-universal status:

An agent should be able to identify whom it represents when representation matters.

Authority should be scoped rather than implied.

Consequential actions should produce durable receipts.

Revocation should propagate quickly.

Delegation should not silently expand privilege.

Disputes should have a defined destination.

High-impact automated decisions should be reconstructible after the fact.

An agent should not be able to create unlimited claims on shared resources merely because speech is cheap.

Notice how unromantic these are.

Good.

The glamorous layer of technology attracts attention. The unglamorous layer prevents collapse.

The swarm will not be civilized by giving every agent a lecture on ethics before deployment. Ethics matters, but populations are governed through structure.

A restaurant is not made sanitary by hiring cooks with beautiful souls. It is made safer through refrigeration, inspections, handwashing rules, supply standards, training, temperature logs, liability, professional norms, and a thousand habits that make good behavior easier than bad behavior.

Agent civilization will be equally infrastructural.

The first treaty worth celebrating may therefore be invisible to almost everyone.

A standard revocation format.

A portable audit receipt.

A common way to express spending authority.

A protocol for refusing unsafe delegation.

A mechanism by which one agent can say, in a form another can verify: I am acting for this principal, for this purpose, within these limits, until this time.

That sentence contains the embryo of law.

And law, at its best, is how strangers cooperate without needing to trust one another completely.
