# Prologue — The Night Shift

At 2:13 in the morning, a refrigerated truck carrying forty thousand dollars' worth of soft cheese broke down outside Indianapolis.

The owner of the cheese was asleep in Milwaukee. The distributor was asleep in Chicago. The buyer, a chain of small groceries, had long since shut its offices. The truck driver was awake, naturally, but he was not in a position to redesign a supply chain. He pulled onto the shoulder, cursed at the dashboard, and called the number printed on his dispatch screen.

No person answered.

A small program did.

It already knew the truck's location, the temperature of the cargo, the delivery windows, the service history of the refrigeration unit, the driver's remaining legal hours, and the contractual penalties for late delivery. It knew that the cheese had about five hours before the probability of spoilage became expensive. It also knew something no dispatcher would have kept in working memory: a bakery delivery van with an unused refrigerated compartment would finish a route thirty-six miles away at 3:05 a.m.

The program did not solve the problem. It recruited a population.

One agent searched repair inventories. Another negotiated with the bakery's logistics system. A third compared the cost of a temporary transfer with the cost of emergency roadside refrigeration. A fourth read the cargo insurer's exclusions. A fifth examined weather and traffic. A sixth generated a chain-of-custody plan and asked another agent, belonging to the insurer, whether that plan would preserve coverage.

There was disagreement. The insurer's agent rejected the first transfer proposal because the temperature logger in the bakery van was not on an approved list. A procurement agent located a compliant logger at an all-night veterinary supply warehouse. A routing agent discovered that sending a courier to collect it would delay the transfer by eleven minutes but reduce expected insurance exposure by several thousand dollars. A finance agent approved the courier. A security agent objected to the bakery van's driver because the identity credential was stale. Another agent refreshed it.

At 3:42 a.m., the cheese changed vehicles in the sodium light of a truck stop parking lot.

At 8:11, the owner woke to a summary.

**Refrigeration failure mitigated. Delivery delay: 47 minutes. Estimated loss avoided: $31,400. Exceptions requiring review: one.**

No machine had been extraordinarily intelligent. None had discovered a new theorem, written a symphony, or announced consciousness. The remarkable thing was much less theatrical. A collection of narrow actors had perceived a situation, recruited help, negotiated across organizational boundaries, spent money, created records, satisfied constraints, and completed a piece of economic life while the humans with legal ownership of the problem were unconscious.

This is the threshold that matters.

For most of the digital age, software waited. It waited for a click, a command, a query, a scheduled job, a person. Even the most impressive language models began as elaborate instruments: ask, receive; prompt, answer. Their intelligence was trapped inside a conversational terrarium.

Agents are what happens when the terrarium gets a door.

Give a model memory and it can carry unfinished business across time. Give it tools and it can alter the world outside the conversation. Give it credentials and it can enter institutions. Give it a budget and it can trade. Give it an identity and others can form expectations about it. Give it a goal and enough time and it can pursue consequences rather than merely produce sentences.

Then give it peers.

The result is not simply a more capable assistant. It is the beginning of an ecology.

Ecologies do not care very much about the brilliance of one organism. They care about populations, niches, energy, reproduction, predation, cooperation, boundaries, and feedback. A wolf is interesting. Wolves and elk and grass and rivers are history.

The coming agent world will be discussed, inevitably, through the language of minds: intelligence, reasoning, autonomy, consciousness. Some of that language will be useful. Much of it will distract us. The institutions most likely to change first—markets, companies, bureaucracies, logistics networks, software systems—are not waiting for machines to become persons. They are waiting for machines that can participate.

Participation is enough.

An agent that can request a quote is a market participant. An agent that can approve an expense is part of a corporation. An agent that can file a permit is part of a bureaucracy. An agent that can choose among contractors is part of a labor market. An agent that can establish a reputation, accumulate resources, delegate subtasks, and defend its access is beginning to look less like a feature and more like an actor.

Once there are millions of such actors, the interesting question is no longer, *What can this model do?*

It is: *What happens when they meet each other in the wild?*

That is the subject of this book.
