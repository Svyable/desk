# Chapter 3 — AI acquisition capability and institutional learning

## Claim under test

Chapter 3 argues that cheap model access does not by itself create public capacity. Agencies still need technical judgment, procurement competence, data rights, testing, institutional memory, and enough internal knowledge to evaluate suppliers and change systems when circumstances change.

## Fresh primary-source evidence

The U.S. Government Accountability Office's April 2026 review of federal AI acquisitions examined 13 acquisitions across the Departments of Defense, Homeland Security, and Veterans Affairs and the General Services Administration. Officials described difficulty obtaining AI technical expertise, including data scientists able to evaluate contractor proposals, and difficulty understanding AI-related costs.

GAO also found that the four agencies were not yet systematically collecting lessons learned from AI acquisitions. Their policies did not require officials to collect those lessons for sharing through the GSA-managed repository envisioned by OMB guidance. GAO warned that this leaves agencies with fewer opportunities to reuse effective practices—such as contract terms governing data rights or testing—or to avoid repeating acquisition mistakes. All four agencies concurred with GAO's recommendations to establish processes for collecting and sharing lessons learned.

The report also records an important distinction in how AI reaches government. Some acquisitions were agency-directed, beginning with a government requirement; in other cases industry introduced capabilities in the absence of a specific AI requirement. That makes internal technical and commercial judgment more, not less, important: an institution must be able to decide whether a capability answers a public need rather than merely whether the demonstration works.

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

This evidence should not be used to argue that private vendors inherently weaken state capacity or that government should build every AI system itself. GAO describes benefits as well as challenges across multiple acquisition approaches, and federal agencies often need private-sector models, infrastructure, expertise, and speed. Nor does the report establish that the reviewed agencies lack competent staff generally; it identifies recurring acquisition challenges and a specific failure to institutionalize lessons learned.

The stronger claim is about retained judgment. Outsourcing execution can expand capacity when the public buyer can specify the problem, evaluate proposals, negotiate data and testing rights, monitor performance, learn from prior procurements, and change course. Dependence becomes dangerous when the institution loses enough expertise or memory that it can no longer perform those functions intelligently.

## Editorial implication

Chapter 3 already says procurement teams need enough technical depth to distinguish a capability from a demonstration and that successful pilots must become dependable infrastructure. GAO's 2026 findings give those lines a concrete contemporary basis. A future manuscript pass could mention the failure to collect acquisition lessons if the example earns its space, but the evidence is most useful as a factual backstop for the chapter's broader argument that complementary institutional capability remains scarce even when AI itself becomes cheap.
