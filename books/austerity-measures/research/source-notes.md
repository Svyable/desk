# Austerity Measures — source notes

Working research notes for the Desk draft. These are not a finished bibliography; re-check figures and publication details before a Shelf release.

## Local government austerity and service mix

Institute for Fiscal Studies, Kate Ogden and David Phillips, **“How have English councils’ funding and spending changed? 2010 to 2024”** (7 June 2024).

https://ifs.org.uk/publications/how-have-english-councils-funding-and-spending-changed-2010-2024

Useful claims for the draft:

- English councils’ real-terms core funding per person fell about 26% between 2010–11 and 2019–20.
- Overall real-terms spending per person fell about 19% over the same period.
- Councils protected statutory and acute services unevenly. Children’s social care excluding youth/Sure Start rose about 11% per person, while highways and transport fell about 45%, culture and leisure 45%, housing 44%, services for young people and Sure Start 67%, and planning and development 58%.
- The IFS describes the result as a greater concentration of spending on statutory duties and residents with the most acute needs.

These figures are useful because they show the institutional composition of austerity, not merely its aggregate size.

## Fiscal consolidation and recession conditions

Olivier J. Blanchard and Daniel Leigh, IMF Working Paper, **“Growth Forecast Errors and Fiscal Multipliers”** (2013).

https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2016/12/31/growth-forecast-errors-and-fiscal-multipliers-40200

The paper finds that, among advanced economies during the crisis, stronger planned fiscal consolidation was associated with lower growth than forecasters expected, with the relationship especially strong early in the crisis. Use carefully: it is evidence about crisis-era forecast errors and fiscal multipliers, not a universal claim that all fiscal consolidation is always contractionary.

## AI exposure in labor markets

International Monetary Fund, **“Gen-AI: Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Work”**, Staff Discussion Note 2024/001.

https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/006/2024/001/article-A001-en.xml

Related IMF overview by Kristalina Georgieva, 14 January 2024:

https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity

Useful claims for the draft:

- Almost 40% of global employment is exposed to AI.
- In advanced economies, the estimate is about 60%.
- Exposure is not equivalent to displacement. Some exposed work may be complemented and become more productive, while other work may face reduced labor demand.

## AI in government

OECD, **“Governing with Artificial Intelligence: The State of Play and Way Forward in Core Government Functions”** (2025).

https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/governing-with-artificial-intelligence_795de142-en.html

Full report:

https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/governing-with-artificial-intelligence_398fa287/full-report.html

Useful claims for the draft:

- The OECD examined 200 AI use cases across 11 core government functions.
- Government AI use is growing but remains fragmented and uneven.
- Common barriers include skill gaps, legacy IT systems, limited or weak data, tight budgets, and the stronger privacy, transparency, representation, and accountability obligations of public administration.
- The report is especially useful for the argument that models alone do not create state capacity; complementary institutional and digital foundations determine whether AI can be used reliably.

## Headcount, contractors, and institutional knowledge

Department for Science, Innovation and Technology and Government Digital Service, **“State of digital government review”** (21 January 2025).

https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/state-of-digital-government-review/state-of-digital-government-review

Useful claims for “The Headcount Illusion”:

- The UK public sector spent about £26 billion on digital and data in 2023.
- Less than 20% of that spend, around £5 billion, went to permanent public-sector staff.
- About 55%, around £14.5 billion, went to contractors, managed-service providers, and IT consultants.
- The review estimates that contractors represented approximately 18% of headcount but around 40% of headcount cost.
- The average contractor in central government cost about three times as much per year as the average civil servant.
- The review says headcount restrictions made it easier to hire more costly third-party resources than permanent staff and that temporary staffing hindered retention of institutional knowledge.
- It also identifies a wider bias toward new programmes over continuous improvement and maintenance, alongside substantial legacy-technology risk.

Use these figures as evidence of a measurement problem, not as evidence that contracting is inherently wasteful. The chapter’s claim is that headcount controls can shift labor into procurement while weakening ownership and memory.

## Government as an intelligent technology client

National Audit Office, **“Government’s approach to technology suppliers: addressing the challenges”** (16 January 2025).

https://www.nao.org.uk/insights/governments-approach-to-technology-suppliers-addressing-the-challenges/

Report PDF:

https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/governments-approach-to-technology-suppliers-addressing-the-challenges.pdf

Useful claims:

- Government needs stronger digital-commercial capability to manage large technology suppliers effectively.
- The NAO describes the need for government to act as an “intelligent client.”
- For digital contracts, the public body remains responsible for outcomes even when technical delivery sits with suppliers.
- Effective contract management therefore requires sufficient understanding of technology, product lifecycles, supplier commercial strategies, costs, integration risks, and delivery progress.

This is the basis for the chapter’s distinction between outsourcing execution and outsourcing judgment.

## Automated administration, review, and redress

Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme, **Final Report** (7 July 2023; corrected edition 11 July 2023).

https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/report

Letters Patent and scope:

https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/letters-patent

Useful claims:

- In November 2019, the Federal Court of Australia declared, with the Commonwealth’s consent, that a demand for payment of an alleged debt under the Robodebt scheme was not validly made.
- The Australian Government later announced that more than 400,000 debts raised under the scheme would be zeroed or repaid.
- Recommendation 17.1 says automated government decision-making should include a clear path to review, plain-language disclosure that automation is being used and how it works, and availability of business rules and algorithms for independent expert scrutiny.
- Recommendation 17.2 recommends a body with power to monitor and audit automated decision-making for technical behavior, fairness, bias, and client usability.

Robodebt is used as an institutional precedent for automated administration, not as an example of generative AI. The point is that contestability, traceability, legal competence, and review capacity are part of the operating system of an automated state.

## AI, human oversight, and trust

OECD, **“Governing with Artificial Intelligence: The State of Play and Way Forward in Core Government Functions”** (2025), discussion of trust, public empowerment, oversight, and redress.

https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/06/governing-with-artificial-intelligence_398fa287/795de142-en.pdf

Useful claim for the chapter:

- The OECD notes that AI decisions can be perceived as rigid or unaccountable when human oversight or avenues for redress are absent, and summarizes research associating inability to challenge incorrect automated decisions with higher psychological and compliance costs.
- Greater transparency and public empowerment are associated with stronger trust in responsible government use of AI.

The manuscript extends this into an operational argument: appeals are not only a legitimacy mechanism but also an information channel through which institutions discover errors at the edge of their automated systems.

## Argument note

The manuscript’s central distinction is between **expenditure** and **capability**. A government can reduce expenditure while increasing capability if automation genuinely removes low-value work and the remaining institution can still understand, change, audit, contest, and maintain its systems. Conversely, a government can reduce headcount while merely transferring capability to contractors or increasing downstream costs elsewhere in the public system.

“The Headcount Illusion” sharpens that distinction: headcount is a useful accounting fact, but a poor proxy for either total cost or sovereign institutional competence. The AI-era test is whether the public institution can remain an intelligent client of suppliers and an intelligent supervisor of its own machines.
