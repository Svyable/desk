# The Fiscal Mirage — source notes

Research trail for Chapter 7. Checked 2026-09-13.

## CBO: how IRS funding changes can increase or reduce the deficit

Congressional Budget Office, **“How Changes in Funding for the IRS Affect Revenues”** (February 29, 2024).

https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60037

CBO modeled three illustrative rescissions of mandatory IRS funding over the 2024–2034 period:

- a $5 billion rescission: about $5.2 billion less revenue and about $0.2 billion more cumulative deficit;
- a $20 billion rescission: about $44 billion less revenue and about $24 billion more cumulative deficit;
- a $35 billion rescission: about $89 billion less revenue and about $54 billion more cumulative deficit.

The nonlinearity matters. CBO assumed the IRS would preserve enforcement activities with higher expected returns and curtail lower-return activity first. The chapter therefore should not turn the estimates into a universal claim that every dollar of IRS funding has the same fiscal return.

CBO also describes a benchmark enforcement return on investment of 6.4 in its modeling, but that figure is an input to a model, not an observed return guaranteed for every additional dollar or every year.

## Current enacted example and budget-boundary issue

Congressional Budget Office, **“Legislation Enacted in the First Session of the 119th Congress That Affects Mandatory Spending or Revenues”** (2026).

https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62240

CBO reports that the Full-Year Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2025 rescinded $20.2 billion of enforcement-related IRS funding. CBO estimated that the act would increase the deficit by $66 billion over 2025–2034, with the net increase resulting almost entirely from lower revenues caused by the IRS rescissions.

For the chapter's accounting-boundary argument, also see CBO's discussion of the 2024 appropriations rescissions:

https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61239

CBO explains that reductions in IRS budget authority and outlays in appropriations are treated as reductions in discretionary spending for congressional budget enforcement, while the resulting revenue effects are accounted for separately. This is a concrete example of two valid accounting entries that can support a misleading political story if only one side is presented.

## Strongest counterevidence / claim boundary

Congressional Budget Office, **“Answers to Questions for the Record Following a Hearing on Oversight of the Congressional Budget Office”** (2026).

https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62053

CBO notes that it reduced early projections of additional revenue from the 2022 IRS funding increase, partly because hiring and training new auditors took longer than expected. The IRS had not provided the full information CBO would need to estimate total 2023–2024 revenue attributable to those initiatives at the time of the response.

This is important counterevidence. The chapter should not imply that projected enforcement returns are mechanically realized. Implementation capacity, hiring lags, the mix of enforcement activity and the size of the funding change all affect returns.

## Editorial use

The chapter uses the IRS example because the budget boundary is unusually observable: a reduction in agency spending can reduce revenue by more than the outlay saved. The broader argument is narrower than “cuts are fake savings.” A saving is real when total public cost falls for the same outcome, or capability rises for the same cost. The error occurs when the accounting unit ends before materially connected downstream effects.

For AI-era administration, treat repeat contacts, appeals, error correction, citizen burden, supplier costs, collections and recovery costs as candidate downstream effects only when there is evidence connecting them to the intervention. Do not assume every later cost was caused by automation, and do not price speculative worst cases as though they were observed costs.
