# The Geography of Capacity — source notes

Working source notes for `ch10-the-geography-of-capacity.md`. These supplement the book-wide notes and should be rechecked before Shelf release.

## Unequal local-government funding cuts

Institute for Fiscal Studies, Kate Ogden and David Phillips, **“How have English councils’ funding and spending changed? 2010 to 2024”** (7 June 2024).

https://ifs.org.uk/publications/how-have-english-councils-funding-and-spending-changed-2010-2024

Useful claims:

- During the 2010s, real core funding per person fell about 35% in the most deprived tenth of English council areas, compared with about 15% in the least deprived tenth.
- The pattern reflected heavier reliance by more deprived areas on central-government grants, which were cut sharply.
- By 2024–25, despite larger funding increases after 2019 for more deprived areas, real funding per person was still estimated to be 26% below 2010–11 in the most deprived tenth and 11% below in the least deprived tenth.
- The report also shows how councils protected statutory and acute services while discretionary and preventive services absorbed larger cuts.

The chapter uses these figures to establish unequal fiscal starting conditions, not to claim that deprivation mechanically determines digital or AI maturity.

## Local-government data capacity

Local Government Association, **“Local government data capacity and capability: Survey of local authorities, October to December 2024”** (1 April 2025).

https://www.local.gov.uk/publications/local-government-data-capacity-and-capability-survey-local-authorities-october

Survey base: 96 responding councils and combined authorities. The report should be treated as a snapshot of respondents rather than a complete census of English local government.

Useful claims:

- 53% of respondents did not have a current data strategy, although 28 percentage points of that total were developing one; 44% reported an existing standalone, combined data/digital, or other data strategy.
- The three most frequently cited barriers to building data capacity and capability were lack of financial resources (73%), legacy systems (71%), and lack of staff capacity / insufficient data professionals (67%).
- 42% said teams could only occasionally obtain data from third-party supplier systems in the format they needed; 51% said they could do so very or fairly often.
- 50% said their organisation understood what in-house specialist data roles it needed, and the extent of its skills gaps, only to a small extent or not at all.
- 53% reported problems recruiting and/or retaining officers with specialist data skills.

These figures support the argument that the complements required for sophisticated AI adoption — usable data, technical staff, accessible supplier-held information, and organisational understanding of skills needs — are uneven and often scarce.

## AI maturity and barriers across councils

Local Government Association, **“State of the sector: Artificial intelligence — 2025 update”** (2 June 2025).

https://www.local.gov.uk/publications/state-sector-artificial-intelligence

The survey received responses from about one third of English councils; the LGA explicitly warns that results should be treated as a snapshot rather than representative of every council.

Useful claims:

- 95% of respondents were using or exploring AI.
- 50% described themselves as at the beginning of their AI journey; 22% were developing AI capacity/capability; 14% reported some use with good practice; 7% described themselves as innovative leaders.
- 68% of respondents using or exploring AI were paying external suppliers or were in the process of procuring external AI tools.
- Among those using/procuring suppliers, reported barriers included understanding where AI adds value (66%), understanding how AI is embedded in a product (61%), and evaluating solutions (58%).
- The most commonly cited deployment barriers were lack of funding (62%), lack of staff capabilities (56%), and lack of staff capacity (52%).

The chapter does not combine this survey statistically with the IFS deprivation data. It uses the two evidence bases separately: one shows unequal fiscal history; the other shows uneven AI maturity and recurring capacity constraints.

## Digital local government, market concentration, and capability asymmetries

Local Government Association, **“State of Digital Local Government”** (21 January 2025).

https://www.local.gov.uk/publications/state-digital-local-government

Relevant claims and observations:

- The report describes wide variation in councils’ digital experience and resources.
- It explicitly identifies “capacity and capabilities asymmetries” in procurement, legal and IT teams. Some councils have specialist technology procurement capability; others have small teams negotiating with large global suppliers.
- It discusses concentrated supplier markets, information asymmetries, inconsistent pricing, interoperability barriers, high switching costs and vendor lock-in in local-government technology.
- It states that emerging-technology use risks perpetuating a sector digital divide because councils cannot take advantage of opportunities equally.
- The report records significant difficulties recruiting and retaining specialist technical staff and describes limited capacity, capability and funding as barriers to emerging-technology deployment.

The chapter uses this report for the argument that the same vendor or technology can meet different public buyers with very different bargaining and implementation capacity.

## Shared digital infrastructure and reusable AI

Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, **“Introducing Local AI”** (16 March 2026).

https://mhclgdigital.blog.gov.uk/2026/03/16/introducing-local-ai/

The Local AI team describes its mission as helping councils use AI responsibly, reduce duplication, build tools once for reuse, scale locally developed tools, strengthen data foundations and help councils navigate the AI market.

Government Digital Service, **“GDS Local: building the foundations for digital collaboration”** (15 April 2026).

https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2026/04/15/gds-local-building-the-foundations-for-digital-collaboration/

Relevant themes include shared digital infrastructure, interoperable services, reusable components, shared data foundations, and stronger collaboration between local and central government.

Government Digital Service, **“Publishing a common architectural model for local government”** (14 April 2026).

https://technology.blog.gov.uk/2026/04/14/publishing-a-common-architectural-model-for-local-government/

This work supports the chapter’s distinction between common technical substrate and local operational judgment.

Government Digital Service, **“We’re piloting NDX:Try, a new platform to help local councils test digital services before they buy”** (17 April 2026).

https://technology.blog.gov.uk/2026/04/17/were-piloting-ndxtry-a-new-platform-to-help-local-councils-test-digital-services-before-they-buy/

The pilot gives councils secure, time-limited access to digital and AI services for experimentation before purchase. The chapter treats this as an example of a shared capability that can reduce the fixed cost of evaluation for smaller or more constrained institutions.

## Current responsible-AI support for councils

Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, **“From engagement to delivery: supporting responsible AI adoption in councils”** (13 July 2026).

https://mhclgdigital.blog.gov.uk/2026/07/13/from-engagement-to-delivery-supporting-responsible-ai-adoption-in-councils/

The Local AI team reports that councils are at very different stages of adoption and repeatedly identified needs around practical implementation, skills and training, procurement, governance and reusable support.

This is useful contemporary evidence for the chapter’s “two-speed state” risk, while also showing that government is already attempting to build shared support rather than leaving every council to solve the problem independently.

## Argument note

“The Geography of Capacity” makes a conditional compounding argument, not a deterministic empirical claim. The evidence establishes three separate facts:

1. English councils entered the AI era after materially unequal fiscal experiences, with more deprived areas absorbing larger funding cuts over the 2010s.
2. Digital, data and AI capability is uneven across councils, with recurring barriers around funding, legacy technology, skills, staffing, procurement and supplier dependence.
3. Central and local government are now building shared tools, architecture, evaluation environments and support intended to reduce duplication and unequal access to digital capability.

The author’s synthesis is that AI can compound existing institutional advantage unless common public infrastructure lowers the fixed cost of becoming a capable adopter. The proposed model is **shared substrate, local judgment**: pool technical capabilities that are genuinely common while preserving local knowledge, accountability and discretion where the facts and trade-offs are local.
