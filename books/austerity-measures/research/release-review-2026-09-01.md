# Release review — 2026-09-01

## Scope

This review rechecks the principal time-sensitive and public-administration claims in *Austerity Measures* before preparing a Shelf release.

Desk source snapshot reviewed: `0c5264a7a61264d6e7e329dca4c4c40a44d26dce`.

The review focuses on claims that could have changed since the original drafting: public-sector digital staffing and contractor dependence, AI labor-market exposure, current government AI lifecycle guidance, public-sector AI capability, local-government AI maturity, and the status of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Historical claims about the 2010s austerity period and Robodebt were checked against their primary institutional sources for wording and scope.

## Digital-government staffing and supplier dependence

The UK Government's January 2025 *State of digital government review* continues to support the figures used in *The Headcount Illusion*:

- public-sector digital and data spending in 2023 was about £26 billion
- less than 20%, around £5 billion, went to permanent public-sector staff
- about 55%, around £14.5 billion, went to contractors, managed-service providers and IT consultants
- contractors represented about 18% of permanent-plus-contractor headcount and about 39–40% of cost
- the average contractor cost about £182,000 per year versus about £61,000 for a permanent employee, approximately three times as much
- the review explicitly says headcount restrictions made it easier to secure more costly third-party resources than permanent staff and that temporary staffing hinders retention of institutional knowledge

Source:
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/state-of-digital-government-review/state-of-digital-government-review

No manuscript correction is required. The chapter already cautions that these figures do not prove contractors are inherently poor value.

## Intelligent-client capability

The National Audit Office's January 2025 work on government technology suppliers continues to support the chapter's use of the phrase “intelligent client.” The NAO says government cannot manage digital contracts effectively without understanding the technology, product lifecycle and commercial strategies of the supply chain, even when technical delivery resides with suppliers.

Source:
https://www.nao.org.uk/insights/governments-approach-to-technology-suppliers-addressing-the-challenges/

No manuscript correction is required.

## Automated administration and Robodebt

The Robodebt Royal Commission's published recommendations continue to support the chapter's description of the institutional safeguards required around automated administration.

Recommendation 17.1 calls for a clear path to review, plain-language disclosure of automated decision-making, and availability of business rules and algorithms for independent expert scrutiny. Recommendation 17.2 calls for a body able to monitor and audit automated decision-making for technical behavior, fairness, bias and client usability.

The Commission's Letters Patent also record that the Federal Court declared in November 2019, with the Australian Government's consent, that a demand under the scheme was not validly made, and that the government subsequently announced that more than 400,000 debts would be zeroed or repaid.

Sources:
https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/report
https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/letters-patent

No manuscript correction is required. The chapter correctly presents Robodebt as a precedent in automated administration rather than as a generative-AI case.

## AI exposure and labor transition

The IMF's 2026 work continues to cite the exposure estimates used in *The Capacity Dividend*: roughly 40% of employment globally and 60% in advanced economies may be affected or exposed to AI. The IMF continues to distinguish exposure from simple job disappearance and emphasizes both complementarity and substitution.

Current sources:
https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/02/03/sp-md-leveraging-artificial-intelligence-and-enhancing-countries-preparedness
https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/006/2026/001/article-A001-en.xml

No manuscript correction is required.

## AI lifecycle, responsibility and internal capability

The UK Government AI Playbook remains current guidance and continues to support the manuscript's claims that public organisations need meaningful human control, lifecycle management, commercial involvement, sufficient skills, assurance, live monitoring, feedback and redress. It explicitly states that responsibility for outputs or decisions made or supported by AI remains with the public organisation, including when AI is purchased commercially.

Source:
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-playbook-for-the-uk-government/artificial-intelligence-playbook-for-the-uk-government-html

The OECD's January 2026 policy brief *Building an AI-ready public workforce* likewise continues to state that internal AI capability is important for accountability, compliance and alignment of AI use with institutional objectives.

Source:
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/building-an-ai-ready-public-workforce_b89244c7-en.html

No manuscript correction is required.

## Local-government divergence

The Institute for Fiscal Studies' 2024 historical analysis continues to support the funding figures used in *The Geography of Capacity*: during the 2010s, real core funding per person fell about 35% in the most deprived tenth of English council areas and about 15% in the least deprived tenth; by 2024–25 those figures remained about 26% and 11% below 2010–11 respectively.

Source:
https://ifs.org.uk/publications/how-have-english-councils-funding-and-spending-changed-2010-2024

The Local Government Association's 2025 AI survey continues to report that 95% of respondents were using or exploring AI, while only 7% described themselves as innovative leaders, and 68% of respondents using or exploring AI were paying external suppliers or procuring external AI tools. The LGA explicitly cautions that its responses came from about one third of English councils and should be treated as a snapshot rather than a representative census. The chapter preserves that caveat and does not statistically combine the survey with the IFS deprivation data.

Source:
https://www.local.gov.uk/publications/state-sector-artificial-intelligence

Current 2026 Local AI work from MHCLG also continues to support the chapter's constructive counterweight: responsible adoption can be supported through reuse, reduced duplication and shared tools rather than requiring every council to build the same capability independently.

Source:
https://mhclgdigital.blog.gov.uk/2026/03/16/introducing-local-ai/

No manuscript correction is required.

## NIST AI RMF status

NIST currently states that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. The book's research note already says this and uses AI RMF 1.0 only as an example of ordinary lifecycle risk-management practice, not as a claim about a final or mandatory standard.

Current source:
https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework

No manuscript correction is required.

## Release decision

The publication review found no time-sensitive factual claim that requires a manuscript correction before release.

The larger editorial change required for publication was structural rather than factual: the original working essay and later companion pieces have now been reshaped into a 12-chapter short book, with the superseded “Privatization of Competence” section removed because *The Headcount Illusion* covers the same ground with stronger evidence.

The release candidate can therefore publish from the committed Desk snapshot after this review lands. Future editions should recheck live government AI guidance, NIST framework status, local-government AI adoption data, and any newer fiscal evidence before replacing the Shelf edition.
