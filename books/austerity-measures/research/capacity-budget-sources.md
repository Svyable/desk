## Public value and spending-control reform

HM Treasury, **“Public Value Framework and supplementary guidance”** (13 March 2019).

https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-value-framework-and-supplementary-guidance

The framework is explicitly presented as a practical tool for understanding how well public money is converted into policy outcomes. The chapter uses it only for the general point that value for money is not identical to expenditure reduction.

HM Treasury, **“Reforming the spending control and accountability framework”** (26 November 2025).

https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/reforming-the-spending-control-and-accountability-framework

Useful claims:

- The reformed framework is intended to improve value for money while increasing capacity and speed of delivery.
- The reforms are also intended to let central government functions spend more effort on building capability.

This is useful evidence that fiscal control and capability-building are not inherently opposing objectives.

## Building an AI-ready public workforce

OECD, **“Building an AI-ready public workforce: Implications and strategies”** (19 January 2026).

https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/building-an-ai-ready-public-workforce_b89244c7-en.html

Useful claims:

- AI can improve public-sector efficiency and service quality, but many institutions are constrained by AI skills gaps.
- The OECD describes internal AI capability as important for maintaining accountability, ensuring compliance, and aligning AI use with institutional objectives.
- The brief points to training different groups of staff, hiring digital and data professionals, and mechanisms for innovation and continuous learning.

The chapter uses this to support the claim that internal competence remains a recurring operating requirement even when models or implementation services are purchased externally.

## UK government AI lifecycle and assurance

Government Digital Service, **“Artificial Intelligence Playbook for the UK Government”** (10 February 2025).

Landing page:

https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-playbook-for-the-uk-government

HTML version:

https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-playbook-for-the-uk-government/artificial-intelligence-playbook-for-the-uk-government-html

Relevant principles include:

- meaningful human control at the right stages
- management of the full AI lifecycle
- commercial involvement from the start
- sufficient skills and expertise to implement and use AI
- appropriate assurance

The playbook also says public organisations remain responsible for outputs and decisions made or supported by commercially purchased AI. It calls for live monitoring, feedback and human-review mechanisms, chains of responsibility, traceability, recourse, and lifecycle management.

These are cited in the chapter as examples of capabilities that persist after procurement rather than one-time launch requirements.

## NIST AI risk-management lifecycle

National Institute of Standards and Technology, **AI Risk Management Framework 1.0** and AI Resource Center Core guidance.

https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework

https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/

Status note: NIST states that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised as of 2026. The chapter therefore uses the current 1.0 material as an example of ordinary lifecycle risk-management practice, not as a claim about a final or mandatory standard.

Relevant capabilities described in the Core include:

- governance and clear organizational responsibility
- trained personnel
- inventories of AI systems
- ongoing monitoring and periodic review
- independent assessment where appropriate
- human oversight
- management of third-party AI risks
- appeal and override mechanisms
- incident response and recovery
- change management
- safe decommissioning

The manuscript’s inference is that cheaper model inference does not make these stewardship functions free; as routine cognition becomes cheaper, they may become a larger share of the remaining institutional cost.

## Argument note

“The Capacity Budget” is the author’s synthesis, not the name of an official government accounting framework. It proposes pairing ordinary fiscal questions with explicit statements of what an institution must still be able to do after a saving is taken and how that capability could be demonstrated.

The chapter deliberately treats capabilities as verbs rather than protected teams: change a rule safely, reconstruct a decision, switch a supplier, recover a service, investigate an exception, monitor an AI system, hear an appeal, and rebuild after failure. This is intended to make capability claims testable enough that they can support cuts as well as resist destructive ones.
