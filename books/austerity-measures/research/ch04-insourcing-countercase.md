# Chapter 4 research note — headcount, capability, and the insourcing countercase

## Why this belongs in the chapter

Chapter 4, “The Headcount Illusion,” already uses the UK government’s January 2025 *State of digital government review* to show how a headcount constraint can move labor from payroll into more expensive external contracts. The argument is sound, but the evidence can do more work than the chapter currently asks of it.

The same primary review contains an internal counterexample: DVLA began building a primarily insourced digital capability in 2015, including a Centre of Digital Excellence with academic qualifications, professional certifications, and apprenticeships. The review says 13% of DVLA’s digital and technology organization was either in that talent pipeline or had graduated from it, and that all junior software engineers, cloud engineers, software-development engineers in test, and business analysts were coming through those programs.

Source: Department for Science, Innovation and Technology and Government Digital Service, *State of digital government review*, 21 January 2025.
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/state-of-digital-government-review/state-of-digital-government-review

## The sharper mechanism

The headline figures remain useful: in 2023 the UK public sector spent about £26 billion on digital and data; less than 20% went to permanent public-sector staff; 55% went to contractors, managed-service providers, and IT consultants. Contractors represented about 18% of the combined contractor/permanent headcount but about 40% of the cost, and the review estimated an average annual cost of £182,000 per contractor versus £61,000 per permanent employee.

But those figures should not be allowed to imply that the only alternatives are “hire a civil servant” or “buy an expensive contractor.” The DVLA example introduces a third variable: **the institution can invest in producing capability**.

That matters because Chapter 4’s deepest subject is not employment classification. It is whether the state retains enough knowledge to specify, test, change, contest, and recover the systems through which it acts.

A useful future prose revision could therefore distinguish three decisions that headcount metrics tend to collapse:

- whether a capability should be owned internally or purchased externally;
- whether a current task is temporary or recurring;
- whether the institution is buying today's output or building tomorrow's competence.

The last distinction is especially important in an AI-era state. Training pipelines can look inefficient against a contractor who arrives already productive. Yet an apprenticeship or junior role can create institutional knowledge and a future senior operator, while a contract can purchase immediate output without replenishing the buyer's own expertise.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The DVLA example is not evidence that insourcing is universally cheaper or better. The government review is a system-level diagnostic, not a controlled evaluation of DVLA’s model. A mature agency with sustained demand may be able to justify permanent technical teams and training pipelines that would be wasteful for a small body with intermittent specialist needs.

Nor does the three-times-average-cost comparison establish that contractors are three times less productive or three times worse value. Contractors can be senior specialists, can cover short-lived needs, and may bring expertise that would be expensive or impossible to maintain continuously in-house. Managed services and consultancy also include outputs and capabilities that are not cleanly comparable with employee salaries.

The stronger claim is narrower: **when a headcount rule makes an external worker easier to fund than an internal worker regardless of the capability being purchased, the accounting boundary can distort the make-or-buy decision.** The January 2025 review itself attributes part of the imbalance to headcount restrictions and says temporary staffing hinders retention of institutional knowledge.

There is also a later policy response worth noting. The UK government’s *Digital and Data Benefits Framework*, published in 2026, repeats the 2025 review’s contractor/capability evidence in guidance for business cases. That does not independently validate the original estimates, but it shows the government has carried the capability problem forward into its investment framework rather than treating it as a one-off rhetorical finding.

## Editorial use

The best later manuscript change is probably small. The existing chapter already contains the line that a government can “buy an impressive AI system” yet lose independence from the seller’s explanation of it. The DVLA countercase can make that argument less defensive and more constructive: internal capability is not merely headcount preserved against cuts; it can be deliberately manufactured through apprenticeships, junior technical roles, and career paths.

That would also sharpen the chapter’s closing image of “a spare engineer.” The capacity at stake is not only spare labor sitting around for an emergency. Some apparently inefficient headcount is a **reproduction system for expertise**. An institution that removes every beginner because an experienced contractor is cheaper this quarter may eventually discover that it has optimized away the route by which its own experienced people are made.

Keep the boundary explicit: this is an argument for capability-aware make-or-buy decisions, not an argument for public-sector self-sufficiency or unlimited permanent staffing.
