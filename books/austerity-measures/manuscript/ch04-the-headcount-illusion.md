# The Headcount Illusion

A government can reduce its headcount without reducing the amount of labor it buys. It only has to change which column the labor appears in.

A civil servant leaves. The position is frozen. The department still needs the work, so a contractor arrives. The salary line disappears and an invoice appears. The organization chart becomes smaller. The dependency does not.

Sometimes this is exactly what should happen. Governments need outside suppliers. A department should not manufacture every laptop, run every data center, or permanently employ a specialist it needs for six months. A good supplier can bring technology, experience and scale that would be wasteful to reproduce inside every institution. The interesting question is not whether government buys expertise. It is whether the government still knows enough to buy it well.

Britain's 2025 review of digital government makes the distinction unusually visible. The public sector spent about £26 billion on digital and data in 2023. Less than one fifth went to permanent public-sector staff. About £14.5 billion—55 percent—went to contractors, managed-service providers and IT consultants. Looking only at permanent staff and contractors, contractors represented about 18 percent of headcount but 39 percent of cost; the review put average annual contractor cost at £182,000, against £61,000 for a permanent employee.

Those figures are not proof that contractors are bad value. A scarce specialist can be worth three ordinary salaries, and the annual averages do not make the jobs interchangeable. The more revealing finding was institutional. The review said headcount restrictions had made it easier to secure more expensive third-party resources than permanent staff, while temporary staffing made institutional knowledge harder to retain. It also found that only 28 percent of surveyed leaders believed their organizations had sufficient internal capability to monitor, track and drive supplier performance.

The useful counterexample arrived a year later. In January 2026 the British government announced plans to halve external-consultancy spending, bring more civil-service training in-house through a new National School of Government and Public Services, and prepare officials for a more digital and AI-heavy state. By March, Government Skills had assembled a cross-government curriculum ranging from basic AI literacy to practitioner material on machine learning, natural-language processing and large language models. The current digital profession framework goes further still, treating capability as something that can be described in roles, skills and levels, assessed, recruited for and planned.

None of those announcements proves that Whitehall has solved the dependency problem. A training catalogue is not competence, a target is not an outcome, and cutting consultancy can become another blunt quota if departments still need the work. What matters is that the policy response itself has moved closer to the right question. The objective is not simply fewer outsiders or more insiders. It is whether the state can use outside capability without surrendering its own capacity to judge it.

This is austerity behaving like water. Block one channel and the pressure finds another.

A department can obey a headcount ceiling while purchasing the missing capability back at a higher day rate. The arithmetic may still be defensible if the external resource is temporary, better, or genuinely cheaper over its useful life. But the headcount number itself no longer tells us whether the state has become leaner. It may simply have become better at renting itself back.

Digital government makes this accounting error more consequential because software is no longer office equipment around the state. It is increasingly one of the ways the state acts. Taxes, benefits, licenses, borders, procurement, identity services and regulatory processes run through technical systems. The people who understand those systems are therefore part of the institution's ability to understand its own decisions.

The National Audit Office calls the necessary buyer an "intelligent client." The phrase matters because it rejects a false choice. Government does not need to build everything itself in order to retain judgment. It needs enough technical and commercial competence to specify what it wants, assess whether progress is real, understand integration risk, challenge cost and schedule claims, and know what remains its responsibility when delivery sits with a supplier.

That last point is easy to lose. Outsourcing execution does not outsource the public obligation attached to the execution. A payment system may be built by a vendor, but the government still owns the fact that citizens get paid. A model may arrive through an API, but a department still owns the decision it allows that model to influence.

Artificial intelligence makes the intelligent-client problem harder because the object being purchased can change underneath the contract. Model behavior can shift across versions. Usage can change cost. Data can pass through several services. Evaluation is probabilistic. A vendor can improve a model while breaking a workflow. A procurement decision can quietly determine who later controls logs, prompts, evaluations and operational knowledge.

A government with little internal technical depth can still buy an impressive AI system. What it may no longer be able to buy is independence from the seller's explanation of that system.

The failure rarely announces itself at contract signature. Each individual purchase can look sensible. One company supplies the platform, another implements it, a consultancy maps the process, a managed-service provider runs operations. Then a case crosses the seams. Two apparently similar claimants receive different outcomes. A rule has been encoded somewhere that nobody in policy recognizes. A model version changes. A contract ends. The audit trail depends on proprietary tooling. The department discovers that it owns the public decision but not a complete explanation of the machinery that produced it.

This is a peculiar form of privatization: not necessarily of the service, and not necessarily of legal authority, but of comprehension.

There is an obvious counterargument. Internal capability is not free, and "institutional knowledge" can become a flattering name for expensive incumbency. Permanent teams can defend obsolete systems, resist standardization and accumulate people around work that should disappear. Contractors can transfer knowledge rather than drain it. Shared platforms can reduce the need for every agency to maintain duplicate expertise. An institution that treats every skill as sovereign will eventually use sovereignty as an excuse for bloat.

The current British strategy makes that objection more useful, not less. It combines partnerships with outside technology companies, a stated reduction in consultancy dependence, in-house learning and an objective to expand the share of digital, data and cyber professionals. That mixture is closer to reality than either "build everything yourself" or "buy whatever the market does better." A competent state will do both. The test is whether partnership enlarges the institution's options or quietly narrows them.

The boundary is therefore functional. What does the state need to know or be able to do in order to remain accountable for the service? Commodity execution can move outward. The capacity to specify, test, contest, switch, recover and learn is harder to surrender safely.

The same boundary appears in maintenance. The British review found a funding bias toward new programs over continuous improvement of existing systems. This is not mysterious. A transformation project has a beginning, a budget and a launch. Maintenance has Tuesday. Institutional memory has Wednesday.

AI intensifies the temptation because a prototype can make the saving look immediate. A model summarizes a case file in seconds, drafts the letter, finds a regulation and recommends the next action. A task that consumed an hour appears to consume a minute. The staffing calculation almost performs itself.

But the prototype measures something narrower than the institution must survive. It demonstrates the common case under observation. Government also gets disputed cases, malicious cases, missing-data cases, new-law cases, outages and cases in which the machine is confidently wrong. Those are not debris around public administration. They are often where public administration reveals whether it can still govern itself.

Australia's Robodebt scandal is useful here precisely because it predates the current generative-AI wave. It involved automated administration built from data matching, rules and debt-raising processes rather than a frontier language model. By 2019 the Federal Court had declared, with the government's consent, that one debt demand was not validly made. The government later announced that more than 400,000 debts would be zeroed or repaid, and a Royal Commission followed.

The Commission's recommendations on automated decision-making are, among other things, a list of capabilities an automated state has to retain. People need a clear route to review. Automated decision-making should be disclosed and explained in plain language. Business rules and algorithms need to be available for independent expert scrutiny. A competent body needs to be able to audit technical behavior, fairness, bias and usability.

None of that happens because a system has an appeals button. Someone has to understand the business rules, preserve the evidence, reconstruct what happened, recognize patterns across disputes and possess enough authority to change the system when those patterns reveal a defect. Automation may reduce the labor required to process routine cases. It does not eliminate the labor required to remain accountable for them.

This is why appeal is easy to misprice. In a throughput spreadsheet, an appeal is a loop in what should have been a straight line. A closed case opens again. Another person touches it. Service time rises. If the original decisions are mostly correct, requiring human review of every case would be wasteful.

A real appeal path, however, is also a sensor. The citizen may be wrong. Or the underlying data may be stale, a form may invite the wrong answer, a software update may have shifted behavior, a rare household may not fit the process, or a local office may be applying guidance differently. Without review, all of those possibilities can collapse into one operational status: processed.

An institution can become extremely efficient at refusing to discover that it is wrong.

The better use of AI is not to preserve every old job around a faster machine. It is to decide which saved hours should become savings and which should become capacity. Routine copying can disappear while disputed cases receive more attention. Automated checks can reduce clerical work while technical staff study recurring failures. A model can prepare an appeal file while a reviewer spends less time assembling it and more time deciding whether the original rule still makes sense.

This is harder to defend in a budget because prevention is shy evidence. A finance ministry can count the engineer's salary. It cannot easily count the procurement mistake the engineer prevents. It can count an appeals team. It cannot readily price the legitimacy created when a citizen discovers that government can hear an objection and correct itself. It can count a permanent employee against a headcount target while missing the bargaining power created by not being the least informed party in a vendor negotiation.

The savings from removing those capacities arrive immediately. Their absence tends to surface later under somebody else's heading: a project overrun, an emergency procurement, a court case, a compensation scheme, a cyber incident, a legacy-system crisis. The spreadsheet has not necessarily been falsified. It has stopped at the organizational boundary.

That is what makes headcount politically attractive. It is concrete. A minister can say that the civil service is smaller. Capability is harder to put in a headline. Can the department change a rule safely? Can it explain a decision without asking the supplier? Can it switch providers without reconstructing the service from observed behavior? Can it detect when an automated system behaves differently across groups? Can it operate during an outage? Does somebody inside understand the contract well enough to reject the next change order?

Those questions are less tidy than employee count, but they describe the institution the public actually depends on.

None of this rescues staffing from scrutiny. Every team can explain why its knowledge is unique. Bureaucracies can defend obsolete work in the language of resilience just as readily as finance ministries can defend arbitrary cuts in the language of efficiency. The answer is not to preserve headcount. It is to cut against an explicit model of capability.

If a task disappears, remove the task and the position around it. If a supplier can perform a commodity function better, buy it. If agencies maintain undifferentiated copies of the same system, consolidate them. If AI reliably removes an hour of clerical work, take the gain. Then ask what the institution must still be able to understand, challenge, modify and recover after the gain has been taken.

The future state may employ fewer people. Machine cognition should allow smaller teams to administer work that once required larger ones. But smaller teams will need deeper ownership: better tools, cleaner data, clearer authority, enough slack to investigate anomalies, and enough technical and commercial judgment to use suppliers without becoming captive to them.

Some capacity will look inefficient on an ordinary day. A maintainer who knows why an old system must not be restarted casually. An appeals officer with time to read. A procurement specialist who recognizes a familiar form of lock-in. Their best work may consist of preventing an event that never appears in the accounts.

That does not make every one of them necessary. It makes necessity the question.

Headcount gives a precise answer to something government should know: how many people it employs. The more useful sequence continues. How much labor does the state buy after those people are removed? What can it still do, understand, contest and repair for the money? Which capabilities are genuinely cheaper to rent, and which become more expensive once the institution has forgotten how to judge the rental?

A government that cuts ten employees and hires their knowledge back through a contract has not necessarily become smaller. A government that automates ten jobs and spends part of the saving on stronger review, engineering or procurement has not necessarily become larger.

The org chart is not the institution. The state becomes lean when it can do more with fewer resources while retaining the ability to govern the machinery doing the work. Lose that ability, and headcount has measured something else: the growing distance between government and its own competence.
