# The Headcount Illusion

A government can reduce its headcount without reducing the amount of labor it buys.

It only has to change which column the labor appears in.

A civil servant leaves. The position is frozen. The department still needs the work done, so a contractor arrives. The salary line disappears and an invoice appears. The organization chart becomes smaller. The dependency does not.

Sometimes this is sensible. Governments need outside suppliers. A department should not build every piece of software it uses, operate every data center, manufacture every laptop, or permanently employ every specialist it may need for six months. External firms can bring skills, competition, speed, technology and experience that would be wasteful to reproduce inside every public institution.

The problem begins when the accounting measure becomes the objective.

In January 2025, the British government published a review of the state of digital government. The numbers were unusually revealing. The public sector had spent about £26 billion on digital and data in 2023. Less than one fifth of that spending went to permanent public-sector staff. More than half went to contractors, managed-service providers and IT consultants.

The review estimated that contractors represented about 18 percent of headcount but roughly 40 percent of headcount cost. The average contractor in central government cost about three times as much per year as the average civil servant.

That alone does not prove that the contractors were poor value. A specialist hired for a difficult migration may be worth several times the cost of a generalist employee. Temporary expertise can be exactly what a temporary problem requires.

The more important finding was the mechanism behind some of the spending. The review said headcount restrictions had made it easier to secure costly third-party resources than permanent staff and that heavy use of temporary staff hindered the retention of institutional knowledge.

This is austerity behaving like water. Block one channel and the pressure finds another.

The state can obey a headcount ceiling while purchasing the missing people back at a higher day rate. It can reduce payroll while increasing dependence. It can call the result a smaller government even when the same work continues, now carried out by people whose knowledge may leave when the contract ends.

There is a difference between making the state leaner and making it rent itself back.

Digital government makes that distinction unusually important because software is not merely another category of office equipment. A modern public institution increasingly acts through technical systems. Taxes are assessed through them. Benefits are paid through them. Borders, licenses, courts, hospitals, schools, procurement systems, identity services and regulatory processes depend on them. Policy becomes software whether policymakers think of themselves as software managers or not.

The people who understand those systems are therefore not a decorative support function around the state. They are part of the state’s capacity to know what it is doing.

This is what makes a headcount target dangerous when applied without regard to capability. The target can be achieved by removing the people who understand a system while preserving the system itself. The institution then becomes dependent on somebody else to explain its own machinery back to it.

The National Audit Office uses a useful phrase for the capability that prevents this: the government must be an intelligent client.

An intelligent client can buy from a supplier without surrendering judgment to the supplier. It understands enough of the technology, product lifecycle and commercial structure to know what it is asking for, whether progress is real, what a change should cost, where integration risk sits, how switching might work, and which reassuring answer deserves another question.

This is not the same as doing everything internally. In fact, a good buyer can often outsource more confidently because it knows what must remain sovereign.

The distinction is familiar outside government. A homeowner does not need to become an electrician before hiring one. But a large organization commissioning a national payment system cannot operate on the homeowner model. It needs people capable of specifying the work, testing the result, understanding the failure modes, negotiating the contract and recognizing when the supplier’s preferred solution happens to increase the supplier’s future leverage.

The more important the system, the more expensive ignorance becomes.

Artificial intelligence raises the stakes.

The ordinary software contract was already difficult enough. An AI system adds new uncertainties: model behavior can change across versions; costs can move with usage; data may flow through several services; evaluation is probabilistic rather than purely functional; a vendor can improve a model while breaking a workflow; safety depends partly on the surrounding tools and permissions; a seemingly small procurement choice can determine who later controls logs, prompts, fine-tuning data, evaluations and operational knowledge.

A government that has lost its technical depth may still be able to buy an impressive AI system.

What it may no longer be able to buy is independence from the seller’s explanation of that system.

This creates a peculiar form of privatization. The visible public service may remain public. The minister remains accountable. The law remains public. The citizen still receives a decision bearing the authority of the state.

But the ability to understand how the decision-producing machinery works can migrate outward.

At first the migration feels efficient. A supplier already has the platform. Another supplier has the implementation team. A consultancy can map the process. A managed-service provider can run operations. An AI vendor can provide the model, evaluation tooling and monitoring dashboard. Each contract is defensible on its own.

Then someone asks a question that crosses the contracts.

Why did this claimant receive a different answer from an apparently identical claimant?

Which system of record supplied the fact the model relied on?

Can the department reproduce the decision without the vendor’s proprietary tooling?

What happens to the audit trail if the contract ends?

Who knows whether the model changed between the two decisions?

Which exceptions were encoded during implementation and never written into policy guidance?

Can another supplier take over without rebuilding the service from observed behavior?

The institution discovers that no single invoice purchased understanding.

Austerity can accelerate this because internal capability is expensive in the most visible way. Skilled engineers, data scientists, lawyers, commercial specialists and experienced operators are salaries every year. They require career paths, managers, training, retention, equipment and enough interesting work to keep them from leaving.

A consulting engagement arrives as a project.

Projects are easier to celebrate.

They have launch dates. They have transformation names. They have budgets that can sometimes be treated as investments rather than recurring operating costs. They produce milestones and steering committees and diagrams showing the future state.

Maintenance is less photogenic.

So is institutional memory.

The British digital-government review described another part of the same problem. Public-sector technology funding has often favored new programs over the continuous improvement of existing systems. Legacy technology accumulates while money is easier to secure for something described as transformation. The result is a state that can sometimes fund the replacement project more readily than the permanent people who would have kept the old system healthy enough to replace deliberately.

This is not peculiar to Britain. It is a recurring property of organizations that treat capability as an expense and novelty as an investment.

AI can make the imbalance worse because almost every institution can now produce a plausible modernization plan.

The prototype is cheap.

The demo works.

The model summarizes the case file in seconds. It drafts the letter. It finds the regulation. It classifies the application. It produces a risk score. It recommends the next action. A task that consumed an hour appears to consume a minute.

The temptation is immediate: multiply the apparent saving by the number of cases and remove the difference from the staffing plan.

But a prototype measures the cost of performing the common case under observation. An institution has to survive the strange cases, the disputed cases, the malicious cases, the missing-data cases, the new-law cases, the vendor-outage cases and the cases where the machine is confidently wrong.

Those cases are not statistical debris around the service.

In public administration, they are often where the state reveals what kind of state it is.

Australia’s Robodebt scandal is useful here precisely because it was not a story about a frontier generative model. It was an earlier form of automated administration, built from data matching, rules and debt-raising processes. The technological ingredients were less fashionable than today’s AI systems. The institutional lesson is therefore harder to dismiss as a temporary defect of immature models.

The scheme used tax-office income data in processes that raised alleged welfare debts. By 2019, the Federal Court had declared, with the government’s consent, that one such demand was not validly made. The government later announced that more than 400,000 debts raised under the scheme would be zeroed or repaid. A Royal Commission followed.

The Commission’s recommendations on automated decision-making read like a specification for state capacity. People affected by automated decisions should have a clear path to review. Government websites should disclose the use of automated decision-making and explain it in plain language. Business rules and algorithms should be available for independent expert scrutiny. An appropriate body should be able to monitor and audit automated systems for technical behavior, fairness, bias and usability.

Notice what these safeguards require.

Someone has to know the business rules.

Someone has to be able to explain the process.

Someone has to preserve enough information to reconstruct what happened.

Someone has to possess the authority and competence to review the machine.

Someone has to investigate patterns across the appeals.

Someone has to change the system when the evidence says the system is wrong.

These are labor requirements.

They may not require the same number of people as the manual process they replace. Often they should not. If software can calculate, copy, compare and draft reliably, preserving armies of clerks for the emotional comfort of familiar headcount would be its own kind of waste.

But the remaining jobs become more consequential, not less.

An automated state needs people who can handle the exception without treating it as an inconvenience to the automation. It needs technical staff who can reproduce behavior instead of accepting a dashboard. It needs lawyers who can distinguish a policy objective from a lawful implementation. It needs frontline workers with enough discretion to notice when the facts do not fit the rule. It needs auditors who can inspect the system rather than merely inspect the documentation generated about the system.

This is why appeal is easy to misprice.

In a throughput spreadsheet, an appeal looks like failure demand. The citizen comes back. A completed case becomes an open case again. Another employee touches the file. Service time rises. The beautiful straight line through the automated process acquires a loop.

The obvious optimization is to reduce the loop.

Sometimes that is correct. A good system should not produce avoidable disputes. Requiring human review of every correct routine decision would destroy much of the value of automation.

But the existence of a real appeal path does something a throughput measure cannot capture.

It gives the institution a sensor for its own errors.

An appeal is information arriving from the edge of the model.

The citizen may be wrong. The caseworker may have misunderstood. The underlying data may be stale. A form may have encouraged the wrong answer. A policy may contain a contradiction. A software update may have shifted behavior. A rare household may not resemble the cases the process was designed around. A fraudster may have discovered a loophole. A local office may be applying guidance differently. A model may have learned a correlation that nobody intended to become a rule.

Without review, these possibilities collapse into one operational category: processed.

A system can become extraordinarily efficient at refusing to discover that it is wrong.

The OECD has noted the connection between redress and trust in government AI. Automated decisions can feel rigid and unaccountable when people cannot challenge them. The practical issue goes beyond sentiment. If a citizen has no meaningful path to correction, the institution loses an important stream of error reports from the people most exposed to its mistakes.

The appeal process is therefore not merely a concession to the citizen.

It is part of the institution’s observability.

Engineers instrument complex software because an unobservable system eventually becomes impossible to operate safely. Public institutions need an equivalent discipline. They need to know not only how many decisions were produced and how quickly, but where decisions are contested, why they are overturned, which groups encounter more errors, which rules generate repeated exceptions, and whether the same failure keeps returning under different names.

A high-capacity AI state would use automation to make this kind of learning cheaper.

Routine cases would move quickly. The machine would prepare the file, check the obvious conflicts, surface relevant rules, draft explanations and preserve a structured record of what evidence supported the decision. Humans would spend less time copying facts between screens.

Some of the saved time would become genuine savings.

Some of it should become capacity.

More attention for the disputed case.

More technical work on the systems that generate recurring errors.

More investigation where fraud models produce weak signals rather than automatic accusations.

More procurement expertise to test the vendor’s claims.

More maintenance of the data the models depend on.

More evaluation before a new model version reaches millions of people.

More ability to change the policy implementation without waiting for a supplier’s next available team.

This is where the politics becomes difficult. A finance ministry can count the salary that remains. It cannot easily count the wrongful decision that never occurs because an evaluator found the problem in testing. It can count the cost of an appeals team. It cannot easily price the legitimacy created when a citizen discovers that the government can hear an objection and correct itself. It can count a permanent engineer against headcount. It may not have a line for the bargaining power created by not being technically captive to a supplier.

The savings from removing these capacities are immediate.

The cost of their absence arrives later and usually under somebody else’s heading.

A procurement failure appears in the project budget.

A cyber incident appears in emergency spending.

A wrongful decision appears in a court case, compensation program or public inquiry.

A brittle legacy system appears as a transformation emergency.

A lost expert appears as six consultants.

The spreadsheet has not been falsified. It has simply stopped at the organizational boundary.

This is one reason headcount is such an attractive political metric. It is concrete. A minister can say that the civil service is smaller. A chief executive can say that the department removed positions. A newspaper can compare one number with another.

Capability is harder to print in a headline.

How quickly can the department change a rule safely?

Can it explain a decision without asking the supplier?

Can it switch providers without losing operational memory?

Can it detect when its own automated system is behaving differently across groups?

Can it operate during an outage?

Can it investigate a citizen’s complaint without manually reconstructing the entire process from five databases?

Does somebody inside the institution understand the contract well enough to say no to the next change order?

Can the state still perform the task when the clever external team leaves?

Those questions describe a government more usefully than its raw employee count.

None of this is an argument for unlimited staffing. Institutional capacity can become its own excuse for accumulation. Every team can explain why its knowledge is unique. Every process can produce a story about the catastrophe it prevents. Bureaucracies are perfectly capable of defending obsolete work with the language of resilience.

The answer is not to stop cutting.

It is to cut against a model of the capability you intend to preserve.

If a task disappears, remove the task and the position around it.

If a supplier can perform a commodity function better, buy it.

If several agencies each maintain the same undifferentiated system, consolidate it.

If AI removes a genuine hour of clerical work, take the gain.

But before converting every gain into a smaller internal workforce, ask what happens to the institution’s ability to understand, challenge, modify and recover the service.

That is the part a headcount target cannot tell you.

The future state may employ fewer people. I suspect many capable governments eventually will. Machine cognition should allow small teams to administer work that once required much larger ones.

But smaller teams will need deeper ownership.

They will need better tools, cleaner data, clearer authority and stronger technical judgment. They will need enough slack to investigate anomalies instead of treating every anomaly as a threat to throughput. They will need career paths that make staying inside government plausible for people who could earn more elsewhere. They will need to be able to use suppliers without becoming the least informed party in the contract.

Most of all, they will need the institutional confidence to keep some capacity that looks inefficient on an ordinary day.

A spare engineer.

An appeals officer with time to read.

A procurement specialist who has seen the trick before.

A maintainer who knows which old system must not be restarted casually.

A policy official who remembers why the exception exists.

These people are easy to call overhead because their best work often consists of preventing something.

That does not make every one of them necessary.

It makes necessity the question.

Austerity Measures began with the problem of a ledger that records expenditure more readily than capability. Headcount is one of the clearest examples. It gives precision to a question that may not matter as much as it appears to.

How many people work for the state?

Useful.

How much does the state spend to accomplish the work after those people are removed?

Better.

What can the state still do, understand, contest and repair for that money?

That is the question worth managing.

A government that cuts ten employees and hires their knowledge back through a contract has not necessarily become smaller.

A government that automates ten jobs and uses part of the saving to build better review, engineering and procurement capability has not necessarily become larger.

The org chart is not the institution.

The invoice is not the capability.

The state becomes lean when it can do more with fewer resources while retaining the ability to govern the machinery doing the work.

If it loses that ability, the headcount has not measured efficiency.

It has measured the distance between the government and its own competence.
