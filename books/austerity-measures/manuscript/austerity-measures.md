# Austerity Measures
## The Politics of Scarcity in an Age of Abundant Intelligence

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There is an old way to make a government more efficient.

You cut it.

A department loses ten percent of its budget. Vacant positions remain vacant. A regional office closes. Training disappears because training is easy to postpone. A software migration is delayed because the old system technically still works. The experienced planner who retires is replaced six months later, if at all. Maintenance becomes next year’s problem. A team that once had four people has three, then two, then one person who knows why the strange exception exists.

The spreadsheet improves immediately.

For a while, the institution may appear to improve with it. Fewer people attend meetings. Projects once described as essential turn out not to have been essential. Managers discover procedures nobody can explain. Contractors are asked uncomfortable questions. Some forms vanish and civilization continues.

There is such a thing as waste.

Then the second-order effects begin.

A permit takes longer because the person who understood unusual applications left. A procurement officer does not have time to investigate a vendor’s technical claims. A maintenance problem becomes a replacement project. A citizen who once could reach someone knowledgeable reaches a portal instead. The remaining employees become very good at emergencies because emergencies consume the time that would otherwise have prevented them.

The institution still exists.

Its capability does not exist in quite the same way.

This distinction matters more in the age of artificial intelligence than it did before.

We are entering an economic period in which many forms of cognition can be produced at dramatically lower cost: drafting, searching, translating, classifying, summarizing, comparing, coding, checking documents, detecting anomalies, preparing analysis, explaining regulations, answering routine questions. The exact pace is uncertain, the systems remain fallible, and different occupations will change in different ways. But useful machine cognition is already becoming abundant enough that governments are being offered the same proposition companies hear every day: do more with less.

That proposition is not obviously wrong.

It is incomplete.

The political danger of the AI age is that governments will confuse cheaper intelligence with less need for institutions. They will automate clerical work, reduce headcount, declare a productivity gain, and discover later that they removed precisely the people who knew what the machines were supposed to accomplish.

Austerity could then perform its final trick: using an age of abundance to manufacture a new scarcity.

## The Spreadsheet Improves First

Austerity is often described as either a moral virtue or an ideological disease. Neither description is especially useful if it prevents us from seeing the ordinary arithmetic underneath it.

Governments cannot borrow without limit. Debt service is real. Large structural deficits can narrow future choices, expose a country to financing shocks, and force adjustment at the worst possible moment. A state that promises everything without considering the resources behind those promises is not being generous. It is making commitments on behalf of people who may not yet be old enough to vote.

The financial crisis of 2008 therefore created a real fiscal problem. Revenues fell. Banking systems required extraordinary support. Economic activity contracted. Borrowing rose sharply. Eventually, public balance sheets had to be repaired.

The argument was over the timing, composition, and speed of that repair.

That distinction mattered. During the crisis, Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh found that stronger planned fiscal consolidations in advanced economies were associated with larger growth disappointments than forecasters had expected, especially early in the crisis. It did not prove that every spending cut is self-defeating or that fiscal discipline can be postponed forever. It did show something less ideological and more inconvenient: the effects of consolidation depend on the condition of the economy in which it occurs.

Government expenditure is also someone else’s income. A cancelled infrastructure project removes work from a contractor. A reduction in local services can shift costs onto households. A dismissed public employee spends less. A delayed maintenance project does not vanish; it waits to return in a different budget year, often with friends.

The macroeconomic argument became famous because recessions are visible. The institutional argument received less attention because institutional decay is slower and harder to place on a chart.

England’s local governments offer a useful example.

The Institute for Fiscal Studies estimates that real-terms core funding per person for English councils fell by roughly 26 percent between 2010–11 and 2019–20. Councils did not respond by reducing every activity proportionally. They could not. Statutory duties remained. Acute needs remained. Children still required protection. Adults still required care. Homeless people still appeared at the door.

So the adjustable parts of the state absorbed more of the pressure.

Over that period, real spending per person on highways and transport fell about 45 percent. Culture and leisure fell about 45 percent. Housing fell about 44 percent. Planning and development fell about 58 percent. Services for young people and Sure Start fell about 67 percent. Spending on children’s social care outside those youth services rose.

Each decision had a logic.

If a council must choose between an adult who needs care tonight and a planning department whose vacancy might make a development take longer next year, the care need wins. If a child is at immediate risk, a youth program designed to reduce future risk can look optional. If a road is still passable, resurfacing can wait.

This is how austerity changes the character of an institution without formally changing its mission.

The state migrates from prevention toward emergency.

It becomes better at administering acute need than preventing acute need. It spends more of its attention on obligations that cannot be deferred and less on capacities whose absence will only be noticed later. Maintenance, planning, research, training, evaluation, data quality, technical expertise and institutional memory are particularly vulnerable because their benefits are partly counterfactual. When they work, something expensive often does not happen.

A bridge remains open.

A child does not enter crisis care.

A planning application does not become a three-year argument.

A contract does not lock a government into the wrong vendor.

A problem is noticed while it is still small.

Nothing happens.

Nothing happening is difficult to defend in a budget meeting.

## The Asset the Ledger Cannot See

There is a basic accounting asymmetry inside public institutions.

The salary of an experienced civil servant appears as an expense this year.

The knowledge lost when that person leaves usually does not appear as an impairment charge.

A training program costs money. The absence of trained people does not show up as a liability until a procurement fails, an incident is mishandled, a system becomes impossible to change, or a contractor discovers that nobody on the client side can challenge the invoice.

A maintenance team is visible in the budget. The bridge closure that team prevents is invisible because it never happens.

This makes state capacity easy to misprice.

Private companies are not immune to the same mistake, but markets at least possess a vocabulary for intangible assets. A buyer may pay a premium for a company’s engineering team, customer relationships, proprietary process, organizational knowledge, brand, data, or ability to execute. The balance sheet may not describe those capabilities perfectly, but everybody understands that the acquired company is worth more than its desks and bank account.

Government often talks about its equivalent capabilities as overhead.

People who understand the tax code deeply are payroll.

People who can negotiate a cloud contract are payroll.

People who know why the benefits system contains a strange exception for a rare family circumstance are payroll.

People who can translate legislation into operational rules are payroll.

People who understand how three legacy databases disagree about an address are payroll.

Then an austerity program removes some of that payroll and records a saving.

No corresponding line says that the institution’s ability to understand itself has fallen by twelve percent.

There is no neat impairment test for institutional memory.

That is a problem in any era. It becomes a larger problem when artificial intelligence arrives, because AI does not make complementary human and institutional capital less important. In many settings it makes that capital more valuable.

A powerful model given clean data, clear rules, reliable tools and an experienced operator can be transformative. The same model dropped into an institution with contradictory databases, unclear authority, undocumented exceptions, obsolete software and nobody empowered to change the workflow becomes a very expensive autocomplete system.

The model may be identical.

The surrounding institution determines the result.

This is the hidden balance sheet of the AI state.

The most valuable assets may be the ones public accounting still treats entirely as current expenditure: clean records, maintainable systems, people who understand the law, technical teams who can test vendor claims, procurement officers who know where lock-in hides, managers with enough slack to improve a process instead of merely surviving it, and frontline workers who can recognize when a citizen’s case does not fit the pattern.

These assets are expensive to build slowly and surprisingly easy to liquidate quickly.

Austerity notices the liquidation proceeds first.

## Cheaper Intelligence, More Expensive Institutions

Imagine two governments purchasing access to the same frontier AI system.

The first has reasonably good data. Its regulations have been converted into operational rules that can be inspected. Its case-management systems expose usable interfaces. Procurement teams understand technical systems well enough to distinguish a capability from a demonstration. Experienced civil servants can describe the odd cases hidden behind apparently simple policies. Security teams can evaluate deployment risks. Lawyers know where automated recommendations require human review. Departments can run controlled trials and measure whether a new process actually improved outcomes.

The second government has spent fifteen years economizing on precisely those capabilities.

Its databases disagree with one another.

Important procedures live in the memories of employees nearing retirement.

Legacy systems are maintained by vendors nobody wants to annoy.

Procurement documents repeat fashionable language because the buyers lack enough technical depth to interrogate it.

Successful pilots struggle to become infrastructure.

Failed pilots leave slide decks.

Both governments have AI.

Only one possesses the complementary assets required to use it well.

This is not hypothetical enough to ignore. In 2025 the OECD surveyed 200 uses of AI across eleven core government functions. It found real opportunity: automation, faster public services, forecasting, support for decision-making, and more responsive administration. It also found adoption to be fragmented and uneven. Skill gaps, legacy information technology, limited data, constrained budgets, and the special requirements of public accountability all slow the move from a successful experiment to a dependable public capability.

The limiting factor is often not model intelligence.

It is everything around the model.

This is easy to miss because demonstrations begin with the machine. The model writes the letter. The model classifies the document. The model finds the suspicious transaction. The model summarizes the regulation. The model predicts the maintenance need.

But intelligence does not act on reality directly.

Institutions act.

Somebody must decide the evidentiary standard required before a citizen is accused of fraud. Somebody must determine whether the planning rule was encoded correctly. Somebody must own the appeal process. Somebody must know which database is authoritative. Somebody must notice when the model’s excellent average performance hides terrible performance for a rare category of case. Somebody must be able to change the system when Parliament changes the law on a Friday afternoon.

Cheap intelligence increases the value of institutional legibility.

The rules have to be findable.

Authority has to be clear.

Data has to mean what people think it means.

Systems need interfaces.

Decisions need provenance.

Exceptions need somewhere to go.

Failures need owners.

Austerity that weakens those capabilities in order to afford AI would be like selling the wiring because electricity has become cheap.

## Automation Can Hide a Hollow Institution

Suppose an agency has a backlog.

An AI system summarizes applications, checks whether documents are present, drafts routine correspondence, classifies cases, retrieves relevant policy, and reduces the number of staff hours required for each file.

Good.

The backlog falls.

Management calculates the saving and removes positions.

The next generation of the system improves further. More positions disappear. The organization is now lean, automated and frequently mentioned in presentations about public-sector transformation.

Then the regulation changes.

Or a court decision changes its interpretation.

Or an unusual class of applications appears.

Or the vendor updates the model.

Or auditors discover that one category of claimant has been systematically misclassified.

Now the agency needs people who understand not merely how to process a case but why the process exists, how the rules interact, where exceptions come from, what evidence matters, and which part of the technical system should change.

Many are gone.

The agency retained throughput and lost comprehension.

Artificial intelligence is unusually capable of concealing this loss for a while.

It can rewrite bad documentation. It can translate between old interfaces. It can generate temporary integrations. It can answer routine questions that once exposed the fact that nobody had written down the answer. It can make a service feel smoother even while the institution behind the service becomes more difficult to understand.

This is one of the strangest risks of successful automation: it can postpone the moment when institutional weakness becomes visible.

The system works because the common cases are common.

Then reality submits an edge case.

Reality has a large regression suite.

The point is not that a human workforce would never have made the mistake. Humans make astonishing mistakes, forget rules, lose files, misunderstand policy, discriminate, improvise badly, and sometimes preserve useless procedures simply because they are familiar. AI can make many public processes more accurate and humane.

The danger is allowing improved average performance to justify removing the institution’s ability to understand exceptional performance.

An agency needs enough knowledge left inside it to know when the automation is wrong.

Otherwise reliability becomes dependence on a system nobody is still qualified to question.

## The Privatization of Competence

There is a version of austerity in which government becomes smaller.

There is another in which government merely stops employing the people who govern it.

The distinction is becoming important.

Consider a ministry that once maintained its own statistical team, software engineers, policy researchers, contract specialists, investigators and operational managers.

Over time these functions are reduced.

Not abolished.

Purchased.

The statistical analysis comes from a consultancy. The software comes from a platform vendor. The fraud detection comes from an AI company. The cloud infrastructure comes from another company. The contact center is outsourced. The consultants overseeing the transformation are also contracted.

The organization chart becomes admirably lean.

The invoices become fascinating.

This arrangement can be perfectly sensible. Private firms possess specialized capabilities governments should often buy. A municipality does not need to manufacture servers. A health department does not need to design its own graphics processors. A tax agency need not reproduce every piece of commodity software internally merely to prove its independence.

The danger begins when procurement stops supplementing state capacity and starts replacing it.

The state retains legal authority.

The contractor acquires operational knowledge.

Artificial intelligence sharpens the problem because advanced systems will not merely provide back-office tools. They will increasingly mediate what institutions notice.

Which tax returns look suspicious?

Which neighborhoods require inspection?

Which infrastructure projects appear most urgent?

Which applications deserve additional scrutiny?

Which companies merit regulatory attention?

Which risks appear to be increasing?

A government that depends entirely on external systems to make these patterns visible is outsourcing more than computation. It is outsourcing part of its nervous system.

The relevant question is not whether the server belongs to the state. Most governments sensibly depend on privately produced technology. The relevant question is whether the institution can independently evaluate what the technology is doing.

Can it reconstruct a decision?

Can it test the system against cases the vendor did not choose?

Can it switch suppliers without reconstructing the service from memory?

Can it operate through a contract dispute?

Can it recognize when the vendor’s incentives differ from its own?

Can it tell technical necessity from a sales presentation?

A state that cannot answer those questions may retain sovereignty in law while losing it operationally.

The minister can still sign the order.

Someone else increasingly knows what the order will do.

## Austerity Is Not Neutral About Power

This is why austerity is not only a question of public spending.

It changes bargaining power.

When government loses engineers, technology suppliers become harder to negotiate with. When regulators lose experienced staff, regulated firms may understand the regulated system better than the regulator. When local authorities lose planning capacity, developers with deep legal and technical teams become relatively more capable. When tax authorities lose specialist knowledge, sophisticated taxpayers gain an advantage over ordinary ones.

Weakness is not distributed evenly.

There are people who benefit when the state cannot see very well.

This matters because AI is sometimes described as a universal solvent for expertise. Give everyone a sufficiently capable model, the argument goes, and the old differences in technical knowledge begin to collapse.

Some differences will collapse.

A novice with AI can perform tasks that once required years of familiarity. A small authority can obtain analysis it could never have afforded from a consultancy. A caseworker can query policy without memorizing a manual. A regulator can search enormous filings. A citizen can ask a system to explain a letter written in language no ordinary person would use voluntarily.

But experts receive the same tools.

A company with hundreds of engineers, lawyers, economists, lobbyists and proprietary data does not remain still while the regulator gains a chatbot. Its experts become augmented too. They can search faster, model more scenarios, draft more arguments, inspect more regulations, and prepare for more possible objections.

AI may compress the gap between novice and competent.

It can also accelerate the people already near the frontier.

The political economy therefore depends on who retains enough underlying expertise to interrogate the machine.

The scarce ability is not producing a plausible answer.

It is knowing which question matters, which evidence is missing, which assumption should be challenged, and when a confident response does not fit the world.

Austerity determines whether government remains strong on that side of the table.

## The Poor Get Automation First

New technology does not arrive evenly.

Affluent people often experience it as convenience.

Poorer people often experience it as administration.

The wealthy receive an AI wealth manager.

The poor receive an AI eligibility check.

The wealthy receive a concierge.

The poor receive a chatbot.

This is not inevitable, and the comparison can become glib if pushed too far. Automated public services can be vastly better than the systems they replace. A good digital assistant can explain eligibility at midnight, translate a confusing form, remind someone about missing evidence, prevent an unnecessary trip across town, or help a person navigate a bureaucracy that was never particularly humane when it was entirely staffed by humans.

The problem is the direction of the fiscal incentive.

Under austerity, automation is especially attractive where it can remove labor costs from high-volume services. Those services are often the ones used most by people who have little ability to purchase an alternative.

A wealthy household can hire an accountant when the tax portal becomes confusing.

A company hires counsel when regulation becomes complicated.

A professional hires an immigration specialist.

A property owner hires a planning consultant.

The person without money encounters the interface alone.

If governments are not careful, the machine will become the default public servant for people with the least private bargaining power, while human expertise remains available to those who can purchase it outside the state.

This is why the familiar phrase “human in the loop” is not enough.

Which human?

Available to whom?

At what stage?

With what authority?

A human whose role is merely to confirm the machine’s recommendation is not meaningful oversight. A human with thirty seconds to review a complex case is not meaningful discretion. A human theoretically reachable after four failed chatbot sessions is not meaningful access.

Machine intelligence is becoming cheaper. Human attention may therefore become more valuable precisely because it is the remaining scarce resource.

Markets already understand this. Premium services frequently sell access to a competent person. The most expensive hotel does not boast that nobody is available at the desk. The wealthiest clients of a financial institution do not enter the same support queue as everyone else.

A democratic state should be cautious about reproducing that hierarchy in public administration.

The purpose of machine intelligence should be to make human judgment more available where judgment matters most, not to reserve it for citizens who can buy their own humans elsewhere.

## The Legitimacy Tax

Governments possess a resource companies do not.

Legitimate coercive authority.

A citizen can stop using a streaming service. She cannot subscribe to a different tax authority. A family cannot opt into a rival planning law because the application process is frustrating. A defendant cannot select a different criminal justice system with a better interface.

Public institutions therefore carry a burden that ordinary consumer products do not. They must do more than produce acceptable average outcomes. They must make the use of public authority contestable enough to remain legitimate.

This becomes harder as decisions become automated.

Consider a citizen denied a public benefit.

Under the old system, the denial might have been slow, bureaucratic and wrong. There was no golden age in which every caseworker was wise and every file was handled fairly. But a recognizable chain of responsibility existed somewhere: a caseworker, a supervisor, a regulation, an appeal.

Now imagine that the decision emerges from a mixture of statutory rules, statistical models, vendor software, identity checks, automated document analysis, fraud indicators and data shared by several agencies.

The result may be more accurate overall.

It may also be harder for the person on the receiving end to understand what happened.

Suppose, purely for illustration, that such a system is correct in 98 percent of cases. Administratively, that sounds excellent. For the person in the remaining two percent, the system’s population-level success is not much comfort. The error occupies the entire case.

Highly reliable systems create a peculiar problem: their mistakes are surrounded by evidence that the system usually works.

That makes explanation and appeal more important, not less.

This creates what might be called a legitimacy tax. The more powerful and automated an administrative system becomes, the more seriously government must invest in provenance, explanation, independent audit, contestability and meaningful human review.

Those things cost money.

Austerity creates pressure to treat them as friction.

Appeals are inefficient if the objective is throughput.

Due process is inefficient if the objective is throughput.

Independent review is inefficient.

Public consultation is inefficient.

Legislative scrutiny is extremely inefficient.

Democracy contains expensive delays on purpose.

The question is not whether technology can remove friction. It can. The harder question is which frictions were protecting legitimacy rather than merely wasting time.

An AI system that processes a million cases overnight is not automatically a better public institution than one that takes a week.

Sometimes the waiting room was where the appeal lived.

## The Temptation of Automated Denial

Austerity also influences what the machine is asked to optimize.

Consider two instructions given to a benefits agency.

One is to use artificial intelligence to help every eligible person receive the service Parliament intended to provide.

The other is to reduce the administrative cost of processing claims.

These objectives overlap considerably.

They are not identical.

A system organized around the second objective may discover that the cheapest application is the application never completed.

Complexity can ration services.

Documentation requests reduce demand. Long waits reduce demand. Repeated identity checks reduce demand. Suspicion scores can reduce demand. Appeals that require substantial effort reduce demand.

Every obstacle can look administratively efficient because people defeated by the obstacle disappear from the workload.

This phenomenon predates artificial intelligence. Bureaucracies have always been capable of rationing services through inconvenience. AI simply makes the machinery more adaptive.

A traditional bureaucracy can lose your form.

An intelligent bureaucracy can learn which people are unlikely to complete it.

A traditional fraud system can inconvenience everyone with the same crude rule.

An intelligent one can concentrate scrutiny on a statistical profile.

A traditional public website can be incomprehensible.

An AI-mediated service can be perfectly comprehensible while steering people toward outcomes that are cheaper for the institution.

No one has to be malicious.

Optimization is enough.

When an agency is told simultaneously to preserve a legal entitlement and reduce the cost of providing it, the metric begins doing politics.

This is why public-sector AI cannot be governed by accuracy alone. A model can be highly accurate while strengthening the wrong institutional objective.

The questions are distributional and constitutional as much as technical.

Who gains capability?

Who becomes easier to monitor?

Who bears the cost of errors?

Who can appeal?

What behavior does the metric silently reward?

What happens when the optimization succeeds?

Austerity asks whether the service can become cheaper.

Democratic government must also ask whether the service still performs the purpose for which citizens created it.

## The Fiscal Mirage

There is a recurring trick in public finance.

Move a cost somewhere else and call it a saving.

Close a preventive service. A later crisis appears in another department.

Delay maintenance. A replacement project arrives under a capital budget years later.

Reduce legal support. Cases reach court less prepared.

Cut planning capacity. Developments take longer and private applicants pay consultants to navigate the delay.

The spreadsheet has borders.

Reality does not.

AI will create new opportunities for this kind of accounting illusion because the immediate gains can be unusually legible.

Headcount fell.

Processing time fell.

Cost per transaction fell.

Wonderful.

What happened to appeals?

What happened to repeat contacts?

What happened to people who gave up?

What happened to error correction?

What happened to the unusual cases that now consume more senior time?

What happened to the institution’s ability to change the system later?

What happened to vendor costs after the introductory contract expired?

A chatbot can reduce telephone calls and still increase the total amount of citizen time required to resolve a problem. An automated eligibility system can reduce processing cost while increasing the burden on appeal tribunals. A predictive maintenance system can make inspection cheaper while creating an expensive blind spot if the model does not recognize a new failure mode. A platform can reduce internal technical staffing and later make migration so difficult that the government effectively pays rent on its own dependence.

These are not arguments against automation.

They are arguments about the accounting unit.

A department can optimize its own budget while making the wider state more expensive. Government can optimize its administrative cost while transferring the cost to citizens. A minister can collect a saving this year while a successor receives the failure later.

The danger is not only bad AI.

It is excellent AI combined with narrow accounting.

## The Capacity Dividend

There is a much better version of this story.

A government automates routine work and the work genuinely becomes cheaper.

The tax inspector spends less time assembling documents and more time investigating complex evasion.

The planner spends less time checking whether boxes are complete and more time resolving difficult applications.

The teacher spends less time preparing repetitive materials and more time with students who need attention.

The regulator spends less time reading standard disclosures and more time investigating anomalies.

The local authority spends less time answering the same question and more time solving the strange one.

The doctor spends less time writing routine notes and more time practicing medicine.

This is not technologically radical.

It is politically radical because it treats productivity gains as a source of capability rather than automatically as a source of headcount reduction.

Suppose a department can deliver the same service with twenty percent fewer labor hours.

It has choices.

Some savings can return to the treasury. Some can reduce waiting times. Some can improve quality. Some can expand enforcement. Some can be used to rebuild technical capacity that was previously outsourced. Some can create enough slack for employees to improve the system instead of merely processing the queue. Some roles can disappear while other roles become more important.

There is no universal ratio.

The distinction is between capturing an efficiency and harvesting an institution.

Organizations often erase productivity gains by immediately filling the freed capacity with more load. A hospital improves scheduling and then increases the number of patients each clinician must see until every saved minute disappears. A school automates administration and then enlarges class sizes. A local government introduces digital permitting and then reduces planning staff until the faster system develops a backlog again.

Productivity rose.

Saturation returned.

The institution is once again operating with no room for surprise.

Private firms do not always behave this way because they sometimes see additional productive capacity as an opportunity for additional revenue. A company that doubles the productivity of a good engineering team does not automatically fire half the engineers. If it has valuable things left to build, it builds more.

Government lacks the same market signal, so the allocation of the dividend becomes an explicit political choice.

What additional public capability is worth buying with cheaper cognition?

Faster courts?

Better tax enforcement?

More responsive planning?

More individualized education?

Better defense procurement?

Stronger competition policy?

Shorter benefit decisions?

Less administrative burden on citizens?

The answers will differ across countries and periods. But this is the argument the AI transition should produce.

The machine does not dictate that half the people disappear merely because it can perform half the tasks.

That is one possible distribution of the gain.

It is not a law of technology.

## Austerity During a Labor Transition

There is another reason public capacity matters now.

The labor market may be entering an unusually rapid period of reorganization.

The IMF estimates that almost 40 percent of global employment is exposed to artificial intelligence, with the figure around 60 percent in advanced economies. Exposure does not mean disappearance. In many jobs AI may complement workers, increase output, remove drudgery, or create demand for new forms of work. In others it may reduce labor demand or weaken bargaining power. We do not yet know the distribution well enough to speak with the confidence of people describing a completed industrial revolution from two centuries away.

That uncertainty is itself an argument for capable institutions.

A labor market changing quickly needs statistical agencies that can see what is happening before the categories become obsolete.

It needs education systems able to change curricula.

Training systems that can respond faster than a four-year degree cycle.

Benefits systems that can handle periods of displacement without converting every disrupted career into a personal administrative emergency.

Local governments that can see which regions are losing employment and which are attracting it.

Tax authorities able to adapt as the location of value creation changes.

Competition authorities able to distinguish productive concentration from durable control over essential infrastructure.

Housing and transport systems able to respond if economic geography shifts.

None of this requires the state to predict the exact future of work.

It requires the state to remain awake while the future arrives.

This is a poor moment to blind it.

A perfectly optimized institution has no spare capacity.

Engineers know what that means. Supply-chain managers relearned it during the pandemic. Financial regulators have learned it during crises. A system that uses every resource in normal conditions often has nothing left when conditions stop being normal.

Slack looks wasteful until the forecast is wrong.

During a major technological transition, the forecast will be wrong often.

## Fiscal Discipline Still Matters

There is a lazy response to this argument, and it should be rejected before it becomes comfortable.

If institutional capability matters, perhaps every existing institution should be protected. Every position is secretly capacity. Every program is preventive. Every budget increase is investment. Every attempt to cut expenditure is austerity and therefore dangerous.

No.

Governments waste money.

Programs persist after their purpose has disappeared. Subsidies survive because beneficiaries are organized and taxpayers are diffuse. Reporting requirements accumulate because nobody has authority to delete them. Paper-era procedures survive inside digital systems. Organizations acquire management layers whose main function is coordination made necessary by other management layers. Technology projects spend enormous sums reproducing processes that should have been removed rather than digitized.

AI will make some public roles smaller.

It should.

There are forms that should disappear, reports nobody should write, reconciliations machines should perform, routine correspondence nobody should manually draft, and queues that exist because information used to move slowly between buildings.

A government that refuses to capture any of those gains is not protecting capacity. It is protecting inertia.

Fiscal discipline remains necessary for the same reason engineering discipline remains necessary: resources have alternative uses.

Every unnecessary administrative process consumes money that could be spent on something else. Debt service consumes fiscal room. A country that enters the next recession with no balance-sheet capacity because it treated every good year as an excuse for permanent expansion has created its own form of brittleness.

The interesting distinction is not between cutting and spending.

It is between expenditure and capability.

A government can reduce expenditure while increasing capability. It can eliminate a manual process, simplify a regulation, close an obsolete office, automate routine work, consolidate duplicative systems, and emerge easier to use and easier to change.

It can also increase expenditure while becoming less capable, especially if money disappears into vendor dependency, fragmented systems, ceremonial management, and programs nobody is willing to evaluate honestly.

The test is what the institution can do afterward.

Can a citizen obtain the service more easily?

Can an unusual case be handled without improvising a crisis?

Can government bargain effectively with suppliers?

Can it understand its own systems?

Can it measure outcomes rather than activity?

Can a decision be appealed?

Can the institution change direction without purchasing its own knowledge back from a consultant?

Can it maintain the system after the transformation funding ends?

Can it distinguish an efficiency gain from a quiet reduction in service?

Those questions produce a more demanding politics than “spend more” or “spend less.”

They require knowing what the money is for.

## From Austerity to Husbandry

There is an older word for the discipline we need.

Husbandry.

To husband a resource is to use it carefully while preserving its productive capacity.

You prune. You repair. You replace. You invest. You remove what no longer works. You do not preserve every branch because it once grew there, and you do not confuse eating the seed corn with reducing agricultural overhead.

Public institutions should be managed with the same distinction.

Routine cognitive work should be automated aggressively where the evidence supports it and the risks are manageable. Savings should be visible. So should the condition of the institution that produced them.

Measure the backlog.

Measure service quality.

Measure repeat contacts and appeals.

Measure whether poor citizens experience the same improvement as rich ones.

Measure dependence on single suppliers.

Measure whether data can leave a vendor’s platform in a usable form.

Measure how long it takes to change a policy and propagate the change through software.

Measure whether staff can explain the system they are accountable for.

Measure whether the agency can detect when its own AI is wrong.

Measure maintenance, not just launch.

The exact metrics will differ by institution. The principle is that the productivity account and the capability account must be read together.

A government that saves ten million pounds and loses the ability to renegotiate a hundred-million-pound contract did not become efficient.

A government that removes five hundred clerical roles while reducing errors, improving access, shortening waits and preserving meaningful appeal may have become much more efficient.

Headcount does not settle the question.

Neither does spending.

Capability is the object being managed.

## The New Scarcity

Artificial intelligence will not abolish scarcity.

Energy remains scarce. Land remains scarce. Housing in desirable places remains scarce. Rare materials remain scarce. Political attention remains scarce. Trust remains painfully scarce. Money remains scarce because every use excludes another possible use.

But one important input is becoming less scarce.

Useful cognition.

That changes the shape of organizations.

The old bureaucracy rationed expertise because expertise was expensive. The citizen received ten minutes with the official because the official had fifty other people waiting. A small municipality could not afford a data scientist. A public defender could not research every possible precedent. A teacher could not prepare thirty individually adapted explanations every evening. A regulator could not inspect every page submitted by every company.

AI relaxes some of those constraints.

It does not eliminate judgment.

It lowers the cost of preparing judgment.

Once that happens, the bottleneck moves.

From producing options to selecting them.

From finding information to trusting it.

From writing policy to implementing it.

From generating code to maintaining systems.

From detecting problems to deciding which problems deserve action.

From cognition to coordination.

This means the AI age may place a higher premium on precisely the capabilities austerity tends to erode: judgment, institutional memory, maintenance, coordination, evaluation, legitimacy and the capacity to act collectively.

A society flooded with possible answers still needs institutions capable of deciding which answers become roads, schools, taxes, regulations, medicines, permits, rights and obligations.

The machine can propose ten thousand futures.

Politics still has to choose one.

This is why it is too simple to imagine AI as a substitute for the state’s cognitive labor. AI is closer to a multiplier.

A capable institution multiplied by cheap intelligence can become dramatically more capable.

A hollow institution multiplied by cheap intelligence can become hollow faster.

The system may answer immediately while nobody remains able to explain why the answer should govern.

## Do Not Automate the State You No Longer Understand

The old austerity argument asked whether government had become too large.

The AI-era argument has to ask something harder.

How much of an institution can disappear before the institution stops understanding itself?

This is not an argument for preserving every job.

Many jobs should change. Some should vanish. There are bureaucratic procedures whose disappearance would constitute a public service. There are layers of coordination designed for information environments that no longer exist. There are forms, reviews, reconciliations and reporting rituals whose original purposes were forgotten several reorganizations ago.

Automate them.

Delete them.

Enjoy it.

But distinguish dead bureaucracy from living capacity.

One is accumulated friction.

The other is the ability of a society to know what it is doing.

That ability is easy to undervalue because it is rarely visible when it works.

The experienced procurement officer prevents the terrible contract.

The maintenance team prevents the closure.

The planner notices the drainage problem.

The regulator understands the accounting trick.

The caseworker recognizes the unusual family circumstance.

The statistician notices the number that should not have moved.

The engineer refuses the vendor’s reassuring explanation.

Nothing happens.

This is success.

Austerity has always had trouble valuing success that looks like nothing happening. AI may make that problem worse because machines will perform visible work so cheaply. Drafting, classification, search and routine analysis will become inexpensive enough that the remaining invisible work—judgment, skepticism, accountability, memory, maintenance, legitimacy—can appear comparatively costly.

That comparison will tempt governments to economize in exactly the wrong place.

The cheapest state is not the state with the smallest payroll.

It is the state that prevents expensive problems from becoming expensive.

The most efficient institution is not the one in which every person is continuously busy.

It is the one that can still respond when the forecast is wrong.

And the strongest government is not the one with the most advanced AI.

It is the one that remains capable of disagreeing with it.

That is the paradox of austerity in the age of artificial intelligence.

We are acquiring machines that can make institutions dramatically more capable at almost the precise moment political systems may be tempted to hollow those institutions out in the name of those same machines.

Handled badly, the public sector becomes technologically sophisticated and institutionally weak.

Beautiful interfaces.

Instant answers.

Automated decisions.

No one quite sure how anything works.

The state will know more and understand less.

Handled well, something more interesting becomes possible.

Government can become smaller where machines genuinely remove drudgery and stronger where judgment, enforcement, care, negotiation, maintenance and adaptation remain scarce. It can return real savings to taxpayers without pretending every saved hour is evidence that another layer of competence should disappear. It can use abundant cognition to give scarce human attention to the cases that deserve it. It can buy technology without selling its ability to judge what it bought.

That is not the end of fiscal discipline.

It is fiscal discipline finally learning what not to cut.
