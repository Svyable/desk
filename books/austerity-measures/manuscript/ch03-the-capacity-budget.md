# The Capacity Budget

Budgets are excellent at counting things that move through accounts.

Money in. Money out. Salaries. Contracts. Grants. Buildings. Interest. Equipment. Transfers. A finance ministry can ask what a department spent last year, what it proposes to spend next year, which line grew, which line shrank, and who approved the difference.

This is indispensable. Governments that cannot account for money eventually become governments that cannot be trusted with it.

The difficulty is that many of the things a state most needs to preserve do not move through the accounts as objects.

The ability to rewrite an operational rule after Parliament changes a law is not a budget line.

The ability to detect that a supplier is failing is not a budget line.

The ability to restore a service after an outage, challenge an automated decision, investigate an unusual case, train a new inspector, interpret a dataset correctly, switch vendors, maintain an old bridge, or notice that a policy is producing the opposite of its intended effect is not a budget line either.

These are capabilities.

They consume money, but they are not the same thing as money. They are closer to verbs than assets. A capable institution can do certain things under pressure, repeatedly, without first assembling an emergency project to rediscover how.

Austerity becomes dangerous when the budget remains precise while the capability being purchased with it becomes vague.

The department knows that it has reduced administrative spending by eight percent. It does not know whether it can still change the benefits calculation without the software supplier. It knows that the regulator has 120 fewer employees. It does not know whether anyone remaining can reproduce the model used to prioritize inspections. It knows that maintenance spending was deferred. It does not know how much additional failure risk entered the system.

The fiscal measure is real.

The missing measure is real too.

A government serious about efficiency in the AI age therefore needs something alongside the ordinary budget: a capability budget.

Not another enormous reporting regime. Government is already perfectly able to bury a useful idea under several thousand mandatory fields. The point is almost the opposite. Before a material saving is accepted, somebody should be able to state what the institution must still be able to do afterward and how anyone will know whether that ability survived.

This changes the conversation.

Consider a tax authority proposing to automate routine correspondence and remove several hundred positions.

The ordinary budget question is straightforward. How much will the technology cost, how much labor will it save, and when does the investment pay back?

Those are good questions.

The capability questions begin one step later.

Can the authority still investigate a complicated taxpayer whose affairs do not resemble the routine cases? Can it update the system quickly after a court ruling? Can it explain how a disputed assessment was produced? Can it detect a new evasion strategy that the historical data does not contain? Can it operate if a vendor service is unavailable? Can it inspect the model rather than merely consume its score? Can a taxpayer obtain meaningful review without hiring someone who understands the system better than the state does?

If the answer remains yes, the positions may genuinely be redundant.

If the answer becomes no, the saving contains an unpriced liability.

The same method works outside administration.

A transport authority can reduce maintenance crews and still meet this year’s service target. The capacity question is whether it can still detect deterioration early and respond before a closure becomes necessary.

A planning department can automate document checking and reduce processing staff. The capacity question is whether it can still resolve a difficult development where law, infrastructure, local politics and engineering collide.

A health service can automate coding and scheduling. The capacity question is whether the saved labor increases clinical attention, disappears into a headcount target, or merely allows the same understaffed system to run faster until it reaches saturation again.

A defense ministry can outsource a technical function. The capacity question is whether the ministry remains able to specify the requirement, evaluate the work, integrate it with other systems and continue operating when the supplier relationship changes.

Capability is not an argument for preserving the existing organization chart.

It is a way to make cuts answer a harder question.

What must still work afterward?

Governments have circled this idea before. The British Treasury’s Public Value Framework was designed to ask how well public money is converted into policy outcomes rather than treating expenditure itself as success. In late 2025, after a review by the Office for Value for Money, the Treasury announced reforms to spending control intended not only to reduce cost but to increase the capacity and speed of delivery, with central functions spending more effort on building capability.

That language matters because it breaks a common false choice.

Fiscal discipline and state capability are not opposites.

A government can spend less and become more capable if it removes work that does not matter, simplifies processes, improves technology, shares commodity infrastructure, consolidates duplicated functions and retains the knowledge needed to operate the resulting system.

It can also spend less and become less capable.

The cash number alone cannot tell you which happened.

This is where AI makes the old budgeting problem unusually visible.

A serious AI deployment creates work that persists after the procurement and after the launch party.

Someone needs to know what the system is for.

Someone needs to understand its limitations.

Someone needs to monitor performance in production rather than assume the evaluation remains true forever.

Someone needs to decide which changes require retesting.

Someone needs to preserve an inventory of where automated systems are used.

Someone needs to investigate incidents.

Someone needs to handle appeals and overrides.

Someone needs to know how the system is retired without losing records or breaking a service.

These are not theoretical embellishments added by critics who would rather the technology not exist. They appear in ordinary technical guidance. The United Kingdom’s AI Playbook for government tells public bodies to maintain meaningful human control at the right stages, manage the full AI lifecycle, work with commercial colleagues, retain the skills required to use AI, and establish appropriate assurance. It says responsibility for decisions made or supported by commercially purchased AI remains with the public organization.

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework describes a similar operational burden in different language. Organizations need governance, clear responsibility, trained personnel, inventories, ongoing monitoring, independent assessment where appropriate, post-deployment feedback, appeal and override mechanisms, incident response, recovery, change management and safe decommissioning.

Notice what happened.

The machine made cognition cheaper.

It did not make stewardship free.

In some respects it made stewardship a larger share of the remaining cost.

This is familiar in engineering. As the price of one component falls, the system’s economics migrate toward the components that remain scarce. Cheap storage made data management more important, not irrelevant. Cheap compute did not eliminate architecture. Cheap software generation does not eliminate testing, integration or maintenance.

Cheap machine intelligence will not eliminate institutional capability.

It will expose which parts of capability were never about producing a first draft.

The OECD made the same point more directly in a 2026 brief on building an AI-ready public workforce. Public institutions can use AI to improve efficiency and service quality, but internal capability remains important for accountability, compliance and keeping AI use aligned with institutional objectives. The brief points toward hiring digital and data professionals, training different groups of staff, and creating mechanisms for innovation and continuous learning.

The phrase to notice is internal capability.

Training is often the first item cut when an organization is under pressure because training produces an immediate cost and an uncertain future benefit. Continuous learning is even more vulnerable. It looks suspiciously like employees not processing the queue.

But an institution using systems that change rapidly cannot freeze its competence at the date of procurement.

The people supervising AI in 2029 will not be able to rely entirely on the practices they learned in 2026. Model architectures will change. Vendors will change. Regulations will change. Attack techniques will change. Evaluation practices will change. The tasks being automated will change as earlier automation alters the surrounding workflow.

A static workforce operating dynamic machinery is another form of technical debt.

A capability budget would make this deterioration visible earlier.

It would begin by expressing essential capabilities as things the institution has to be able to do, not teams it has to preserve.

That distinction is important.

“Keep the AI governance unit at 47 people” is an input target. It may be justified, but the number tells us little by itself.

“Be able to identify every material AI system affecting the public, name an accountable owner, reproduce the evidence behind a disputed decision, detect material performance drift, suspend the system safely, and complete an independent review after a serious incident” describes capabilities.

Perhaps 47 people are required.

Perhaps twelve people with better tools can do it.

Perhaps some functions can be shared across government.

Perhaps an external evaluator should perform part of the work.

Once the desired capability is explicit, staffing and procurement become engineering questions rather than tribal claims on the budget.

This is one reason the capability budget should not become a list of protected departments.

Institutions are means.

Capability is the thing being preserved.

A department may be abolished while its necessary capabilities move elsewhere. Two teams may merge. A shared platform may replace twenty local systems. A manual review may disappear because a better automated control makes it unnecessary. A supplier may take over commodity infrastructure while the government retains architecture, security, contract and operational competence.

The test is whether the relevant ability survives at acceptable cost and risk.

Some capabilities are surprisingly easy to test.

If a department claims it can leave a technology supplier, run an exit exercise. How long would migration take? Which data can be exported? In what format? What functions exist only inside proprietary tooling? Which people understand the replacement path? What does the contract say? When was any of this last verified?

If an agency claims it can recover a service, run the recovery procedure.

If it claims an AI system can be challenged, file a realistic appeal and watch what happens.

If it claims policy changes can be implemented quickly, measure the time from a rule change to a tested production change.

If it claims internal technical competence, ask the internal team to explain the architecture without the supplier in the room.

If it claims its automated process treats unusual cases properly, sample the cases where humans overrode the machine and find out what the machine failed to see.

Capability becomes much less mystical when somebody has to demonstrate it.

This also protects the budget from an obvious objection.

Every public organization can tell a moving story about the disaster its funding prevents. If all counterfactual benefits are accepted without challenge, the language of capability becomes a universal defense against scrutiny. Nothing can be cut because everything might someday matter.

That is not discipline. It is storytelling with a purchase order.

Capabilities should therefore have evidence and owners.

If a team says reducing staff will destroy an essential function, ask how the function is measured now. Ask how often it is used. Ask what failure looks like. Ask whether technology can reduce the labor required. Ask whether another part of government already performs the same function. Ask whether the risk could be accepted explicitly rather than hidden inside a permanent staffing level.

Some claimed capabilities will not survive this examination.

Good.

The capacity budget should make intelligent cuts easier, not harder.

The harder cases are the capabilities whose value lies in optionality.

A country does not need surge administrative capacity every day. It needs it when a pandemic, war, financial crisis, migration shock, cyber incident, natural disaster or abrupt technological transition changes the workload faster than normal staffing can adapt.

A regulator does not need its deepest technical expert in every meeting. It needs that expertise when a new market structure appears and the regulated firms arrive with forty pages explaining why nothing important has changed.

A public body does not need to exercise a vendor exit every month. It needs enough retained knowledge that exit remains a real option rather than a contractual fiction.

Optionality is expensive because much of the time it looks unused.

The military has never solved this problem by concluding that readiness is waste because most equipment is not firing most days. Hospitals understand that a bed occupied one hundred percent of the time leaves no bed for the next patient. Electrical grids carry reserve margins. Financial institutions hold capital against events they hope will not occur.

Public administration has fewer accepted measures for reserve capacity, but the principle is the same.

The aim is not maximum utilization.

The aim is dependable performance under the range of conditions the institution is expected to survive.

AI complicates this further because it can make nominal spare capacity look larger than it really is.

A department may reason that if workloads surge, its staff can simply ask the model to do more. Sometimes this will be true. Machine labor can scale in ways human labor cannot.

But the tasks that remain during a crisis are rarely a random sample of normal work.

Routine cases can often be automated first. The residual workload becomes disproportionately strange, contested, high-risk or novel. A model trained on yesterday’s normality may be least useful precisely when the institution departs furthest from normal conditions.

A surge of ordinary document classification is a compute problem.

A new legal category created during an emergency is a governance problem.

The capacity budget has to know the difference.

It should also care about time.

A capability that can be rebuilt in two weeks is different from one that takes five years to reconstruct.

This is where many austerity decisions become asymmetric.

It may take a month to eliminate a training program and years to rebuild the expertise it produced.

A specialist team can be dispersed during one spending round and later prove almost impossible to reassemble because the labor market changed.

A maintenance backlog can be created gradually and then require a large capital program to reverse.

A public institution can lose technical ownership of a system one contract extension at a time until switching suppliers becomes a multiyear transformation.

Cuts should therefore account not only for what capability is lost but for the cost and delay of reacquiring it.

Call this whatever the accountants prefer. The name matters less than forcing the fact into the decision.

If a capability is cheap to replace, holding expensive permanent reserve may be unnecessary.

If it is slow to rebuild, liquidation deserves a higher hurdle.

This principle is especially useful for skills.

A junior analyst can be hired relatively quickly in a healthy labor market. A team combining deep tax law, twenty years of case history, data engineering knowledge and the credibility to challenge a major supplier cannot be purchased from a catalogue on Monday morning.

The state may be able to buy substitutes.

That is not the same as rebuilding the capability.

The most useful capacity measures will therefore look a little strange beside conventional financial indicators.

How long does it take to implement a statutory change safely?

How many critical systems have a tested supplier-exit path?

What proportion of high-impact automated decisions can be reconstructed from retained evidence?

How quickly are serious model errors detected and corrected?

What share of appeals overturn the original decision, and are recurrent causes fed back into the system?

How much maintenance is deferred beyond the economically preferred interval?

How many services depend on one person, one supplier, one unsupported system, or one dataset nobody trusts?

How long would it take to restore an essential capability if the current team disappeared?

These are not universal key performance indicators. Turning them into a mandatory dashboard for every agency would recreate the problem in a new costume.

They are examples of the questions that reveal whether a saving removed waste or merely removed the ability to notice future waste.

There is also a democratic advantage to making capability explicit.

Political arguments about the size of government are often conducted at a level of abstraction that makes agreement nearly impossible. One side says the state is bloated. The other says public services have been cut to the bone. Both claims may be true in different functions at the same time.

A government can have too many people processing a redundant form and too few people maintaining the system that could eliminate the form.

It can have too many approval layers and too little procurement expertise.

It can have a large administrative workforce and almost no ability to change the software through which that workforce acts.

It can spend heavily and remain incapable.

It can spend modestly and be remarkably effective.

Capability gives the argument a more useful unit.

Should the state be able to do this thing?

If yes, what is the cheapest robust way to retain that ability?

If no, stop paying for it.

That is austerity with an object.

The AI transition makes this discipline more urgent because the opportunity for genuine savings is large enough to tempt governments into crude arithmetic.

There will be tasks that no longer justify human labor at their current scale. There will be teams whose work collapses from weeks to hours. There will be forms, reviews, reconciliations, searches, translations, summaries and routine decisions that can be handled with far less effort. Public payrolls should respond to this reality.

But the response should follow the work, not precede the understanding of it.

Automate the task.

Observe the new system.

Measure the failure modes.

Decide what capability the institution still requires.

Then take the saving that remains.

The order matters.

If the headcount target comes first, the technology becomes a justification for a number chosen elsewhere. The organization is instructed to remove twenty percent of its people and then invited to discover how AI makes that safe.

This is backwards engineering.

Sometimes the target will accidentally be right.

Sometimes the machine will be forced into work it should not do because the humans have already been removed from the budget.

A capability budget reverses the burden of proof. It does not ask why every existing job should remain. It asks what the institution is responsible for being able to accomplish, what evidence shows the capability exists, and what combination of humans, machines, suppliers, procedures and reserves delivers it most economically.

That is a much more demanding standard than protecting bureaucracy.

It is also a much more demanding standard than cutting it.

The finance ministry still gets to ask whether the country can afford the institution.

The institution has to answer what the country receives for the money.

And when a saving is proposed, both sides have to say what will no longer be possible afterward.

Sometimes the answer will be: nothing important.

Those are the good cuts.

Sometimes the answer will be: we will no longer employ the people who manually copy this information because the system now does it correctly.

Also good.

Sometimes the answer will be: we can still perform the function, but only while this supplier cooperates, this model behaves as expected, this one employee remains, nobody appeals, the law stays unchanged and the emergency does not happen this year.

That is not a saving yet.

It is a bet.

Governments make bets constantly. They have to. Resources are finite and uncertainty cannot be abolished by adding staff.

The improvement is to know when a budget decision is a bet and what capability has been placed on the table.

Artificial intelligence may let governments run with fewer people, fewer repetitive processes and much lower administrative cost. That would be an achievement worth wanting.

But a lean state and a hollow state can have the same headcount.

The difference is what happens when something changes.

A lean state can still understand the problem, alter the system, challenge the supplier, hear the appeal, recover from the failure and explain what it did.

A hollow state has to place an order.

The budget should know which one it is buying.
