# The New Scarcity

Artificial intelligence will not abolish scarcity. Energy, land, housing in desirable places, rare materials, political attention and trust remain scarce. Money remains scarce because every use excludes another possible use. But useful cognition is becoming less scarce, and that changes the shape of organizations built around rationing expertise.

The old bureaucracy rationed expertise because expertise was expensive. The citizen received ten minutes with the official because the official had fifty other people waiting. A small municipality could not afford a data scientist. A public defender could not research every possible precedent. A teacher could not prepare thirty individually adapted explanations every evening. A regulator could not inspect every page submitted by every company. AI relaxes some of those constraints. It does not eliminate judgment; it lowers the cost of preparing judgment.

As preparation gets cheaper, the bottleneck moves. Producing options matters less than selecting among them; finding information matters less than deciding what can be trusted; generating code matters less than maintaining the systems it enters. A society flooded with possible answers still needs institutions capable of deciding which answers become roads, schools, taxes, regulations, medicines, permits, rights and obligations. The machine can propose ten thousand futures. Politics still has to choose one.

That places a higher premium on capabilities austerity tends to erode: judgment, institutional memory, maintenance, coordination, evaluation, legitimacy and the capacity to act collectively. AI is therefore too simple to understand as a substitute for the state’s cognitive labor. It behaves more like a multiplier. A capable institution given cheap intelligence may become dramatically more capable; a hollow institution can automate its hollowness, producing an answer immediately while nobody remains able to explain why that answer should govern.

The old austerity argument asked whether government had become too large. The AI-era argument has to ask how much of an institution can disappear before the institution stops understanding itself. That does not mean preserving every job. Many jobs should change, and some should vanish. There are bureaucratic procedures whose disappearance would constitute a public service, along with layers of coordination designed for information environments that no longer exist and reporting rituals whose original purposes were forgotten several reorganizations ago. Automate those. Delete them. Enjoy it.

The harder work is distinguishing dead bureaucracy from living capacity. The experienced procurement officer prevents the terrible contract; the maintenance team prevents the closure; the planner notices the drainage problem; the regulator understands the accounting trick. A caseworker recognizes the unusual family circumstance. A statistician notices the number that should not have moved. An engineer refuses the vendor’s reassuring explanation. Their best result may be that nothing happens, which is one reason austerity has always had trouble valuing this kind of work.

AI may make the problem worse because machines will perform visible work so cheaply. Drafting, classification, search and routine analysis can become inexpensive enough that judgment, skepticism, accountability, memory and maintenance look comparatively costly precisely because they are what remains. A government can save payroll today and discover later that it also sold the ability to renegotiate a supplier, challenge a model, repair an old system, hear an unusual case or change course after the law changes. Those losses tend to reappear elsewhere: emergency procurement, compensation, delay, litigation, failed projects, crisis services, or citizens doing unpaid administrative work because the state no longer knows how to resolve the exception.

The cheapest state is therefore not simply the one with the smallest payroll. A smaller workforce can be a genuine achievement when technology removes drudgery and the institution retains enough technical, operational and human judgment to govern what remains. It becomes something else when every saved hour is treated as proof that another layer of competence can disappear.

The distinction matters most when conditions change. On an ordinary day, a hollow state can look impressively efficient: forms move, interfaces are clean, answers arrive quickly. The weakness appears when a court changes the interpretation, a vendor changes the model, an appeal exposes a repeated error, a crisis changes the workload, or the common case stops being common. Then somebody inside has to understand the problem well enough to alter the system, challenge the supplier, hear the appeal, recover from the failure and explain what happened.

Artificial intelligence gives governments a chance to return real savings to taxpayers while making scarce human attention more available where judgment matters. Smaller teams can administer work that once required larger ones if they have better tools, cleaner data, clearer authority, enough slack to investigate anomalies, and enough retained knowledge to remain more than customers of their own machinery.

The expensive part of intelligence will increasingly be what happens after an answer can be generated cheaply: deciding, maintaining, contesting, coordinating, remembering and taking responsibility for the result. Fiscal discipline still matters. It matters enough to learn the difference between a cost and a capability before either one is cut.
