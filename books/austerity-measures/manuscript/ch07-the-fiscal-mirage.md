# The Fiscal Mirage

In Washington, it is possible to cut twenty billion dollars from a government agency and make the deficit larger. This is not a paradox. It is what happens when the line labeled *cost* stops before the line labeled *consequence*.

The Congressional Budget Office supplied an unusually clean example in 2024 while examining possible rescissions of additional funding for the Internal Revenue Service. In one scenario, removing $20 billion of IRS funding reduced projected federal revenue by about $44 billion over the 2024–2034 period. The apparent saving therefore increased the cumulative deficit by roughly $24 billion. A larger $35 billion rescission produced an even larger mismatch: CBO estimated about $89 billion less revenue and a deficit increase of about $54 billion.

The mechanism was ordinary enough. Tax enforcement costs money, but some of that spending produces collections that would otherwise not occur. CBO expected the IRS to protect the enforcement work with the highest returns and cut lower-return work first. That is why the effect was not linear. Its illustrative $5 billion rescission barely worsened the ten-year deficit, while deeper cuts reached progressively more productive enforcement activity.

This is a useful complication. The lesson is not that every dollar spent by a revenue agency is secretly worth several dollars, or that an agency can declare itself an investment and escape scrutiny. Marginal returns matter. So does execution. CBO later acknowledged that its early projections for the 2022 IRS funding increase had been reduced in part because hiring and training auditors took longer than expected. A budget can overstate a saving. It can also overstate the return on the spending that survives.

What matters is the boundary of the calculation.

Congressional scorekeeping makes the boundary unusually visible. Rescinding IRS budget authority can appear as a reduction in discretionary spending, while the resulting loss of tax collections shows up as a revenue effect. Both numbers can be correct. A political argument that mentions only the first can still be economically backward.

Public administration is full of less legible versions of the same error. Close a preventive service and a later crisis may appear in another department. Delay maintenance and the replacement arrives years later under a capital budget. Reduce legal support and the court inherits a worse-prepared case. Remove planning staff and the payroll falls while applications slow, private applicants buy more consultancy, and development waits for decisions. The spreadsheet has borders. The institution does not.

AI will make these mirages easier to create because its immediate gains can be spectacularly easy to count. A model handles ten thousand routine contacts, so call-center hours fall. Automated document review shortens processing time, so the unit cost of a case declines. A fraud model closes more files per investigator. A vendor platform makes an internal engineering team look redundant.

Every one of those gains may be real. The accounting becomes misleading only when it stops there.

Did repeat contacts rise because the automated answer was fast but incomplete? Did appeals move into a tribunal financed by somebody else? Did a stricter fraud screen prevent improper payments while also delaying legitimate ones? Did a smaller engineering team lower payroll while increasing contract cost, recovery time, or dependence on a supplier? Did citizens save time too, or did the agency convert its own minutes into theirs?

The hard part is the counterfactual. A government cannot simply add every downstream problem to the cost of automation; some would have happened anyway. Nor should every future risk be capitalized at the most frightening imaginable value. That is how a sensible warning becomes an accounting system in which no reform can ever pay for itself.

The better test is narrower. Before claiming a saving, decide which outcomes the institution is responsible for and measure far enough downstream to see whether the intervention moved those outcomes rather than merely moving the expense. Track rework, appeals, error correction, collections, citizen effort, supplier spend and failure recovery when they are materially connected to the change. Compare them with a credible baseline. Then keep looking long enough to discover whether the first-year gain survives contact with the second year.

This standard cuts both ways. If AI genuinely removes work without increasing error, shifting burden, eroding revenue or creating a larger dependency elsewhere, the saving should be taken. Refusing to recognize a real productivity gain because some indirect cost might exist is only the mirror image of the original mistake.

The fiscal question is therefore not whether an institution spent less on the activity that changed. It is whether the public system became cheaper to accomplish the same purpose, or became capable of accomplishing more for the same money.

That distinction is where an efficiency becomes a dividend rather than a mirage.
