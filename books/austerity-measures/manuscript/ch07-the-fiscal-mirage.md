# The Fiscal Mirage

In July 2016, Australia’s Department of Human Services began running an online compliance system that compared Centrelink recipients’ reported earnings with income data held by the Australian Taxation Office. The attraction was obvious. Compliance work that had required staff could be pushed through a digital channel at much greater scale. A government looking for savings could see the intervention, the debt it produced and the labor it appeared to avoid.

The harder costs were less cooperative.

The system became known as Robodebt. One of its central failures was not automation in the abstract but the way annual tax-office income was used to infer what people had earned in particular fortnights. By 2017, the Commonwealth Ombudsman was already reporting problems with the automated process. An [Australian National Audit Office review](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/human-services-compliance-strategies) later noted an awkward detail for any simple efficiency story: many customers preferred staff assistance during compliance checks associated with the budget savings measures, and the department allocated additional staff as it changed the program.

Then the accounting moved again. In November 2019, the Commonwealth stopped raising debts solely from averaged tax-office income. In the class action that followed, the Commonwealth accepted that there had been no proper legal basis for using averaged ATO income data to raise and recover those debts. Services Australia’s 2022–23 annual report put estimated refunds at $752.7 million, with more than $749 million already returned by April 2023. The settlement added another $112 million, including legal costs. The [Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme](https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/report) later recommended clearer review paths, disclosure and independent scrutiny for automated government decision-making.

It would be convenient to turn that history into a morality play about machines. That would miss the useful part. Robodebt does not show that automated compliance is inherently more expensive than human administration, or that governments should stop using data matching to find genuine overpayments. Compliance programs can recover real money, and digitization can remove real clerical work. Nor do the refunds and settlement, by themselves, prove that every fiscal estimate associated with the program was negative once every cost and recovery is counted.

What the episode shows is how much depends on the border drawn around the calculation. A ledger can count a debt when the system raises it without yet counting the staff time required to help a confused recipient challenge it, the legal work required to defend it, the repayment if the debt should never have existed, or the years of administrative repair afterward. It can count the agency’s processing cost while ignoring the hours transferred to citizens. It can record this year’s saving while leaving a successor government the correction.

AI will make this temptation stronger because its immediate gains can be unusually legible. Headcount, processing time and cost per transaction may all fall. The omitted costs are more likely to arrive as appeals, repeat contacts, abandoned claims, unusual cases, vendor dependence and remediation. Some will land in another department. Some will land years later. Some will never enter a public account because citizens absorb them privately.

This is why an automated service can be both cheaper inside the agency and more expensive in the world. A chatbot may reduce telephone calls while increasing the total citizen time needed to resolve a problem. A predictive system may lower routine inspection costs while making a novel failure harder to see. A platform may reduce internal technical staffing and later make migration so difficult that government pays to purchase its own knowledge back.

The accounting unit is therefore part of the policy. A department can optimize its budget while making the wider state more expensive. Government can reduce its administrative cost by transferring work to citizens. A minister can collect a saving this year while a successor receives the failure later.

None of this makes efficiency imaginary. The next question is harder and more useful: when technology genuinely reduces the total burden of doing the work, what should government do with the capacity it has created?
