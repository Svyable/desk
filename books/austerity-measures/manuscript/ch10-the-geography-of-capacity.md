# The Geography of Capacity

Austerity does not happen to an average place.

It happens to Birmingham and Blackpool, Westminster and Wokingham, a London borough with a large legal department and a district council where the person responsible for digital transformation may also be responsible for procurement, information governance, cyber security and whatever failed on Tuesday.

National arguments flatten these places into one public sector.

They are not one public sector.

They begin with different tax bases, different populations, different inherited systems, different labor markets, different levels of need and very different room for error. A policy that looks administratively neutral in Whitehall can therefore arrive as a minor adjustment in one council and an operating crisis in another.

The geography of austerity matters because the geography of artificial intelligence will not be neutral either.

A capable organization can use cheap intelligence to become more capable. It can automate routine work, clean data, build internal tools, test competing suppliers, train staff, redesign services and reinvest some of the saving in whatever bottleneck appears next.

A weaker organization receives the same model but not the same multiplier.

It may lack the clean records the model needs. It may not know which system contains the authoritative version of an address. It may have no data engineer. Its procurement team may be too small to run a serious evaluation. Its legal staff may be carrying several other urgent matters. Its software may sit inside a contract signed years before anyone imagined that the supplier would add an AI layer. Its managers may have no spare capacity to redesign the service because all of their time is spent keeping the existing service open.

Both organizations can announce an AI pilot.

Only one can easily turn the pilot into institutional learning.

This is how a general-purpose technology can widen a public-capacity gap without anybody designing it to do so.

The mechanism is ordinary compounding.

The council with more capacity can experiment more cheaply. Because it can experiment, it learns faster. Because it learns faster, it becomes a better buyer. Because it becomes a better buyer, it gets more value from suppliers and retains more knowledge internally. Because the technology works, it can justify further investment.

The council with less capacity has a different cycle. It postpones the experiment until the business case is obvious. By then, the market has matured around products designed elsewhere. It buys because building or evaluating internally looks unaffordable. The supplier solves a real problem, but much of the learning stays with the supplier. The council becomes more dependent on a system it had less opportunity to shape.

The first council accumulates capability.

The second rents it.

There is a temptation to explain this difference as leadership. Sometimes leadership is the difference. Some small public institutions are astonishingly inventive because a few competent people refuse to accept the obvious constraints. Some large institutions waste enormous resources while describing themselves as transformed.

But leadership does not repeal starting conditions.

England’s local-government finances make the point unusually clearly. The Institute for Fiscal Studies estimates that during the 2010s, real core funding per person fell by about 35 percent in the most deprived tenth of council areas and about 15 percent in the least deprived tenth. The reason was partly structural: more deprived councils relied more heavily on central grants, and those grants were cut sharply. The funding formula changed later, but the earlier losses were not undone.

By 2024–25, the IFS estimated that funding per person in the most deprived tenth was still about 26 percent below its 2010–11 level in real terms. In the least deprived tenth, the reduction was about 11 percent.

These numbers should not be stretched beyond what they show. They do not prove that the most fiscally pressured councils are always the least digitally capable. They do not map neatly onto any ranking of AI readiness. The relevant surveys were not designed to establish that causal relationship, and local government is full of exceptions.

What the figures establish is a more basic asymmetry.

Some places entered the AI transition after absorbing much larger reductions in the resources available to maintain services and invest in future capability.

At the same time, the services they could least avoid were becoming more expensive.

A council serving a population with high social-care needs, homelessness, poor health, fragile housing and low household incomes cannot simply decide that these conditions are strategically inconvenient. Acute demand arrives before the transformation program. The adult needs care before the data architecture is repaired. The family needs temporary accommodation before the procurement team has time to redesign the housing system. The child at risk cannot be placed in next year’s innovation roadmap.

This is one of austerity’s most durable geographical effects. Places with the highest need can become the places with the least discretionary capacity to invest in reducing that need later.

Artificial intelligence could interrupt that cycle.

It could also accelerate it.

The Local Government Association’s recent surveys show a sector that is interested in AI but unevenly prepared to use it. In a 2024 survey of 96 councils and combined authorities, 53 percent of respondents did not yet have a current data strategy, although more than half of those were developing one. The most commonly reported barriers to building data capacity were lack of financial resources, cited by 73 percent; legacy systems, 71 percent; and lack of staff capacity, 67 percent.

Those are not separate from AI readiness.

They are much of AI readiness.

A language model can summarize a document without a data strategy. It can draft a letter on the first day. This is why generative AI spreads so easily: the entry cost is low and the demonstration is immediate.

The harder uses begin when the system must understand the institution rather than merely the sentence in front of it.

A model helping with social care needs access to reliable records, permissions, case history, policy, safeguarding rules and an escalation path. A planning assistant needs structured application data, local plans, constraints, prior decisions and a way to distinguish guidance from law. A fraud system needs data provenance and an appeal process. A maintenance system needs asset data that somebody has kept accurate for years.

If the underlying information is fragmented or trapped inside supplier systems, the model becomes another interface over the fragmentation.

The same LGA survey found that 42 percent of respondents said teams could only occasionally obtain data from third-party supplier systems in the format they needed. Half of respondents said their organization understood its in-house specialist data roles and skills gaps only to a small extent or not at all.

Again, this is not a story about stupidity.

It is what institutional scarcity looks like from inside the institution.

There is always something more urgent than building a data catalogue.

Until the day the data catalogue would have made the urgent thing easier.

AI brings that deferred maintenance forward. The technology is hungry for the very complements austerity made easiest to postpone: clean data, interoperable systems, technical staff, process ownership, evaluation, procurement competence and the time to redesign work rather than merely accelerate it.

The 2025 LGA survey on artificial intelligence showed the same pattern from another angle. Nearly all responding councils were using or exploring AI, but only 7 percent described themselves as innovative leaders. Half said they were at the beginning of the journey. Among respondents, the largest barriers to deployment were lack of funding, staff capability and staff capacity.

More than two-thirds of those using or exploring AI were paying external suppliers or procuring supplier-provided tools. Many reported difficulty not only buying the technology but understanding where AI added value, how it was embedded in a product, and how to evaluate the result.

This is exactly the problem from The Headcount Illusion, distributed across a map.

A national vendor can sell the same system to many councils.

The councils do not all meet the vendor with the same bargaining power.

The LGA’s State of Digital Local Government report describes the asymmetry directly. Some councils have dedicated technology-procurement specialists. Others have small procurement and IT teams attempting to negotiate with global companies. Long contracts, expensive exits, limited alternatives and concentrated local-government software markets can leave individual authorities with weak leverage.

One council may have enough expertise to challenge the proposed architecture, insist on data portability, negotiate audit rights and test the exit plan before signing.

Another may be relieved that the supplier has a standard contract.

The difference can survive for a decade.

This matters because local government is where many abstract promises of the state become physical experiences.

A national government can publish an AI strategy.

The resident experiences whether the housing application works.

Whether the pothole is repaired.

Whether the social worker can see the relevant record.

Whether the council can explain the bill.

Whether the planning decision makes sense.

Whether somebody answers when the automated service fails.

A country can therefore have an advanced national AI policy and a deeply unequal AI state.

That inequality would not necessarily look dramatic.

There may be no explicit decision that residents of one town deserve worse machine-assisted government than residents of another. The divergence can emerge from thousands of individually reasonable choices.

One council renews the legacy system because migration is expensive.

Another builds an open interface.

One cannot fill a data-engineering vacancy.

Another creates a graduate pipeline.

One buys the AI module already offered by its incumbent supplier.

Another runs a competitive evaluation.

One automates because it needs an immediate saving.

Another automates and uses some of the saving to redesign the underlying service.

Five years later, the difference is called digital maturity.

Ten years later, it may simply look like the natural order of things.

This is how capacity becomes geography.

The obvious response would be centralization.

If three hundred councils struggle separately with the same suppliers, standards, assurance questions and technical skills, perhaps the center should simply decide everything once.

There is a real efficiency argument here.

Every council does not need to invent its own definition of secure cloud procurement. Every authority does not need to evaluate the same generic AI transcription product from first principles. Hundreds of teams should not separately pay lawyers to discover the same unacceptable contract term. Commodity infrastructure should be shared where sharing reduces cost and raises quality.

The British government has begun moving in this direction. The Local AI team created by the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government says its mission includes building tools once for reuse, scaling locally developed products, improving data foundations and helping councils navigate the AI market. GDS Local is working on common architecture, shared components and collaboration between central and local government.

This is promising because shared infrastructure can operate as a capacity equalizer.

A small council may never support a large permanent AI assurance team.

It should not need one merely to use ordinary tools safely.

The same is true of technical standards, model-evaluation templates, secure experimentation environments, procurement clauses, identity components, data schemas, audit patterns and common software for genuinely common problems. If the public sector solves each of these once well, a local authority can spend scarce attention on the part that is actually local.

This distinction is crucial.

Shared substrate is not the same as centralized judgment.

A national team can provide a secure model gateway.

It cannot decide whether a particular family is about to become homeless.

It can build a reusable planning component.

It cannot know the drainage history of a field better than the people responsible for the place.

It can provide procurement leverage.

It cannot determine which local service should be cut when demand exceeds the budget.

The best architecture for local AI may therefore look less like one giant national brain and more like a strong common floor beneath many institutions that retain local knowledge and democratic responsibility.

Build once where the problem is genuinely common.

Decide locally where the facts and trade-offs are genuinely local.

That approach also avoids another austerity trap: forcing every authority to become self-sufficient in capabilities that only make economic sense at larger scale.

Capacity does not have to live entirely inside the organizational boundary to remain public capacity.

A shared public service, a cross-council technical team, an open-source project maintained collectively, a national evaluation facility, a common procurement function or a reusable government component can all increase local capability without requiring every council to hire the same specialists.

The test from the previous chapters still applies.

Can the institution use the capability without becoming helpless if one supplier leaves?

Can it understand the system well enough to remain accountable?

Can it adapt the shared component to legitimate local needs?

Can it contest a decision?

Can it recover?

Can it leave?

If yes, pooling can create scale without creating dependence.

This is where the politics of austerity and the politics of place meet.

Austerity often asks each institution to become individually cheaper.

But some forms of efficiency are only available collectively.

A council with a small technology team may never negotiate like a national buyer on its own. Fifty councils acting together might.

A district may never justify building a sophisticated evaluation environment for occasional AI procurement. A shared service can.

A small authority may not have enough specialist work to offer an attractive career to a senior data engineer. A regional or cross-council team might.

Fragmentation can be expensive even when every fragment is frugal.

The AI age should make us more willing to distinguish local autonomy from local duplication.

The state does not prove its respect for local government by making every council solve the same technical problem separately.

Sometimes autonomy is increased by shared capability because the local institution is no longer forced to accept the only supplier or system it can afford to evaluate.

There is a second side to the geography of capacity.

Citizens themselves have unequal reserves for dealing with automated institutions.

A person with time, education, money, reliable internet and professional confidence can survive a badly designed digital service. They can search for guidance, make repeated attempts, phone at a quieter hour, hire advice, complain effectively, or escalate the matter through political channels.

A person working two jobs, caring for a relative, living with poor connectivity, speaking limited English or already facing several institutions at once has less spare capacity.

The same administrative error therefore imposes a different burden depending on where it lands.

Place concentrates these burdens. Areas with higher poverty, worse health, weaker transport, insecure housing and more residents dependent on public services do not merely have more demand. Their residents may have less private capacity to compensate when the public interface fails.

This is why the geography of AI administration cannot be reduced to broadband maps and model availability.

The relevant infrastructure includes institutional competence on one side of the screen and human room for error on the other.

A bad chatbot is annoying to everyone.

It is more consequential when the next available human is forty minutes away, the library has reduced hours, the advice center closed, the deadline is tomorrow and the person cannot afford a professional intermediary.

The public-sector version of digital inequality is therefore not only unequal access to technology.

It is unequal access to recovery when technology fails.

That is a capacity problem too.

The danger is a two-speed state.

In one part of the country, artificial intelligence becomes a compounding public asset. Routine work gets cheaper. Staff learn to use the tools. Savings fund better systems. Data improves because the organization can finally afford to fix it. Procurement gets smarter. Residents receive faster service without losing access to judgment.

Elsewhere, AI arrives as another module in a legacy contract purchased under pressure to save money this year. It reduces some work. Positions disappear. The supplier relationship deepens. The local team remains too busy to redesign the service. The data remains awkward because replacing the underlying system would consume the savings several times over.

Both places have adopted AI.

The gap between them has grown.

There is nothing inevitable about this outcome.

In 2026, central and local government in Britain are already experimenting with the opposite model: reusable tools, common architecture, shared learning, test-before-you-buy environments and more coordinated support for responsible AI adoption. The important part is not any particular program name. Programs change. The durable idea is that capability can be pooled before it is purchased privately.

That principle deserves to survive the initiative that currently carries it.

If useful machine intelligence becomes cheap, the remaining expensive work is building the institutional conditions that let many places use it well.

Those conditions should not be treated as luxury goods for the best-resourced authorities.

A national government worried about regional inequality should care not only where factories, universities, data centers and investment locate. It should care where competent administration can still be found.

State capacity is part of economic geography.

A place that can approve housing predictably, maintain infrastructure, coordinate services, evaluate vendors, enforce rules, understand its data and respond to change is easier to build in, invest in and live in.

A place that cannot do these things pays an invisible tax on almost every other policy.

AI will not remove that tax automatically.

It may make the difference between capable and incapable places more visible because the same technology will produce such different results in each.

The policy question is therefore larger than how to help lagging councils adopt AI.

It is how to keep machine intelligence from becoming another technology whose returns accumulate where institutional advantage already exists.

The answer is not equal spending on every place or identical software in every council.

Need differs. Systems differ. Local choices should differ.

The objective is a common minimum of public capability: enough technical competence to buy intelligently, enough data competence to know what the system is seeing, enough operational ownership to change a service, enough redundancy to recover, and enough human access that a resident is not trapped behind the machine.

Above that floor, places can experiment.

Below it, autonomy becomes theoretical because the institution lacks the capacity to exercise it.

Austerity taught governments to look for savings one organization at a time.

Artificial intelligence gives them a reason to look across the map.

The cheapest council is not necessarily part of the cheapest state.

A country that saves money by allowing local capability to diverge can end up paying repeatedly for the consequences: emergency support, failed procurement, duplicated technology, stalled development, crisis services and residents pushed from one overburdened institution into another.

The spreadsheet has borders.

The map does too.

Capability crosses both.
