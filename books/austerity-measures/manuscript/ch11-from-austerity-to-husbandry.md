# From Austerity to Husbandry

There is an older word for the discipline we need: husbandry.

To husband a resource is to use it carefully while preserving its productive capacity. You prune, repair, replace and invest. You remove what no longer works. You do not preserve every branch because it once grew there, and you do not confuse eating the seed corn with reducing agricultural overhead.

Public institutions should be managed with the same distinction. Routine cognitive work should be automated aggressively where the evidence supports it and the risks are manageable. Savings should be visible, but so should the condition of the institution that produced them.

That means reading the productivity account beside the capability account. A department should know whether backlogs and repeat contacts fell, whether appeals reveal recurring defects, whether poor citizens experienced the same improvement as rich ones, whether data can leave a supplier in usable form, whether policy changes can still propagate through software, whether staff can explain the systems they remain accountable for, and whether someone can detect when the AI is wrong. Maintenance belongs in the same picture as launch.

The exact measures will differ because the capabilities differ. The point is not to manufacture one more mandatory dashboard. It is to stop calling a saving efficient when the saving quietly destroys something more expensive.

A government that saves ten million pounds and loses the ability to renegotiate a hundred-million-pound contract did not become efficient. A government that removes five hundred clerical roles while reducing errors, improving access, shortening waits and preserving meaningful appeal may have become much more efficient.

Headcount does not settle the question, and neither does spending. Capability is the object being managed.
