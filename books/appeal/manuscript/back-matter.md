# Back Matter

## The Appeal Test

When an institution makes a consequential decision, ask ten questions.

1. **Decision:** Can the affected person identify exactly what was decided?
2. **Reason:** Can they understand the material reason well enough to locate a disagreement?
3. **Record:** Can the institution reconstruct the evidence, policy, model, and material steps that produced the answer?
4. **Route:** Is there a clearly named path for challenging the decision?
5. **Time:** Is the deadline visible and realistically usable?
6. **Evidence:** Can the person correct facts, add missing context, or identify why existing evidence was misunderstood?
7. **Independence:** Can the second decision fail differently from the first?
8. **Authority:** Can the reviewer actually reverse, remand, restore, or otherwise change the consequence?
9. **Urgency:** Can irreversible harm be paused or mitigated while a serious dispute is reviewed?
10. **Learning:** Do reversals feed back into policy, data, training, interfaces, and models so the same error becomes less likely?

A system that cannot answer these questions does not yet have an appeal architecture. It has a disagreement channel.

## Notes on terminology

**Appeal gap** — The distance between a formal right to challenge a decision and the practical ability to obtain meaningful review.

**Decision debt** — The future correction burden created when first decisions are made more cheaply or quickly than the institution can responsibly reconsider them.

**Error-correction layer** — The people, rules, records, procedures, and authority through which an institution identifies and repairs wrong decisions.

**Appeal provenance** — The information needed to reconstruct a consequential decision sufficiently for meaningful review.

**Contestability metadata** — Information attached to a record indicating that its accuracy, interpretation, or consequence is disputed.

**Cascade control** — Mechanisms that pause, reopen, or repair downstream consequences when a foundational decision is contested or reversed.

**Procedural slack** — Temporary arrangements that prevent irreversible harm while review is pending.

**Restoration gap** — The distance between reversing a decision and repairing the consequences the wrong decision already caused.

**Bounded corrigibility** — The principle that institutions should remain meaningfully open to correction while still providing clear limits and eventual finality.

## Source note

This book draws on public law, agency guidance, regulatory materials, institutional documentation, and public information about appeals in courts, Social Security, health coverage, credit reporting, electronic banking, federal tax administration, veterans' benefits, immigration, online platforms, and European digital regulation. The working research brief and source ledger are maintained in the book's `research/` directory.

The examples are used to compare institutional designs, not to provide individualized legal, medical, financial, benefits, immigration, or tax advice. Rules, deadlines, and procedures change; readers facing an actual decision should consult the current official source governing their case.
