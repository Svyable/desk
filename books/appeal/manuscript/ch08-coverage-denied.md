# Chapter 8 — Coverage Denied

A health insurance denial can compress three different questions into one word.

No.

No, the plan does not cover this service.

No, the plan covers it in principle but does not believe the medical criteria are met.

No, the service may be appropriate but the paperwork, authorization, network, timing, coding, or documentation does not satisfy the plan's rules.

To the patient, these distinctions can feel academic.

The treatment is not being paid for.

To an appeal system, the distinctions are everything.

A coverage dispute sits at the intersection of medicine, contract, regulation, evidence, time, and money. It is one of the clearest examples of why a second decision sometimes needs to come from somewhere other than the institution that made the first.

Under federal health-plan protections in the United States, many people have rights to an internal appeal and, for qualifying decisions, external review. HealthCare.gov explains the architecture plainly: a person can ask the insurance company to conduct a full and fair internal review, and an external review can place the dispute before an independent third party so the insurer no longer has the final say over whether to pay the claim.

That sentence contains an entire theory of institutional power.

The company may decide first.

It does not always decide last.

## Medical necessity is a sentence with two nouns

“Not medically necessary” sounds like a medical conclusion.

It is often also a coverage conclusion.

The phrase can involve clinical evidence, plan definitions, treatment guidelines, documentation, benefit design, and the relation between a particular patient's condition and the proposed service. A doctor can believe treatment is clinically appropriate while an insurer concludes the governing coverage criteria are not satisfied.

The appeal therefore cannot be reduced to “doctor versus insurance company.”

The real conflict may be between different definitions, different evidence, different timing, different interpretations of medical literature, or different views of what the plan promises to pay for.

This is why a useful denial notice matters.

If the reason is simply “not medically necessary,” the patient and clinician have little to work with.

Which criterion failed?

Which evidence was missing?

Was the diagnosis disputed?

Was a prerequisite treatment required first?

Was the service excluded regardless of necessity?

Did the reviewer rely on a guideline that does not fit the case?

Was the wrong code used?

Did the plan misunderstand what was requested?

An appeal becomes meaningful only when the denial is specific enough to attack.

A reason is not courtesy.

It is procedural equipment.

## Internal review

The first appeal usually stays inside the plan.

This can sound suspect. Why ask the institution that denied the claim to reconsider itself?

Because many errors are best corrected internally.

A missing record can be found.

A coding error can be fixed.

A clinician can provide additional documentation.

A reviewer can discover that a plan rule was applied to the wrong service.

A higher-level medical reviewer can reassess the case.

The institution has the records, systems, and authority to pay. An internal reversal can be faster and cheaper than outside adjudication.

The problem is not internal review itself.

The problem is making internal review the only meaningful review when the institution has a financial interest in the answer.

That is where external review changes the architecture.

## The insurer does not get the last word

The idea of external medical review is simple enough to fit on a government website and radical enough to deserve more attention than it gets.

A private company makes a coverage decision.

A separate reviewer can decide whether that decision should stand within the applicable review framework.

The plan can lose control of finality.

This is appeal functioning as a check on institutional self-interest.

The outside reviewer does not become the patient's advocate. Independence does not mean “someone more likely to say yes.” It means the reviewer is not supposed to decide the case simply as an arm of the payer whose determination is under challenge.

The distinction is crucial.

An appeal system that exists to produce more approvals is not necessarily fair.

An appeal system that exists to produce a genuinely independent second judgment has a defensible purpose even when it affirms the denial.

Trust comes from the possibility of disagreement, not a guaranteed reversal.

## Time is clinical

Health appeals expose a flaw in the ordinary administrative clock.

Most bureaucratic deadlines are measured in days.

Medicine is measured in biological change.

A thirty-day delay means something different when the dispute concerns a service that can safely wait than when a person's health could seriously deteriorate during ordinary review.

That is why health appeal systems include expedited or urgent pathways in qualifying circumstances. HealthCare.gov states that if a case is urgent, the insurer must speed up the internal appeal process. Medicare similarly provides fast appeal rights in defined situations, including when covered services are ending too soon.

The design recognizes a principle that should travel far beyond medicine:

**Appeal speed should be tied to the decay rate of the remedy.**

If a later reversal still fully repairs the harm, ordinary review may be acceptable.

If the value of the remedy falls each day, review must accelerate.

This is not impatience.

It is preservation of jurisdiction over reality.

A reviewer can technically reverse a decision after the treatment window has passed. The system has won the appeal and lost the point.

## The five floors of Medicare

Medicare makes the layered nature of health appeals unusually visible.

The precise path varies by the type of Medicare coverage, but Medicare's public guidance explains that there are generally five levels of appeal. If a person disagrees with a decision at one level, they can usually move to the next, receiving decision letters that explain how.

Different Medicare arrangements use different names for the early stages, but the architecture can progress from review within the coverage system through independent review, administrative adjudication, the Medicare Appeals Council, and ultimately federal district court when legal requirements are met.

Five levels can look like generosity.

They can also look like five places to get lost.

Both are true possibilities.

Layering creates correction capacity.

Layering also creates procedural burden.

A well-designed hierarchy therefore needs good handoffs. Each decision should clearly tell the person what was decided, what remains contestable, where the next appeal goes, what deadline applies, what evidence can be added, and whether special timing rules exist.

Every level should reduce uncertainty about the next one.

Otherwise the existence of review becomes a maze.

## Dollars at the courthouse door

Medicare's system also demonstrates that not every dispute reaches every level regardless of size.

For some forms of federal court review, a minimum amount in controversy applies and is adjusted over time. Medicare's 2026 public guidance lists a $1,960 threshold for certain federal district court review.

This is a reminder that appeal rights can be rationed by stakes.

The legal system does not necessarily devote its most expensive forum to every small-dollar disagreement.

That can be rational.

It can also create a class of low-value errors that are too small individually to justify high-level review and large enough collectively to matter enormously.

The same problem appears everywhere automated decisions scale.

A company can make a ten-dollar mistake one million times.

No individual has an economic reason to litigate.

The aggregate consequence is ten million dollars.

Appeal architecture needs mechanisms for these dispersed errors: regulators, class procedures where lawful and appropriate, ombuds systems, audits, aggregate reporting, automated correction, or other forms of collective accountability.

Individual appeal is necessary.

It is not sufficient for mass error.

## The patient as courier

One of the least dignified features of modern institutional life is the amount of information a person must transport between organizations that already possess sophisticated information systems.

The insurer says it lacks a record.

The patient calls the doctor's office.

The doctor's office says it sent the record.

The insurer says a different department needs it.

The patient asks for a copy.

A portal message arrives.

A fax number is involved for reasons nobody can explain.

The appeal deadline continues.

The patient becomes an unpaid integration layer between systems.

This burden is particularly perverse in health care because illness reduces administrative capacity precisely when the administrative work increases.

A person managing pain, fatigue, anxiety, cognitive impairment, treatment, caregiving, or recovery is asked to coordinate a dispute among entities with dedicated staff.

Appeal design should minimize the need for the individual to act as courier where lawful data exchange can do the work.

That does not mean giving every institution unrestricted access to medical information. Privacy matters. Consent matters. Minimum necessary disclosure matters.

It means that procedural burden should not be created simply because organizations failed to build interoperable correction paths.

## The physician's appeal

Health appeals often contain a second translator: the clinician.

The patient knows the lived effect of the condition.

The insurer knows the coverage framework.

The clinician can connect the medical record to the requested treatment.

This role is sometimes formal, sometimes informal. A doctor may provide documentation, write a letter, speak with a reviewer, clarify diagnosis, or explain why a standard pathway is inappropriate for the individual case.

The existence of this expert intermediary shows why appeals are expensive.

A first denial can be generated from structured rules and existing records.

A successful challenge may require scarce professional time from someone whose primary job is treating patients.

The appeal cost does not disappear because the insurer does not pay it.

It is transferred into clinics, families, advocates, employers, and the patient's own time.

That transfer should be visible when evaluating whether a first-decision system is actually efficient.

A denial process that saves ten minutes for the payer and creates two hours of physician-office work may be locally efficient and socially wasteful.

## Automation changes both sides

Health coverage is an obvious target for automation because the documentary workload is enormous.

Models can summarize clinical records, compare requests with criteria, identify missing documentation, draft denial notices, and triage appeals. Agents can collect records and prepare cases. Used well, these tools could reduce administrative burden for both plans and patients.

They can also industrialize denial.

The danger is not simply “AI says no.”

The danger is asymmetry.

One side has automated capacity to generate, document, and defend decisions at scale.

The other side must manually reconstruct the case one patient at a time.

A fair automated system should therefore automate appealability too.

If the model identifies the criterion that failed, the denial should expose that criterion in usable language.

If the record is missing a document, the system should identify the document rather than merely reject.

If an appeal supplies the missing evidence, the case should be rerun promptly.

If the second model reaches the same conclusion for the same reason, high-stakes cases should have a route to independent human judgment.

If a denial category is repeatedly reversed, the system should flag upstream review.

Automation should make correction cheaper, not merely refusal cheaper.

## The external reviewer as constitutional idea

There is something constitution-like about external health review even though the institutions are private, statutory, regulatory, contractual, and technical rather than constitutional in the ordinary sense.

Power is divided.

The payer has authority to administer coverage.

The reviewer has authority to check certain contested decisions.

The law or regulatory framework defines the relationship.

The patient has a procedural route from one to the other.

This structure exists because we do not assume that expertise, contract, and financial responsibility should automatically produce unreviewable power.

That principle will recur as AI systems make more consequential private decisions.

Whoever runs the first system should not always own the last word.

The higher the stakes, the stronger the case for a route outside the original decision factory.

## The denial letter as interface

Most people will never read the statute, regulation, plan document, review contract, or case law behind their appeal rights.

They will read the letter.

The letter is the interface between institutional law and human action.

A good denial notice should answer at least five questions.

What exactly was denied?

Why?

What evidence or rule mattered?

How can I challenge it?

How quickly must I act, including whether urgent review is available?

The quality of the entire appeal system can be defeated by failure at this one point.

A right that is not communicated is a right discovered by luck.

This is why plain-language notice is not cosmetic. It is part of due process in the broadest sense: the affected person must know enough to participate.

## When no becomes provisional

A coverage denial feels definitive because health decisions are immediate.

The letter says no while the body keeps moving through time.

Appeal inserts a possibility between denial and finality.

The first reviewer may have been right.

The second may agree.

The independent reviewer may agree again.

That does not make the process pointless.

The value lies in the fact that the insurer's conclusion can be tested against evidence, procedure, expertise, and—where the framework provides it—an institution outside the insurer itself.

The patient does not receive a guarantee of yes.

They receive something more modest and more important to institutional legitimacy.

The payer's no is not automatically the last word simply because the payer said it first.
