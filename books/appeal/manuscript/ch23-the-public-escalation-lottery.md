# Chapter 23 — The Public Escalation Lottery

Some institutions have an appeal system they never meant to build.

It is called going viral.

A person is locked out of an account, denied a refund, trapped in a bureaucratic loop, or hit by an obviously absurd automated decision. They try the official channel. The official channel fails. They post the story publicly. The post travels. A reporter notices. An employee notices. An executive notices. Suddenly the case that could not be solved in three weeks is solved in twenty minutes.

The institution calls this escalation.

The public experiences it as a lottery.

The case was not corrected because the appeal architecture worked. It was corrected because attention became expensive.

That distinction matters.

## The unofficial court of attention

Public pressure can produce accountability. It can surface patterns hidden inside support queues. It can force institutions to confront harms they would otherwise ignore. It can protect people who lack formal power.

But virality is a terrible substitute for appeal.

It rewards storytelling skill, network size, novelty, outrage, timing, and platform dynamics.

A quiet person with a devastating case may receive no attention.

A charismatic person with a minor case may receive millions of views.

A case that fits an existing public narrative may spread rapidly.

An equally serious but harder-to-explain case may disappear.

This is not adjudication.

It is attention allocation.

When institutions rely on it to discover their own mistakes, correction becomes socially unequal.

## The backchannel class

Public escalation is only one unofficial path.

There is another: knowing someone.

A friend works at the company.

A former colleague knows the right team.

A lawyer has an internal contact.

An investor can text an executive.

A journalist can reach communications staff.

A doctor knows a medical director.

A lobbyist knows an agency office.

A large customer has an account manager who can override the ordinary queue.

These routes are often extremely effective.

They also reveal something uncomfortable.

The institution may already possess the authority and expertise required to fix the problem. What it lacks is a fair public path to that authority.

The hidden route works because someone can reach the real reviewer.

The ordinary route fails because the ordinary user cannot.

This creates a shadow appellate class: people who know how to access discretion that formally exists but is operationally hidden.

## Secret mercy is not due process

Every large organization needs exceptions.

Policies cannot anticipate every fact pattern. Senior staff need emergency powers. Security teams need escalation. Executives need mechanisms for systemic failures. Agencies need supervisory review. Hospitals need urgent consultation.

The problem is not that hidden discretion exists.

The problem is when hidden discretion becomes the only effective correction mechanism.

Secret mercy is not due process.

A system is not meaningfully appealable if the best advice is “find an employee.”

Nor is it meaningfully appealable if the strongest strategy is “post until someone important is embarrassed.”

Those techniques may work.

Their success is evidence that the formal system is underpowered.

## Why institutions like the lottery

The public escalation lottery has one advantage for institutions: it is selective without requiring them to publish a selection rule.

Most cases remain in the ordinary queue.

A small number become reputational emergencies.

The institution spends expensive senior attention only when public pressure makes the case costly enough.

From a narrow operational perspective, this can look efficient.

Why build deep review capacity for everyone when only a few cases become crises?

Because the crisis threshold is unrelated to the merits.

The institution is effectively using reputational damage as a priority score.

That means quiet wrongs wait.

Loud wrongs jump the line.

The organization is not correcting the worst errors.

It is correcting the most visible errors.

## Visibility bias

This produces visibility bias in institutional learning.

Executives see the cases that reach executives.

Policy teams see the cases that become tickets.

Communications teams see the cases that become stories.

But the population of visible cases may be very different from the population of actual errors.

People with language barriers may complain differently.

People with less time may abandon the process earlier.

Small businesses may lack media reach.

Vulnerable users may fear public disclosure.

Some harms are embarrassing to describe.

Some decisions concern health, immigration, family, finances, or employment and cannot safely be turned into public content.

A correction system that learns mainly from public escalation therefore learns from a biased sample.

## Executive intervention as telemetry

Hidden interventions should be measured.

Every time an executive office, communications team, VIP support desk, legal escalation, or employee backchannel fixes a case, the institution should ask why the normal route could not.

Was the official appeal unavailable?

Was the reviewer underpowered?

Was the evidence format too rigid?

Was the case stuck between teams?

Was the deadline unrealistic?

Did the automated system lack an exception category?

Was the user unable to identify the real issue?

Did the support team have no authority to reopen the decision?

These cases are not merely anecdotes.

They are diagnostic tests of the formal system.

The shadow appeal path should generate telemetry for the public one.

## The escalation tax

Virality imposes costs on everyone.

The affected person must expose their problem publicly.

Employees are interrupted by one-off emergencies.

Communications teams become case managers.

Executives spend time on individual disputes.

Support staff feel undermined when a case is fixed outside their process.

Public audiences are recruited as leverage.

The institution may make inconsistent remedies because the case is politically sensitive.

This is the escalation tax.

A functioning appeal architecture reduces it.

Not to silence public criticism.

To ensure public criticism is not required for ordinary correction.

## The fairness problem

Imagine two people receive the same wrong suspension.

One has 400,000 followers.

One has 140.

The first posts a screenshot. Within an hour, the case is restored.

The second files the official form and waits ten days.

The institution may insist both users had access to the same appeal mechanism.

Operationally, they did not.

One had access to a second mechanism: reputational leverage.

That leverage will always exist in some form. Powerful people can attract attention. Large customers can create commercial pressure. Politicians can call agencies. Media can spotlight cases.

The goal is not to abolish influence.

It is to make the ordinary route strong enough that influence is less necessary.

## The employee override problem

Employees often become unofficial appellate judges.

They see a friend's post.

They recognize that the system made a mistake.

They ask internally for help.

If they have sufficient access, the case moves.

This can be humane.

It can also be dangerous.

An informal override may bypass security controls, create favoritism, or expose sensitive information. Employees may not know the full record. A system that encourages personal intervention can create corruption risk alongside compassion.

The solution is not to forbid employees from noticing injustice.

It is to give them a safe escalation channel with auditability.

An employee should be able to say, “This case appears inconsistent with policy; please review,” without becoming the decision-maker.

The institution should preserve the reason for escalation, the reviewer, and the outcome.

Informal concern can enter a formal process.

## The celebrity test

A useful design exercise is the celebrity test.

Suppose the person affected were famous.

Would the institution solve the case differently?

Would a senior person look at it?

Would someone read the actual evidence?

Would the case be routed around the automated queue?

Would policy be interpreted with more care?

Would restoration happen faster?

If the answer is yes, the institution has revealed what meaningful review looks like.

The next question is whether that quality can be made available by rule rather than fame.

Not every case deserves executive attention.

But many systems can identify the qualities that triggered the executive response: high stakes, obvious contradiction, credible evidence, systemic pattern, irreversible harm, or unusual facts.

Those qualities can become routing signals.

The celebrity test turns favoritism into a design specification.

## Public pressure still matters

A strong appeal architecture should not be designed to make public criticism unnecessary in the political sense.

Institutions can be wrong systematically, not just individually.

A platform may enforce a bad policy correctly.

An agency may apply an unjust rule consistently.

A company may refuse to recognize a category of harm.

An appellate process can correct misapplication while leaving the policy itself intact.

Public pressure remains essential for changing the rule.

This book is not an argument for moving every dispute into private procedure.

It is an argument for distinguishing two functions.

Appeal asks whether this decision should stand.

Politics, advocacy, journalism, and public debate ask whether this system should stand.

The two can inform each other.

They should not be confused.

## The no-virality standard

A mature institution should aspire to a simple standard:

A person should not need to become content in order to obtain review.

They should not need a large audience.

They should not need a journalist.

They should not need an employee friend.

They should not need an executive email address scraped from the internet.

They should not need to threaten reputational damage just to reach someone capable of reading the evidence.

The official path should be the best path for ordinary cases.

Public escalation should remain available for systemic failure, abuse of power, or institutional refusal to learn.

But it should be exceptional because the appeal system works, not routine because the appeal system does not.

## From lottery to architecture

Every hidden successful escalation contains a blueprint.

Who noticed the case?

What information did they obtain?

What authority did they use?

What rule did they interpret?

What evidence changed the answer?

How quickly did they act?

What downstream systems did they repair?

That sequence can often be formalized.

The institution can build a route that gives ordinary people access to the same essential capabilities without giving everyone access to an executive.

That is how a shadow appellate system becomes a public one.

The goal is not to eliminate discretion.

It is to distribute access to discretion by relevant criteria rather than social proximity.

The goal is not to eliminate escalation.

It is to make escalation legible.

The goal is not to eliminate public pressure.

It is to stop requiring public pressure for private correction.

When the loudest case is the fastest case, the institution is not merely hearing appeals.

It is auctioning correction for attention.

Civilization can do better than that.
