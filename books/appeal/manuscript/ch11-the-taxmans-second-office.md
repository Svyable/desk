# Chapter 11 — The Taxman's Second Office

The Internal Revenue Service has an office whose public description begins with separation.

The Independent Office of Appeals is separate from the IRS examination and collection functions that make tax assessments and initiate collection actions.

The wording is almost architectural.

A wall inside the institution.

On one side, the government examines returns and tries to collect what it concludes is owed.

On the other, an office is supposed to resolve tax controversies fairly and impartially without litigation when possible.

The same agency houses both functions.

The functions are not supposed to be the same.

This is a useful answer to a common objection about appeals: if the reviewer remains inside the institution, how can the review be real?

Sometimes independence is created not by moving the reviewer outside the building but by designing a boundary strong enough to change the job.

## A government arguing with itself

Tax administration creates an unavoidable conflict.

The government needs to collect revenue lawfully owed.

The taxpayer has a right to contest what the government says is owed.

The same institution that identifies a tax issue may possess the technical expertise and records needed to resolve it efficiently. Requiring every disagreement to begin in court would be expensive for taxpayers and government alike.

So the system builds a second office.

Appeals describes its mission as resolving controversies without litigation, on a basis fair and impartial to both the government and the taxpayer, in a way intended to support confidence in the integrity and efficiency of the tax system.

That mission is revealing because it rejects two caricatures.

The appeals officer is not supposed to be another collector.

The appeals officer is also not the taxpayer's advocate.

The role is institutional neutrality inside an institution with a substantive mission.

That is harder than independence by distance.

It requires the organization to permit one part of itself to disagree with another part.

## The word independent

Organizations use *independent* generously.

Independent review.

Independent committee.

Independent audit.

Independent expert.

The word should always trigger a second question.

Independent from what?

IRS Appeals offers a concrete answer: separate from Examination and Collection, the functions that created the assessment or collection controversy. The IRS also describes independence and impartiality as core values and has rules governing certain communications intended to protect the appeals process from improper influence.

The interesting feature is not that the wall is perfect.

No institutional boundary is.

The interesting feature is that the system identifies the direction from which pressure is most likely to come and builds a rule against it.

That is what appeal architecture should do.

If the first team's incentive is fraud prevention, protect the reviewer from the pressure to affirm every fraud flag.

If the first team's incentive is cost containment, protect the reviewer from an automatic preference for denial.

If the first team's incentive is fast content removal, protect the reviewer from treating reversal as operational failure.

If the first team's incentive is tax assessment, protect the reviewer from becoming an extension of examination.

Independence is a response to a predicted source of correlated judgment.

## Less formal, not less serious

The IRS tells taxpayers that Appeals is generally less formal and less costly than going to court and is not subject to the same complex rules of evidence or procedure. It also states that going to Appeals does not, in itself, mean giving up the right to go to court where judicial review is otherwise available.

This is appeal hierarchy doing what it should.

Put a cheaper correction layer before the expensive one.

The internal independent forum can resolve many disputes without consuming judicial resources.

The court remains beyond it as a different institutional check.

The taxpayer gets a place where technical disagreement can be negotiated and evaluated without immediately converting the dispute into full litigation.

The government gets a chance to correct or settle cases before spending the costs of court.

The judiciary is reserved for disputes that survive.

This is not merely convenience.

It is filtration.

The appeal layer clarifies which disagreements are factual, which are legal, which can be compromised, which require additional documentation, and which remain irreconcilable.

A good intermediate appeal does not block the courthouse.

It sends fewer, sharper disputes there.

## The hazards of informal power

Informality has advantages.

It can lower cost, reduce intimidation, speed resolution, and allow practical problem-solving.

It also creates risk.

Formal procedure makes power visible. Everyone knows which evidence is in the record, who may speak, which deadlines apply, what standard governs, and what remedies are available.

Informal negotiation can blur those boundaries.

Was the taxpayer offered a compromise because the legal position was uncertain or because the reviewer wanted to close the case?

Did the government disclose the basis of its position clearly enough for meaningful response?

Did one side have much more expertise than the other?

Could the reviewer exercise discretion consistently across similar cases?

The answer is not to formalize everything until the process becomes a miniature court.

The answer is to preserve enough structure that informality does not become invisibility.

An appeal should be easier than litigation without becoming harder to audit.

## The settlement function

Courts decide.

Appeal offices can also settle.

That distinction matters.

Tax disputes can involve uncertainty about facts, law, valuation, documentation, and litigation risk. An administrative appeal may resolve the controversy without requiring a binary judicial answer on every issue.

This can make purists uncomfortable.

If the correct tax is X, why compromise around X?

Because institutions operate under uncertainty and cost.

A taxpayer may believe the government's position is wrong but face litigation expense.

The government may believe its position is strong but recognize that evidence or law creates real risk.

Both sides can spend years proving who was right.

Settlement converts uncertainty into finality.

Appeal systems outside law often do the same thing without naming it.

A bank may restore part of a disputed charge as a service resolution.

A marketplace may reinstate an account with conditions.

An insurer may approve an alternative treatment.

A school may offer a modified accommodation.

A platform may restore content but retain a warning.

These outcomes are not pure reversals.

They are negotiated state changes.

The key question is whether the process has authority to create them fairly rather than merely pressure the weaker party into accepting less.

## The shadow of court

Negotiation works differently when both sides know a more formal forum exists.

Lawyers call this bargaining in the shadow of the law. The possible court outcome shapes the administrative settlement even when no lawsuit is filed.

The same principle appears in other appeal systems.

An insurer's internal review changes when external review can overturn it.

A platform's internal moderation changes when an independent body or regulator can scrutinize the process.

A company handles a complaint differently when the customer has statutory rights beyond customer service.

A public agency's appeal office operates differently when federal judicial review exists.

External review disciplines internal review even when rarely used.

The highest layer does not need to hear every case to influence the layers below.

This is another reason purely internal appeal systems can become weak. If there is no credible outside check, the internal reviewer eventually operates in the shadow of the same institution.

## Ex parte and the invisible conversation

One of the great dangers in appeal is information the challenger cannot see.

The first decision-maker tells the reviewer why the case should stand.

The reviewer hears arguments or receives context the challenger never gets to address.

The appeal becomes a private conversation between colleagues about the person who supposedly invoked review.

Tax administration has specific rules and safeguards around certain ex parte communications precisely because this danger is obvious in adjudicative settings.

The broader principle is simple:

**A reviewer should not be secretly persuaded by the first decision-maker on disputed substance in a way the challenger has no meaningful chance to answer.**

This is going to become a major issue in AI appeals.

Imagine the first model generates not only a decision but an invisible internal explanation shown to the second model.

The person sees a short denial reason.

The appellate model sees a long chain of internal risk signals, confidence labels, prior flags, and a machine-generated argument defending the first outcome.

The second model is not reviewing a neutral record.

It is receiving an ex parte brief from the first machine.

The design may be operationally efficient and procedurally tilted.

If internal context matters, the challenge is deciding what can safely and lawfully be disclosed, summarized, contested, or independently verified.

Appeal fairness depends on informational symmetry enough to make disagreement possible.

## The tax protestor and the legitimate dissenter

The tax system also demonstrates why appeal rights need filters.

Some disputes concern genuine factual or legal uncertainty.

Some are repetitive arguments long rejected by courts and agencies.

A review system that treats every assertion as equally novel wastes resources and can become a vehicle for obstruction.

But aggressive filtering creates the opposite risk: the institution can dismiss unfamiliar arguments because they resemble familiar nonsense.

This is a classic classification problem.

The appeal layer needs to distinguish:

Argument already decided and unsupported.

Argument previously decided but materially changed by new facts.

Argument legally weak but sincere.

Argument raising a novel question.

Argument exposing an institutional mistake.

Automated triage will make this harder, not easier.

Models are very good at recognizing resemblance. Appeals often matter because the case that looks like the category is not actually the category.

A filtering system must preserve a route for the exceptional argument to prove it is exceptional.

## Appeals as voluntary compliance

The IRS explicitly connects the mission of Appeals to confidence in the integrity and efficiency of the tax system and to voluntary compliance.

That is a profound institutional claim.

Appeal is not merely a cost imposed by people who refuse to accept an answer.

It can support obedience.

People are more willing to comply with a system when they believe errors can be corrected without extraordinary effort and when they perceive the reviewing process as fair.

This is not the same as making every taxpayer happy.

A person can lose an appeal and still regard the process as legitimate.

Procedural justice research across many domains has repeatedly found that voice, neutrality, respectful treatment, and trustworthy motives affect how people perceive authority. Appeal concentrates those elements at the moment authority is contested.

A tax system relies heavily on mass voluntary participation. The government cannot personally litigate every return.

Confidence is an operational resource.

So is the belief that there is somewhere to go when the ordinary system gets it wrong.

## The second office as organizational design

The Independent Office of Appeals suggests a model for large private institutions.

Many companies place appeals inside the same team that created the first decision because that team knows the issue best.

That is efficient until expertise becomes self-review.

Another option is a **second office**: a function with enough subject-matter knowledge to review decisions and enough structural separation to disagree.

Its charter should answer several questions.

What decisions can it review?

What information can it obtain from the first team?

What communications are restricted?

What standards does it apply?

What authority does it have to reverse, modify, remand, or settle?

How is it measured?

Who leads it?

Who can overrule it?

Does it publish aggregate data about reversals and recurring errors?

How does learning flow back to the first team without letting the first team control individual outcomes?

These are governance questions, not support questions.

An appeals function should be designed more like audit, risk, or adjudication than like a queue for angry users.

## You can go to court

The most important feature of an internal second office may be the door beyond it.

IRS Appeals notes that taxpayers are not necessarily required to use Appeals before going to court in every situation and that using Appeals does not itself eliminate judicial rights that otherwise apply.

That relationship creates a healthy tension.

The internal forum has a chance to resolve the dispute.

The taxpayer retains access to an institution the IRS does not control.

Neither layer replaces the other.

This is what mature appeal systems look like: nested forms of review with different costs, expertise, independence, and authority.

The first office decides at scale.

The second office specializes in disagreement.

The court exists outside both.

The purpose is not to make the tax system hesitant.

The purpose is to let it act with confidence because its errors are not trapped inside the same chain of command that produced them.

A strong institution does not weaken itself by building an office authorized to say the first office was wrong.

It strengthens the credibility of every decision that survives the second look.
