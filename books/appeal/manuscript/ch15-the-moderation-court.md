# Chapter 15 — The Moderation Court

The largest appeal system most people have never thought of as an appeal system may already be running on social media.

Its cases are posts, comments, videos, listings, profiles, and accounts.

Its first judges are mixtures of policy, software, models, user reports, contractors, staff, and automated enforcement.

Its remedies are removal, demotion, suspension, restriction, restoration, warning, account closure, and sometimes nothing at all.

Its docket is enormous.

In the European Union, the Digital Services Act turned parts of this machinery into an explicit system of contestable decisions.

The result gives us a rare view into what happens when appeal moves from thousands of cases to hundreds of millions.

## Nine billion first decisions

European Commission reporting says online platforms reported more than nine billion content-moderation decisions in the first half of 2025 alone under the DSA transparency framework. Ninety-nine percent were proactive decisions based on the platforms' own terms and conditions rather than responses to reports of illegal content.

Nine billion is not a court docket.

It is weather.

No human institution could hold nine billion individualized hearings.

The first layer must be highly automated, standardized, or both.

That scale makes a common policy fantasy impossible: simply put a human in charge of every important moderation decision.

There are not enough humans.

Even if there were, human moderators also disagree, tire, misunderstand context, and require management.

The real design question is not whether first decisions are automated.

It is how the system identifies the subset that deserves expensive reconsideration.

That is exactly what appeal does.

## One hundred sixty-five million challenges

The Commission reported in 2026 that since application of the DSA's regime, users in the EU had appealed more than 165 million content-moderation decisions from very large online platforms and search engines through internal mechanisms.

Almost 30 percent of those appealed decisions were reversed.

The Commission separately described this as roughly fifty million reversals over two years.

The number is startling because it destroys two comfortable stories at once.

The first story says automated moderation can become accurate enough that appeal is marginal.

Fifty million reversals are not marginal.

The second story says appeals are mostly angry users refusing to accept legitimate enforcement.

Many appeals no doubt fail because the original decision was sound. But tens of millions succeeded. The second look changed the result.

Again, this does not imply a 30-percent error rate across all moderation. Appealed cases are selected. Users choose what to contest. Platforms can reverse because context or evidence changes. The appeal population is not the original population.

That is the point.

Appeal concentrates scarce attention on the part of the distribution most likely to contain disagreement worth reviewing.

## The law requires more than a second model call

Article 20 of the Digital Services Act requires covered online platforms to provide internal complaint-handling systems for specified decisions. The law also says decisions on those complaints are to be taken under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff and not solely on the basis of automated means.

That sentence is a direct response to the echo problem.

Automation can make the first decision.

The appeal cannot be nothing but automation answering itself.

The requirement does not mean a human personally decides every detail without software. Supervision and tooling can take many forms. The point is that the second layer must contain qualified human responsibility.

This is an important regulatory design choice because it treats appeal as a distinct safety function.

The law does not forbid automated moderation.

It requires a different architecture for contested moderation.

The ordinary case can remain cheap.

The disputed case gets more expensive.

That is precisely how scarce review should be allocated.

## Out of court

The DSA goes further.

Users addressed by certain moderation decisions can select certified out-of-court dispute-settlement bodies under the regulation's framework.

This creates a second boundary.

First decision: platform.

Internal complaint: platform review.

Out-of-court dispute: a certified body outside the platform.

Court: still a separate legal possibility under applicable law.

The architecture looks less like customer service and more like a miniature justice system.

The early data is small compared with the internal appeal volume but revealing. In the first half of 2025, out-of-court bodies reviewed more than 1,800 disputes involving content on Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok in the EU, according to the Commission, and reversed platform decisions in 52 percent of closed cases.

A 52-percent reversal rate should not be overread. The sample is highly selected. People who continue to an outside forum after internal processes are likely to have stronger motivation, different case types, or unresolved disagreements.

But that selection is exactly why an outside layer exists.

The hardest cases should look unusual.

## A moderation decision is often a bundle

Content moderation sounds singular.

The system may actually make several decisions at once.

Is the content allowed?

Should it be visible to everyone?

Should it be recommended?

Should it count as a strike?

Should the account lose features?

Should past strikes escalate the penalty?

Should linked accounts be investigated?

Should revenue be withheld?

Should the content remain available in some countries and not others?

A user may want to appeal one part of the bundle and accept another.

“This post can stay down, but the strike is wrong.”

“The account can be age-restricted, but it should not be treated as fraud.”

“The content violates a recommendation guideline, but deletion is disproportionate.”

“The removal was correct under local law, but the global account penalty is not.”

A sophisticated appeal system should allow remedy-level contestation.

Otherwise the user must challenge the entire action to correct one downstream consequence.

Courts learned long ago that remedies can be severed from liability. Digital moderation systems will need similar granularity.

## Context is the expensive feature

Why are moderation errors so persistent even as models improve?

Because context is expensive.

The same sentence can be threat, joke, quotation, journalism, counterspeech, satire, historical documentation, harassment, reclaimed slur, or literal instruction depending on speaker, target, audience, surrounding material, language, and event.

A first-pass system cannot deeply reconstruct every context at nine-billion-decision scale.

Appeal is where context can be purchased selectively.

The user can say: this is a quotation from the politician I am criticizing.

This image documents abuse rather than celebrates it.

This word is used by members of the targeted group about themselves.

This account is a news archive.

This apparent duplication is a legitimate syndicated feed.

This transaction is part of a promotion the system misread as spam.

The first model saw the object.

The appeal supplies the frame.

This suggests a powerful general theory:

**Appeal is a market for missing context.**

The institution does not pay to gather maximum context for every first decision.

It creates a route through which affected people can supply context when the first abstraction fails.

The challenge is making that route accessible enough that context does not become a privilege of sophisticated users.

## The Meta Oversight Board experiment

Before the DSA's full regime matured, Meta helped create another unusual review institution: the Oversight Board.

The Board reviews selected content cases and issues decisions within the governance framework Meta established. Those case decisions can require Meta to restore or remove content under the Board's rules, while broader policy recommendations operate differently.

The Board is deliberately selective.

It cannot hear the ocean of moderation disputes.

Its value lies partly in precedent and public reasoning.

A chosen case becomes an opportunity to examine a policy problem in depth, explain competing values, and push the platform toward broader changes.

This is the supreme-court model of moderation.

Do not hear everything.

Choose disputes that illuminate the rules.

The contrast with internal appeals is instructive.

The internal system is high-volume error correction.

The Oversight Board is low-volume governance.

A mature appeal architecture often needs both.

## Precedent in a private rule system

Platforms have terms and standards that function like a private codebook.

At scale, vague rules create inconsistent enforcement.

What counts as dangerous praise?

What counts as harassment?

What counts as newsworthiness?

When does artistic context change the outcome?

Which account-level penalties should follow repeated violations?

A public appellate decision can clarify the boundary.

The specific case matters.

The explanation matters more.

This is how a review institution creates precedent without necessarily using the same doctrine as a court.

An appeal can therefore produce three levels of output.

Restore this content.

Clarify this rule.

Change this system.

Most private appeals stop at the first.

The future will demand the other two because machine-scale decisions generate machine-scale patterns of error.

## Moderation is not only speech

The public debate tends to frame moderation appeals around free expression.

That is important, especially where laws and fundamental rights apply.

But platform moderation increasingly governs commerce and access too.

Seller listings.

Marketplace eligibility.

Advertising permissions.

Monetization.

Recommendation.

Account verification.

Live-streaming privileges.

Developer access.

A moderation appeal can be an economic appeal.

The DSA itself spans more than social speech; it regulates a broad category of intermediary and online platform services in the EU.

This matters because the remedy may affect livelihoods rather than merely visibility.

A system designed around “post restored or not” is inadequate for an economy built inside platforms.

The review layer has to understand which kind of power is being exercised.

## Speed versus virality

Content has a half-life.

A political post matters before the vote.

An emergency warning matters during the emergency.

A sale matters before it ends.

A news report matters while the event is current.

A live stream matters while it is live.

A correction delivered a week later may be formally complete and practically useless.

This gives moderation appeals the same urgency problem as medical review, but for different reasons.

The remedy decays with attention rather than biology.

Appeal systems should therefore classify time sensitivity.

Not every removed meme deserves emergency review.

Some cases do.

Election-related public-interest content, imminent safety information, major commercial events, or account actions affecting ongoing business may justify faster pathways under an institution's lawful policies.

The principle is not “important people go first.”

It is “time-sensitive consequences go faster.”

That is a more defensible queue.

## Appeals as a training set

A moderation system that reverses tens of millions of appealed decisions possesses an extraordinary dataset.

Each reversal asks a question about the first system.

Was the model wrong?

Was the policy unclear?

Did the user provide missing context?

Was language or dialect misunderstood?

Did human reviewers disagree?

Did enforcement fail to consider newsworthiness?

Was the penalty disproportionate?

Was the content correctly classified but the account consequence wrong?

If the platform stores only “reversed,” it wastes most of the information.

If it stores structured reasons, appeals become the highest-value evaluation set the platform owns because the examples came from real harms users cared enough to contest.

The same is true across every domain in this book.

Appeals are adversarial labels from reality.

## Fifty million reminders

The number fifty million deserves to return at the end of this chapter.

In February 2026, the European Commission summarized two years of DSA operation by saying that almost fifty million platform content or account decisions had been reversed after user challenge through internal mechanisms.

Fifty million is too large to describe as the occasional correction of edge cases.

It is an institution.

A hidden one, distributed across companies, interfaces, laws, contractors, reviewers, and automated systems—but an institution all the same.

The internet used to treat moderation as publication policy.

It is becoming adjudication infrastructure.

That does not mean platforms should become courts in every respect. It means they are learning the same lesson courts learned centuries ago.

When decisions become consequential, repeated, and rule-bound, the quality of the second decision becomes part of the legitimacy of the first.

The moderation court is already here.

It just does not look like a courthouse.
