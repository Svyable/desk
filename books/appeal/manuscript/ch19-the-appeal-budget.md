# Chapter 19 — The Appeal Budget

Every institution has an appeal budget whether it admits it or not.

The budget may appear as judges, examiners, ombudsmen, call-center staff, medical reviewers, investigators, compliance officers, trust-and-safety teams, outside experts, software, deadlines, queues, or simply the hours senior people spend fixing decisions that should not have become final.

Sometimes the budget is visible.

Often it is hidden.

But there is always a cost to being correctable.

There is also a cost to not being correctable.

That second cost is easier to ignore.

## Accuracy is not free

Organizations love first-pass accuracy because it is easy to measure.

How many claims were processed?

How many applications were approved automatically?

How many moderation decisions were made per hour?

How many fraud alerts were resolved without manual intervention?

How much did automation reduce handling cost?

These are useful numbers. They are also incomplete.

A decision system has at least two economic lives.

The first is the cost of producing an answer.

The second is the cost of living with the answer when it is wrong.

A cheap denial can create an expensive appeal.

A cheap suspension can destroy a merchant's week of revenue.

A cheap identity mismatch can generate hours of support work.

A cheap medical decision can move cost into emergency care, delay, deterioration, or litigation.

A cheap bureaucratic error can force the affected person to become an unpaid investigator of the institution that made it.

The invoice did not disappear.

It changed hands.

## Error externalities

Economists have a familiar language for costs pushed onto someone else: externalities.

Decision systems produce them constantly.

When an institution makes a mistake and provides no usable correction path, the affected person absorbs the labor of error.

They search for a phone number.

They collect documents.

They repeat the story.

They miss work.

They wait on hold.

They pay interest.

They lose access.

They hire counsel.

They live with uncertainty.

The institution can therefore make its own process look efficient by exporting the cost of correction.

This is one reason a low administrative cost can coexist with a terrible system.

The spreadsheet counts the institution's labor.

It does not count the citizen's.

A serious appeal budget has to account for both.

## The appeal gap

Earlier we met the appeal gap: the distance between having a formal right to challenge a decision and having a realistic ability to use that right.

Money widens that gap.

So does time.

So does complexity.

A right that requires forty hours of procedural labor is not equally available to a salaried lawyer and a shift worker caring for two children.

A right that requires a specialist is not equally available to someone with and without money.

A right that takes eighteen months is not equally valuable when the disputed thing is a payment needed next Tuesday.

This makes the economics of appeal inseparable from its legitimacy.

A process can be formally universal and practically regressive.

The poorer the person, the more expensive the same delay becomes.

## Spend review where error is expensive

No institution can deeply reconsider every decision.

Nor should it.

The solution is not infinite appeal.

The solution is intelligent allocation of correction capacity.

Think of review as a scarce resource.

Where should it go?

One answer is where the expected cost of error is highest.

That cost rises when the consequence is severe, the action is hard to reverse, the first-stage confidence is low, the evidence is ambiguous, the affected person is unlikely to recover without help, or the decision can propagate into other systems.

A mistaken restaurant recommendation and a mistaken transplant decision should not receive the same review budget.

A temporary content demotion and permanent account deletion should not necessarily receive the same review budget.

A reversible payment hold and irreversible deportation are not the same class of decision.

The architecture should know this before the appeal arrives.

High-stakes systems need correction capacity in proportion to the damage their errors can do.

## The reversal rate is not a score

Institutions often look at reversal rates and draw the wrong conclusion.

A high reversal rate can mean the first-stage process is poor.

It can also mean appeals are successfully selecting the hardest cases.

A low reversal rate can mean the first-stage process is excellent.

It can also mean the appeal process is inaccessible, deferential, or fake.

The number alone does not tell you which world you are in.

This is important because organizations can game an appeal metric without consciously intending to.

Make appeal harder and fewer weak decisions are challenged.

Define success as affirmance and reviewers learn to preserve the first answer.

Reward speed and difficult cases receive less attention.

Measure only completed appeals and the people who give up vanish from the denominator.

The right metric is not “How often did we win against the appellant?”

The institution is not supposed to be playing against the appellant.

The better question is whether the combined decision-and-review system reaches better outcomes at acceptable cost.

## Correction capacity

A useful institution has correction capacity the way a power grid has reserve capacity.

Most of the time, the reserve looks inefficient.

Then something goes wrong.

A policy change creates an unexpected wave of disputes.

A software update misclassifies thousands of accounts.

A new fraud pattern causes a model to overreact.

A court decision changes the governing rule.

A disaster changes what ordinary evidence looks like.

A data vendor sends bad records.

Suddenly the appeal system is not a customer-service accessory. It is the institution's ability to recover.

Underbuilt review systems accumulate queues precisely when correction matters most.

The institution then faces a cruel choice: rush the second decision or make people wait for it.

Both choices degrade trust.

Capacity therefore has to be built before the error wave.

That feels wasteful in calm periods.

So do lifeboats.

## Provisional truth

One way to reduce the cost of appeal is to stop treating every disputed first decision as fully final while review is pending.

This is the logic behind provisional credit in some payment disputes and stay mechanisms in law.

The broader principle is **provisional truth**.

A system can say: this is our current decision, but because it is contested and the cost of premature finality is high, some consequences will wait.

That is a profound institutional move.

It separates decision from immediate irreversible execution.

Not every domain can do this. Some harms occur if action is delayed. Some security decisions require immediate containment. Some benefits cannot be paid indefinitely on assertion alone.

But the design question should always be asked.

What must happen now?

What can wait until review?

What can be temporarily restored?

Who should bear the cost of uncertainty?

The default answer should not always be the person with less power.

## The price of a second look

As first decisions become cheaper, organizations will be tempted to increase their volume.

More screening.

More scoring.

More ranking.

More automated enforcement.

More micro-decisions attached to ordinary life.

That creates a paradox.

The marginal cost of deciding approaches zero while the marginal cost of disputing remains stubbornly human.

This is the appeal budget problem of the machine age.

If a system can make ten million consequential decisions overnight, but the institution can seriously review only ten thousand disputes, then automation has created a leverage mismatch.

The first layer scales computationally.

The second layer scales institutionally.

That gap is where uncorrected power accumulates.

The answer cannot simply be to hire one human reviewer for every automated decision. That would erase much of the benefit of automation.

The answer is to design the first layer so that uncertainty, novelty, severity, and credible contestation route scarce review attention intelligently.

Machines should not merely make decisions.

They should help decide which decisions deserve a second look.

And they should preserve the evidence needed for that second look to mean something.

## A budget reveals a value

Budgets are moral documents in an unromantic disguise.

An institution that spends heavily on making decisions but almost nothing on correcting them has revealed something about itself.

It values throughput over recoverability.

It values authority over learning.

It values the appearance of certainty over the cost of being wrong.

A healthy appeal budget does not imply distrust of every first decision.

It implies humility about systems operating at scale.

We budget for maintenance because machines wear out.

We budget for backups because storage fails.

We budget for insurance because improbable events happen.

We budget for courts because disputes cannot be wished away.

We should budget for correction because decisions fail too.

The first answer has a price.

So does the second.

Civilization becomes more reliable when it is willing to pay both.
