The central thesis is that as software and artificial intelligence make first decisions cheaper, faster, and more numerous, the scarce institutional resource becomes the second decision: the ability to challenge a consequential answer and obtain a genuinely different review with power to correct it. The book treats appeal not as legal ornament or customer-service courtesy but as civilization's error-correction layer, moving from courts and public benefits through health coverage, credit reporting, banking, tax, veterans' review, immigration, online platforms, content moderation, and machine decision systems before proposing an architecture for bounded corrigibility.
