# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*APPEAL* asks what happens after a consequential institution says no, and whether the capacity to reconsider a decision should be understood as core infrastructure rather than procedural overhead.

The durable thesis is narrower than “appeals are good”:

> As first decisions become cheaper and more automated, trustworthy institutions need a proportionate error-correction layer: legible reasons, preserved decision provenance, a usable route to contest material facts or rules, review capable of a different failure mode, authority to change the result, protection against irreversible harm where appropriate, restoration after reversal, and a learning loop back into the first-stage system.

The book should not imply that every dispute deserves unlimited review, that human reviewers are inherently superior to automated systems, or that reversal proves misconduct. Finality, fraud prevention, administrative capacity, consistency, and the costs imposed on other parties are genuine constraints. The design problem is bounded corrigibility.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Do formal appeal rights work in practice?** Compare the existence of a review path with deadlines, representation needs, evidentiary access, delay, and reviewer authority.
2. **Does a second layer actually differ from the first?** Look for independence in personnel, hierarchy, evidence, incentives, methods, institution, and remedial authority.
3. **What does reversal data reveal?** Treat reversal rates as telemetry requiring context rather than a simple score of first-stage quality.
4. **Who bears uncertainty while review is pending?** Examine stays, provisional credit, urgent medical review, benefit continuity, detention, account suspension, and other allocations of interim risk.
5. **Does reversal restore the person?** Track the restoration gap between changing the headline decision and undoing downstream consequences.
6. **Do institutions learn from appeals?** Look for feedback into policy, forms, training, source data, models, thresholds, and explanations.
7. **What changes in the AI era?** Test whether automated appeals create correlated failure, whether model diversity can provide useful review, and what provenance is necessary for meaningful machine-age contestability.

## Falsification and counter-evidence

- Appeals can be used strategically to delay legitimate enforcement or exhaust an opposing party.
- Additional review can add cost and inconsistency without improving outcomes.
- A human reviewer can share the same bias, incentives, incomplete evidence, or institutional culture as an automated first stage.
- High reversal rates do not by themselves prove the first-stage system is defective; appeals may select unusually difficult cases or introduce new evidence.
- Low reversal rates do not prove first-stage excellence; inaccessible procedures can suppress meritorious challenges.
- Some decisions must be executed immediately because delay itself creates unacceptable risk.
- Perfect restoration is often impossible once time-sensitive opportunities have passed.
- Transparency has legitimate limits, including privacy, security, anti-fraud controls, and protected information.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Courts and administrative review

U.S. Courts of Appeals and the distinction between trial and appellate functions; Social Security reconsideration, administrative-law-judge hearings, Appeals Council review, and federal court; IRS Independent Office of Appeals; veterans' Supplemental Claims, Higher-Level Review, and Board Appeals; immigration appeals to the Board of Immigration Appeals.

### II. Health and money

Health-insurance internal appeals and external review; Medicare's multi-level appeals structure; Fair Credit Reporting Act dispute rights; electronic-funds-transfer error resolution and provisional credit.

### III. Platforms and digital regulation

Private account suspensions and restoration; platform moderation appeals; Meta Oversight Board; European Union Digital Services Act internal complaint and certified out-of-court dispute settlement mechanisms.

### IV. Automated decisions

GDPR protections concerning certain solely automated decisions; EU AI Act explanation requirements for certain high-risk systems; technical and institutional literature on contestability, human review, correlated model errors, provenance, and algorithmic recourse.

### V. Institutional design

Administrative burden, procedural justice, ombuds institutions, independent review, appeals metrics, queueing and capacity, error externalities, and mechanisms for propagating reversals to downstream records.

## Sourcing conventions

Sources are logged in `research/source-ledger.csv` using the schema `id,year,author_or_institution,title,source_type,book_use,url`. Prefer statutes, courts, regulators, official agency guidance, peer-reviewed work, and primary institutional documentation. Company sources may establish a company's stated process but should not be treated as neutral evidence of effectiveness. Allegations are labeled as allegations. Procedures and deadlines are checked against current official sources before publication. No invented quotations, statistics, or named case studies.
