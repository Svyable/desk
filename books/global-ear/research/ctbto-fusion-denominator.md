# CTBTO case note: fusion is not simultaneity

## Editorial target

Chapter 1 currently uses the 12 February 2013 DPRK announced nuclear test as the clean example of serious multisensor evidence: seismic, infrasound, radionuclide and political declaration carrying different parts of the causal story. Keep the example, but make its timing and attribution harder. The radionuclide channel did **not** simply arrive as another near-simultaneous vote for the same event.

The better lesson is that evidence fusion has at least two denominators: **physical independence** and **attribution independence**. Different sensors may measure genuinely different physics while the later association of those measurements still depends on a model linking source, time and transport.

## What the primary record supports

CTBTO's event page reports that the 12 February 2013 event was registered by 94 seismic stations and two infrasound stations. By 12 March, CTBTO explicitly reported that no radioactive noble gases attributable to the event had yet been detected. The later radionuclide evidence arrived only in April.

On 23 April, CTBTO reported xenon-131m and xenon-133 at Takasaki, Japan, with lower levels at Ussuriysk, Russia. The isotope ratio was consistent with a fission event more than 50 days earlier; 55 days separated the announced test from the Takasaki measurement. CTBTO's technical account says atmospheric transport modelling made the DPRK test site a possible source and that regional information helped exclude alternative nuclear installations. The observations were interpreted as a delayed release around 7 April, after gases had apparently remained contained underground.

That sequence is more interesting than a tidy four-sensor checklist:

1. **Seismic localization/classification** established an explosion-like event and a location.
2. **Infrasound** supplied a different propagation channel, but was still generated by the same mechanical event.
3. **Radioxenon** supplied qualitatively different evidence of fission, but only after a long delay.
4. **Atmospheric transport modelling** was needed to connect the later gas measurement back toward a candidate source region.
5. **Source exclusion and context** mattered because reactors and radiopharmaceutical production can also emit radioxenon.
6. **The DPRK announcement** was not an independent physical sensor at all; it was political/contextual evidence.

This is a stronger model of multisensor reasoning because it refuses to count every channel as one equally independent vote.

## Proposed concept: the fusion denominator

Before saying that `N sensors confirmed the event`, ask what inferential work each channel actually performs.

A useful audit is:

| Channel | Distinct physics? | Distinct source ambiguity? | Requires linking model? | Main evidentiary job |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Seismic | yes | yes | event location/classification | locate and characterize explosion-like source |
| Infrasound | yes | partly | atmospheric propagation | corroborate atmospheric/mechanical coupling |
| Radioxenon | yes | yes | decay + atmospheric transport + source exclusion | establish fission-compatible material |
| State announcement | no physical measurement | political ambiguity | attribution/context | contextual corroboration |

The number of instruments is not the denominator. The denominator is the number of **independent failure modes actually closed**.

This would sharpen the chapter's existing sentence that serious multisensor evidence means “different physics rather than several displays downstream of one track.” Different physics is necessary but not sufficient. If the channels are fused by a shared localization error, transport assumption, clock error, source catalogue or analyst prior, that dependency belongs in the evidentiary accounting.

## Strongest counterargument

Do not overcorrect into generic skepticism about the 2013 case. The convergence was unusually strong. The xenon isotope ratio fit a fission event at approximately the relevant age; atmospheric transport modelling made the DPRK site a possible source; CTBTO consulted regional Member States to help exclude other sources; and the DPRK itself announced a nuclear test. The point is not that the radionuclide association was weak. It is that its strength came from a **chain of constrained inferences**, not from the mere existence of a fourth sensor modality.

Also avoid claiming that the xenon by itself uniquely proved the DPRK source. CTBTO's contemporaneous wording was careful: the gases “could be attributed” to the announced test, and the isotope ratio established consistency with fission at the relevant age. Fission products can also arise from nuclear energy and medical-isotope production.

## Suggested manuscript move

In the Chapter 1 DPRK passage, replace the implication of immediate parallel confirmation with a short temporal beat:

- the seismic/infrasound detection happened on 12 February;
- a month later there was still no attributable noble-gas detection;
- then, 55 days after the event, xenon appeared at Takasaki;
- the isotope clock and atmospheric transport model had to be reconciled with the earlier event.

That gives the reader a miniature detective story and earns the book's epistemic claim instead of announcing it. It also sets up a reusable rule for later anomaly chapters: **multisensor evidence is strongest when each channel closes a different failure mode and the model that joins the channels is itself exposed to failure.**

## Publication hold

Do not turn “consistent with” into “uniquely identified.” Preserve the distinction among detecting fission products, estimating their age, modelling their transport and attributing them to a particular event.
