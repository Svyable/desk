# GLOBAL EAR — research and evidence ledger

## Selected collision

Three evidence streams collide:

- **SOFAR / SOSUS:** long-range ocean acoustics developed for rescue and anti-submarine purposes; after U.S. Navy access widened, NOAA researchers used SOSUS-derived hydroacoustic data to detect and respond to submarine volcanic eruptions on the Juan de Fuca Ridge.
- **MOGUL:** the U.S. Army Air Forces pursued upper-atmosphere low-frequency acoustic sensing for long-range detection of Soviet nuclear detonations and missile signatures, explicitly drawing on Maurice Ewing's ocean sound-channel work.
- **CTBTO International Monitoring System:** a global verification network built to detect nuclear explosions also records earthquakes, volcanoes, whales and meteors; the 2013 Chelyabinsk fireball generated the largest infrasound event then recorded by the network and was detected thousands of kilometres away.

The surprising thesis is not that governments possess magical sensors. It is that **mission-specific strategic sensing repeatedly becomes general-purpose anomaly science once the same instrument is allowed to notice the wrong source.** Classification therefore changes the observing denominator of science without licensing imaginary hidden data.

## Class 1 — verified / declassified / documented

### SOFAR

NOAA explains that low-frequency sound can become trapped in the deep-ocean SOFAR channel and propagate hundreds or thousands of miles. WWII-era experiments demonstrated long-distance transmission.

Source: https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/sofar.html

### SOSUS becomes an Earth-science instrument

NOAA/PMEL states that in 1991 the U.S. Navy allowed NOAA access to SOSUS hydrophone arrays in the northeast Pacific. The resulting acoustic monitoring made remote detection of Juan de Fuca and Gorda Ridge volcanic events possible; by late 1999 three spreading-center eruptions had been detected acoustically and verified in the field.

Source: https://www.pmel.noaa.gov/pubs/outstand/embl2063/introduction.shtml

### Project MOGUL's stated acoustic mission

The U.S. Air Force's historical Roswell report describes Maurice Ewing's transfer of the ocean sound-channel idea to a hypothesized upper-atmosphere sound channel. MOGUL sought expendable microphones, telemetry and long-duration balloon platforms for long-range detection of Soviet nuclear detonations and ballistic-missile acoustic signatures.

Source: https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf

Important boundary: this official report is useful evidence for MOGUL's mission and engineering history. Its conclusions about Roswell are not required for this book's thesis and should not be imported wholesale.

### CTBTO's global sensor system

CTBTO describes the International Monitoring System as a global network using seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound and radionuclide technologies. The system is designed for nuclear-test verification but detects many natural phenomena.

Sources:
- https://www.ctbto.org/our-work/verification-regime
- https://www.ctbto.org/our-work/ims-map

### Chelyabinsk

CTBTO reported that the 15 February 2013 Chelyabinsk fireball produced the largest infrasound event then recorded by the IMS. Twenty infrasound stations detected it; a station in Antarctica roughly 15,000 km away recorded the signal. The network's analysis could distinguish the moving meteor source from a fixed explosion.

Source: https://www.ctbto.org/resources/for-the-media/press-releases/russian-fireball-largest-ever-detected-ctbtos-infrasound

### Nuclear-event discrimination is multimodal

CTBTO's account of the 12 February 2013 DPRK announced nuclear test reports detection by 94 seismic stations and two infrasound stations, followed later by radionuclide detections consistent with the event. This is a useful control against treating a single modality as ontology.

Source: https://www.ctbto.org/our-work/detecting-nuclear-tests/2013-dprk-nuclear-test

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- Historical operational details of classified acoustic networks that appear in participant histories but lack complete contemporaneous release.
- Claims about exact historical detection thresholds, geographic gaps and retained archives where authoritative public technical documentation is incomplete.
- Statements that particular non-target events were noticed internally before public release, unless an archival record establishes timing.

These can guide research. They cannot carry decisive narrative claims until sourced.

## Class 3 — disputed allegations / publication holds

Do not publish as fact without event-specific records:

- that a classified U.S. or allied sensor network has detected a particular UAP event;
- that classified hydroacoustic/infrasound systems contain proof of non-human technology;
- that absence of public data implies deliberate suppression;
- that every large atmospheric or oceanic energetic event must be detectable by every strategic network;
- that a lack of released records is evidence records exist;
- that CTBTO coverage or classification thresholds can be back-projected unchanged into earlier Cold War systems.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis / open conjecture

### Observing denominator

For an alleged event, the useful denominator is not simply `scientific instruments existed`. It is approximately:

**coverage × sensitivity × frequency band × geometry × duty cycle × archive retention × analyst/search policy × access**.

A negative becomes meaningful only after those terms are priced for the event.

### Mission aperture

A sensor can physically record phenomena outside its intended mission while organizational processing ignores them. The difference between **instrument aperture** and **mission aperture** may explain why scientifically valuable signals can exist before anyone asks the right question.

### Secrecy horizon

Classification can delay scientific exploitation of a sensing capability. This is a hypothesis about access and institutional use, not a claim that classified archives contain any particular anomaly.

### Wrong-source dividend

A mature sensor network may produce its greatest scientific surprise when the source violates the ontology it was built to classify: a submarine network hears an eruption; a nuclear-test network hears a meteor.

## Contradictions and incentives

### Security vs science

Strategic sensing benefits from concealment of coverage, sensitivity and processing. Science benefits from reproducibility, open methods and accessible archives. The same dataset can therefore have incompatible institutional value functions.

### Better sensors do not imply omniscience

Long propagation range is not uniform global coverage. Noise, geometry, atmospheric/oceanic state, station uptime, filtering and analyst priorities matter.

### Public release can distort historical inference

The observations that become scientifically famous may be the ones agencies chose or were able to release. Release history is not necessarily detection history.

### Anomaly communities have an incentive to invent hidden confirmation

Once historical cases prove that secret sensors existed, it becomes rhetorically cheap to say `they must have the data`. This is precisely the inference the book rejects.

## Defeat conditions

The book's strong form weakens if historical cases show that dual-use scientific discoveries were trivial public-sensor rediscoveries rather than meaningful transfers of strategic sensing capability; if mission filtering made off-mission detection physically unavailable rather than merely institutionally neglected; or if the proposed observing-denominator framework cannot produce stronger negatives as coverage information improves.

The thesis does **not** require classified anomaly records to exist.
