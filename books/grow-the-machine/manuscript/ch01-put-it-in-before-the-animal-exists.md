# 1. Put It in Before the Animal Exists

The beetle was already cheating.

That is the first thing to understand.

By 2009, engineers could put a radio, microcontroller, battery and stimulators on a living beetle and tell it, from across the room, to take off. They could stop flight. They could alter elevation. They could make it turn by electrically stimulating flight muscles. The animal supplied the wings, actuators, fuel conversion, balance, damage tolerance, sensory preprocessing and most of the control system. The engineers supplied a small box and an argument with its nervous system.

Calling the result a robot makes the robot look better than it was.

Calling it an animal makes the electronics disappear.

So the literature found the obvious compromise: cyborg beetle.

The phrase is irresistible and therefore dangerous. It pulls attention toward the spectacle—the bug obeying radio commands—and away from the engineering bargain underneath it. A tiny flying machine is hard to build because every gram becomes an argument. Wings need actuators. Actuators need power. Power needs storage. Control needs sensing. Sensing needs computation. Structure has mass. Batteries have mass. The smaller the aircraft, the less room there is to hide one engineering problem inside another.

An insect arrives with the difficult parts already solved.

Not solved in the way an aerospace engineer would solve them. Solved by being alive.

That difference is not poetic. It is the whole proposition.

In a 2008 DARPA program compendium, the agency described its Hybrid Insect MEMS program as an effort to integrate microsystem payloads on insects. The payloads, if the program succeeded, would draw power from the insect, control locomotion and carry defense-relevant sensors. Then the document says something much stranger. The technology would exploit biological entry points in insect development—metamorphosis, specifically—to create reliable interfaces to insect sensors and tissue.

The manufacturing process included becoming an insect.

There are easier ways to make a reader laugh at a Pentagon research program. One can say “spy bugs.” One can imagine a moth at a window wearing a microphone. One can mention that the stated objective included controlling locomotion to within five meters over a hundred-meter trip. The comedy writes itself because the nouns are doing all the work.

But consider the engineering move without the moth.

You have a machine whose final geometry is difficult to access. You need a component inside it. Instead of opening the finished machine, placing the component and repairing the damage, you insert the component while the machine is still constructing itself. Growth proceeds around the foreign object. Remodeling becomes assembly. Healing becomes packaging. Development does work that your factory cannot easily do.

Now remove the word *insect*.

The idea gets bigger.

## The pupa is not a box

The crude version of the story says that researchers implanted electronics into a caterpillar, waited, and a cyborg moth emerged. This is close enough to make a headline and bad enough to ruin a book.

Metamorphosis is not a 3-D printer with a convenient pause button.

During complete metamorphosis, larval tissues undergo extensive remodeling. Some structures are destroyed; others persist; imaginal tissues proliferate and differentiate; nerves and muscles reorganize; the body changes geometry while remaining very much alive. A foreign object inserted into that process does not simply sit on a conveyor belt waiting to be overmolded. It can be rejected, displaced, encapsulated, damage developing structures, interfere with emergence, alter behavior or survive physically while failing electrically.

The biological process is not working for the engineer. The engineer is trying to negotiate with it.

That is a more useful description of biohybrid engineering in general.

A steel bracket does not decide to wall itself off from a bolt. A printed circuit board does not molt. A gearbox does not heal badly. An antenna does not develop into a different antenna because the humidity was wrong during its juvenile stage.

Living substrates bring capabilities conventional machines do not have and failure modes conventional engineering vocabulary tends to domesticate. We say *variability* when we mean two nominally identical organisms may respond differently to the same stimulus. We say *adaptation* when we mean the controlled system may change because it is being controlled. We say *healing* as though healing necessarily restores the interface we wanted. We say *self-powered* when the power source is an organism with its own energy budget and priorities.

The organism is not a component in the ordinary sense.

It has a vote.

## What actually flew

The 2009 peer-reviewed beetle work matters because it gives us something better than a program slide.

Researchers demonstrated remote radio control of free-flying insects using a miniature system containing neural and muscular stimulators, a radio transceiver, microcontroller and battery. Neural stimulation was used to initiate, stop and modulate flight; muscular stimulation was used to induce turns. This is a real laboratory result. Later work improved steering by identifying and stimulating a flight muscle involved in graded turns. Another line of work demonstrated high-success-rate flight initiation by stimulating thoracic flight muscle rather than relying on more delicate neural targets.

These results deserve to be taken literally.

Literally is stronger than enthusiastically.

A beetle receiving radio commands and changing flight is extraordinary enough. We do not improve the result by quietly upgrading it into an autonomous reconnaissance platform.

The distinction becomes especially important because the original military objective was not “make an insect turn.” The objective described a mission geometry: move an insect roughly one hundred meters and bring it within roughly five meters of a target, keep it there, and return sensor information. That is a systems problem. Steering is one piece. Navigation, localization, command link, payload, power, environmental robustness, behavioral reliability, target retention and data transmission are other pieces.

A left turn is not a mission.

This sounds pedantic until one notices how technological myths are manufactured. A proposal supplies the destination. A laboratory demonstration supplies one working subsystem. A magazine illustration supplies the rest. Twenty years later, memory compresses the three into a machine that existed.

This book will not do that.

## The better failure

There is another reason not to inflate the cyborg insect.

Failure is more interesting here.

Suppose a developmental implant survives metamorphosis but the adult animal cannot be steered reliably. That tells us something about interface survival but not mission control. Suppose electrical stimulation reliably produces a turn but repeated stimulation habituates or degrades the response. That tells us control authority is time-dependent. Suppose the animal flies beautifully but cannot carry enough battery and sensor mass. That turns biology's apparent free lunch back into a mass budget. Suppose an artificial microrobot eventually beats the animal on controllability but still loses on endurance or collision recovery. Now we have an engineering comparison rather than a novelty act.

The relevant question is not whether cyborg insects are creepy, brilliant or ridiculous.

The question is where the biological bargain closes.

In 2025, Berkeley researchers reported an untethered subcentimeter artificial flying robot weighing only 21 milligrams, driven by an external alternating magnetic field. It could hover, recover from collisions and adjust its route. The machine is not a direct replacement for a beetle—the power architecture alone makes the comparison unfair—but it is exactly the sort of unfair comparison we need. The artificial side keeps moving.

So does the biological side. Recent terrestrial cyborg-beetle work has investigated sustained locomotion control, stimulation decay and autonomous navigation strategies. Other systems use an insect's own obstacle sensing and climbing behavior instead of trying to reproduce those functions electronically. The frontier is not “nature versus machine.” It is an argument over which pieces of the stack should be grown, borrowed, stimulated, modeled or fabricated.

That argument is much larger than insects.

## The factory nobody owns

Manufacturing usually means imposing form on matter.

Cut this. Deposit that. Etch here. Cure. Weld. Polish. Assemble. Inspect.

Biology has another verb: develop.

A fertilized egg becomes an animal without a foreman placing the liver. A wound closes through local activity without a repair technician specifying every cell trajectory. Bone remodels under load. Vasculature grows into tissue. Immune systems inspect interfaces. Plants alter architecture in response to light, damage, gravity and water. None of these processes are magic and none are centrally scripted in the way a CAD file scripts a machined part.

They are conditional construction processes.

Engineers have spent centuries learning how to suppress those processes when they interfere with devices. Stop biofouling. Prevent scar formation. Make the implant inert. Keep tissue from growing where it should not. Stabilize the substrate. Freeze the geometry.

HI-MEMS pointed in the opposite direction.

What if the instability is the tool?

What if you put the object in before the tissue finishes deciding what the object is surrounded by?

This is where the story becomes dangerous to overstate. A pupal implant does not prove that metamorphosis can manufacture arbitrary machines. A beetle is not a programmable factory. The surviving public record does not establish operational insect reconnaissance. And developmental biology is full of examples where intervention produces deformity, death or compensation rather than the neat integration an engineer drew on a slide.

Good.

Those constraints are the subject.

A useful technology has to survive contact with all of them.

The old dream of robotics was to make machines more animal-like. Better legs. Better eyes. Better reflexes. Better autonomy. Better adaptation.

There is a second route that sounds similar and is not.

Do not copy the animal.

Do not merely ride the animal.

Enter while it is becoming one.
