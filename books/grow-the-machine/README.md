# Grow the Machine

## How Metamorphosis Became a Manufacturing Process

**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson

## Governing thesis

In the 2000s, a DARPA program called Hybrid Insect Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (HI-MEMS) pursued an engineering move stranger than simply strapping electronics to animals: insert components during insect development and let metamorphosis build living tissue around the machine. The important story is not that the Pentagon once wanted cyborg bugs. It is that development itself became a proposed fabrication step. This book follows that idea from pupal implants and radio-controlled beetles into developmental bioengineering, biohybrid robotics, organoid interfaces, self-healing materials, and the larger question of what changes when engineers stop assembling finished organisms and instead intervene while organisms are assembling themselves.

The book does **not** claim that HI-MEMS produced operational spy insects, that developmental integration automatically creates stable neural interfaces, or that current biohybrid systems have escaped laboratory constraints. Program goals, laboratory demonstrations, operational capability, and speculative extrapolation remain separate evidence classes.

## Why this book is distinct

Recent Desk books include *Borrowed Senses*, about military systems recruiting evolved animal capabilities, and *When the Forest Reports Back*, about organisms and ecologies becoming sensing infrastructure. *Grow the Machine* is narrower and stranger. Its object is **development as fabrication**: metamorphosis, growth, wound healing, tissue remodeling and morphogenesis as processes an engineer might deliberately recruit to construct the interface itself. The beetle is the opening machine shop, not the destination.

## Architecture

1. **Put It in Before the Animal Exists** — HI-MEMS and the conceptual break.
2. **The Factory That Eats Itself** — what complete metamorphosis actually does and does not do.
3. **A Specification Written for a Pupa** — reconstructing DARPA's stated milestones without confusing them with achieved capability.
4. **The Beetle Takes Off** — what wireless free-flight control demonstrated in 2009.
5. **Steering Is Not Navigation** — laboratory actuation versus autonomous mission performance.
6. **Scar Tissue Is an Engineer** — wound healing, encapsulation, interface stability and biological failure.
7. **The Moving Target** — electrodes inside tissue that grows, remodels and changes state.
8. **Power From the Passenger** — energy harvesting and the fantasy of infrastructure-free machines.
9. **The Animal Pushes Back** — habituation, variability, fatigue, competing behavior and loss of control authority.
10. **Build It or Grow It?** — insect-scale artificial robots as the control group.
11. **Developmental Manufacturing** — when biological development becomes a production process rather than a nuisance.
12. **Organoids With Wires** — neural and tissue interfaces where growth occurs around engineered structures.
13. **Self-Repair Is Not Maintenance-Free** — healing, regeneration and the costs hidden by biological language.
14. **Evolution Is Not Your Supplier** — what engineers inherit when they appropriate a living platform.
15. **The Ethics of the Small** — why moral intuitions change with animal scale and why engineering cannot outsource the question.
16. **Machines That Finish Themselves** — programmable morphogenesis, living materials and the broader design space.
17. **What Would Count as a Breakthrough?** — quantitative criteria for developmental integration to beat conventional assembly.
18. **Grow the Machine** — the boundary between making an object and arranging conditions under which an object makes itself.
