# Chapters 14–18 evidence ledger

## Chapter 14 — Henry Ford, 1906–1915

### Working claim

Ford's distinctive leverage was acceleration of mass affordability rather than invention of the automobile or assembly line from nothing. The Model T, moving assembly methods, price reductions, and the five-dollar day helped make the automobile an ordinary mass-market object early enough to reorganize manufacturing, labor discipline, household mobility, roads, and consumption.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- Ford Motor Company, “The Moving Assembly Line and the Five-Dollar Workday,” describing the 1913 Highland Park moving assembly line and the 1914 five-dollar workday while explicitly noting Ford did not invent the automobile: https://corporate.ford.com/articles/history/moving-assembly-line/
- The Henry Ford, Ford Motor Company chronology, including the 1908 Model T, 1913 assembly-line experiments, 1914 five-dollar/eight-hour policy, and millionth Ford in 1915: https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/popular-research-topics/ford-motor-co-chronology-1903-2003
- The Henry Ford, “Ford's Five-Dollar Day,” on high turnover, assembly-line discipline, and the mixed motives behind the wage policy: https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/fords-five-dollar-day
- Ford Motor Company, Highland Park history, for the scale of Model T production and price decline: https://corporate.ford.com/corporate/corporate/articles/history/highland-park/

### Secondary checks

- David L. Lewis, *The Public Image of Henry Ford* and related Ford scholarship.
- Stephen Meyer III, *The Five Dollar Day: Labor Management and Social Control in the Ford Motor Company, 1908–1921* (SUNY Press, 1981).
- David Hounshell, *From the American System to Mass Production, 1800–1932* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1984), for the long manufacturing lineage before Ford.
- Douglas Brinkley, *Wheels for the World* (Viking, 2003), checked against institutional histories.

### Strongest counterargument

Automobile mass production was already crowded with innovators. Ransom Olds pioneered assembly methods; Cadillac advanced standardization; William Durant built General Motors; road construction, petroleum, rubber, finance, and public policy were independent causes. Woodrow Wilson also created persistent institutional change through the Federal Reserve, FTC, and Progressive legislation.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if Ford's acceleration effect proves small—if a comparably cheap mass automobile, similar manufacturing organization, and equivalent labor-market changes would likely have emerged within only a few years through rival firms—or if Wilson's institutional reforms generate substantially larger counterfactual distance across the decade.

## Chapter 15 — Alice Paul, 1916–1925

### Working claim

Paul did not create the woman suffrage movement and did not alone secure the Nineteenth Amendment. Her distinctive leverage was as the movement's pressure edge: the National Woman's Party, White House picketing, imprisonment, hunger strikes, partisan accountability, and a relentless federal-amendment strategy made presidential avoidance more costly while mainstream organizers pursued complementary lobbying and state campaigns. Her immediate post-ratification pivot to the Equal Rights Amendment extended the constitutional method into the next fight.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- Library of Congress, National Woman's Party Records finding aid, including formation in 1916, 1917–18 arrests, 1919–20 ratification lobbying, and the 1923 Equal Rights Amendment: https://findingaids.loc.gov/repositories/19/resources/1951
- Library of Congress, “From Nineteenth Amendment to ERA,” on Paul's leadership and the 1923 introduction of the Equal Rights Amendment: https://www.loc.gov/exhibitions/women-fight-for-the-vote/about-this-exhibition/hear-us-roar-victory-1918-and-beyond/ratification-and-beyond/from-nineteenth-amendment-to-era/
- Library of Congress, National Woman's Party timeline, 1921–1929: https://www.loc.gov/collections/women-of-protest/articles-and-essays/historial-timeline-of-the-national-womans-party/1921-to-1929/
- Library of Congress, “Today in History — January 11: Alice Paul,” for biographical context and the ERA: https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/january-11/
- Library of Congress, *Women of Protest* National Woman's Party photographs and detailed chronology: https://www.loc.gov/collections/women-of-protest/

### Secondary checks

- J. D. Zahniser, *Alice Paul: Claiming Power* (Oxford University Press, 2014).
- Christine Lunardini, *From Equal Suffrage to Equal Rights: Alice Paul and the National Woman's Party, 1910–1928* (NYU Press, 1986).
- Elaine Weiss, *The Woman's Hour* (Viking, 2018), for Tennessee ratification.
- Martha S. Jones, *Vanguard: How Black Women Broke Barriers, Won the Vote, and Insisted on Equality for All* (Basic Books, 2020), as a corrective to white-suffrage-centered accounts.

### Strongest counterargument

Carrie Chapman Catt and NAWSA commanded greater organizational breadth and pursued the Winning Plan that combined state and federal work. The suffrage movement was generational and interracial despite segregation and racism within major organizations. Woodrow Wilson's wartime presidency, the political effects of World War I, and state suffrage victories may have made ratification likely without Paul's confrontational tactics.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if the best scholarship shows that Paul's militant pressure contributed little or negatively to congressional and state movement, with Catt or another organizer supplying more personal leverage, or if Wilson's wartime and international decisions dominate the decade's persistent American transformation by a clear margin.

## Chapter 16 — Franklin D. Roosevelt, 1926–1935

### Working claim

Roosevelt's decisive leverage was not single-handedly ending the Great Depression. It was making federal responsibility for household-scale economic security politically durable through banking stabilization, mass communication, relief, financial reform, deposit insurance, and Social Security. The New Deal converted several previously contested risks from private misfortune into recurring fields of national administration.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- National Archives, Social Security Act of 1935, including Roosevelt's January 1935 message and August 15 signing: https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/social-security-act
- National Archives, “FDR's Fireside Chat on the Recovery Program,” on the Hundred Days and New Deal relief, recovery, and reform: https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/fdr-fireside
- FDR Presidential Library, presidency and fireside-chat chronology: https://www.fdrlibrary.org/fdr-presidency
- FDIC, 1930–1939 institutional timeline, including the 1933 bank holiday, Emergency Banking Act, and creation of deposit insurance: https://www.fdic.gov/history/1930-1939
- Federal Reserve History, Banking Act of 1933 / Glass-Steagall, including creation of the FDIC and Roosevelt's initial skepticism about deposit insurance: https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/glass-steagall-act

### Secondary checks

- David M. Kennedy, *Freedom from Fear* (Oxford University Press, 1999).
- Ira Katznelson, *Fear Itself: The New Deal and the Origins of Our Time* (Liveright, 2013), especially for the role of southern segregationist power in New Deal legislation.
- Eric Rauchway, *The Money Makers* and *Why the New Deal Matters*, for monetary and institutional interpretation.
- Amity Shlaes, *The Forgotten Man* (Harper, 2007), as a conservative counter-reading of New Deal intervention, checked against economic historiography.

### Strongest counterargument

Many New Deal ideas predated Roosevelt, were authored by Congress, or emerged from state experiments and pressure movements. Frances Perkins was central to Social Security and labor policy; Henry Steagall forced deposit insurance into the 1933 banking settlement; Huey Long and Townsend moved the political boundary from outside. Full employment did not return until wartime mobilization, and economists dispute the growth effects of specific New Deal programs.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if another plausible Democratic administration would likely have produced the same banking, regulatory, social-insurance, and relief architecture on nearly the same timetable, or if Hoover, Perkins, Long, Keynes, or another figure demonstrates greater personal leverage over the decade's durable institutional settlement.

## Chapter 17 — J. Robert Oppenheimer, 1936–1945

### Working claim

Oppenheimer did not authorize the Manhattan Project, run its overall military-industrial system, or decide to use atomic bombs against Japanese cities. His distinctive leverage was scientific integration at Los Alamos: recruiting and coordinating exceptional specialists so that distributed nuclear knowledge became working weapon designs, including the implosion device demonstrated at Trinity. The chapter also treats the Manhattan Project as a state system, not a lone-genius invention.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- National Park Service, “Manhattan Project Scientists: J. Robert Oppenheimer,” noting Groves's 1942 appointment of Oppenheimer to lead Site Y and his direction of Los Alamos through Trinity: https://www.nps.gov/people/manhattan-project-scientists-j-robert-oppenheimer.htm
- National Park Service, “The Life of J. Robert Oppenheimer: The Manhattan Project Years (1941 to 1946),” on Oppenheimer's early bomb-design coordination and Los Alamos leadership: https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/the-life-of-j-robert-oppenheimer-the-manhattan-project-years-1941-to-1946.htm
- National Park Service, “Manhattan Project Science at Los Alamos,” on recruitment, laboratory organization, and Los Alamos's role in weapon design and testing: https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/manhattan-project-science-at-los-alamos.htm
- U.S. Department of Energy, Trinity Site history, recording the July 16, 1945 first nuclear explosion: https://www.energy.gov/lm/trinity-site-worlds-first-nuclear-explosion
- National Park Service, Leslie Groves biography, on the military leadership, industrial scope, and Groves's selection of Oppenheimer: https://home.nps.gov/people/manhattan-project-leaders-leslie-richard-groves-jr.htm

### Secondary checks

- Kai Bird and Martin J. Sherwin, *American Prometheus* (Knopf, 2005).
- Richard Rhodes, *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* (Simon & Schuster, 1986).
- Alex Wellerstein, *Restricted Data: The History of Nuclear Secrecy in the United States* (University of Chicago Press, 2021), for secrecy and institutional legacy.
- Barton J. Bernstein and other historians of the atomic-bomb decision, used to preserve the contested motives and alternatives around Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

### Strongest counterargument

Leslie Groves may have been more indispensable to actual project execution because he controlled procurement, construction, security, industrial sites, and military coordination. Franklin Roosevelt authorized the project; Harry Truman made the final use decisions; other physicists could plausibly have led Los Alamos. If the U.S. industrial-scientific machine would have delivered a bomb in 1945 under another laboratory director, Oppenheimer's counterfactual leverage narrows substantially.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if credible reconstruction shows that replacing Oppenheimer produces little or no delay in the implosion program, Trinity, or wartime bomb availability, while Roosevelt, Groves, Truman, Hitler, Marshall, Eisenhower, or another figure creates much greater American counterfactual distance over the same interval.

## Chapter 18 — George C. Marshall, 1946–1955

### Working claim

Marshall's leverage was converting wartime logistical credibility into political support for a durable peacetime commitment to European recovery. The Marshall Plan was a Truman-administration and transatlantic project, not Marshall's personal blueprint; his distinctive contribution was framing economic reconstruction as security policy and lending nonpartisan credibility to a program that required Congress and the public to accept sustained overseas responsibility.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, George C. Marshall biography, including his wartime Army role and leadership in securing congressional support for the European Recovery Program: https://history.state.gov/departmenthistory/people/marshall-george-catlett
- U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, “Marshall Plan, 1948,” including the June 5, 1947 Harvard proposal and congressional passage of the Economic Cooperation Act: https://history.state.gov/milestones/1945-1952/marshall-plan
- U.S. Department of State, “The Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan,” on the economic-recovery logic and connection to containment: https://history.state.gov/departmenthistory/short-history/truman
- NATO and State Department historical materials on the North Atlantic Treaty negotiations for the wider institutional context.

### Secondary checks

- Benn Steil, *The Marshall Plan: Dawn of the Cold War* (Simon & Schuster, 2018).
- Melvyn P. Leffler, *A Preponderance of Power* (Stanford University Press, 1992), for early Cold War strategy.
- Michael J. Hogan, *The Marshall Plan: America, Britain, and the Reconstruction of Western Europe, 1947–1952* (Cambridge University Press, 1987).
- Daniel Yergin, *Shattered Peace* (Houghton Mifflin, 1977), for the formation of the Cold War order.

### Strongest counterargument

Harry Truman made a wider set of decisive choices: atomic policy, Truman Doctrine, recognition of Israel, NATO, armed-forces desegregation, and Korea. Dean Acheson was central to the alliance and containment architecture, while Kennan supplied the canonical containment framework. European recovery also depended overwhelmingly on European institutions, labor, industrial capacity, national policy, and cooperation—not American aid alone.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if Marshall's reputation and advocacy prove peripheral to congressional passage and European response, or if Truman's personal decisions create clearly larger counterfactual distance across the whole decade than Marshall's contribution to reconstruction and postwar commitment.
