# Chapters 9–13 evidence ledger

## Chapter 9 — Abraham Lincoln, 1856–1865

### Working claim

Lincoln's decisive leverage was sequencing: maintaining a Union coalition long enough to win while transforming the war's meaning from preservation alone toward emancipation, Black military service, a national language of equality, and constitutional abolition. The chapter rejects the shorthand that Lincoln alone “freed the slaves”; enslaved people's self-emancipation, abolitionists, Congress, Black soldiers, Union armies, and military victory are independent causes.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- National Archives, Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863, with limitations and context: https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/emancipation-proclamation
- National Archives, featured-document overview noting the proclamation's transformation of the war and Black military service: https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/featured-documents/emancipation-proclamation
- National Archives, Gettysburg Address exhibit context: https://www.archives.gov/press/press-releases/2004/nr04-23
- Library of Congress, Abraham Lincoln Papers: https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/
- National Archives, America's Founding Documents / Thirteenth Amendment resources: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27

### Secondary checks

- Eric Foner, *The Fiery Trial: Abraham Lincoln and American Slavery* (W. W. Norton, 2010).
- James M. McPherson, *Battle Cry of Freedom* (Oxford University Press, 1988).
- David W. Blight, *Frederick Douglass: Prophet of Freedom* (Simon & Schuster, 2018), for pressure on Lincoln from Black abolitionism and recruitment.
- Chandra Manning, *What This Cruel War Was Over* (Knopf, 2007), for soldiers' understandings of slavery and Union.

### Strongest counterargument

Ulysses S. Grant supplied the military victories without which emancipation in Confederate territory and Union survival could not be secured. Enslaved people themselves forced emancipation onto the wartime agenda. Frederick Douglass and Radical Republicans pressed Lincoln beyond his initial position. Jefferson Davis and Confederate leadership made choices that prolonged the war and shaped its outcome.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if research shows that a different plausible Republican president could have maintained the same coalition, adopted emancipation on a similar timetable, empowered Black enlistment, won reelection, and secured congressional passage of the Thirteenth Amendment with little change in outcome.

## Chapter 10 — Ulysses S. Grant, 1866–1875

### Working claim

Grant's distinctive leverage was executive enforcement. Congressional Reconstruction and the Reconstruction Amendments created rights and federal powers before his presidency, but Grant's administration used the Enforcement Acts, newly organized Justice Department, federal prosecutors, marshals, and troops against Klan terrorism. The chapter treats the later retreat from enforcement as evidence of political choice rather than proof that enforcement had never been effective.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- Federal Judicial Center, Civil Rights Act / Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871: https://www.fjc.gov/history/timeline/civil-rights-act-1871
- Federal Judicial Center, South Carolina Ku Klux Klan Trials of 1871–1872: https://www.fjc.gov/history/spotlight-judicial-history/ku-klux-klan-trials-1871-1872
- U.S. Senate Historical Office, Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871: https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/generic/EnforcementActs.htm
- Federal Judicial Center, “The Judiciary During the Gilded Age,” including Reconstruction enforcement and the Civil Rights Act of 1875: https://www.fjc.gov/history/public-speaking-resources/judiciary-during-gilded-age
- White House Historical Association, “The White House and Reconstruction,” for Grant administration context: https://www.whitehousehistory.org/the-white-house-and-reconstruction

### Secondary checks

- Brooks D. Simpson, *Let Us Have Peace: Ulysses S. Grant and the Politics of War and Reconstruction, 1861–1868* (UNC Press, 1991).
- Ron Chernow, *Grant* (Penguin Press, 2017).
- Fergus M. Bordewich, *Klan War: Ulysses S. Grant and the Battle to Save Reconstruction* (Knopf, 2023).
- Eric Foner, *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* (Harper & Row, 1988).

### Strongest counterargument

Thaddeus Stevens and congressional Republicans designed much of Reconstruction's legal architecture; Charles Sumner pushed a broader civil-rights vision; Andrew Johnson's obstruction helped trigger congressional Reconstruction. Grant's enforcement was geographically uneven and weakened by the mid-1870s, while his administration's scandals damaged support for federal intervention.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if Grant's personal role in Klan suppression and civil-rights enforcement proves substantially replaceable by congressional momentum and Justice Department actors, or if Stevens, Sumner, Johnson, or another figure generated more persistent leverage across the full decade.

## Chapter 11 — Thomas Edison, 1876–1885

### Working claim

Edison did not invent the incandescent lamp alone. His exceptional leverage was system commercialization: organized laboratory research, practical lamp design, generators, distribution, financing, manufacturing, and a central-station utility model. The Pearl Street station crossed the threshold from electrical demonstration to paid urban service.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- Library of Congress, “Life of Thomas Alva Edison,” including Menlo Park, the 1878 lighting focus, system development, and Pearl Street: https://www.loc.gov/collections/edison-company-motion-pictures-and-sound-recordings/articles-and-essays/biography/life-of-thomas-alva-edison/
- Smithsonian National Museum of American History, model and history of Edison's Pearl Street power station, which began service September 4, 1882: https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_712587
- Thomas Edison National Historical Park, National Park Service: https://www.nps.gov/edis/
- Library of Congress, Edison motion-picture and sound-recording collections for later continuity and company records: https://www.loc.gov/collections/edison-company-motion-pictures-and-sound-recordings/

### Secondary checks

- Paul Israel, *Edison: A Life of Invention* (Wiley, 1998).
- Jill Jonnes, *Empires of Light* (Random House, 2003), for Edison, Westinghouse, Tesla, and the system competition.
- Thomas P. Hughes, *Networks of Power* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1983), for electric systems rather than lone-inventor mythology.

### Strongest counterargument

Alexander Graham Bell's telephone created another transformational network beginning in 1876. Joseph Swan developed incandescent lighting in Britain; Edison's staff supplied critical work; Westinghouse and alternating current ultimately provided a more scalable transmission architecture. Electrification was almost certainly coming without Edison.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if Edison's system-building role proves too replaceable—particularly if Bell's telephony or another industrial figure produced greater decade-specific leverage and a non-Edison electrical utility would likely have appeared on almost the same timetable.

## Chapter 12 — J. P. Morgan, 1886–1895

### Working claim

Morgan's leverage came from occupying an institutional gap: the United States had national-scale railroads, corporations, securities markets, and financial crises before it had a central bank or mature public machinery for coordinating them. His railroad reorganizations, industrial financing, and 1895 gold syndicate made private reputation perform quasi-public stabilizing functions—with enormous democratic legitimacy costs.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- Morgan Library & Museum, digitized syndicate records of Drexel, Morgan & Co. and J. P. Morgan & Co., including 1895–99 records: https://www.themorgan.org/morgan-syndicate-books
- Library of Congress / U.S. congressional serial material on Union Pacific indebtedness and the role of Drexel, Morgan and J. P. Morgan & Co.: https://www.loc.gov/item/2025882821/
- Library of Congress Chronicling America newspapers for contemporary reporting on Morgan, railroads, gold, and industrial consolidation: https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/
- Reuters historical review, September 2, 2026, noting the 1895 use of Wall Street financiers to stabilize the Treasury's gold position: https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/history-financing-america-six-crisis-episodes-2026-09-02/

### Secondary checks

- Jean Strouse, *Morgan: American Financier* (Random House, 1999).
- Ron Chernow, *The House of Morgan* (Atlantic Monthly Press, 1990).
- Richard White, *Railroaded* (W. W. Norton, 2011), as a corrective to banker-centered accounts of railroad development.
- Charles R. Geisst, *Wall Street: A History* (Oxford University Press, later editions).

### Strongest counterargument

John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil had deeper direct industrial dominance; Samuel Gompers and the AFL shaped a durable labor model; Ida B. Wells transformed the national and international anti-lynching campaign. Morgan's 1895 gold rescue also depended on government authority, European banks, and a monetary crisis larger than one financier.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if Morgan's railroad and Treasury roles were more substitutable among contemporary banking houses than the chapter implies, or if Rockefeller's control of oil generated greater persistent leverage across the economy and politics of the decade.

## Chapter 13 — Theodore Roosevelt, 1896–1905

### Working claim

Roosevelt did not create American overseas empire; McKinley's administration fought Spain, annexed Hawaii, took Puerto Rico and the Philippines, and began the Philippine-American War. Roosevelt's distinctive role was making energetic national power into a durable presidential theory across foreign and domestic policy: antitrust enforcement, labor mediation, conservation, Panama, great-power diplomacy, and the Roosevelt Corollary.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- U.S. National Park Service, Theodore Roosevelt biography and presidency overview: https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/theodore-roosevelt-biography.htm
- U.S. Department of Justice Antitrust Division, institutional history noting the Roosevelt administration's 1903 antitrust enforcement capacity: https://www.justice.gov/atr/history-antitrust-division
- Library of Congress, Theodore Roosevelt timeline, 1900–1905, including coal-strike intervention, Panama, Northern Securities-era enforcement, and Russo-Japanese mediation: https://www.loc.gov/collections/theodore-roosevelt-films/articles-and-essays/timeline-of-theodore-roosevelts-life/1900-to-1905/
- U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine: https://history.state.gov/milestones/1899-1913/roosevelt-and-monroe-doctrine
- U.S. National Park Service, Theodore Roosevelt and conservation, including Forest Service creation and protected lands: https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/theodore-roosevelt-and-conservation.htm
- U.S. National Park Service, Theodore Roosevelt timeline: https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/theodore-roosevelt-timeline.htm

### Secondary checks

- Edmund Morris, *Theodore Rex* (Random House, 2001).
- Kathleen Dalton, *Theodore Roosevelt: A Strenuous Life* (Knopf, 2002).
- Kristin L. Hoganson, *Fighting for American Manhood* (Yale University Press, 1998), for gender and expansionist culture.
- Paul A. Kramer, *The Blood of Government* (UNC Press, 2006), for the Philippine-American imperial context.

### Strongest counterargument

William McKinley made the central 1898–1900 choices that produced an overseas American empire and a new Republican political order. William Jennings Bryan transformed opposition politics, and W. E. B. Du Bois was becoming a defining intellectual voice on race and citizenship. Roosevelt's personality can easily steal causal credit from McKinley, Congress, military institutions, corporate regulators, Gifford Pinchot, and foreign actors.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if McKinley's choices in war, annexation, and party realignment prove to have greater persistent individual leverage than Roosevelt's later executive model, or if the major Roosevelt policies would likely have developed similarly under another Republican president.
