# Research brief and source ledger — The Decisive 25

## Research standard

This book makes twenty-five unusually strong causal claims in public: that one person had more individual leverage over each ten-year turn of United States history than any other plausible finalist. The prose should therefore be more skeptical than the title.

Every chapter must distinguish four layers:

1. what happened;
2. what the selected person actually did;
3. what institutions, movements, allies, opponents, material conditions, and accidents also caused the outcome;
4. why the counterfactual without this person still moves farther than the counterfactual without the strongest finalist.

Do not convert a memorable story into sole-cause history. Do not invent dialogue, thoughts, sensory detail, or quotations. Quotations belong only when verified against a primary or reputable documentary source. Moral judgment and causal importance remain separate.

## Chapter 1 — George Washington, 1776–1785

### Working claim

Washington's decisive contribution in this interval was not tactical genius by itself. It was keeping the Continental Army politically and militarily coherent long enough for the rebellion to survive, then using his unmatched military prestige to reinforce civilian supremacy rather than convert victory into personal rule.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- U.S. House of Representatives, History, Art & Archives, “General George Washington Resigned his Commission in Annapolis, Maryland,” December 23, 1783: https://history.house.gov/HistoricalHighlight/Detail/36498
- Founders Online, National Archives, editorial note on Washington's resignation as commander in chief: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/01-06-02-0319-0001
- Founders Online, National Archives, George Washington to Lewis Nicola, May 22, 1782, rejecting monarchical ideas: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/99-01-02-08501
- Founders Online, National Archives, Washington's Newburgh Address to the officers of the Army, March 15, 1783: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/99-01-02-10840
- U.S. National Park Service, “Experiencing the Story of the Battle of Princeton,” on the Ten Crucial Days and the army's recovery after the 1776 defeats: https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/experiencing-the-story-of-the-battle-of-princeton.htm
- National Archives, “The Founding Fathers: Virginia,” biographical overview including Washington's resignation and later return to constitutional politics: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/founding-fathers-virginia

### Secondary checks

- Ron Chernow, *Washington: A Life* (Penguin Press, 2010).
- Joseph J. Ellis, *His Excellency: George Washington* (Knopf, 2004).
- Edward J. Larson, *The Return of George Washington, 1783–1789* (William Morrow, 2014).

### Counterargument

Benjamin Franklin may have had greater leverage over the international coalition that made victory possible, particularly French support; Nathanael Greene may be underrated in preserving the southern war effort. A Washington chapter fails if it treats French money, troops, naval power, state militias, enlisted soldiers, Black participants, Indigenous nations, or other generals as scenery.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if deeper research supports the view that Washington's military and political functions were substantially replaceable while another finalist supplied a less replaceable condition of independence. The chapter should make the counterfactual case, not rest on Washington's later presidential fame.

## Chapter 2 — Alexander Hamilton, 1786–1795

### Working claim

The Constitution created legal capacity; Hamilton's Treasury program made that capacity financially operational. Funding federal obligations, assuming state debts, establishing revenue streams, supporting a national bank, and articulating a broader manufacturing strategy helped transform a weak postwar confederation into a government that could borrow, tax, administer, and act on a national scale.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- Founders Online, National Archives, Hamilton, “Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit,” January 9, 1790: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-06-02-0076-0002-0001
- Founders Online, National Archives, final version of the first report on further provision for public credit, December 13, 1790: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-07-02-0227-0003
- Founders Online, National Archives, final version of the Report on a National Bank, December 13, 1790: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-07-02-0229-0003
- Founders Online, National Archives, Hamilton's draft act to incorporate the Bank of the United States: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-07-02-0279
- Founders Online, National Archives, Hamilton's opinion on the constitutionality of the Bank, February 23, 1791: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-08-02-0060-0002
- Founders Online, National Archives, Hamilton's first draft of the Report on Manufactures: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-10-02-0001-0003

### Secondary checks

- Forrest McDonald, *Alexander Hamilton: A Biography* (W. W. Norton, 1979).
- Ron Chernow, *Alexander Hamilton* (Penguin Press, 2004).
- Max M. Edling, *A Revolution in Favor of Government* (Oxford University Press, 2003), for the Constitution's fiscal-military state context and a corrective to overly personal Hamilton narratives.

### Counterargument

James Madison has the stronger claim if constitutional design is treated as the decade's deepest lever; Washington's legitimacy may have been the prerequisite that made Hamilton's program politically possible. Hamilton also did not invent public credit, banking, or industrial policy, and his program emerged from vigorous congressional bargaining and existing Anglo-American financial practice.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if research shows that a broadly similar fiscal consolidation was politically inevitable under the new Constitution and another competent Treasury secretary would likely have produced it on nearly the same timetable. The chapter needs to show what was specifically Hamiltonian about the architecture, not merely that Hamilton held the office when it appeared.

## Chapter 3 — Toussaint Louverture, 1796–1805

### Working claim

The Haitian Revolution belongs inside United States history. Louverture became its most internationally powerful leader during the period when France's attempt to preserve or restore a slave empire in Saint-Domingue collided with U.S. trade, Atlantic geopolitics, and Napoleon's ambitions in North America. French defeat in Saint-Domingue was one major reason Louisiana lost strategic value to Napoleon, helping create the opening for the 1803 Louisiana Purchase and a radically larger United States.

The chapter must not compress the Haitian Revolution into Louverture alone. The revolt began before his dominance; thousands of enslaved and free people fought; Jean-Jacques Dessalines carried the war to independence after Louverture's capture; yellow fever devastated French forces; renewed war with Britain increased Napoleon's incentive to sell Louisiana. The claim is about Louverture's exceptional leverage inside a multi-causal revolution, not sole authorship of the Purchase.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, “The United States and the Haitian Revolution, 1791–1804,” including U.S. policy shifts toward Louverture and the link between French defeat and the Louisiana Purchase: https://history.state.gov/milestones/1784-1800/haitian-rev
- U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, “Napoleonic Wars and the United States, 1803–1815,” noting that the loss of Haiti made Louisiana strategically undesirable while renewed war with Britain approached: https://history.state.gov/milestones/1801-1829/napoleonic-wars
- National Museum of African American History and Culture, Smithsonian, “Toussaint Louverture,” biographical and revolutionary overview: https://nmaahc.si.edu/latinx/toussaint-louverture
- National Archives, “Jefferson Buys Louisiana Territory, and the Nation Moves Westward”: https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2003/spring/louisiana-purchase.html
- National Archives, Louisiana Purchase Treaty, April 30, 1803: https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/louisiana-purchase-treaty

### Secondary checks

- Philippe R. Girard, *Toussaint Louverture: A Revolutionary Life* (Basic Books, 2016).
- Ronald Angelo Johnson, *Diplomacy in Black and White: John Adams, Toussaint Louverture, and Their Atlantic World Alliance* (University of Georgia Press, 2014).
- Laurent Dubois, *Avengers of the New World: The Story of the Haitian Revolution* (Harvard University Press, 2004).

### Counterargument

Thomas Jefferson signed and constitutionalized the Louisiana Purchase; Napoleon made the offer; Dessalines and other Haitian commanders completed the defeat of the French expedition; disease destroyed French manpower; impending war with Britain made a vulnerable North American possession less attractive. Any one of these can support a rival selection.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if the best scholarship indicates that Napoleon would probably have sold Louisiana on roughly the same terms even with a securely French Saint-Domingue, or that Louverture's individual contribution to the Haitian outcome was sufficiently replaceable that another revolutionary leader deserves the causal credit. The chapter should be especially careful with the phrase “caused the Louisiana Purchase.” The evidence supports a major contributing mechanism, not a monocausal slogan.

## Research queue for chapters 4–25

Before drafting each later chapter, build the same five-part packet: working claim, primary/institutional anchors, serious secondary checks, strongest finalist counterargument, and falsification note. For the 2016–2026 chapter, freeze a dated evidence window before prose drafting so live political developments do not silently change the argument underneath the manuscript.
