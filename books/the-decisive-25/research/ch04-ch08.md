# Chapters 4–8 evidence ledger

## Chapter 4 — Tecumseh, 1806–1815

### Working claim

Tecumseh's exceptional leverage was not that he was the only Native leader resisting U.S. expansion, but that he tried to change the scale of the sovereignty dispute. His confederacy challenged the U.S. practice of acquiring land through separate treaties by arguing that no single Native community could alienate land belonging to Native peoples more broadly. If that coalition had survived the War of 1812 as a British-backed buffer, U.S. expansion through the Old Northwest could have developed very differently.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- U.S. National Park Service, “Tecumseh”: https://www.nps.gov/people/tecumseh.htm
- U.S. National Park Service, “Summer 1811: Tecumseh attempts to negotiate with white American settlers,” including Harrison's assessment that Tecumseh's absence was an opportunity to break up the confederacy: https://www.nps.gov/articles/tecumseh.htm
- U.S. National Park Service, War of 1812 resources on the northwestern theater and Native alliances: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/warof1812/index.htm
- Library of Congress, *A Century of Lawmaking for a New Nation*, treaty and congressional materials for the period: https://memory.loc.gov/ammem/amlaw/

### Secondary checks

- R. David Edmunds, *Tecumseh and the Quest for Indian Leadership* (Little, Brown, 1984; later editions).
- John Sugden, *Tecumseh: A Life* (Henry Holt, 1997).
- Adam Jortner, *The Gods of Prophetstown* (Oxford University Press, 2012), especially for Tenskwatawa and the danger of turning the confederacy into Tecumseh alone.

### Strongest counterargument

James Madison presided over the War of 1812; Andrew Jackson's victories at Horseshoe Bend and New Orleans transformed southern expansion and his national political future; William Henry Harrison destroyed Prophetstown in 1811 and defeated the British-Native force at the Thames in 1813. Tecumseh also could not overcome political differences among Native nations, and the British were never guaranteed to sacrifice imperial priorities for an Indigenous buffer state.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if the best scholarship indicates that the confederacy had already become strategically nonviable before Tecumseh's death, such that his personal removal changes little, while Madison, Harrison, Jackson, or Tenskwatawa supplied a less replaceable cause of the decade's outcome.

## Chapter 5 — John Marshall, 1816–1825

### Working claim

Marshall's leverage was institutional persistence. In a run of cases including *McCulloch v. Maryland* and *Gibbons v. Ogden*, the Marshall Court made federal supremacy, implied congressional power, federal appellate authority, and national control over interstate commerce more operational. The claim is not that Marshall invented judicial review or acted alone, but that his Court made the Supreme Court a recurring national arbiter whose rules survived the politicians and disputes that produced them.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- National Archives, *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819), decision and transcript: https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/mcculloch-v-maryland
- Law Library of Congress, official U.S. Reports, *M'Culloch v. State of Maryland*, 17 U.S. 316 (1819): https://www.loc.gov/item/usrep017316/
- Law Library of Congress, official U.S. Reports, *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1 (1824): https://www.loc.gov/item/usrep022001/
- Supreme Court Historical Society, “The Marshall Court, 1801–1835”: https://supremecourthistory.org/history-of-the-courts/the-marshall-court-1801-1835/

### Secondary checks

- Charles F. Hobson, *The Great Chief Justice: John Marshall and the Rule of Law* (University Press of Kansas, 1996).
- R. Kent Newmyer, *John Marshall and the Heroic Age of the Supreme Court* (LSU Press, 2001).
- G. Edward White, *The Marshall Court and Cultural Change, 1815–1835* (Oxford University Press, 1988).

### Strongest counterargument

Henry Clay helped build the legislative nationalism of the American System and brokered the Missouri Compromise; James Monroe's administration included the Florida acquisition and Monroe Doctrine; John Quincy Adams supplied much of the diplomatic design. Economic integration and federal legislation may have driven nationalization even under a narrower Court.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if Marshall's key rulings prove substantially less causally important than the chapter claims—for example, if Congress, state adaptation, and commercial integration would likely have produced the same federal structure on nearly the same timetable under a more state-deferential chief justice.

## Chapter 6 — Andrew Jackson, 1826–1835

### Working claim

Jackson fused several transformations into one political style: expanded mass white-male electoral participation, durable national party organization, a presidency claiming direct popular legitimacy, destruction of the Second Bank's federal role, forceful resistance to nullification, and aggressive Indian removal. “Democracy” in the chapter is explicitly bounded: political inclusion widened for many white men while federal power helped dispossess Native nations and protected a slaveholding order.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- National Archives, President Andrew Jackson's 1830 message to Congress on Indian removal: https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/jacksons-message-to-congress-on-indian-removal
- National Archives, “The Bank War”: https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/treasures_of_congress/text/page9_text.html
- U.S. Senate Historical Office, “Senate Censures President,” on the 1834 bank/deposits confrontation: https://www.senate.gov/about/parties-leadership/censure-president-jackson.htm
- U.S. Senate Committee on Finance, historical account of the Second Bank dispute: https://www.finance.senate.gov/about/history
- Library of Congress, Andrew Jackson Papers timeline, 1822–1837: https://www.loc.gov/collections/andrew-jackson-papers/articles-and-essays/andrew-jackson-timeline-1767-1845/pursuing-the-presidency-1822-1837/
- Federal Reserve History, “The Second Bank of the United States”: https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/second-bank-of-the-us

### Secondary checks

- Daniel Feller, *The Jacksonian Promise: America, 1815–1840* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1995).
- Robert V. Remini, *Andrew Jackson and the Course of American Democracy, 1833–1845* and related volumes, used with newer scholarship on removal and race.
- Claudio Saunt, *Unworthy Republic: The Dispossession of Native Americans and the Road to Indian Territory* (W. W. Norton, 2020).

### Strongest counterargument

Broad white-male suffrage, party organization, Indigenous dispossession, and hostility to the national bank all predated Jackson. William Lloyd Garrison, John C. Calhoun, Henry Clay, and Martin Van Buren each supplied less theatrical but potentially more durable ideas or institutions. The economic consequences of the Bank War and Panic of 1837 are multi-causal and must not be assigned to Jackson alone.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if research shows that Jackson's distinctive executive choices were mostly expressions of transformations already certain to occur and that Van Buren's party-building, Calhoun's constitutional theory, Garrison's abolitionism, or another finalist created more persistent personal leverage.

## Chapter 7 — Samuel Morse, 1836–1845

### Working claim

Morse's significance is the practical separation of information speed from transportation speed in a rapidly expanding continental republic. He did not invent electrical signaling alone; Alfred Vail, Joseph Henry, Ezra Cornell, prior European telegraphy, federal funding, and later business builders are essential to the story. The personal-leverage claim concerns Morse's assembly of a workable American system, code, patent position, lobbying effort, and public demonstration at a historically consequential moment.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- Library of Congress, Samuel F. B. Morse Papers, “Invention of the Telegraph”: https://www.loc.gov/collections/samuel-morse-papers/articles-and-essays/collection-highlights/invention-of-the-telegraph/
- Library of Congress, original first telegraphic message, May 24, 1844: https://www.loc.gov/item/mmorse000107/
- Library of Congress, Morse timeline, including the March 3, 1843 $30,000 congressional appropriation and May 24, 1844 demonstration: https://www.loc.gov/collections/samuel-morse-papers/articles-and-essays/timeline/1840-1872/
- Library of Congress, Samuel F. B. Morse Papers collection: https://www.loc.gov/collections/samuel-morse-papers/

### Secondary checks

- Kenneth Silverman, *Lightning Man: The Accursed Life of Samuel F. B. Morse* (Knopf, 2003).
- Richard R. John, *Network Nation: Inventing American Telecommunications* (Harvard University Press, 2010).
- Tom Standage, *The Victorian Internet* (Walker, 1998), useful for network effects but checked against primary scholarship.

### Strongest counterargument

Electrical telegraphy was being independently developed in Britain and elsewhere; some form of rapid long-distance signaling was likely without Morse. Alfred Vail and Joseph Henry deserve substantial technical credit. James K. Polk, Joseph Smith, and others may have had larger direct effects on territory, religion, and politics in the same years.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if the timing counterfactual collapses—if a practical U.S. telegraph network was so close to inevitable under other inventors that removing Morse barely changes adoption, standards, business structure, or the timing of national communications.

## Chapter 8 — Frederick Douglass, 1846–1855

### Working claim

Douglass's leverage in this decade was not causing the sectional crisis but changing who possessed authority to define it. His transition from fugitive witness to independent Black editor, constitutional thinker, abolitionist organizer, and women's-rights advocate challenged both slavery and a reform culture in which white intermediaries often controlled the platform. His newspaper and speeches made Black civic authorship itself part of the antislavery case.

### Primary / institutional anchors

- Library of Congress, Frederick Douglass Papers timeline, including publication of the 1845 *Narrative*, British tour, manumission, and later political work: https://www.loc.gov/collections/frederick-douglass-papers/articles-and-essays/frederick-douglass-timeline/
- Library of Congress, Frederick Douglass Newspapers, 1847–1874, collection overview: https://www.loc.gov/collections/frederick-douglass-newspapers/about-this-collection/
- Library of Congress, *The North Star*, publication record beginning December 3, 1847: https://www.loc.gov/item/sn84026365/
- Library of Congress, resources on the July 5, 1852 Fourth of July address: https://www.loc.gov/collections/frederick-douglass-newspapers/articles-and-essays/
- U.S. National Park Service, Frederick Douglass biography and abolitionist work: https://www.nps.gov/people/frederick-douglass.htm
- Library of Congress, African-American Mosaic, Douglass's press and abolitionist influence: https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/african/afam006.html

### Secondary checks

- David W. Blight, *Frederick Douglass: Prophet of Freedom* (Simon & Schuster, 2018).
- John Stauffer, *Giants: The Parallel Lives of Frederick Douglass and Abraham Lincoln* (Twelve, 2008).
- Manisha Sinha, *The Slave's Cause: A History of Abolition* (Yale University Press, 2016), to keep the movement larger than one speaker.

### Strongest counterargument

Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin* reached an extraordinary mass audience; Stephen A. Douglas's Kansas-Nebraska Act helped destroy the existing party system and catalyze the Republican Party; the Mexican Cession and Fugitive Slave Act structurally intensified the conflict regardless of Frederick Douglass. His causal claim is therefore about authority, political language, and movement leadership, not sole responsibility for antislavery growth.

### Falsification note

Change the selection if Stowe's mass opinion effect, Stephen Douglas's legislative leverage, or another finalist proves substantially more decisive to the political trajectory of the decade than Douglass's cumulative role in turning Black testimony into independent national political authorship.
