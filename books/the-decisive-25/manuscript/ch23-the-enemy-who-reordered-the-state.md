# 1996–2005: Osama bin Laden — The Enemy Who Reordered the State

The most consequential person in an American decade does not have to be American.

They do not have to want the United States to succeed.

They do not have to survive the decade.

They can matter because the country reorganizes itself against them.

That is the case for Osama bin Laden.

He did not create Islamist militancy.

He did not create American involvement in the Middle East.

He did not create al Qaeda alone, and he did not personally execute the September 11 attacks. The plot depended on organizers, pilots, facilitators, financiers, trainers, safe havens, intelligence failures, aviation-security weaknesses, and nineteen hijackers who carried out the operation. Khalid Sheikh Mohammed was central to the conception and planning of the 9/11 plot. The Taliban provided sanctuary in Afghanistan. Earlier wars, authoritarian regimes, religious movements, regional grievances, U.S. policy, the Soviet-Afghan war, and transnational networks all shaped the environment in which al Qaeda emerged.

The 9/11 Commission nevertheless described the attack plainly: it was driven by bin Laden.

His historical leverage lies in what the attack caused the United States to build around itself afterward.

Before September 11, 2001, terrorism was a serious national-security problem.

After September 11, counterterrorism became an organizing principle of the state.

That distinction is enormous.

The decade from 1996 to 2005 begins with the internet commercializing, globalization accelerating, the post-Cold War United States enjoying extraordinary military superiority, and policymakers debating what a world without a peer competitor meant.

It ends with American troops in Afghanistan and Iraq, a Department of Homeland Security, a reorganized intelligence system, a new legal architecture for surveillance and detention, airport security transformed, executive war powers expanded, and a generation of citizens accustomed to the idea that an attack by a small transnational network could redirect the machinery of a superpower.

Bin Laden did not control that machinery.

He triggered it.

That is a dangerous form of power.

Al Qaeda developed from networks shaped by the anti-Soviet war in Afghanistan and later conflicts. Bin Laden, a wealthy Saudi who had participated in the Afghan jihad milieu, helped form the organization and used money, contacts, ideology, propaganda, training infrastructure, and sanctuary to build a group capable of increasingly ambitious attacks.

His rhetoric drew on a selective and extremist interpretation of Islam, grievances about U.S. troops in Saudi Arabia, American support for regional governments and Israel, and anger at broader Western influence. None of those grievances made al Qaeda representative of Muslims generally. The organization was a violent ideological movement, not a civilization speaking with one voice.

That distinction became politically important after 9/11 because fear has a tendency to enlarge the enemy until identity itself becomes suspicion.

Bin Laden wanted enlargement.

Terrorism relies on asymmetry.

A small organization cannot defeat a state like the United States conventionally. It can attempt to provoke the state into responses that are expensive, polarizing, strategically distracting, or useful to the terrorist's recruiting story.

The attack is therefore only half the strategy.

The reaction is the other half.

This does not mean every response helps the attacker or that inaction is wise. A state that refuses to defend itself can invite further attack. The strategic problem is harder: punish and disrupt the organization while controlling the scale, duration, and political meaning of the reaction.

A weak adversary often benefits when the stronger one confuses emotional proportionality with strategic proportionality.

The stronger state can always spend more.

That does not mean it should spend everything the weaker enemy has induced it to spend.

Al Qaeda attacked U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania in 1998, killing hundreds of people, most of them African civilians. It attacked the USS *Cole* in Yemen in 2000, killing seventeen American sailors. The pattern demonstrated increasing operational confidence and a willingness to strike American targets across borders.

The United States responded, but terrorism remained one threat among many.

Then came September 11.

Nineteen hijackers took control of four commercial aircraft. Two were flown into the World Trade Center in New York. One struck the Pentagon. Passengers and crew on United Flight 93 resisted the hijackers; the aircraft crashed in Pennsylvania before reaching the attackers' intended target.

Nearly three thousand people were killed.

The physical destruction was concentrated in a few places.

The institutional aftershock spread everywhere.

The attacks succeeded tactically because ordinary systems had been designed around different assumptions.

Commercial aviation security was built primarily against hijackings in which passengers might be held hostage, not against hijackers using fully fueled aircraft as suicide weapons. Intelligence agencies possessed pieces of information that were not integrated into a complete warning. Domestic and foreign intelligence authorities operated within structures shaped by law, culture, jurisdiction, and Cold War history. Airport screening, cockpit security, watch lists, immigration processes, and interagency information sharing all contained vulnerabilities that looked different after the attacks than before them.

This is the retrospective problem in warning.

Once the catastrophe occurs, every missed clue acquires a bright outline.

Before it occurs, the same clue competes with thousands of possibilities.

The distinction matters because “the government should have connected the dots” is easier to say after the picture is visible. Institutions receive warnings constantly. Analysts have to distinguish signals from noise without knowing which piece of noise will later be circled in red. A system optimized to chase every imaginable catastrophe would drown in false alarms and consume enormous resources.

The failure before September 11 was not that uncertainty existed.

It was that an enemy exploited seams between systems while the institutions responsible for seeing across those seams had not adapted enough to the threat.

The 9/11 Commission's great contribution was resisting the comfort of a single failure. It described institutional weaknesses across government and emphasized that the country had not fully adapted to a threat that crossed old bureaucratic boundaries.

The American response adapted rapidly.

Congress authorized the use of military force against those responsible for the attacks and those who harbored them. The United States invaded Afghanistan in late 2001 with allies and Afghan partners, helping overthrow the Taliban government that had sheltered al Qaeda.

The immediate military objective had a clear relationship to 9/11.

The long war that followed became far less clear.

Bin Laden escaped the early campaign and remained at large for years. The Taliban regrouped. Nation-building goals expanded. American forces and civilians became embedded in a conflict whose political end state was never as easy to define as the initial objective of disrupting al Qaeda.

That is one measure of the attacker's leverage.

A one-day operation helped initiate a war lasting two decades.

The phrase “helped initiate” matters.

A terrorist attack does not choose the duration of the victim state's war. Governments choose strategy. Military commanders choose campaigns within that strategy. Legislatures authorize and fund. Allies participate or refuse. Domestic politics changes the tolerance for casualties and cost. Local partners have their own interests. Enemies adapt.

If the war lasts longer than the attacker lives, causal responsibility becomes distributed across decisions made after the original provocation.

That is precisely why bin Laden's influence is so unsettling. His leverage does not require us to grant him authorship of the American response. It requires us to notice how much American agency became organized around a problem he successfully placed at the center of national attention.

The domestic state changed at similar scale.

Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act, expanding and modifying surveillance, information-sharing, financial, investigative, and law-enforcement authorities. Supporters argued that agencies needed tools appropriate to a networked enemy operating across jurisdictions. Civil-liberties advocates warned that emergency powers could become normal, oversight could lag, and broad authorities could affect people far beyond actual terrorist networks.

Both predictions contained truth.

Some reforms improved coordination against genuine threats.

Some surveillance programs later generated serious legal and constitutional controversy.

The deeper institutional question was how a democracy prices a low-probability catastrophic risk.

Before 9/11, many security measures looked too expensive, intrusive, or administratively burdensome relative to the perceived probability of attack. After 9/11, the perceived cost of a miss exploded. Measures that had seemed excessive could suddenly look obviously prudent. The threshold for intervention moved.

That movement rarely affects only terrorists.

Security systems operate on populations. Watch lists produce matches and false matches. Screening delays millions of innocent travelers to find a tiny number of dangerous people. Surveillance rules collect information in environments where suspicion is incomplete. Border policies distinguish categories that do not perfectly map onto risk.

Once a society decides that one successful miss is intolerable, it begins paying for risk reduction in other people's time, privacy, mobility, and bureaucratic exposure.

Sometimes the price is justified.

The important thing is that the price exists.

The attacks also accelerated creation of the Department of Homeland Security, bringing together agencies responsible for borders, immigration functions, emergency management, transportation security, infrastructure protection, and other missions under a new cabinet department.

The federal bureaucracy literally changed shape.

A reorganization chart can look administrative compared with a burning building.

It is one of the more durable forms of historical consequence.

Departments create careers, budgets, reporting chains, procurement systems, legal offices, congressional committees, professional vocabularies, databases, and constituencies for the mission. Once the state creates an organization around a problem, the problem gains institutional continuity even when public attention moves elsewhere.

This is how an emergency becomes a sector of government.

Airport security became one of the most visible changes in daily life.

The Transportation Security Administration federalized passenger screening. Cockpit doors were hardened. Watch-list practices expanded. Rules about carry-on items changed repeatedly. Air travel acquired rituals that children born after 2001 would consider normal and travelers before 2001 would have found extraordinary.

This is the chapter's title at household scale.

A terrorist leader hiding thousands of miles away changed how an American family packed toothpaste.

Power can travel through institutions in strange ways.

The mundane consequence matters because normality is one of the ways emergency power becomes durable. A new security practice is politically dramatic when introduced. Years later it becomes the environment. People budget the time. Airports are redesigned around it. Companies change procedures. Children grow up never knowing the earlier baseline.

The absence of memory makes reversal harder.

The intelligence community changed too.

The 9/11 Commission recommended major organizational reforms. Congress created the position of Director of National Intelligence and the National Counterterrorism Center. The stated goal was to improve integration across agencies whose separate authorities and cultures had contributed to fragmented understanding before the attacks.

Reorganization cannot abolish uncertainty.

It can change who is responsible for assembling it.

That distinction sounds bureaucratic until the next warning arrives.

Centralization can reduce seams by forcing information into common channels. It can also create new bottlenecks, produce consensus pressure, or give leaders the false confidence that because an office exists the underlying epistemic problem is solved. No organization chart can guarantee that ambiguous fragments become foresight.

The larger political effect was a security consensus powerful enough to reorder priorities across parties.

For years after 9/11, politicians faced strong incentives to avoid appearing insufficiently aggressive against terrorism. The cost of a false negative—a missed attack—seemed catastrophic and politically unforgivable. That pushed institutions toward accepting more false positives: more surveillance, more watch-list matches, more preventive action, more suspicion, more secrecy, and greater tolerance for measures justified by worst-case scenarios.

This is a general rule of frightened systems.

When the cost of one miss feels infinite, the system begins spending everyone else's inconvenience to reduce it.

Sometimes that trade is rational.

Sometimes it becomes self-perpetuating.

The asymmetry appears in budgeting too.

An attacker needs to find one vulnerability cheap enough to exploit. The defender is asked to secure airports, ports, borders, embassies, public events, financial networks, communications systems, energy infrastructure, military bases, and countless soft targets. Absolute prevention is impossible, so the defender is constantly deciding which residual risks to tolerate.

The attacker can impose costs simply by forcing the defender to consider the possibility.

A suspicious package closes a street. A threat changes a flight. A rumor moves police resources. A warning changes diplomatic security. No attack has to occur for the security system to spend.

That is why terrorism's economic and political leverage can exceed its physical capability.

The war in Iraq shows how far the security frame traveled beyond bin Laden's direct control.

The United States invaded Iraq in 2003 under President George W. Bush. The administration argued that Saddam Hussein's regime possessed weapons of mass destruction programs, violated international obligations, and posed an unacceptable post-9/11 risk. Senior officials also discussed terrorism, though Iraq was not responsible for the September 11 attacks and no operational partnership with al Qaeda justified treating Saddam as a planner of 9/11.

The central WMD intelligence claims did not hold as presented after the invasion.

The war removed a brutal dictator.

It also produced occupation, insurgency, sectarian violence, enormous Iraqi suffering, American casualties, fiscal cost, regional instability, and strategic consequences that would extend for decades.

Bin Laden did not order the United States to invade Iraq.

The Bush administration chose to.

That distinction is morally and analytically essential.

But the decision is difficult to imagine in the same political form without 9/11.

The attacks changed the country's tolerance for preventive war.

An adversary no longer had to be shown to be on the verge of attacking if leaders could argue that waiting for certainty was itself intolerably dangerous.

That doctrine enlarged the strategic consequences of terrorism far beyond al Qaeda's actual material power.

A decentralized extremist network had helped change how a superpower reasoned about uncertainty.

This is where the rival case for George W. Bush becomes strongest.

If a president receives a catastrophic attack and then chooses among several possible strategies, perhaps the chooser matters more than the provocateur.

Another president almost certainly responds militarily in Afghanistan after September 11. Another president might still expand surveillance and homeland security. But the Iraq invasion is not an automatic mechanical consequence of the attacks. It was a policy decision made by a specific administration through specific arguments about risk.

Remove Bush and bin Laden's leverage could be smaller because the response might remain more tightly focused on al Qaeda and Afghanistan.

Remove bin Laden, however, and Bush faces a different presidency entirely.

That is the counterfactual hinge.

Without a 9/11-scale attack, the administration has far less political basis for making catastrophic uncertainty the organizing principle of national security. The same officials may hold hawkish views. Saddam Hussein remains a problem in American policy. But the domestic coalition, urgency, legal authorities, public fear, and international environment surrounding preventive war change substantially.

This is why bin Laden narrowly wins the decade even though Bush exercised more formal power.

The attacker changed the choice set inside which the formal power was used.

The consequences reached American Muslim, Arab, Sikh, and South Asian communities directly.

Hate crimes and harassment increased after 9/11. Mosques and communities faced suspicion. Sikh Americans were sometimes attacked by people who ignorantly associated turbans with the attackers. Immigration and detention policies affected thousands of people. Public debate repeatedly blurred legitimate counterterrorism with religious and ethnic generalization.

This was a strategic gift to extremists who framed the conflict as a civilizational war.

Every act that treated ordinary Muslims as enemies made the propaganda easier.

American leaders, including President Bush, publicly insisted that the country was not at war with Islam.

The practice of a frightened society did not always honor that boundary.

Bin Laden's personal importance therefore has an ugly circular quality.

He built an organization that wanted to prove coexistence impossible.

The American challenge was to destroy the organization without helping prove its story.

That is much harder than destroying camps.

It is also why measuring counterterrorism only by attacks prevented can be incomplete. A strategy can suppress plots while simultaneously creating political damage, legal precedents, fiscal burdens, or recruiting narratives that matter on a longer horizon. The benefits of security are often invisible because success is an event that does not happen. The costs are distributed across institutions and time.

That asymmetry makes democratic oversight difficult.

Officials can point to absence of attack as evidence that a measure worked. Critics can point to absence of proof that the measure was necessary. Both face a counterfactual they cannot observe directly.

Emergency institutions thrive in that uncertainty.

By 2005, al Qaeda's original core had been damaged but the ideology and organizational model had dispersed. Affiliates, imitators, insurgent groups, and later movements would adapt to the pressure. The internet lowered the cost of propaganda and recruitment. The Iraq War created new militant networks and grievances. Counterterrorism became a persistent field rather than a campaign with a clear endpoint.

The state had been reordered around a threat that itself was changing form.

Was bin Laden really the most important person of 1996–2005?

George W. Bush remains the strongest rival.

The United States did not respond automatically to 9/11. Bush and his administration chose the shape of the response. He launched the Afghanistan war, created or supported the domestic security architecture, articulated a doctrine of preventive war, and ordered the invasion of Iraq. Remove Bush and the attacks still occur but the American reaction could differ enormously, especially over Iraq.

Khalid Sheikh Mohammed is a different rival because he attacks bin Laden's personal leverage inside the plot. If KSM supplied central operational conception and planning, perhaps an al Qaeda without bin Laden still finds a way to attempt catastrophic aviation terrorism. The chapter fails if “driven by bin Laden” is allowed to erase the distributed operational organization required to turn intent into an attack.

Alan Greenspan is another rival because Federal Reserve policy, financial globalization, and the long credit expansion helped shape the economy that would later crash. Larry Page and Sergey Brin founded Google and changed how knowledge on the web would be found. Jeff Bezos transformed e-commerce and logistics. Steve Jobs returned to Apple and began the product strategy that would culminate in the smartphone era. Rupert Murdoch and cable news altered political media. Newt Gingrich helped reshape congressional partisanship in the 1990s.

Bin Laden wins because his successful attack forced decisions across domains that would otherwise have developed on different timetables.

Remove Bush and another president still faces 9/11.

Remove bin Laden and the exact al Qaeda network may be less capable of organizing the attacks, though Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and others could still pursue catastrophic terrorism. If no 9/11-scale attack occurs in 2001, the United States probably does not create the same Homeland Security bureaucracy on the same schedule. The PATRIOT Act does not pass in the same form or urgency. Afghanistan may remain a distant conflict. Iraq is much harder to sell as an immediate American security necessity. Airport security evolves gradually rather than overnight. Intelligence reform follows another path.

That is enormous counterfactual distance.

The conclusion has to be phrased carefully because terrorists seek historical significance.

Calling bin Laden consequential is not granting him strategic genius, moral stature, or control over everything that followed. It is not saying the American state was a puppet responding exactly as he intended. It is saying that a comparatively small organization found a vulnerability whose exploitation changed the priorities of a vastly more powerful country.

That is the disturbing asymmetry of modern power.

The United States spent vastly more responding to al Qaeda than al Qaeda spent attacking it.

This does not mean the response should have been cheap or passive.

A state has a duty to protect citizens and dismantle organizations planning mass murder.

The lesson is different.

A powerful country can still allow a weaker enemy to choose part of its agenda.

That is what terrorism tries to achieve.

The attacker's greatest victory is not automatically the explosion.

It is the durable institution, war, fear, or political identity the explosion persuades the stronger society to build.

Some American responses were necessary.

Some were effective.

Some were disastrous.

All became part of the history bin Laden helped set in motion.

He never possessed anything close to American military or economic power.

For a decade, he nevertheless changed what that power was for.
