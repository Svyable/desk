# 1976–1985: Ronald Reagan — The Government Becomes the Problem

Ronald Reagan did not shrink the federal government into insignificance.

He did something more durable.

He changed the presumption surrounding it.

For much of the political era created by Franklin Roosevelt, national campaigns argued over how the federal government should manage prosperity, regulate markets, support households, protect rights, build infrastructure, and respond to crisis. Conservatives opposed many programs, but the New Deal settlement proved remarkably difficult to dislodge. Social Security endured. Federal regulation expanded under presidents of both parties. Richard Nixon created the Environmental Protection Agency. Even Republicans often governed inside institutions they had once criticized.

Reagan changed the grammar.

In his 1981 inaugural address, he gave the new era its cleanest sentence: government was not the solution to the problem; government was the problem.

The line was carefully bounded to the crisis he was describing, but it escaped its paragraph and became a political philosophy.

That is the leverage.

The decade from 1976 to 1985 opened with the United States exhausted by Vietnam, Watergate, inflation, oil shocks, urban fiscal stress, declining confidence in institutions, and a sense that the postwar economic formula no longer produced its old results.

Jimmy Carter entered the presidency promising honesty and competence after Watergate.

He confronted a harder environment than those virtues could solve.

Inflation remained severe. Energy prices rose. Industrial competition intensified. The Iranian Revolution and hostage crisis damaged the administration. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan ended hopes for a simpler détente. Americans heard repeated arguments about limits while their expectations had been built in an era of expansion.

Reagan offered the opposite emotional proposition.

The country did not need to become accustomed to decline.

It needed to recover confidence by releasing private energy from an overgrown state.

That argument had been developing for decades.

Barry Goldwater's 1964 campaign had given movement conservatism a national political language. William F. Buckley Jr. and conservative magazines built intellectual infrastructure. Business groups organized against regulation and taxation. Evangelical political activism grew. Anti-communism remained powerful. White backlash against civil-rights enforcement, school integration, crime, busing, and cultural change reshaped party coalitions. Tax revolts, most famously California's Proposition 13 in 1978, demonstrated anger at government cost.

Reagan did not invent the conservative movement.

He made it presidential.

That distinction mirrors several earlier chapters.

A movement can spend years accumulating arguments before one person discovers how to make those arguments sound like national common sense.

Reagan had practiced the language as a broadcaster, corporate spokesman, Goldwater supporter, governor of California, presidential challenger, and candidate.

His unusual talent was tonal.

Conservatism could be angry.

Reagan made it optimistic.

He argued that government had become too large while speaking as though Americans themselves were larger than the problem.

That optimism mattered because retrenchment is difficult to sell if it sounds only like subtraction.

Reagan described tax cuts, deregulation, military strength, and smaller domestic government as restoration.

The country would not receive less.

It would become itself again.

The economic crisis gave the argument force.

By the late 1970s, the United States faced a combination of high inflation and weak growth that challenged familiar policy assumptions. The Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker, appointed by Carter in 1979, adopted extremely tight monetary policy to break inflation.

This is essential to the chapter because later political memory often assigns the entire recovery to Reagan.

The most painful anti-inflation action came from the central bank.

High interest rates contributed to a severe recession in the early 1980s. Unemployment rose sharply. Manufacturing regions suffered. Farmers faced debt pressure. Housing and other interest-sensitive sectors contracted.

Reagan supported Volcker's anti-inflation stance politically even as the recession threatened him.

At the same time, his administration pursued a different economic revolution through fiscal and regulatory policy.

The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 reduced individual tax rates over several years, lowered the top marginal rate, accelerated depreciation for business investment, and expanded incentives for saving and investment.

The legislation did not descend from Reagan's speeches fully formed. Congress wrote, amended, bargained, and passed it. Democrats participated. Later tax increases and revisions would modify parts of the program.

But the political direction was unmistakable.

For decades, progressive politics had often treated steeply graduated tax rates as a legitimate tool for financing government and limiting concentration.

Reagan argued that high marginal rates themselves suppressed growth and freedom.

Tax policy became not only a way to pay for the state but a theory of what the state was preventing.

That theory survived him.

Later Republican candidates would disagree over spending, trade, foreign policy, and social issues while treating lower taxes as near-axiomatic. Even Democratic administrations operated in a political environment where restoring pre-Reagan top rates became difficult.

This is one measure of ideological victory.

Your opponents start negotiating from terrain you chose.

Reagan's budgets cut or constrained many domestic programs, but the federal government did not become small in a literal sense. Defense spending rose. Social Security remained. Medicare remained. Interest costs mattered. Federal deficits expanded substantially.

The contradiction is important.

“Smaller government” often meant smaller in selected domains.

Reagan wanted less federal direction of markets and domestic social provision while wanting more military capacity, stronger executive leadership, aggressive law enforcement in certain areas, and a national state powerful enough to confront the Soviet Union.

Government was the problem until government was the instrument.

This tension is not unique to Reagan or conservatism.

Every political philosophy tends to discover exceptions for the powers it values.

The more revealing question is which powers become presumptively legitimate.

Labor learned that lesson in 1981.

Members of the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization went on strike against the federal government. Federal employees were legally prohibited from striking. Reagan gave the controllers an ultimatum to return to work and then fired thousands who refused.

The PATCO confrontation had immediate operational and legal dimensions.

Its larger symbolic effect extended beyond federal labor relations.

A president had demonstrated that organized labor could be confronted rather than accommodated, even when the union had endorsed him in the prior election.

Employers across the private sector were not legally situated the same way as the federal government in PATCO, so it would be simplistic to say Reagan's action “caused” union decline.

Union density had been pressured by deindustrialization, globalization, regional shifts, employer resistance, labor-law structure, automation, and changing employment patterns for years.

But signals matter.

PATCO became a signal that the bargaining climate had changed.

Management could interpret the federal government's posture differently.

Organized labor could no longer assume the political environment created in the New Deal era would protect its leverage indefinitely.

Reagan's regulatory program sent a similar signal to business.

Some deregulation had begun under Carter, particularly in airlines, trucking, and other sectors. Reagan accelerated the broader ideological shift toward skepticism of federal rules, cost-benefit review, and market mechanisms.

The key transformation was not the repeal of every regulation.

It was the burden of proof.

Regulators increasingly had to explain not only why a problem existed but why federal intervention would improve it without imposing larger costs.

Again the grammar changed.

Roosevelt's era had taught Americans that unmanaged markets could produce catastrophic public risk.

Reagan's era taught them to ask whether unmanaged government could do the same.

Both questions are legitimate.

The political struggle concerns which one is asked first.

Reagan's foreign policy reinforced the restoration story.

He entered office sharply critical of the Soviet Union and what he saw as American weakness after Vietnam and the Iranian hostage crisis. His administration increased defense spending, supported anti-Communist forces abroad, pursued strategic modernization, and used moral language that rejected the idea that the Cold War was merely a permanent balance to be managed.

In 1983, he called the Soviet Union an “evil empire.”

The phrase offended diplomats and delighted supporters because it turned geopolitics into moral clarity.

Yet Reagan's later relationship with Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev would become more flexible and consequential than the early rhetoric suggests.

That belongs partly outside this decade, but the seeds were already present in Reagan's belief that strength and negotiation were compatible.

He was not simply trying to maintain détente.

He wanted to pressure the Soviet system while leaving space for a different relationship if Soviet leadership changed.

The Strategic Defense Initiative, announced in 1983, embodied both technological ambition and strategic controversy. Reagan imagined defenses that could reduce dependence on mutual vulnerability. Critics doubted technical feasibility and feared destabilizing arms-race effects.

The proposal mattered even without achieving its grandest promises because it communicated that the United States was willing to compete technologically at enormous scale.

How much Reagan personally caused the Soviet Union's later collapse remains deeply contested.

The Soviet economy had structural weaknesses. The war in Afghanistan imposed costs. Oil prices mattered. Nationalist movements mattered. Gorbachev's reforms mattered. Eastern European dissidents mattered. Long-term containment mattered. Nuclear competition predated Reagan.

A serious chapter does not award one American president victory over a complex multinational system.

Reagan's leverage lies elsewhere: he changed American willingness to believe the competition could end on terms better than managed stalemate.

That belief changed policy.

Domestic politics changed with it.

Reagan assembled a coalition that included economic conservatives, anti-communists, evangelical Christians, suburban voters, business interests, and many white working-class voters who had once supported Democrats. The phrase “Reagan Democrats” captured part of the migration.

Race remained embedded in the realignment.

Republican gains among white southern and working-class voters cannot be explained only by taxes, patriotism, or religion. Federal civil-rights enforcement, affirmative action, urban policy, welfare, crime, school integration, and racially coded political appeals all shaped the changing coalition. Reagan's 1980 campaign launch near Philadelphia, Mississippi, and his invocation of states' rights became controversial precisely because that phrase had a history in resistance to federal civil-rights action.

At the same time, voters are never reducible to one motive.

The coalition was economically, culturally, regionally, and religiously heterogeneous.

The point is not to find one hidden cause.

It is to see that Reagan gave many different dissatisfactions a common story.

The story was freedom from government.

That story had limits when applied to social policy.

The administration supported a conservative agenda on abortion and family issues, though the federal policy results were less sweeping than movement activists wanted. The War on Drugs and criminal-justice policies expanded state coercion rather than shrinking it. Reagan's response to the AIDS crisis was widely criticized as slow and inadequate during years when the epidemic devastated gay communities and others.

Again, government did not simply retreat.

It retreated unevenly.

Power moved.

That is the more accurate description of ideological eras.

They do not erase the state.

They decide which risks the state treats as public, which markets it trusts, which behaviors it polices, which institutions it funds, and which groups it asks to absorb more uncertainty privately.

Reagan's America placed greater faith in markets and private initiative while expanding military and certain coercive capacities.

The result transformed the policy baseline for the next generation.

Bill Clinton would later declare that the era of big government was over while preserving major federal programs. Democrats embraced market-friendly policies in areas that earlier liberal coalitions might have approached differently. Deregulation, free trade, financial innovation, welfare reform, and deficit reduction all developed inside a post-Reagan environment even when Democrats held power.

This is what it means to change an era.

The ideas travel farther than the party.

Was Reagan the most important person of 1976–1985?

Paul Volcker is the strongest institutional rival.

If the decade is judged by economic consequence, Volcker's decision to maintain tight monetary policy through deep recession in order to break inflation may have mattered more than any tax cut. The decline of inflation changed interest rates, investment, politics, and household expectations for decades. Volcker also demonstrated the power of an independent central bank to impose short-term pain for long-term credibility.

Jimmy Carter is underrated. He appointed Volcker, pursued significant deregulation, elevated human rights in foreign policy, normalized relations with China, negotiated the Camp David Accords, and began the military buildup and strategic response to the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan that Reagan inherited.

Ayatollah Khomeini, an external candidate, reshaped American politics through the Iranian Revolution, hostage crisis, energy shocks, and the long U.S.-Iran antagonism that followed. Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak launched Apple and helped push personal computing toward a mass market. Bill Gates's software strategy was already becoming consequential. Jerry Falwell and other religious-right organizers changed party politics.

Reagan wins because he converted dispersed conservative energy into a national governing presumption.

Remove Volcker and inflation could last longer, perhaps destroying Reagan's presidency.

Remove Reagan and a Republican or conservative shift still likely occurs. The old coalition was already weakening. Tax revolt was real. Business mobilization was real. Carter himself deregulated parts of the economy. The Soviet confrontation would continue.

But without Reagan, the shift may feel defensive rather than triumphant.

No other Republican figure of the moment combined ideological clarity, television skill, humor, optimism, and electoral strength at the same scale. George H. W. Bush represented a more traditional Republican establishment. Howard Baker and Bob Dole were formidable legislators but not movement symbols. Goldwater had supplied the insurgent language without winning the presidency.

Reagan made the insurgency feel like the center.

That is counterfactual distance.

After him, politicians could still defend federal action.

They had to answer a question Reagan had made permanent:

Why should government do this at all?

The question is useful.

It can discipline bad programs, expose captured regulation, limit bureaucracy, and force public institutions to justify coercion and cost.

It can also become an alibi for neglect when problems require collective action.

Every governing presumption reveals some failures and hides others.

Reagan's importance is that his presumption endured.

Franklin Roosevelt had taught Americans to see government as protection against market catastrophe.

Ronald Reagan taught them to see government itself as a possible catastrophe.

American politics since has lived between those two fears.
