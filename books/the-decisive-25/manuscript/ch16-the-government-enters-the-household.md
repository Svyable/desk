# 1926–1935: Franklin D. Roosevelt — The Government Enters the Household

The Great Depression did not begin with Franklin Roosevelt.

It did not end with him either.

That is why his importance has to be found somewhere more precise than the claim that he “saved the economy.”

The decade from 1926 to 1935 contains the prosperity of the late twenties, the crash of 1929, bank failures, mass unemployment, Hoover's increasingly aggressive but inadequate emergency responses, Roosevelt's election, the banking holiday, the Hundred Days, the New Deal, and the creation of programs that would outlive the emergency by generations.

Roosevelt's leverage was institutional intimacy.

Before the Depression, the federal government already touched everyday life through tariffs, land policy, pensions, postal service, regulation, courts, immigration law, public works, war mobilization, and monetary institutions. Washington was never absent from American society.

But after Roosevelt, ordinary households increasingly understood economic security itself as a legitimate federal concern.

A bank account could be federally insured.

An old-age pension could be organized through national law.

A mortgage market could be supported by federal institutions.

A farmer's production decisions could be shaped by Washington.

An unemployed worker could enter a federally funded relief or public-works program.

The securities market could be federally regulated.

A president could speak by radio directly into millions of homes and explain why the banks were closed.

Government entered the household not because agents physically appeared at every door, but because risk that had once been treated as private misfortune became part of public administration.

That was a revolution in expectations.

The scale of the Depression made older expectations difficult to sustain.

During the 1920s, productivity, consumer credit, automobiles, radios, home appliances, construction, securities markets, and corporate profits helped create a powerful image of modern prosperity. The gains were real, though uneven. Agriculture had struggled through much of the decade. Wealth and income were highly concentrated. Installment credit expanded household purchasing power. The financial system contained fragile institutions and speculative excess.

Then the economy collapsed.

The stock market crash became the most famous symbol, but the Depression was not simply a bad week on Wall Street. Investment fell. Demand contracted. International trade shrank. Deflation increased the real burden of debt. Banks failed. Businesses closed. Unemployment reached catastrophic levels. Farm prices collapsed. Families lost savings, homes, work, and confidence.

The pain traveled through institutions faster than most institutions could respond.

Herbert Hoover is often flattened into a caricature of a president who did nothing.

He did act.

His administration supported public works, encouraged voluntary cooperation, backed the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, and accepted forms of federal intervention that would have seemed substantial earlier. The Federal Reserve also had tools it could have used differently. Congress acted. States and cities attempted relief. Private charities struggled to absorb demand.

The problem was not literal inactivity.

It was scale, timing, ideology, and institutional design.

The federal government remained hesitant to assume direct responsibility for mass economic suffering at the level Roosevelt would soon attempt.

By the 1932 election, the political question had become larger than whether a particular emergency measure worked.

Americans were asking what government was for when markets failed catastrophically.

Roosevelt's answer was experimental.

That word matters because later memory can make the New Deal look like a coherent machine designed in advance.

It was not.

Roosevelt's administration contained competing advisers, improvised programs, contradictory goals, legislation copied or adapted from state experiments, ideas with conservative roots, ideas promoted by progressives, measures demanded by Congress, and programs that failed or were struck down.

Roosevelt's distinctive capacity was not possessing one master plan.

It was converting uncertainty into permission to act.

When he took office in March 1933, the banking system was in crisis.

States had already declared banking holidays. Depositors were trying to protect cash. Confidence itself had become a destabilizing force: if enough people believed banks might fail and withdrew funds, even institutions with viable assets could be overwhelmed.

Roosevelt declared a national bank holiday.

Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act with extraordinary speed. Banks reopened only after authorities determined that they could resume operations. The immediate crisis eased.

Then Roosevelt went on the radio.

His first fireside chat explained the banking system and the government's actions in conversational language.

The technical content was important.

The emotional architecture was more important.

A president was telling households that their fear was part of the policy problem and that the government had a process for restoring trust.

Millions of Americans did not need to understand bank balance sheets in detail.

They needed to believe that reopening a bank was not a signal to run from it.

Roosevelt made confidence itself a public object.

That changed presidential communication.

The radio had already transformed politics and culture. Roosevelt did not invent broadcasting. But he understood that mass media could bypass intermediaries and make executive power feel personal.

A national government could now speak at household scale.

That relationship would grow through the century—radio, television, cable, social media—until presidents became permanent presences in private life.

Roosevelt's fireside chats are an early model of that intimacy.

The Hundred Days followed at legislative speed.

The administration and Congress created or expanded programs dealing with banks, agriculture, industrial recovery, public works, unemployment, housing, securities, conservation, and regional development.

Some programs are remembered by their initials because the federal government itself became a landscape of initials: CCC, AAA, TVA, NRA, PWA, later WPA and others.

The alphabet can hide the human question.

What kinds of failure should be survivable?

That was the emerging argument.

If a bank failed because its managers made bad loans, should depositors lose everything?

If an elderly worker reached the end of employability with no family support or savings, was destitution simply the private consequence of insufficient thrift?

If mass unemployment resulted from a national collapse rather than individual refusal to work, did relief demean the citizen or protect the republic?

If farmers all increased production while prices fell, could individual effort solve a collective glut?

If a utility monopoly controlled access to electricity, could public power be a legitimate competitor or benchmark?

These questions had existed before Roosevelt.

The New Deal turned them into federal institutions.

Deposit insurance is a clean example.

The Banking Act of 1933 separated key banking activities and created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. Roosevelt himself had been wary of deposit insurance, concerned that stronger banks might be forced to subsidize weaker ones and that insurance could encourage bad behavior. Congressional advocates, including Representative Henry Steagall, pushed the measure.

That history complicates the habit of calling every New Deal institution Roosevelt's personal invention.

He signed and administered a coalition's legislation.

The result changed household behavior.

A depositor no longer had to evaluate the solvency of a bank with the same existential stakes. Federal insurance made small deposits safer and reduced the incentive for destabilizing runs.

The program altered the relationship between private banking and public trust.

Social Security went further.

In 1935, Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act, creating a federal system that included old-age benefits and unemployment-related provisions alongside other forms of support.

The law was limited and exclusionary.

Its original coverage left out many agricultural and domestic workers, exclusions that disproportionately affected Black workers and women. The structure reflected political compromises, administrative choices, constitutional concerns, regional power, and the racism of the congressional order that helped determine what could pass.

A program can be transformative and unjustly incomplete at the same time.

That is often how American institutions are born.

The lasting principle was enormous: aging and unemployment were not only family problems.

They were risks a national society could pool.

This is the moment government truly enters the household in the chapter's sense.

The federal state is no longer merely the entity that taxes, prosecutes, regulates trade, owns land, fights wars, and delivers mail.

It is becoming a guarantor against specific forms of economic ruin.

The political consequences lasted far beyond Roosevelt.

Programs create constituencies.

A person who receives a benefit experiences government differently from a person who encounters government only as command. An institution that works becomes difficult to abolish. Political argument shifts from whether government should exist in the field at all to how generous, efficient, universal, or conditional the program should be.

Roosevelt's opponents understood this.

They accused the New Deal of centralizing power, undermining federalism, discouraging private initiative, threatening property rights, and creating dangerous precedents for executive government. Some criticism came from business conservatives. Some came from constitutional lawyers. Some came from politicians worried about patronage or federal intrusion.

Other critics attacked Roosevelt from the left.

Huey Long argued that wealth concentration remained intolerable and built a national following around his Share Our Wealth program. Dr. Francis Townsend mobilized older Americans around pensions. Father Charles Coughlin used radio to build a huge political audience that mixed economic populism with increasingly poisonous rhetoric, including antisemitism.

The New Deal was pressured from both directions.

That helps explain its expansion.

Roosevelt was not simply imposing a leftward program on a passive country.

He was governing inside a crisis that made more radical alternatives plausible.

Reform can stabilize a system partly by proving that the system can change.

This is why the phrase “saved capitalism” remains tempting even though it is too neat.

The New Deal regulated capitalism, subsidized parts of it, constrained parts of it, supported labor in important ways, protected banks while disciplining them, and created social insurance around market outcomes. It did not replace private enterprise. It made a political argument that private enterprise required public legitimacy and public shock absorbers.

That bargain would define much of the mid-twentieth century.

Roosevelt's leadership also had darker boundaries.

New Deal programs could reproduce segregation. Local administration often empowered white officials in ways that discriminated against Black workers. Agricultural programs could benefit landowners while displacing tenants and sharecroppers. Mexican and Mexican American communities endured repatriation drives during the Depression, much of them conducted by local and state authorities but within a national climate of economic scapegoating. Native policy shifted in meaningful ways under the Indian Reorganization Act, but federal paternalism did not disappear.

The federal government became more present.

Presence did not guarantee equality.

Roosevelt's personal politics reflected the compromises he chose to make. He depended on southern Democratic committee chairs and segregationist legislators. He declined to spend his full political capital on federal anti-lynching legislation. His administration's civil-rights record cannot be separated from the coalition that sustained the New Deal.

Eleanor Roosevelt pushed the administration and the country further on race, women, labor, refugees, and human rights than Franklin often wished to go. Her influence is one reason the story should never be written as a solitary-president drama.

Nor can the economic results be assigned solely to Roosevelt.

The Depression's causes and recovery remain subjects of scholarly debate. Monetary policy mattered. Banking reforms mattered. Fiscal policy mattered. Gold-standard decisions mattered. International conditions mattered. New Deal spending provided relief and employment but did not restore full employment before World War II mobilization. Some New Deal policies may have impeded recovery in certain sectors even while stabilizing institutions and reducing suffering.

The chapter does not require Roosevelt to have solved the Depression completely.

It requires his personal absence to move American political development a long way.

That counterfactual is easier to defend.

Without Roosevelt, the banking crisis still demands action. Congress still contains reformers. State experiments still exist. Pressure for pensions, relief, jobs, farm support, financial regulation, and public works still rises from below. Another Democratic administration almost certainly expands federal intervention beyond Hoover's model.

But the form, speed, coalition, and communicative legitimacy could differ dramatically.

Roosevelt had an unusual combination of elite confidence, political flexibility, optimism, theatrical instinct, and willingness to absorb ideas from competing factions without insisting on ideological purity.

He could make experimentation sound like steadiness.

That is rare.

Huey Long is a serious rival for the decade because he demonstrated how much more radical the politics of economic redistribution might become. Herbert Hoover is a rival because the collapse unfolded under his presidency and his choices shaped the baseline Roosevelt inherited. John Maynard Keynes, though British, is an external candidate because his economic thought would transform how governments understood demand and depression, though his direct American policy leverage in this specific decade is less personal than Roosevelt's.

Frances Perkins deserves special attention.

As Roosevelt's labor secretary and the first woman to serve in a U.S. Cabinet, she was central to labor policy and Social Security. She brought years of reform experience and administrative seriousness into the government. A president-centered story that treats Perkins, Harry Hopkins, Harold Ickes, Robert Wagner, Congress, union organizers, Black activists, social workers, state reformers, and millions of relief recipients as assistants to Roosevelt would repeat the great-man error this book is trying to avoid.

Roosevelt wins the decade because he changed the political container within which all those actors operated.

He made federal experimentation electorally durable.

The New Deal coalition would dominate national politics for decades. Even later conservative administrations often preserved core institutions created or consolidated in the Roosevelt era. Social Security survived. Deposit insurance survived. Securities regulation survived. The expectation of federal responsibility during recession survived.

Opponents could shrink, redirect, privatize, deregulate, or attack parts of the system.

They could not return easily to 1928.

That is persistence.

Roosevelt also changed the presidency in a way that Theodore Roosevelt had anticipated.

The energetic executive now had an administrative state large enough to act through.

The president became organizer, broadcaster, coalition manager, legislative strategist, emergency narrator, and symbol of government itself.

The office grew because the public's questions grew.

A farmer's mortgage.

A depositor's savings.

An old person's income.

A worker's unemployment.

A river valley's electricity.

A city's public works.

These were no longer obviously local stories.

They became national political stories.

The transformation can be stated without romance.

Before Roosevelt, Americans already expected things from Washington.

After Roosevelt, a national economic catastrophe that destroyed household security could no longer be treated as something Washington had no responsibility to address.

The argument moved from whether the federal government belonged in the room to what it should do once it arrived.

Government had entered the household.

It has never really left.
