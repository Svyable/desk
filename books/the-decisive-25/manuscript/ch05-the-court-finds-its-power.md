# 1816–1825: John Marshall — The Court Finds Its Power

John Marshall did not have an army.

He did not control the Treasury. He did not command a party. He could write an opinion and then watch the executive branch decide whether the country would live by it.

That limitation is what makes his decade interesting.

Between 1816 and 1825, the Supreme Court became something the Constitution had not fully specified: a national institution capable of telling states that some of their laws would not stand, telling Congress that some implied powers did stand, and making constitutional interpretation part of the ordinary machinery of American government.

Marshall had been chief justice since 1801. His most famous early opinion, *Marbury v. Madison*, came in 1803, outside this chapter's decade. Judicial review did not spring fully formed from one case, and American courts had engaged in constitutional review before *Marbury*. But the Marshall Court's later work matters because a power becomes real through repeated use. Between the end of the War of 1812 and the middle of the 1820s, the Court applied national constitutional principles to fights over banks, contracts, interstate commerce, and state sovereignty in ways that made the federal system more than a treaty among jealous governments.

The country had just learned something expensive about institutional weakness.

The War of 1812 exposed deficiencies in finance, logistics, military administration, transportation, and national coordination. The British burned Washington. New England Federalists met at Hartford to protest the war and discuss constitutional remedies. The Treasury struggled to finance military operations after the charter of the First Bank of the United States had expired in 1811. Peace brought nationalism, but also a practical question: what could the federal government actually do when national problems did not fit neatly inside state lines?

Congress answered part of that question by chartering the Second Bank of the United States in 1816.

The bank's early management was poor enough to produce its own scandals, but its existence revived the constitutional fight Alexander Hamilton had helped start a generation earlier. If Congress could charter a national bank, could a state tax it? And if a state could tax a federal instrument, how far could that power go?

Maryland decided to find out.

The state imposed a tax on banks not chartered by Maryland. The Baltimore branch of the Second Bank refused to pay. James McCulloch, the branch cashier, became the named defendant in a dispute that reached the Supreme Court.

In 1819, Marshall wrote the opinion in *McCulloch v. Maryland*.

The case did two things that altered the architecture of the Union.

First, the Court upheld Congress's authority to create the bank even though the Constitution nowhere says, in so many words, “Congress may charter a bank.” Marshall treated the federal government as possessing not only powers explicitly named but reasonable means of carrying those powers into effect. Taxation, borrowing, spending, and other legitimate constitutional ends would be difficult to exercise if Congress were forbidden to choose institutions merely because the framers had not listed each tool in advance.

Second, the Court held that Maryland could not tax the federal bank.

The logic was larger than the tax bill. Federal law made within constitutional authority had to be supreme inside the states. A state could not use its sovereign power to burden or potentially destroy an instrument created by the national government to carry out national powers.

The decision did not make states subordinate administrative districts. It did something more durable: it announced that American federalism contained a hierarchy when valid federal and state authority collided.

That sounds obvious now because later Americans inherited a country in which federal statutes, federal courts, and federal agencies are permanent features. In 1819, the political identity of the Union was still young enough that the answer could not be assumed.

The opposition was immediate.

Critics accused Marshall of reading national power too broadly. Virginia's political establishment was particularly hostile to doctrines that seemed to let the federal government define its own reach. Spencer Roane, a judge on Virginia's highest court and a formidable constitutional thinker, attacked the Marshall Court in newspaper essays. The argument was not merely legal. It concerned who would police the boundary of a federal union: states themselves, the political branches, or a national judiciary whose members held life tenure.

Marshall's answer, across case after case, was the Court.

The same year as *McCulloch*, the Court decided *Dartmouth College v. Woodward*. The dispute arose after New Hampshire attempted to alter Dartmouth's colonial charter and turn the institution into a public university. The Court treated the charter as a contract protected by the Constitution's Contract Clause.

The decision became associated with greater protection for private corporations from some forms of state interference. Its later economic significance can be overstated if one treats every nineteenth-century corporation as an inevitable child of Dartmouth. States adapted by writing reservation clauses into later charters, preserving greater power to amend them. But the case reinforced a broader pattern: state legislatures were not always the final judges of the legal consequences of their own acts.

Then came *Cohens v. Virginia* in 1821.

The case sounds small. Two brothers had sold District of Columbia lottery tickets in Virginia contrary to state law. But the jurisdictional question was large: could the Supreme Court review a state criminal case when the defendant claimed federal law protected the conduct?

Marshall said yes.

If each state's highest court could finally decide federal questions arising within that state, national law could mean one thing in Virginia and another in Pennsylvania. A union with federal law but no federal judicial authority to maintain some degree of uniformity would remain partly confederal in operation.

The Court's reach expanded again in 1824 with *Gibbons v. Ogden*.

New York had granted an exclusive steamboat franchise in its waters. Thomas Gibbons operated vessels under a federal coasting license and challenged the monopoly. The dispute reached the Commerce Clause: what did it mean for Congress to regulate commerce “among the several States”?

Marshall read commerce broadly enough to include navigation and treated interstate commercial activity as falling within national authority. New York's monopoly could not stand against the federal license.

The case arrived at the right moment to expose the practical absurdity of purely local economic sovereignty. Steamboats did not stop being commerce when they crossed an invisible state line. Rivers moved through jurisdictions. Goods crossed borders. Insurance, credit, roads, canals, ports, and markets increasingly linked places whose legislatures might prefer different rules.

The young republic was becoming an economy larger than its states.

Marshall helped make constitutional doctrine catch up with that fact.

It would be easy to tell this as a triumphal story of national unity over parochial obstruction. That would miss the stakes on the other side.

States were not irrational to fear a national government that could discover new implied powers whenever an economic problem crossed a border. The federal government had already demonstrated the ability to tax, borrow, field armies, enforce laws, and charter corporations. The same supremacy doctrine that protected a useful national bank could later support national policies a state considered oppressive. Judicial review itself concentrated authority in unelected judges who could invalidate the work of elected legislatures.

Marshall's nationalism did not answer the democratic objection. It institutionalized the argument.

That distinction matters because the Supreme Court's long-term power cannot be evaluated only by outcomes modern readers like. A Court capable of protecting federal supremacy in *McCulloch* was also a Court capable of making catastrophic constitutional judgments in later eras. Judicial authority has no moral direction built into it. It is a mechanism for making some interpretations harder for majorities to reverse.

Marshall himself would participate in cases involving slavery and Native sovereignty that reveal the limits of any simple heroic portrait. His constitutional nationalism strengthened a federal system that would later be used both to destroy slavery and to protect it, both to enforce civil rights and to restrict them. Courts do not possess virtue by structure.

They possess leverage.

The question for this decade is how much of that leverage belongs specifically to Marshall.

The strongest rival is Henry Clay.

Clay was Speaker of the House, a leading War Hawk, an architect of the American System, a supporter of the Second Bank, a defender of protective tariffs and internal improvements, and a central broker in the Missouri Compromise of 1820. If the decade's decisive problem was how to bind a continental republic economically and politically, Clay can make the argument from the legislature that Marshall makes from the bench.

James Monroe is another contender. His presidency encompassed the so-called Era of Good Feelings, the acquisition of Florida through the Adams-Onís Treaty, the Missouri crisis, and the Monroe Doctrine of 1823. John Quincy Adams, as secretary of state, supplied much of the diplomatic intelligence and design behind that doctrine and the Florida settlement.

Why Marshall?

Because most political victories expire unless institutions carry them forward.

Clay could pass a tariff that a later Congress could repeal. Monroe could announce a foreign-policy doctrine whose practical force depended on circumstances and, in the short run, British naval power. Marshall's opinions created rules about who would decide future conflicts after the people in the first conflict were dead.

That is persistence in its purest form.

Consider the counterfactual.

Replace Marshall with a chief justice more deferential to state sovereignty. The Supreme Court still exists. *Marbury* may still have established or expressed judicial review in some form. But *McCulloch* could come out more narrowly, allowing Maryland greater room to burden the bank or restricting implied powers. *Cohens* could leave state high courts with more final authority over federal claims. *Gibbons* could define interstate commerce more cautiously, preserving broader state monopolies and regulations.

None of those outcomes necessarily breaks the Union.

The country might develop through more political bargaining and less judicial nationalism. Congress might specify its powers more carefully. States might retain larger economic domains. Federal supremacy could still strengthen through war, legislation, and later courts. The Civil War might eventually settle questions the Marshall Court tried to settle in doctrine.

But the timing changes.

A constitutional order is partly a set of expectations about where a loser goes next. A merchant who loses in a state court asks whether a federal forum is available. A legislature asks whether a charter can be altered without judicial intervention. A state asks whether it can tax a national institution. A corporation asks whether a contract with a state is politically reversible. Congress asks whether a useful instrument is forbidden merely because the Constitution did not name it.

Marshall made the national Supreme Court a more credible answer to those questions.

He also understood a fact that later justices would rediscover in different forms: courts accumulate power by acting as though their judgments belong in the structure before everyone agrees that they do.

The Supreme Court had no independent enforcement arm. It depended on compliance, institutional habit, and political acceptance. Marshall often wrote opinions designed not merely to decide the case but to explain a constitutional system in language broad enough to govern the next dispute. The persuasive style mattered because persuasion was part of enforcement.

This did not always work.

The Court's authority remained vulnerable. In the 1830s, conflicts involving Cherokee sovereignty and Georgia would reveal the gap between judicial principle and executive willingness. Later presidents would denounce judicial decisions; Congress would change the Court's jurisdiction and size; constitutional amendments would overturn doctrines; public confidence would rise and fall.

But institutions do not need unlimited power to change history. They need enough accepted power that actors begin planning around them.

By 1825, Americans planning banks, steamboat companies, colleges, contracts, and state policies had reason to include the Supreme Court in their calculations.

That was new.

The Constitution had created “one supreme Court” in a sentence. It did not explain how large a political presence the Court would become. It did not say that the justices would routinely arbitrate the boundary between national and state power. It did not guarantee that state courts would accept federal appellate review without resistance. It did not guarantee that Congress's implied powers would be interpreted generously.

People had to build those expectations out of cases.

Marshall was unusually well positioned to do it because he combined legal reasoning with a political nationalist's understanding of the Union's fragility. He had served in the Revolutionary War, in the Virginia ratifying convention, in Congress, as secretary of state, and then as chief justice. He did not approach federalism as a geometry problem. He had lived through a government too weak to act and a political generation afraid that a stronger one might act too much.

His answer consistently favored a workable national state.

That answer had winners.

Creditors and commercial actors often benefited from rules that restrained state interference. National institutions benefited from supremacy. Businesses operating across state lines benefited when local monopolies could not close markets. The federal government benefited from a doctrine that treated implied means as constitutional.

It had losers too.

State governments lost some control. Democratic majorities discovered that legislation could become vulnerable to judges. Communities suspicious of concentrated finance watched a federal bank receive constitutional protection. The legal order became more national and therefore more distant from purely local political correction.

This is why “the Court finds its power” is better than “Marshall strengthens the Constitution.” The latter assumes the direction of the strengthening is self-evidently good. The former describes what happened.

A branch of government that began with neither purse nor sword learned to convert interpretation into authority.

Marshall did not accomplish that alone. His fellow justices often joined him. Lawyers such as Daniel Webster developed arguments the Court could use. Congress passed the statutes that created disputes. State politicians resisted, giving federal doctrine something to push against. Economic integration made national rules increasingly useful. The public came to treat constitutional litigation as a normal arena for political conflict.

The institution grew through the ecosystem around it.

Still, remove Marshall and the ecology changes.

A different chief justice might have written narrower rules one case at a time. Marshall repeatedly connected cases to a vision: the people had created a national government with real powers; those powers had to be capable of execution; federal law had to be supreme within its sphere; and the Supreme Court had a role in saying where that sphere lay.

By 1825, that vision was no longer only a theory.

It was case law.

Presidents leave office. Congresses adjourn. Banks fail. Monopolies collapse. Political parties disappear.

An interpretation, once embedded in an institution that claims the right to interpret again, can wait.
