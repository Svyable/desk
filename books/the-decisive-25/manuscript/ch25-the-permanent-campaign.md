# 2016–2026: Donald Trump — The Permanent Campaign

Donald Trump did not create American polarization.

He discovered that polarization could be operated as a permanent governing system.

That is the distinction that makes him the final selection.

The forces that produced his rise were older than his candidacy: declining trust in institutions, partisan media, racial and cultural conflict, deindustrialization, immigration politics, the long aftermath of the financial crisis, geographic sorting, congressional dysfunction, social media, resentment toward professional elites, the conservative movement, the Tea Party, talk radio, cable news, and a Republican electorate increasingly dissatisfied with its own party leadership.

Trump did not invent any of these.

He fused them into a political identity that proved capable of surviving victory, defeat, impeachment, criminal and civil litigation, the January 6 attack on the Capitol, a four-year absence from office, and a return to the presidency.

That is historically unusual.

The decade from 2016 through 2026 contains three presidential elections with Trump at the center of each.

In 2016, he won 304 electoral votes while Hillary Clinton received 227 after faithless electors were counted. In 2020, he lost to Joseph Biden, 306 electoral votes to 232. In 2024, he returned and won 312 electoral votes to Kamala Harris's 226.

Those numbers reveal more than two victories and one defeat.

They show that Trump became a political era rather than a single administration.

Most defeated presidents recede.

Trump did not.

He remained the dominant figure in his party, contested the legitimacy of his 2020 defeat, shaped Republican primaries and congressional politics, and returned as the party's nominee and then president.

By 2026, the Republican Party was not merely a coalition that supported Trump.

Its national identity had been substantially reorganized around him.

That transformation is his first claim to the decade.

The second is communication.

Trump inherited the technological country built in the previous chapter.

The smartphone had placed a permanent screen in the electorate's hand. Social media had weakened the ability of newspapers, television networks, party officials, and other intermediaries to control access to mass attention.

Trump understood the opportunity instinctively.

A politician no longer needed to wait for the evening news.

He could create the evening news.

A post could trigger coverage, outrage, defense, fundraising, counterattack, and another post before traditional institutions had decided how to interpret the first one.

The campaign never had to end because the communications channel never closed.

This changed presidential time.

Earlier presidents campaigned constantly in a broad sense. Franklin Roosevelt mastered radio. Kennedy understood television. Reagan understood prime-time address. Clinton's White House studied rapid response. George W. Bush and Barack Obama built sophisticated campaign organizations. Obama in particular demonstrated digital organizing at national scale.

Trump added a different model.

The president could act as his own continuous opposition broadcaster.

He could govern while speaking as though he remained an insurgent against the government.

That paradox became central to his power.

When institutions blocked him, the blockage became evidence that the institutions were corrupt.

When allies hesitated, hesitation became betrayal.

When journalists contradicted him, the contradiction became proof of media hostility.

When prosecutors charged him, prosecution became political persecution in the eyes of supporters.

When courts ruled against his administration, the ruling could be folded into the same narrative of entrenched resistance.

This is the permanent campaign as epistemology.

Every event arrives already assigned a side.

That makes coalition loyalty unusually durable because disconfirming evidence can be interpreted as confirmation of the coalition's belief that hostile institutions are coordinating against it.

This mechanism did not begin with Trump. Parties, movements, religions, and ideological communities have always developed ways to explain hostile evidence.

Digital media increased the speed and density of the feedback loop.

Trump supplied the central personality around which it organized.

His 2016 campaign broke many expectations about what a successful presidential candidate was supposed to sound like.

He insulted rivals openly. He attacked party leaders. He made immigration restriction and a border wall defining issues. He rejected important parts of the post-Cold War Republican consensus on trade, alliances, and foreign intervention. He treated political correctness as elite censorship. He used celebrity and conflict to dominate attention far beyond what conventional campaign spending alone would have purchased.

Supporters heard candor.

Critics heard demagoguery.

Both heard something unmistakably different from the scripted language of professional politics.

That difference mattered because distrust had made polish suspicious.

A verbal mistake that would damage another candidate could strengthen Trump's claim that he was not managed by consultants.

The ordinary political penalty for violating norms weakened when norm violation became the product.

This is why attempts to understand Trump only through policy miss the structural change.

His policy record mattered enormously.

His first administration signed major tax legislation, appointed three Supreme Court justices, changed immigration enforcement, withdrew from or renegotiated international agreements, imposed tariffs, shifted the Republican approach to trade, moved the U.S. embassy in Israel to Jerusalem, pursued deregulation, confronted China more aggressively, and altered the tone and priorities of alliances and foreign policy.

But another Republican president could have pursued some of those outcomes.

The judicial appointments in particular depended on a Republican Senate, conservative legal networks, vacancies, and Mitch McConnell's institutional strategy as much as presidential preference.

Trump's less replaceable effect was making personal loyalty and political combat central organizing features of the party.

The Republican establishment did not simply move right.

It changed what counted as establishment legitimacy.

Positions once associated with insurgent factions became tests of belonging. Skepticism toward free trade, stronger immigration restriction, hostility toward elite institutions, and a more transactional view of alliances became more central. Politicians who had criticized Trump often adapted, retired, lost primaries, or reconciled themselves to his leadership.

The party learned that opposing its most active voters could be more dangerous than violating its recent orthodoxy.

That is a deep institutional change.

Then came the pandemic.

COVID-19 was not caused by Trump, and no leader had a simple solution available in early 2020. The virus created a global health emergency under conditions of scientific uncertainty. Governments had to make decisions about travel, testing, schools, workplaces, hospitals, economic support, vaccines, and public behavior while knowledge changed quickly.

Trump's response became inseparable from the country's polarization.

Public-health measures, masks, school closures, vaccine development, economic restrictions, and scientific institutions became political symbols. The administration supported Operation Warp Speed, which accelerated vaccine development and manufacturing through an extraordinary public-private effort. At the same time, Trump's public communication repeatedly generated controversy over risk, treatments, experts, and the balance between reopening and restriction.

The pandemic therefore amplified the permanent campaign.

Even a virus entered partisan identity.

This was not uniquely American, but American political structure intensified it.

The 2020 election produced the decade's constitutional crisis.

Biden won the Electoral College 306 to 232.

Trump refused to accept the result and pursued efforts to reverse or block it through litigation, political pressure, alternate-elector efforts, pressure on state officials, and pressure surrounding the congressional count.

Courts rejected numerous challenges. States certified their results. The Electoral College voted. Congress met on January 6, 2021, to count the votes.

A mob attacked the Capitol.

The violence interrupted the constitutional proceeding for hours. Law-enforcement officers were assaulted. Members of Congress and Vice President Mike Pence were evacuated or sheltered. The count resumed after the building was secured, and Biden's victory was certified early the next morning.

The House later created a Select Committee to investigate the attack. Its final report concluded that Trump was central to a multi-part effort to overturn the election result. Trump and his allies rejected the committee's framing and described the investigation as partisan. Whatever interpretation one places on the committee's motives, the underlying constitutional sequence is not ambiguous: Trump lost the certified election, sought to prevent that result from taking effect, and the transfer of power was accompanied by an attack on the Capitol.

This is the chapter's hardest fact.

It also makes the 2024 election historically remarkable.

The political system did not remove Trump from public life.

He survived a second impeachment, Senate acquittal, investigations, criminal indictments, civil judgments, rivals within his party, and a presidential campaign conducted under legal and political pressures unprecedented for a major-party nominee.

Then voters returned him to office.

The 2024 Electoral College result—312 to 226—made him the second person in American history, after Grover Cleveland, to win nonconsecutive presidential terms.

That return changed the meaning of the first term.

Trump could no longer be described as an accidental one-off produced by a narrow 2016 Electoral College path.

A second victory demonstrated a durable constituency and a party remade enough to carry him back after defeat.

Supporters interpreted the comeback as vindication.

They saw a leader who had endured institutions they distrusted and returned with a stronger mandate to change them. Many supported stricter immigration policy, tariffs and economic nationalism, more presidential control over the executive branch, conservative social policy, reduced deference to bureaucratic expertise, and confrontation with institutions they regarded as ideologically captured.

Critics interpreted the same comeback as a warning.

They saw a president who had refused a lawful election defeat, repeatedly attacked institutional constraints, personalized the executive branch, undermined independent sources of authority, and demonstrated that electoral support can coexist with serious pressure on democratic norms.

A book about importance cannot resolve that dispute by pretending it does not exist.

It can identify the shared fact beneath it.

Both supporters and critics believe Trump is trying to change the relationship between elected power and institutions that claim some independence from it.

They disagree over whether that is democratic accountability or dangerous concentration.

That disagreement defines the second term.

The phrase “deep state,” once obscure, became common political language. Career civil servants, intelligence agencies, prosecutors, regulators, universities, media organizations, public-health agencies, election administrators, and courts increasingly appeared in partisan narratives not as neutral or semi-neutral institutions but as political actors.

Some skepticism was earned.

Institutions contain bias, incentives, error, groupthink, self-protection, and ideological cultures. Experts can be wrong. Bureaucracies can resist elected policy. Media organizations make mistakes. Prosecutors possess discretion. Universities are not politically neutral communities. Courts divide along jurisprudential lines with political consequences.

Democratic government requires the ability to challenge institutions.

The counterproblem is that an elected leader can use real institutional imperfections to argue that no independent constraint deserves legitimacy when it conflicts with him.

That is where accountability can become personalism.

Trump's importance lies partly in forcing the country to confront that boundary repeatedly.

The second-term administration that began in 2025 moved quickly on immigration, trade, executive control, regulatory policy, and the federal workforce. By September 2026, Trump remained the central figure of the midterm election environment; even candidates running for Congress were repeatedly interpreted through their relationship to his presidency and political organization.

That persistence is unusual for a second-term president approaching midterms.

Trump's political apparatus also blurred boundaries between campaign, movement, media ecosystem, merchandise, fundraising, and governing identity more completely than earlier presidencies.

The presidency became a brand without a real off-season.

That is the permanent campaign in organizational form.

Policy becomes content.

Content becomes fundraising.

Fundraising becomes loyalty evidence.

Loyalty becomes candidate selection.

Candidate selection changes Congress.

Congress changes the set of policies the president can pursue.

The loop returns to policy.

A political movement becomes self-reinforcing because every institutional confrontation produces more movement energy.

This has consequences beyond Republicans.

Democrats reorganized around opposition to Trump for much of the decade. Fundraising, turnout, activist networks, candidate recruitment, media strategy, and coalition identity were shaped by the need to defeat or constrain him. The 2018 midterms, 2020 election, January 6 investigations, 2022 midterms, 2024 campaign, and 2026 midterm environment all carried Trump as a central reference point even when he was not on the ballot.

He changed his opponents too.

That is one of the best tests of importance.

A truly consequential political figure forces adversaries to reorganize.

Trump also changed journalism.

News organizations struggled with how to cover a politician who produced false, exaggerated, contradictory, inflammatory, or attention-dominating claims at high frequency. Live television could amplify statements before fact-checking. Refusing coverage could seem like suppression. Constant correction could make the correction itself part of the spectacle. Outrage drove audiences. The economics of digital media often rewarded the very content institutions claimed to deplore.

Trump understood attention as leverage better than most journalists understood attention as vulnerability.

That asymmetry helped him.

Social-media companies faced the same problem.

Platforms wanted engagement, open expression, advertising revenue, and political legitimacy. They also confronted misinformation, harassment, incitement, foreign influence, and pressure to moderate public figures. Decisions to label, limit, suspend, or restore Trump's accounts became political events in themselves.

Private companies had become governors of speech infrastructure.

The Gates and Jobs chapters had already built this world.

Trump demonstrated its political consequence.

Who else could claim 2016–2026?

Barack Obama remains a powerful rival at the beginning of the interval because Trump's rise was partly a reaction to the political order Obama represented. Joe Biden defeated Trump in 2020, enacted major legislation, appointed judges, led the country through the later pandemic, supported Ukraine after Russia's 2022 invasion, and governed during a consequential inflationary and industrial-policy period.

Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk are serious platform-era candidates. Zuckerberg's Facebook and Meta shaped political communication, advertising, social identity, and misinformation at enormous scale. Musk changed electric vehicles and spaceflight and later acquired Twitter/X, placing him directly inside the political information system. The unidentified creator or creators of Bitcoin changed finance and political debate around money. Anthony Fauci became a symbol—admired by some, distrusted by others—of expertise and public health during the pandemic, though the structural changes cannot be assigned to him personally.

Vladimir Putin is an external rival. Russian interference in the 2016 election, the U.S.-Russia confrontation, and the invasion of Ukraine shaped American politics, alliances, defense spending, energy policy, and domestic arguments across the decade.

Trump wins because he became the central organizer of the political system's attention.

Remove Zuckerberg and the digital environment changes.

Remove Musk and electric vehicles, commercial space, and online politics develop differently.

Remove Biden and the 2020–2024 policy record changes substantially.

Remove Trump and the entire party system takes another path.

A conventional Republican may still defeat Clinton in 2016. Conservative judges still reach the Supreme Court if Republicans control the presidency and Senate. Immigration remains contentious. China policy hardens. Social media still polarizes. The Republican coalition still moves away from its older establishment in some form.

But the personal fusion is missing.

There is no exact Trump movement.

There is no first impeachment over Ukraine in the same form. There is no 2020 election denial led by the incumbent in the same form. There is no January 6 attack arising from the same effort to stop certification. There is no four-year campaign for restoration centered on a defeated former president. There is no 2024 comeback returning the same person to office after all of it.

That is extraordinary counterfactual distance.

The final irony is that Trump rose by attacking a political class that never seemed to stop campaigning.

Then he perfected the permanent campaign.

He made politics more direct, more participatory, more emotionally legible, and more difficult to escape.

Supporters experienced that intensity as representation after years of being ignored.

Opponents experienced it as exhaustion and institutional threat.

Both reactions became fuel.

The country entered the phone in the previous decade.

In this one, the campaign followed it there and stopped going home.
