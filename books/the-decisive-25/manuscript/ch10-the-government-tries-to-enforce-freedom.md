# 1866–1875: Ulysses S. Grant — The Government Tries to Enforce Freedom

The Civil War settled whether slavery would survive.

It did not settle whether freedom could survive local government.

That distinction defines the decade after Appomattox.

The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery, except as punishment for crime. The Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and constitutional protections against state denial of due process and equal protection. The Fifteenth Amendment prohibited denial of the vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

On paper, the constitutional order had changed more radically in five years than at any time since the founding.

On the ground, a Black citizen trying to vote in a southern county could still be beaten, expelled from work, murdered, or confronted by officials who either participated in the violence or had no intention of stopping it.

The problem was enforcement.

Ulysses S. Grant's importance in the decade from 1866 to 1875 comes from a hard and imperfect fact: for a period during his presidency, the federal government seriously attempted to use national law, federal courts, prosecutors, and troops to suppress organized white-supremacist terrorism and protect Black political participation.

The attempt was incomplete. It did not permanently defeat the counterrevolution against Reconstruction. Grant's administration contained corruption, inconsistency, bad appointments, and retreat. Northern political will weakened. The Supreme Court narrowed federal tools. White southern paramilitary organizations adapted. By the end of the decade, the commitment was already fraying.

That makes the attempt more important, not less.

It shows that the collapse of Reconstruction was not simply the natural expiration of an impossible policy. There was a period in which the national government possessed tools, used them, and achieved results. The later retreat was a political choice made after Americans had seen that enforcement could work at least temporarily.

Grant did not create Reconstruction.

Congressional Republicans had already fought President Andrew Johnson over the terms on which former Confederate states would return. Johnson, a Tennessee Unionist who became president after Lincoln's assassination, favored rapid restoration with limited protection for freedpeople and clashed bitterly with Congress. Southern legislatures enacted Black Codes restricting Black mobility, labor, and civil rights. Violence exploded, including massacres in Memphis and New Orleans in 1866.

Congress responded with the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the Fourteenth Amendment, military Reconstruction legislation, and measures designed to reshape southern state governments. Radical Republicans such as Thaddeus Stevens and Charles Sumner argued that emancipation without political power would leave freedpeople vulnerable to the same class that had controlled them under slavery.

Grant entered the presidency in 1869 after much of this architecture already existed.

What remained unresolved was whether the executive branch would defend it.

His military career shaped expectations.

Grant had accepted Lee's surrender. He had supervised armies across vast distances. He understood the difference between announcing an objective and supplying enough force to make the objective real. As general of the Army under Johnson, he had also been pulled into the political conflict over Reconstruction and the Tenure of Office Act.

The presidency presented a harder battlefield because the enemy did not wear one uniform.

White resistance to Reconstruction operated through elections, labor relations, state politics, newspapers, economic pressure, murder, and clandestine terror. The Ku Klux Klan and related organizations attacked Black voters, Republican officeholders, teachers, ministers, organizers, and white allies. Night riding and disguise allowed perpetrators to terrorize communities while preserving deniability. Local juries and sheriffs could be sympathetic to the attackers.

A civil-rights regime that depended entirely on those local institutions could be defeated county by county.

The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, made the voting-rights issue constitutionally explicit. Black men voted and held office across the South at levels unimaginable before the war. They served in state legislatures, constitutional conventions, Congress, and local government. Reconstruction governments built public institutions, including expanded systems of public education.

The political participation itself triggered violence because it redistributed power.

This is a recurring mistake in stories about rights. We imagine violence as evidence that a reform has failed. Often violence is evidence that the reform has begun to matter.

If Black votes were meaningless, nobody needed a secret army to suppress them.

Congress enacted Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871, culminating in legislation commonly called the Ku Klux Klan Act. These laws expanded federal authority to prosecute interference with voting and conspiracies against constitutional rights. They allowed federal supervision of elections in some circumstances and gave the president extraordinary powers when ordinary law enforcement could not suppress organized terror.

Grant used them.

The clearest case came in South Carolina.

The upcountry Klan campaign after the 1870 election involved beatings, whippings, sexual violence, murder, and attacks intended to destroy Republican political organization and Black voting. Federal investigators gathered evidence. Grant eventually declared a state of rebellion in several counties and suspended habeas corpus under statutory authority. Federal troops made large numbers of arrests.

The Department of Justice, itself newly created in 1870, prosecuted cases in federal court.

Attorney General Amos T. Akerman became one of the administration's most determined civil-rights enforcers. A former Confederate officer who had become a Republican, Akerman treated Klan violence as an assault on the constitutional order. Federal prosecutors and marshals pursued conspirators under the Enforcement Acts. The South Carolina Klan trials of 1871 and 1872 produced convictions and disrupted the organization.

The Federal Judicial Center's account is careful about the limits: relatively few cases went to full trial, legal rulings did not create the durable broad constitutional protection prosecutors hoped for, and important perpetrators escaped. But the immediate result was substantial. The organized Klan campaign was broken in the affected area and the 1872 election proceeded under a different security environment.

That is the counterfactual hinge.

For a brief period, a Black voter in part of the South could know that an attack by masked men might bring not only a local sheriff but the federal government.

That changed what citizenship meant.

The Fourteenth Amendment had created national citizenship as a constitutional concept. Enforcement made national citizenship a relationship.

If a state failed to protect you, there was another government to which you could appeal.

The promise was revolutionary because the prewar federal system had often worked in the opposite direction. The Fugitive Slave Act had required federal power to help return escaped people to slavery even when northern states resisted. Reconstruction attempted to use federal power against state and private systems of racial domination.

The same national capacity was pointed in a different moral direction.

Grant did not always point it consistently.

His administration's record included scandals that damaged public trust. The Whiskey Ring involved tax fraud by distillers and government officials. The Crédit Mobilier scandal concerned the Union Pacific and implicated prominent Republicans, though its central conduct predated Grant's presidency and Grant himself was not shown to have participated. The Sanborn contracting controversy and other episodes reinforced an image of an administration too tolerant of corrupt associates.

Grant was personally capable of loyalty long after loyalty became bad judgment.

That weakness mattered politically because Reconstruction required northern voters to trust an unusually active federal government. Every corruption story gave opponents another argument that national intervention in the South was not principle but patronage.

The Panic of 1873 made the political environment worse.

A severe economic depression shifted attention toward jobs, currency, debt, and business failure. Northern voters who had supported Reconstruction when the war was fresh became less willing to sustain expensive and politically contentious enforcement. Democrats gained strength. Liberal Republicans attacked corruption and centralized power. White southern “Redeemer” movements presented themselves as restoring home rule while paramilitary violence helped make Republican government impossible.

Federal resolve weakened precisely when resistance learned how to wait it out.

This exposes the limit of Grant's model.

Military and prosecutorial suppression can break an organization. It cannot by itself create a self-sustaining democratic order if local economic power, land ownership, courts, employers, and political culture remain aligned against equal citizenship.

Most freedpeople received no large-scale land redistribution. Sharecropping and debt constrained economic independence. Federal troops were too few to police the entire South indefinitely. Prosecutors depended on witnesses willing to risk retaliation. Congress's willingness to authorize new powers had limits. The Supreme Court would later narrow Reconstruction enforcement through decisions that restricted federal reach.

Freedom needed institutions deeper than emergency intervention.

Grant recognized some of this but did not solve it.

His “Peace Policy” toward Native nations, another major part of his presidency, shows how an administration could pursue reform rhetoric while sustaining coercive expansion. Grant sought to reduce corruption in Indian affairs and placed religious organizations in some agency roles. Yet western military campaigns and reservation policy continued to dispossess Native peoples, suppress autonomy, and clear space for American settlement. The president who used federal force to protect Black citizenship in South Carolina also led a government using federal power to confine Indigenous nations in the West.

National capacity still had no moral direction built into it.

Grant's civil-rights commitment also encountered moments when he did not intervene decisively enough.

The Colfax Massacre in Louisiana in 1873, where white attackers killed large numbers of Black men after a contested election, revealed how far organized violence could go. Federal prosecutions followed, but the Supreme Court's later decision in *United States v. Cruikshank* undermined federal convictions and sharply limited the reach of the Fourteenth Amendment against private violence.

In Mississippi in 1875, the “Mississippi Plan” used organized intimidation and violence to suppress Republican voting and retake state power. Governor Adelbert Ames requested federal assistance. Grant's administration, facing political exhaustion and a hostile national environment, did not provide the intervention needed to reverse the campaign.

This was not the Grant of South Carolina in 1871.

The difference is crucial.

Presidents are often evaluated as though they contain one fixed quantity of conviction. In reality, policy competes with elections, congressional support, public opinion, legal doctrine, cabinet advice, economic crisis, and fatigue. The same Grant who had been willing to suspend habeas corpus against the Klan could later conclude that further intervention lacked sustainable political backing.

The counterrevolution noticed.

Violence is strategic when it tests whether the state will respond.

If the answer becomes no, the violence can become politics by other means.

Grant signed the Civil Rights Act of 1875 near the end of this decade. The law sought to guarantee equal access to inns, public transportation, theaters, and other public accommodations. It was an ambitious attempt to extend federal civil-rights protection beyond voting and formal legal status into everyday public life.

The Supreme Court would strike down key parts in the Civil Rights Cases of 1883, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment restricted state action rather than purely private discrimination.

The sequence is almost a summary of Reconstruction: constitutional revolution, federal legislation, attempted enforcement, judicial narrowing, political retreat.

Who matters most inside it?

Thaddeus Stevens is the strongest rival for the early part of the decade. He understood more clearly than many contemporaries that political equality without economic restructuring could leave southern power relations largely intact. He fought Johnson, supported Black suffrage, and pushed Reconstruction in Congress before his death in 1868.

Andrew Johnson is the destructive rival. His lenient restoration policy, vetoes, racism, and conflict with Congress helped shape the constitutional confrontation that produced congressional Reconstruction. Remove Johnson and some of the decade's bitter institutional conflict changes dramatically.

Charles Sumner carried an expansive vision of equal civil rights and pressed the country beyond what Grant was often willing to support.

Grant wins the full interval because laws acquire different meaning depending on whether an executive uses them.

The Enforcement Acts on the page did not arrest Klansmen.

Federal soldiers did.

The Justice Department on an organization chart did not make witnesses safe enough to testify.

Prosecutors, marshals, grand juries, and troops did, imperfectly.

The president possessed powers Congress had deliberately created for a crisis of democratic violence. Grant chose, at critical moments, to invoke them.

Remove him and another Republican president might do the same. The party's Reconstruction wing was still powerful. But there were also Republicans eager for reconciliation, patronage, economic development, or political quiet. A president less personally committed to defeating armed resistance could have treated Klan violence as a state problem and allowed the new constitutional amendments to become nominal much sooner.

That is why the failures of the later 1870s should not erase the earlier success.

The Klan was not invincible.

White-supremacist terror was not beyond the reach of law.

The federal government demonstrated that organized political violence could be investigated, prosecuted, and suppressed when national institutions were willing to spend authority on Black citizenship.

Then the country demonstrated the opposite lesson: rights protected only while the majority remains interested are rights on probation.

Grant's decade therefore ends without triumph.

The federal government had tried to enforce freedom.

It had shown that enforcement could work.

It had also shown how quickly constitutional promises could become local again when the national government decided the cost was too high.
