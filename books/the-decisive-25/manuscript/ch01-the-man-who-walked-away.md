# 1776–1785: George Washington — The Man Who Walked Away

On December 23, 1783, George Washington entered the Maryland State House in Annapolis carrying a piece of paper that had once made him the most dangerous man in America.

It was his military commission.

Eight and a half years earlier, Congress had placed an army under his command before there was a United States government capable of doing most of the things governments ordinarily do for armies. Congress could not reliably tax. It could not reliably pay. Its authority depended on states that guarded their own money, men, supplies, and political dignity. Washington spent the war begging legislatures for food, uniforms, enlistments, horses, ammunition, and cash while trying to keep a national army alive inside a political system suspicious of national armies.

Now the war was over. The British had evacuated New York. The Treaty of Paris recognized American independence. Washington stood before Congress, drew out the commission, and surrendered it.

The important thing about the scene is not that it was theatrical. It was. Congress arranged a public audience, and everyone involved understood the symbolism. The important thing is that the theater made a claim about who owned force in the new country. The victorious commander was not the state. The army was not the state. Military glory did not entitle its possessor to continue governing after the emergency that had created his authority.

This chapter begins at the end of the war because Washington's best claim on the first American decade is not simply that he won battles. He lost too many for that version to survive contact with the record. His claim is that he made two forms of survival possible at once: the army survived long enough for independence to become real, and civilian government survived having an army whose commander possessed more national prestige than almost any civilian institution around him.

Remove Washington from 1776–1785 and the first question is not who replaces him on a monument. It is whether those two survivals still happen together.

The military case begins badly.

In the summer of 1776, independence was declared in Philadelphia while an enormous British expedition gathered around New York. The new country had announced itself in prose before proving that it could remain in existence by force. Washington's army suffered defeat on Long Island. The British took New York City. American forces retreated north, then across New Jersey. Enlistments were expiring. Desertion and disease reduced units that had never been as solid as later paintings made them appear. Congress fled Philadelphia for Baltimore as the British threat widened. By December, the Revolution had reached the kind of moment that later generations tend to edit out of founding stories: not noble uncertainty, but the plausible prospect of failure.

Washington's answer was not strategic elegance. It was movement.

On Christmas night his forces crossed the Delaware and struck the Hessian garrison at Trenton. A week later, after maneuvering away from a dangerous position, he attacked at Princeton. The engagements were small beside the great European battles of the era, but scale can mislead. The victories interrupted a sequence in which British success had begun to look normal and American collapse had begun to look possible. They restored a sense that the Continental Army could still surprise an opponent, still recruit, still generate political hope.

That did not solve the war. It prolonged the argument.

Prolongation was Washington's central military talent. He gradually learned that the destruction of his own army would matter more than the occupation of any particular city. Philadelphia could fall and the Revolution could continue. New York could remain in British hands for years. Washington did not need to conquer the British Empire. He needed to deny it a clean end to the rebellion until distance, cost, European rivalry, French intervention, and American persistence changed the calculation.

That sounds modest only after victory has made survival look easy.

An insurgent political project with a weak treasury and expiring enlistments can lose by trying too hard to look like a conventional state. If Washington had repeatedly wagered the army on decisive set-piece battles against a stronger professional force, one glorious defeat might have ended the political experiment. His strategic importance lay partly in learning what kind of war he was actually fighting. Territory mattered. Cities mattered. Public confidence mattered. But the continued existence of a Continental Army mattered more, because it kept alive the claim that the rebellion had a national military instrument and therefore a future.

The army itself was an argument.

Every winter it remained in being told Britain that occupation was not settlement. Every campaign season forced London to spend again. Every surviving regiment allowed diplomats in Europe to describe independence as more than a declaration waiting to be reversed. The longer the war continued, the more other powers could imagine using it against Britain. Time was not neutral. Time converted American survival into British cost and European opportunity.

Washington did not discover that logic alone, and he did not execute it perfectly. He could be aggressive, impatient, and wrong. His officers disagreed with him. Congress interfered. State politics constrained him. The war produced its own teachers. But by the middle years, his command had become less about producing a single decisive triumph than about keeping an imperfect coalition from exhausting itself before Britain did.

This is not the same thing as saying Washington carried the Revolution on his back. He did not.

Benjamin Franklin's diplomacy in France helped secure the alliance without which Yorktown is difficult to imagine. French loans, troops, arms, and naval power altered the war. The Marquis de Lafayette became a crucial political and military bridge. Nathanael Greene conducted a southern campaign in which losing ground could still exhaust British control. Local militias, state governments, private suppliers, sailors, spies, women who sustained households and war economies, enslaved and free Black people who served on different sides, and Native nations defending their own interests all shaped the conflict. The British made consequential mistakes. The French made consequential choices.

A history that puts Washington alone at the center of Yorktown becomes less accurate precisely where it tries to make him larger.

His real leverage appears in coordination.

By 1781, Washington was commanding not merely troops but a coalition. The Yorktown campaign depended on French General Rochambeau's army, Admiral de Grasse's fleet, American forces, uncertain timing, secrecy, long-distance movement, and a decision to shift attention away from New York toward Virginia. The victory became possible because several independent actors converged at the right place. Washington's prestige and position helped make that convergence governable. He was not the author of every ingredient. He was the commander around whom the ingredients could be assembled.

That distinction matters for the book's method. A person can be decisive without being sufficient. In fact, most decisive people are not sufficient. Their leverage comes from arranging, timing, legitimizing, or redirecting forces that already exist. The mistake is to imagine that because the forces are larger than the person, the person therefore does not matter. The opposite mistake is to treat the forces as scenery once a famous person enters the frame.

Yorktown is useful because neither mistake survives it.

Without the French fleet, the trap around Cornwallis fails. Without Rochambeau's army, the allied force is weaker. Without British strategic decisions, there is no vulnerable army in Virginia to encircle. Without years of American resistance, France has less reason to make the gamble. Washington's contribution was to operate at the joint where those independent conditions could become one campaign.

Then victory created a different danger.

Wars are easier to start than states are to finish.

The Continental Army had accumulated grievances that were not ceremonial. Officers and soldiers had gone unpaid. Promises of compensation and pensions depended on a Congress with weak revenue powers and on states that did not always meet requisitions. Men who had spent years being told that they were the instrument of liberty could reasonably ask why the republic they had defended could not honor its obligations to them.

The problem was constitutional before the Constitution existed.

Congress had asked soldiers to obey national authority while lacking the fiscal power to behave like a durable national sovereign. Officers could see the contradiction more clearly than most civilians because they lived inside it. They had accepted commands from a Continental institution, risked their lives for a Continental cause, and then discovered that payment depended on a loose chain of requisitions and state compliance. The army's anger was not proof of disloyalty. It was evidence that the political structure had made national promises without creating reliable national means to keep them.

This is the background to a strange letter Washington received in May 1782 from Colonel Lewis Nicola.

Nicola's argument grew from real military frustration. The existing republican arrangements, he thought, were weak. Congress could promise what it lacked the power to deliver. A stronger form of government might solve the problem. In the course of proposing a military settlement and a different constitutional order, Nicola raised the possibility that the head of such a system might even carry the title of king.

The later American legend simplified this into an offer of a crown. The document is more complicated than that, and the distinction matters. There was no national plebiscite begging Washington to become monarch. There was, however, a serving officer writing to the commander in chief about monarchical possibilities at a moment when the army had legitimate reasons to distrust civilian performance.

Washington's reaction was immediate and severe. He wrote that nothing in the war had given him more painful sensations and told Nicola to banish the idea.

That response matters because Washington had incentives to entertain the complaint even if he rejected the title. He shared the army's anger over unpaid obligations. He had spent years experiencing congressional weakness firsthand. He believed the national government needed more energy. Within a few years he would support constitutional reform that gave the federal government powers the Confederation lacked. He was not defending every defect of the existing system.

He was defending the boundary between remedy and seizure.

The distinction would become one of the most important recurring arguments in American political history. A government can be weak enough that reform is necessary without making extra-constitutional force legitimate. An institution can have a justified grievance without being entitled to impose the solution. A leader can believe that civilian officials are failing and still refuse to convert that failure into personal authority.

Those propositions are easy to endorse once they are embedded in a political culture. They are harder when the army is armed, the treasury is empty, the civilian institutions are demonstrably weak, and the commander has spent eight years being told that national survival depends on his judgment.

The boundary became more important in March 1783 at Newburgh, New York.

Peace seemed close, but the army's financial claims remained unsettled. Anonymous addresses circulated among officers, calling for a meeting and suggesting that the army should apply greater pressure to Congress. The possibility of military action against civilian authority did not need to take the form of a formal coup to be dangerous. A national army with weapons, accumulated grievances, and a weak legislature could change the constitutional balance simply by demonstrating that promises were enforceable when soldiers chose to enforce them.

This is one reason the word coup can obscure as much as it clarifies. A republic does not need to wake up one morning under a general's formal dictatorship for military politics to deform civilian government. If legislators begin bargaining under the implicit knowledge that the army can collectively punish them, the constitutional relationship has already changed. If officers learn that armed solidarity succeeds where ordinary petition fails, the precedent survives even if no one takes a throne.

Washington intervened.

His Newburgh address did not tell the officers that their complaints were imaginary. That would have been insulting and false. He instead argued that an army created to defend political liberty could destroy the object of its sacrifice if it allowed grievance to become military coercion. He asked them to trust a constitutional solution and used his own standing with Congress to press for justice.

The famous story from the meeting concerns Washington putting on spectacles and remarking that he had grown gray, and nearly blind, in the country's service. The line survives because it dramatizes something deeper than sentiment. Washington could ask restraint from the army because he had shared the war with it. A civilian politician appearing at Newburgh to lecture unpaid officers about patience would not possess the same leverage.

His authority was therefore useful because it was personal, and safe because he chose not to turn the personal authority into a competing sovereignty.

That combination is rarer than either quality by itself.

A leader with no personal following cannot easily calm a mutinous or politicized military. A leader with a powerful following can calm it but may also discover that the following is a political asset worth keeping. Washington's significance lies in possessing enough prestige to make refusal effective.

This is where the counterfactual begins to bite.

A different general might have been a better battlefield tactician. A different general might also have won the officers' affection. But the replacement needs to satisfy several conditions at once. He must survive the disasters of 1776 without allowing the army to disintegrate. He must accept dependence on French power without surrendering American command. He must endure years of congressional dysfunction without making the army a rival government. He must possess enough prestige by 1783 that angry officers listen when he tells them not to use their leverage. And then, after all of that, he must give the power back.

This is not an impossible person. History should not be converted into a claim that only Washington could have done anything Washington did. But the number of plausible substitutes narrows when the requirements are combined.

Benjamin Franklin is the strongest rival for the decade.

Without Franklin's work in France, the international conditions of victory change drastically. The French alliance mattered not as decoration but as money, matériel, troops, and naval force. Franklin possessed a kind of leverage Washington did not: he could make the American cause useful and attractive inside European power politics. The United States needed a foreign patron with reasons of its own to weaken Britain. Franklin helped turn that geopolitical interest into an alliance Americans could actually use.

If the question were which American did most to internationalize the war, Franklin might win.

Thomas Paine is another rival because *Common Sense* altered the political imagination at the beginning of the conflict. A rebellion seeking better terms inside the British Empire is one kind of project. A movement demanding independence from monarchy is another. Paine did not invent independence, but he helped make a previously radical conclusion speakable at mass scale.

Nathanael Greene deserves a harder look than public memory usually gives him. His southern campaign forced the British to spend men and attention across difficult territory, making apparent tactical victories strategically costly. A different southern outcome could change the road to Yorktown.

One can even make the case for France's leaders rather than any American. If this book were titled *The Most Important Person to America* instead of *The People Who Made America*, the first decade might already force us outside the new nation's citizenry.

Washington still wins this book's test because his leverage spans war and peace. He sits at the joint between force and government.

Franklin's diplomacy helps make victory possible. Paine helps make independence imaginable. Greene helps make British occupation expensive. Washington's unusual contribution is that the military instrument remains coherent long enough to benefit from all three kinds of leverage and then does not become the political owner of the victory it helped secure.

That does not make the joint clean.

The republic whose army Washington subordinated to civilian rule was not a republic of equal liberty. Washington was an enslaver. The plantation to which he returned after resigning depended on the labor of enslaved people. His wealth and ambitions were tied to western land at a time when American expansion meant pressure on Indigenous nations whose sovereignty the new country would repeatedly violate. The political liberty secured from Britain existed beside slavery, dispossession, restricted suffrage, and legal hierarchies that excluded much of the population from the republican citizenship celebrated in public language.

These are not later footnotes placed beside an otherwise uncomplicated founding. They are part of what was founded.

The contradiction should not be repaired for him.

Washington could understand the danger of military domination while participating in human domination on his own estate. He could help establish a norm of civilian political legitimacy while living inside a racial order that denied millions the political standing on which that legitimacy supposedly rested. A national story that uses his resignation as proof of uncomplicated devotion to liberty turns one real act of restraint into a solvent poured over the rest of his life.

The act does not need that protection.

Its significance is narrower and stronger when the contradiction remains visible. A person can establish an important republican precedent while participating in systems that deny freedom to others. The country can inherit both things. Importance does not require a verdict of moral coherence.

It requires us to notice what the resignation changed.

In many revolutions, a victorious army becomes the most organized national institution in a weak political order. The person who controls it therefore begins the peace with an advantage over legislators, judges, merchants, farmers, and ordinary voters. A commander does not need to declare himself king to distort a republic. He can remain indispensable. He can allow emergency authority to harden into permanent authority. He can cultivate the idea that only he can protect the revolution from its own politicians.

Washington did something less dramatic and, for that reason, easier to underestimate. He made himself dispensable.

On December 23, he told Congress that the work assigned to him was finished. He surrendered the commission. He left Annapolis and went home.

He would not stay home forever. The weaknesses of the Confederation would draw him back toward public life. His presence at the Constitutional Convention would matter enormously, and he would become the first president under the new Constitution. One could therefore object that the resignation was only an intermission.

That objection improves the story because it forces a distinction between withdrawing from politics forever and withdrawing from military authority when military authority no longer has a legitimate emergency purpose.

Washington did not become a hermit. He became a private citizen and later returned through a civilian constitutional process. He accepted political office after the new system created a lawful office for him to accept. He did not carry wartime command into peacetime sovereignty and ask the country to ratify the accomplished fact.

An intermission can establish a rule.

The later presidency strengthened the precedent because Washington again confronted the question of whether personal indispensability should become permanent. The two-term example belongs mostly outside this chapter's decade, but its later power rested partly on a reputation already established in 1783: this was a man who could possess authority and leave it.

The first American decade is full of more quotable people. Paine wrote the pamphlet that made monarchy look ridiculous to readers who had recently lived under one. Jefferson drafted the Declaration. Franklin turned diplomacy into strategy. Lafayette carried the Revolution across an ocean. Greene made British victories expensive. Thousands of people whose names did not survive made independence physically possible.

Washington's first-place claim is therefore not a claim that he was the Revolution.

It is a claim about what revolutions become after they survive.

A successful rebellion can end with a durable republic. It can also end with military guardianship, factional civil war, personal dictatorship, or a new state so weak that armed actors determine which civilian decisions count. Independence from an empire does not automatically produce civilian supremacy. One achievement has to be joined to the other.

The most consequential individual act of the decade may therefore have been an act of subtraction.

Washington took the authority everyone could see and removed himself from it.

The state was weak. The army was real. The commander was famous. The grievance was legitimate. The temptation was imaginable.

He walked away.
