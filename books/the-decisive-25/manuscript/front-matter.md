## Dedication

For everyone who has ever argued over a dinner table about who really changed the country.

## A note on the word important

This is not a book about the twenty-five best Americans.

It is not a ranking of virtue. It is not a pantheon. It is not an attempt to decide whose face deserves marble, whose name belongs on a school, or whose private character should be forgiven because history happened to move through him or her.

The word *important* is doing colder work here.

Imagine that every ten years since 1776 is a room with one load-bearing wall. Remove the wrong chair and the room still looks like itself. Remove the wrong painting and almost nothing changes. Remove the wall and the next room is built differently.

I am looking for the wall.

The test is counterfactual. If this person vanished from the decade, how much would the path of the United States change? Would the war end differently? Would a constitutional power arrive later or in another form? Would a market fail to organize? Would a population remain outside the electorate? Would a technology wait? Would an enemy choose another target? Would an institution acquire a different shape? Would the country's vocabulary of itself be altered?

That definition immediately creates trouble, which is useful. It means a destructive person can be more important than a good one. It means an outsider can matter more to the United States than a president. It means a famous achievement can lose to a quiet institutional change whose consequences compound for a century. It means the person selected for a decade may have begun the work earlier or lived long after it. The decade is the hinge, not the biography.

It also means no chapter can honestly claim that one person acted alone. Great-man history is usually bad causal history. Armies contain armies. Movements contain organizers whose names disappeared. Inventions inherit laboratories, suppliers, patents, capital, public infrastructure, and previous failures. Presidents operate inside parties, courts, legislatures, bureaucracies, markets, foreign governments, and events they did not choose. Even dictators discover that other people possess agency.

So the person in each chapter is not offered as the sole cause of a decade. The question is narrower: among all the people inside the system, whose individual choices most changed the system's next available choices?

That is why George Washington opens the book not simply as the commander who helped win a revolution, but as the commander who repeatedly refused to convert military prestige into personal rule. Alexander Hamilton follows because a paper republic with war debts became something closer to a financial state. Then the book leaves the United States altogether. Toussaint Louverture belongs in the American story because the revolution in Saint-Domingue helped break Napoleon's western empire and changed what became possible on the North American continent.

The pattern continues. Some choices will be obvious. Abraham Lincoln is difficult to dislodge from 1856–1865 without performing acrobatics for the sake of novelty. Other choices are meant to be argued with. Does Samuel Morse deserve a decade over James K. Polk? Does J. P. Morgan matter more than John D. Rockefeller? Does Alice Paul outrank Woodrow Wilson in a decade that includes world war? Does the person who attacked the United States belong in a book about people who made it? If Osama bin Laden changed the American security state, foreign policy, military posture, surveillance architecture, and political psychology after September 11, the moral answer and the historical answer cannot be forced to be the same.

The disagreement is part of the book.

Each chapter therefore carries an implicit bench of finalists. The winner is not chosen because the others were unimportant. The choice is a claim about leverage. I want the reader to be able to close a chapter and say, *I see why you chose this person, and I still think you are wrong.* That is a better outcome than producing twenty-five names so blandly canonical that nobody feels the need to argue.

## Twenty-five American decades

The country does not cooperate with tidy calendar math. The Declaration was adopted in 1776, but the 1770s had already begun. The semiquincentennial arrives in 2026, which makes the familiar labels—1780s, 1790s, 1800s—awkward if the goal is to divide 250 years into exactly twenty-five pieces.

So this book uses twenty-five ten-year American intervals beginning in 1776: 1776–1785, 1786–1795, and so on, ending with the long present turn, 2016–2026. The labels are editorial containers, not claims that history resets every ten years. A person can cross the boundary. A cause can mature after the chapter closes. The point of the interval is to force a choice.

The choice must survive four questions.

First: **scale**. How large was the change in territory, law, political participation, economic organization, technology, war, culture, or state capacity?

Second: **persistence**. Did the effect last, compound, or create institutions and habits that later Americans inherited?

Third: **personal leverage**. Was the person merely standing near a transformation that would probably have happened anyway, or did decisions particular to that person alter the timing, direction, or form of the outcome?

Fourth: **counterfactual distance**. If the person disappears, how far does the plausible alternative history move from the one we know?

None of these can be measured with a clean number. History does not provide a control group. But the questions are stricter than fame. They force a chapter to show mechanism rather than reputation.

There is one more rule: consequence is not innocence.

Washington enslaved people. Hamilton's financial architecture distributed gains and losses unevenly and strengthened a government capable of coercion. Andrew Jackson widened democratic participation for many white men while driving Native nations from their lands. Woodrow Wilson, who does not win his decade here, helped build an internationalist language while presiding over segregation in the federal government. The men who industrialized the country also built systems that consumed workers, towns, forests, and competitors. The state that defeated fascism imprisoned Japanese Americans. The movement that expanded formal voting rights did not end voter suppression. The digital revolution made extraordinary tools ordinary and ordinary surveillance extraordinary.

A national history becomes propaganda when every contradiction is polished into a lesson. It becomes cynicism when every achievement is dissolved into its cost. The more difficult task is to keep both in view long enough to understand what actually changed.

That is the wager of these twenty-five lives.

America was never made once. It keeps being made, decade after decade, by people who inherit a structure they did not design and then discover that, for a few years or sometimes a few minutes, more of its weight rests on them than anyone could have predicted.

This is the story of those moments.
