# 1966–1975: Richard Nixon — The Realignment and the Rupture

Richard Nixon left office in disgrace.

That can make it difficult to see how much of the political country survived him.

Watergate dominates memory because it should. A president's campaign organization was connected to a break-in at the Democratic National Committee. The investigation exposed abuses of power, secret payments, obstruction, misuse of government agencies, and a White House effort to treat political survival as a justification for conduct the constitutional system could not accept. When the evidence became impossible to contain, Nixon resigned rather than face near-certain impeachment.

No American president before him had resigned.

That rupture alone would make the decade historic.

But Nixon's deeper importance is that the same presidency helped reorganize American politics, foreign policy, regulatory government, and distrust in ways that did not resign with him.

The decade from 1966 to 1975 begins with the Great Society and civil-rights revolution at high tide, Vietnam escalating, urban uprisings, a rising antiwar movement, Black Power, a conservative backlash, and a Democratic coalition coming apart.

It ends with Saigon falling, Watergate burned into political memory, the presidency weakened by scandal and new statutory restraints, and a Republican Party increasingly positioned to convert white disaffection, anti-liberal reaction, cultural anxiety, and distrust of federal reform into a durable electoral strategy.

Nixon stood in the middle of that transition.

His genius was grievance.

That word is easy to use lazily.

Every coalition contains grievances. Farmers, workers, business owners, racial minorities, religious groups, veterans, taxpayers, activists, immigrants, suburban homeowners, and regional blocs all make political claims rooted partly in the belief that something is being taken, ignored, or disrespected.

Nixon's distinctive skill was identifying groups who felt the country changing around them and telling them that their discomfort represented a political majority.

The “silent majority” was not a demographic fact waiting to be discovered.

It was a political identity waiting to be named.

By 1968, the country had endured the assassinations of Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert Kennedy, riots and uprisings in cities, massive protests over Vietnam, campus unrest, and televised conflict at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago. Lyndon Johnson had declined to seek another full term. George Wallace ran a segregationist third-party campaign and carried five Deep South states.

Nixon promised order.

The phrase “law and order” was not neutral in the context of the period. It spoke to genuine fears about crime and unrest. It also allowed politicians to discuss racial and urban conflict through language that could seem formally race-neutral while resonating with voters angered by civil-rights change, desegregation, riots, or federal intervention.

The later label “Southern Strategy” can become too tidy if it implies one secret memo transformed the parties overnight.

The realignment had deeper roots.

Black voters had been moving toward the Democratic Party since the New Deal era. National Democrats' increasing support for civil rights accelerated white southern movement toward Republicans, though the process varied by state and took decades to mature. Suburbanization, religion, taxation, Cold War politics, economic change, crime, school integration, and cultural conflict all mattered.

Nixon did not cause this rearrangement alone.

He learned how to campaign inside it.

That difference is enough.

Once in office, Nixon governed less ideologically than his campaign rhetoric suggested.

This is one of the reasons he resists simple classification.

He was a conservative Republican who presided over the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency. His administration supported major environmental laws, workplace-safety regulation, and forms of federal intervention that later conservatives would often resist. He proposed versions of health reform and welfare reform that did not fit neatly into the later Republican program. He imposed wage and price controls in 1971, an extraordinary intervention in the economy.

The Nixon presidency shows that political realignment is not the same thing as immediate policy realignment.

Parties inherit institutions before they learn how to change them.

The environmental case is especially revealing.

By the late 1960s, pollution had become a mass political concern. Smog, contaminated waterways, pesticides, oil spills, and highly visible environmental disasters increased pressure for federal action. Rachel Carson's earlier work had helped change public awareness. Earth Day in 1970 demonstrated broad support. Congress was active on a bipartisan basis.

Nixon responded to that environment.

He proposed consolidating federal environmental functions into a new agency, and the Environmental Protection Agency began operating in 1970.

This should not be romanticized into “Nixon the environmentalist.”

He was a politician responding to a changed public agenda.

That is precisely why the institution matters.

A new political demand had become strong enough that even a president elected partly on backlash against liberal disorder incorporated it into government.

Institutions survive when opponents decide they must work through them rather than simply ignore them.

Nixon's foreign policy created another durable transformation.

For two decades after the Chinese Communist victory in 1949, the United States had no formal diplomatic relationship with the People's Republic of China and treated the government on Taiwan as the legitimate government of China. The Korean War, Taiwan Strait crises, ideological hostility, and Cold War alliance structures hardened the separation.

Then the Sino-Soviet split created an opening.

Nixon and his national security adviser Henry Kissinger saw an opportunity to improve the American strategic position by engaging Beijing.

Nixon's anti-Communist reputation helped.

A politician long identified with aggressive Cold War politics could make a move that a liberal president might have found politically harder to defend.

In 1972, Nixon traveled to China and met Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai. The Shanghai Communiqué established principles that would support the later normalization of relations under Jimmy Carter.

The trip did not resolve Taiwan.

It changed the triangle.

The United States could now maneuver between the Soviet Union and China rather than treating international communism as one unified bloc.

The same strategic flexibility shaped détente with the Soviet Union.

Nixon and Kissinger pursued arms-control negotiations and a less purely confrontational relationship with Moscow while continuing to compete globally.

The result was not peace in a sentimental sense.

It was management of rivalry.

That distinction belongs to the nuclear age described in the Oppenheimer chapter.

Once two superpowers can destroy one another, foreign policy becomes partly the art of keeping hostility from becoming automatic escalation.

Nixon was good at that art abroad.

He was terrible at accepting constraint at home.

Vietnam exposes both qualities.

Nixon inherited a war already deeply Americanized under Johnson. He promised to end it, pursued “Vietnamization,” reduced American ground forces, and eventually signed the Paris Peace Accords in 1973. But the path included expanded bombing, secret bombing in Cambodia, the 1970 Cambodian incursion, intensified domestic protest, and immense violence in Southeast Asia.

The war's political management became tangled with secrecy and distrust.

The Pentagon Papers, published beginning in 1971, documented years of government deception across multiple administrations. Nixon's own administration responded with the “Plumbers,” a covert White House unit created to stop leaks. The mindset that enemies were everywhere and normal institutional channels could not be trusted fed directly into later abuses.

This is where Watergate becomes more than a burglary.

The burglary was stupid.

The governing logic was dangerous.

Nixon's White House operated with an expanding theory of political threat. Journalists, protesters, leakers, bureaucrats, Democrats, and internal critics could be treated not simply as opponents but as enemies. Executive secrecy, campaign intelligence, law enforcement, tax authority, and covert operations began to blur.

The presidency possessed tools built for national security.

Nixon's circle increasingly considered whether those tools could serve political security.

That is the rupture.

After five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee offices at the Watergate complex in June 1972, the administration attempted to contain the investigation. Nixon won reelection that November in a landslide.

For a time, the cover-up worked politically.

Then institutions began pulling at the story.

Journalists investigated. The FBI investigated. Federal prosecutors acted. A Senate committee held televised hearings. Former officials testified. Courts issued orders. The existence of a White House taping system became public.

The tapes transformed the evidence.

Nixon fought their release.

The Supreme Court ultimately ruled in *United States v. Nixon* that the president had to comply with a subpoena for relevant recordings in the criminal process. The House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment. Republican support collapsed as the evidence clarified.

Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974.

The constitutional system had survived a confrontation with the president.

That success came at a cost.

Trust did not recover with the resignation.

Watergate combined with Vietnam, the Pentagon Papers, intelligence abuses, inflation, the oil shock, and broader social conflict to deepen public skepticism toward government. Congress enacted reforms dealing with campaign finance, war powers, intelligence oversight, and executive accountability. The presidency entered a period of institutional constraint even as later presidents would rebuild parts of its authority.

The suffix “-gate” became a permanent piece of political language.

That trivial-seeming linguistic inheritance is evidence of something deeper.

Scandal itself became a governing frame.

Every administration after Nixon operated in a media and congressional environment more ready to suspect cover-up, abuse, secret money, and executive deception. Sometimes the suspicion was justified. Sometimes it became theatrical. But the baseline changed.

Nixon helped create the modern politics of distrust by violating trust dramatically enough to make distrust feel prudent.

The paradox is that he also helped create modern conservative political opportunity.

His electoral coalition showed Republicans a route to national power through voters alienated from liberal institutions and rapid social change. The route was not identical to Reagan's later coalition. Nixon still accepted much of the New Deal–Great Society state's basic architecture. He was not a movement conservative in Reagan's sense.

But he changed the map.

He demonstrated that Democrats could lose parts of the white working class, the South, and culturally conservative voters without Republicans abandoning national-scale government immediately.

That bridge matters.

Political eras rarely change in one election.

They change when a new coalition learns which old institutions it can inherit, which constituencies it can recruit, and which grievances it can translate into identity.

Nixon learned quickly.

His opponents also changed.

The antiwar movement radicalized parts of the Democratic coalition. Civil-rights victories made formal segregation politically indefensible at the national level while leaving fierce conflict over schools, housing, policing, affirmative action, and metropolitan inequality. Feminism, gay-rights activism, environmentalism, consumer politics, and youth culture expanded the set of issues parties had to absorb.

The culture-war terrain of later decades was forming.

Nixon did not invent it.

He recognized its electoral potential.

Was he the most consequential person of 1966–1975?

Lyndon Johnson is the strongest rival at the beginning of the interval. His Great Society programs, civil-rights legislation, and escalation in Vietnam shaped everything Nixon inherited. If the decade began two years earlier, Johnson might win.

Henry Kissinger is another rival because he supplied much of the strategic architecture of détente, China opening, and Vietnam diplomacy. But Kissinger's power depended on Nixon's presidency and political authority.

Cesar Chavez transformed labor and consumer activism through the farmworker movement. Gloria Steinem became a major public figure in second-wave feminism. Betty Friedan and the National Organization for Women helped build institutional feminist politics. George Wallace demonstrated the electoral power of racial and cultural backlash. Muhammad Ali changed sport, race, religion, dissent, and celebrity in ways that exceeded boxing. Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein became symbols of investigative journalism, though Watergate was also exposed through prosecutors, congressional investigators, judges, and insiders.

Nixon wins because his fingerprints appear on both sides of the decade's institutional change.

He helped construct a new Republican path to power.

He changed the strategic relationship with China.

He pursued détente.

He signed and administered major regulatory expansion.

He wound down direct U.S. participation in Vietnam while widening the war into Cambodia.

He then abused presidential power so severely that the country rebuilt part of its constitutional immune system around the injury.

Remove Nixon and another Republican may still win in 1968.

The South still realigns gradually. Conservative backlash still grows. Environmental regulation still comes. The Sino-Soviet split still exists. Vietnam still poisons politics. Watergate as we know it does not happen.

That last fact is enough to move the country substantially.

A different president might open China later or differently. A different administration might not create the same combination of secrecy, enemies lists, covert political operations, and taped self-incrimination. Without Watergate, Congress may impose fewer post-Vietnam restraints. The media's relationship with the presidency evolves differently. Public distrust has a different symbol. Gerald Ford never issues a pardon to Nixon because Nixon never resigns.

History does not become cleaner.

It becomes unfamiliar.

Nixon's chapter is ultimately about the danger of confusing electoral legitimacy with personal exemption.

He won enormous elections.

He possessed genuine strategic intelligence.

He could recognize openings abroad that others missed.

He could create agencies that outlived him and coalitions that benefited his party long after he was gone.

None of that protected him from the Constitution.

The presidency can survive a failed policy.

It can survive a lost war.

It can survive unpopularity.

What it cannot safely absorb is the idea that the president's enemies are the country's enemies and therefore any method used against them becomes public service.

Nixon helped realign America.

Then he showed the country where executive power breaks when a president begins to believe the state belongs to him.
