# 1786–1795: Alexander Hamilton — The Debt That Became a Nation

The United States emerged from the Revolution with a problem that victory had not solved.

It owed people money.

It owed foreign governments. It owed soldiers. It owed merchants and contractors. The states owed their own creditors. Congress could make promises, but under the Articles of Confederation it had no dependable national taxing power with which to honor them. It could ask the states for funds. Asking is not the same thing as collecting.

This is the part of a founding story that rarely makes it onto a monument. A country can declare independence in an afternoon. It takes much longer to persuade strangers that its promises are worth cash.

By the middle of the 1780s, the Revolution had produced a sovereign United States without producing a sovereign balance sheet. Public securities traded at discounts. Veterans who needed money could sell claims on future payment for less than their face value. States pursued different fiscal policies. Creditors had different legal claims. The national government could neither make a unified settlement nor convince the world that it possessed a reliable revenue stream behind its obligations.

The weakness was not abstract. Governments that cannot borrow cheaply cannot do much when emergencies arrive. They struggle to pay troops, purchase supplies, build infrastructure, enforce policy, or survive wars that do not wait for legislatures to become fiscally elegant. The Revolution had been financed in part by improvisation. A permanent country could not indefinitely operate as an improvisation.

The Constitution changed the legal architecture. It gave Congress power to tax, borrow, regulate commerce, and legislate with an authority the Confederation had lacked. James Madison therefore has an obvious claim on this decade. Without the constitutional settlement, Alexander Hamilton would have inherited a Treasury Department with no treasury worth the name.

But constitutions contain verbs that someone still has to conjugate.

Hamilton became the first secretary of the Treasury in 1789, at thirty-four years old. The department he took over was new enough that almost every consequential choice could become precedent. He was not merely administering a mature system. He was deciding what the system should do when confronted with the accumulated financial debris of the Revolution.

His answer was audacious because it treated debt not only as a burden to be reduced but as a structure through which national power could be organized.

The first great question concerned the federal debt itself. In his January 1790 report on public credit, Hamilton argued that the new government should honor the obligations it had inherited and establish a credible plan for servicing them. The point was partly moral: promises made in war were obligations of the government that survived the war. The point was also strategic. A government that paid would be able to borrow again. A government that improvised its way out of old promises would pay for that improvisation the next time it needed money.

This was not bookkeeping. It was constitutional engineering by financial means.

Public credit links the future to the present. A government borrows today because a lender believes that future taxpayers, future lawmakers, future institutions, and future political stability will make repayment possible. The interest rate therefore contains a judgment about the state itself. A weak state's debt is expensive because the lender is pricing not only money but uncertainty.

Hamilton wanted to make the United States legible to capital as a continuing political object.

That required more than paying interest. It required deciding whose claims would be recognized, at what value, under which authority, and with what revenue behind them. These decisions immediately produced conflict because every rule shifted wealth.

Many Revolutionary securities had changed hands. A veteran who had sold a certificate at a steep discount might watch a speculator receive full value once federal credit recovered. Critics argued that justice required distinguishing original holders from later purchasers. Hamilton resisted the attempt to reconstruct ownership morally after the fact. The securities, in his view, needed to become reliable property. If the government changed their value according to the biography of the current holder, the very transferability that made a debt market useful would become uncertain.

There was nothing politically neutral about that position. It favored a particular conception of contract, marketability, and national credit. It also meant that the gains from restoring federal promises did not fall evenly on the people who had suffered to acquire those promises in the first place.

The dispute is useful because it prevents a flattering version of Hamilton from taking over the chapter. The same policy could strengthen the state and enrich people who had bought claims from distressed sellers. A government can become more capable while distributing the benefits of capability unevenly.

Then Hamilton made the proposal that transformed the argument from federal bookkeeping into the structure of federalism.

The national government, he argued, should assume much of the states' remaining Revolutionary War debt.

On its face, assumption was a debt-management plan. In political effect, it was far larger. If the federal government became responsible for servicing state war debts, national taxation would acquire a durable justification, state creditors would gain a direct interest in federal solvency, and the financial reputations of the states would become more tightly bound to the reputation of the Union.

Debt could become adhesive.

This was exactly why opponents distrusted it.

Some states had worked harder than others to reduce their obligations. Why should taxpayers in one state help carry obligations accumulated in another? Why should financial power migrate toward a national government that many Americans had accepted only after bitter ratification debates? Why should creditors become a constituency for federal authority? Hamilton's plan did not merely solve a problem created by the Revolution. It changed which level of government would own the solution.

The politics were fierce. Madison, Hamilton's former ally in the campaign for the Constitution, became one of the most important critics. Thomas Jefferson, serving as secretary of state, would later describe a private dinner at which the assumption dispute and the location of the national capital were discussed. The story became famous because it compresses a large constitutional fight into a room small enough to imagine. But the eventual settlement was not a magic bargain made by three men over wine. Congress contained factions, regional interests, creditors, state calculations, and repeated votes. The residence question and assumption became politically linked, but the institutional change was larger than any single dinner.

What mattered was the result. The federal government emerged not only with constitutional powers on parchment but with national obligations that made those powers necessary in practice.

Hamilton then needed revenue.

A credit system without taxes is a story about future default. The federal government adopted tariffs, which became a major source of revenue, and Congress enacted an excise tax on distilled spirits. The whiskey tax has survived in popular memory mostly because farmers hated it and because, a few years later, resistance in western Pennsylvania became the Whiskey Rebellion.

But the tax belongs in the financial story before it belongs in the rebellion story.

It shows the new government learning how to turn constitutional authority into routine extraction from the economy. A tariff is collected at ports. An internal excise reaches farther into domestic life. It requires officers, records, enforcement, legal process, and the political willingness to make people pay even when they believe the tax is unfair.

The country was becoming administrative.

Hamilton's next great proposal made the architecture even more visible: a national bank.

The Bank of the United States was designed as a public-private institution with substantial private capital and a federal relationship. It could hold government funds, facilitate payments, issue notes, extend credit, and create financial infrastructure for a country whose commerce otherwise operated through a thinner and more fragmented banking system. Hamilton's report on the bank was therefore not simply about creating a corporation. It was about whether a national economy could be given institutions commensurate with the national government the Constitution had just created.

The bank also forced an argument that would echo for two centuries: where does federal power end when the Constitution does not list every instrument the government may use?

Jefferson and Madison objected that chartering a bank exceeded enumerated powers. Hamilton answered with a broader reading of implied authority. If the Constitution granted legitimate ends—collecting taxes, borrowing, regulating fiscal operations—the government could choose reasonable means for carrying them out unless the Constitution prohibited those means.

This was not merely a lawyer's quarrel over one corporation. It helped establish two competing ways Americans would read national power. One treated enumerated authority suspiciously, fearing that implied powers could swallow the limits. The other treated the Constitution as a grant of capacities whose execution required practical instruments not individually named in the document.

President Washington signed the bank bill.

That decision mattered, but Hamilton had written the logic that made the signature intellectually possible.

He did not stop there.

His Report on Manufactures argued for a country that would not remain dependent on agriculture alone. Hamilton wanted diversified production, manufacturing capacity, immigration, capital formation, and policies that could accelerate economic development. Much of the report's proposed program was not immediately adopted. Its importance lies partly in the ambition of the state it imagined. The federal government was not merely a night watchman guarding contracts. It could help shape the productive composition of the nation.

That idea, too, would become one of America's permanent arguments.

Hamilton is sometimes presented as though he personally invented modern finance in a nearly empty room. He did not. Britain and the Netherlands already possessed sophisticated public-credit systems. American merchants understood credit. States had banks and debt practices. European financial institutions provided models, warnings, and capital. The Constitution supplied legal authority Hamilton had not created alone. Congress had to enact his proposals. Washington's political legitimacy helped protect a program that might have been easier to destroy under a weaker president.

The question is therefore not whether Hamilton created public credit, banking, or industrial policy from nothing.

The question is whether another American in 1789 was as likely to combine these pieces into the particular national architecture that followed.

This is where personal leverage becomes visible.

Hamilton saw apparently separate problems as one problem. The federal debt, state debts, taxation, the bank, customs administration, manufacturing, and constitutional interpretation could reinforce one another. Federal obligations justified revenue. Revenue made the debt credible. Credible debt improved borrowing. Assumption linked state creditors to national solvency. A bank facilitated government finance and private commerce. A broader interpretation of federal power made the instruments constitutionally usable. The result was not a single policy but a set of mutually supporting institutions.

He was building a machine from dependencies.

Every machine creates pressure at its edges.

In western Pennsylvania, the whiskey excise became the place where fiscal capacity met political resistance. Distilled spirits were not a frivolous luxury to many frontier communities. Whiskey could serve as a marketable product where transport was difficult and grain was bulky. Small producers believed the federal tax burdened them unfairly and favored larger operators. Resistance escalated from evasion and intimidation into organized defiance.

By 1794, the new government faced a question it had not yet answered: could federal law be enforced against substantial domestic resistance without the Union coming apart?

Washington called out a militia force. Hamilton accompanied the expedition part of the way. The rebellion collapsed without a major battle.

The episode is often used as a simple contrast with Shays' Rebellion under the Confederation: weak old government, strong new government. The comparison is real, but the lesson should not be polished too smooth. The Whiskey Rebellion was also evidence that stronger capacity meant stronger coercion. Farmers who considered the tax unjust experienced federal strength not as an abstraction but as collectors, warrants, and armed men.

A state that can pay its creditors can also send troops to enforce its tax laws.

Hamilton understood the two capabilities as related. Later Americans would repeatedly try to separate the benefits of national capacity from the coercive instruments that made capacity real. Sometimes they would succeed. Often they would discover the instruments arrive together.

By the end of 1795, the fiscal landscape of the United States looked different from the one Hamilton had inherited.

The federal government had a funded debt structure, an established Treasury apparatus, national revenues, a bank, a customs service, and a political constituency that took federal credit seriously. The United States had not become financially serene. It still depended on trade, faced international crises, carried debt, and contained violent disagreements over who should bear public costs. Nor had Hamilton's program produced a universally accepted economic constitution. Jeffersonian opposition hardened partly in response to it, helping create the first party system.

That opposition is itself evidence of Hamilton's importance.

Parties form around many things, but one of the earliest durable national divisions in American politics grew around the question of what kind of government Hamilton was building. Was it energetic or dangerous? Commercially modern or aristocratic? Necessary to independence or too reminiscent of the British financial order the Revolution had escaped? The fact that opponents organized to stop the program tells us something about the amount of power they believed the program was moving.

James Madison remains the strongest rival for the decade.

Madison helped design the Constitution, shepherded the Bill of Rights, and articulated a theory of extended republican government that shaped the country's political structure. Remove Madison from the 1780s and the constitutional path becomes less certain. George Washington also belongs in the final round: his support made the new government credible, and his willingness to serve as first president gave the Constitution a figure trusted by people who distrusted one another.

Hamilton wins this decade under the narrower rule of the book because the constitutional framework was only one step in making the United States governable. Someone had to decide what national power would mean in accounts, bonds, taxes, institutions, and the expectations of creditors.

Hamilton filled in that blank with unusual speed.

Imagine a different Treasury secretary: competent, cautious, inclined to retire debt gradually, reluctant to assume state obligations, skeptical of a national bank, determined to avoid a broad theory of implied powers. The Constitution still exists. Congress still taxes. The government still borrows. But the country's financial center of gravity may remain more dispersed among the states. The federal government may develop a weaker creditor constituency. Banking may grow through different institutions. National administrative capacity may accumulate more slowly. The political fight over implied powers may arrive in another form and at another time.

The United States probably does not disappear.

That is an important limit on the counterfactual. Hamilton was not Washington at Valley Forge. The country would have had a government without him.

The question is what sort of government.

Hamilton's answer was a government whose promises could circulate.

That sounds less dramatic than an army crossing a river. But a bond is a political object. Someone buys it because the state is expected to exist next year. A bank note moves because strangers accept an institution they cannot personally inspect. A tax payment arrives because law reaches farther than persuasion. Public credit becomes a mechanism through which confidence in the future can be converted into power in the present.

That mechanism also creates dependency. The state needs revenue. Creditors need the state. Businesses organize around the financial system. Political factions fight over its privileges. Regions measure whether the arrangement benefits them. The machine does not eliminate conflict. It gives conflict new channels.

Hamilton's genius, and his danger, were the same: he understood that institutions become harder to reverse once enough interests depend on them.

He had seen how weak a government could be when it possessed obligations without resources. He wanted the opposite. He wanted obligations that produced resources, resources that produced credibility, credibility that produced options, and options that produced national power.

By 1795, the United States was still a young republic with a fragile party system, unresolved constitutional disputes, vulnerable borders, slavery expanding into its future, and no guarantee that its experiment would endure.

But it had acquired something it had not possessed at independence.

It had a financial reputation worth defending.

The Revolution had created the debt.

Hamilton made the debt help create the nation.
