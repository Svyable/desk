# 1896–1905: Theodore Roosevelt — The Republic Goes Abroad

Theodore Roosevelt did not make the United States an empire.

He inherited one.

That distinction is the beginning of his case, not the end of it.

When Roosevelt became president in September 1901 after William McKinley's assassination, the United States had already fought Spain, annexed Hawaii, taken Puerto Rico and Guam, established control over the Philippines, and asserted a permanent political role in Cuba. American forces were fighting a brutal war against Filipino independence fighters who had expected Spain's defeat to produce their own freedom rather than a new colonial ruler.

The old continental republic had crossed an ocean before Roosevelt entered the White House.

What he did was make the crossing feel like policy instead of accident.

Between 1896 and 1905, Roosevelt helped turn a burst of wartime expansion into a durable theory of American power: the president could act aggressively at home and abroad, the federal government could discipline private corporations without abandoning capitalism, the United States could claim a policing role in the Western Hemisphere, and national resources could be managed as a long-term public interest rather than merely liquidated into private ownership.

The same man who preserved forests also helped normalize intervention.

The contradictions are not side effects. They are the chapter.

The decade began with the election of 1896, one of the great rearrangements in American political history.

William McKinley defeated William Jennings Bryan in a campaign fought over money, industrialization, class, and the gold standard. McKinley represented a Republican coalition closely associated with business, protective tariffs, and urban-industrial growth. Bryan electrified Democrats and Populists with free silver and an attack on creditor power.

McKinley won.

The victory helped establish a Republican national advantage that would shape the next generation. Economic recovery followed. Then foreign crisis overwhelmed domestic monetary politics.

In 1898, war with Spain began amid conflict over Cuba, humanitarian outrage, expansionist pressure, sensational press coverage, strategic calculation, and the explosion of the USS *Maine* in Havana Harbor under circumstances that remain historically distinct from the certainty with which American newspapers initially assigned blame.

The war was short.

Its consequences were not.

American forces defeated Spain in Cuba and the Philippines. The Treaty of Paris transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to U.S. control. Cuba emerged formally independent but under conditions that gave the United States substantial influence. Hawaii was annexed during the same expansionist moment.

A country created through revolt against European empire now governed overseas people without their consent.

The irony was obvious to anti-imperialists at the time.

Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, William James, former political leaders, labor figures, and others opposed annexation of the Philippines and the broader imperial turn for different reasons. Some made principled arguments about self-government. Some mixed anti-imperialism with racism and fear of incorporating nonwhite populations. The debate did not divide neatly into enlightened critics and racist expansionists. Racism ran through both camps in different forms.

Filipinos themselves supplied the central argument through action.

The Philippine-American War began in 1899. Filipino forces under Emilio Aguinaldo and others resisted American rule. The conflict included guerrilla warfare, atrocities, civilian suffering, reconcentration policies, torture, and racialized violence. American officials described colonial rule as tutelage and benevolent assimilation while an army imposed it by force.

Roosevelt supported the imperial project.

Before the war with Spain, he had been assistant secretary of the Navy and an energetic advocate of naval preparedness. He resigned to help organize the volunteer cavalry regiment known as the Rough Riders and returned from Cuba with national fame. His political ascent—from war hero to New York governor to vice president—was inseparable from the expansionist politics of the moment.

When McKinley died, Roosevelt became, at forty-two, the youngest person to assume the presidency.

He treated the office as an instrument waiting to be used.

Roosevelt later articulated a “stewardship” theory: the president could do anything in the public interest not forbidden by the Constitution or law. The formulation captured his temperament. Where earlier presidents might ask for an explicit grant, Roosevelt was inclined to ask whether anything explicitly stopped him.

That instinct appeared first in domestic crisis.

In 1902, anthracite coal miners in Pennsylvania struck for better wages, shorter hours, and union recognition. Coal was essential for heating and industry. As the strike continued toward winter, the possibility of widespread shortage became a national emergency.

Presidents had intervened in labor disputes before, often on the side of employers or through troops used to restore order. Roosevelt tried something different. He brought union and management representatives to Washington and pressured the parties toward arbitration. When mine owners resisted, he considered extraordinary options, including the possibility of federal seizure or operation of the mines.

The eventual settlement did not recognize the union as it wanted, and Roosevelt was not a labor radical. But the episode changed expectations. The president had treated labor and capital as parties whose conflict could create a public interest larger than either side.

Government did not have to be only the policeman of property.

Then Roosevelt went after a railroad combination associated with J. P. Morgan.

Northern Securities had been created as a holding company controlling major northwestern railroads. The concentration brought together interests linked to Morgan and James J. Hill and threatened to consolidate enormous transportation power. Roosevelt's Justice Department filed suit under the Sherman Antitrust Act.

The government won in the Supreme Court in 1904.

Roosevelt became known as a trustbuster, though the label exaggerates his hostility to large enterprise. He did not believe bigness itself was evil. He distinguished between corporations he considered responsible and combinations he believed abused power. His goal was regulation and federal supervision of industrial capitalism, not its dismantling.

This is a key transition from the Morgan decade.

Morgan had supplied private coordination when public institutions were weak.

Roosevelt's administration asserted that private systems large enough to shape national life were legitimate objects of national government.

The argument was not socialist. It was managerial.

Large corporations were here to stay. Therefore public authority had to grow large enough to bargain with them.

The same logic governed conservation.

The nineteenth-century federal government had often treated public land as something to distribute—through homesteads, railroad grants, mining access, timber use, and sale. By the turn of the century, the scale of resource extraction made the assumption of inexhaustibility increasingly difficult to defend.

Roosevelt worked closely with Gifford Pinchot and other conservationists to expand federal forest reserves and professional management of public resources. In 1905, the U.S. Forest Service was created within the Department of Agriculture, with Pinchot as its first chief.

Roosevelt's conservation was not modern environmentalism in every sense.

He was an avid hunter. He favored efficient use as well as preservation. Conservation policy could disregard or constrain Indigenous relationships to lands being reclassified as federal resources. The creation of protected landscapes has often been narrated as saving empty wilderness when those places had Indigenous histories, communities, and management practices.

Still, the federal shift mattered.

The national government was claiming responsibility for Americans not yet born.

A forest could possess public value because leaving it standing, managing its watershed, or regulating its use would matter decades later. This was an assertion of state capacity across time.

Roosevelt made a parallel assertion across space.

Panama is the most revealing example.

The United States wanted a canal linking the Atlantic and Pacific. A canal would shorten naval and commercial routes and increase American power in both oceans. Negotiations with Colombia, which controlled Panama, failed when the Colombian Senate rejected a treaty.

In 1903, Panamanian separatists declared independence.

Roosevelt's administration rapidly recognized the new government. U.S. naval presence helped prevent Colombia from suppressing the revolt. The resulting Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty gave the United States rights to build and control the Panama Canal Zone on terms that many Panamanians would later regard as deeply unequal.

The canal itself was an engineering achievement of global importance.

The method of obtaining the zone demonstrated a new American confidence that strategic infrastructure in the hemisphere could justify extraordinary political intervention.

Roosevelt did not hide the philosophy for long.

In 1904, he announced what became known as the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine. The original Monroe Doctrine had warned European powers against renewed colonization or intervention in the Americas. Roosevelt added a more active proposition: chronic instability or inability to meet international obligations in Latin American countries could justify U.S. intervention to prevent European involvement.

The United States was no longer merely telling Europe to stay out.

It was claiming the right to go in.

The Office of the Historian describes the long-term consequence plainly: the corollary became a justification for U.S. intervention in countries including Cuba, Nicaragua, Haiti, and the Dominican Republic.

This is one of the places where Roosevelt's language of order must be read from outside Washington.

A U.S. official could describe intervention as stabilizing finance, preventing European aggression, or maintaining order. A citizen of the country being occupied could reasonably describe it as imperial control.

Power changes vocabulary depending on where you stand.

Roosevelt's foreign policy also contained genuine diplomacy. He helped mediate the Russo-Japanese War, leading to the Treaty of Portsmouth in 1905 and eventually the Nobel Peace Prize. He sought a balance of power in the Pacific and understood that American security now extended beyond the coastline.

Peace brokerage and coercive regional policing were not opposites in his worldview.

Both were expressions of a country entitled to shape the international environment.

That is the conceptual leap.

McKinley had acquired an empire in war.

Roosevelt supplied a theory for using national power after the acquisition.

This is why McKinley is the strongest rival for the decade and why the choice remains genuinely close.

Remove McKinley and the Spanish-American War may still occur, but the timing, annexations, Philippines policy, and Republican political order could change substantially. McKinley's administration made the decisive choices that turned victory over Spain into overseas possession. He deserves more historical responsibility for American empire than narratives focused on Roosevelt's personality often grant him.

William Jennings Bryan is another finalist because his 1896 campaign transformed Democratic politics and the language of economic populism even in defeat. W. E. B. Du Bois, whose *The Souls of Black Folk* appeared in 1903, became a central intellectual and organizational figure in the fight over race, citizenship, and the failures of the post-Reconstruction order.

Roosevelt wins by persistence across domains.

He changed the expectations surrounding the presidency itself.

A president could mediate a strike.

A president could direct antitrust enforcement against a giant holding company.

A president could treat conservation as national management.

A president could facilitate a new country in order to obtain a canal route.

A president could claim policing authority across a hemisphere.

A president could become the dominant narrator of national life through publicity, travel, speeches, and deliberately staged action.

Modern presidential power was acquiring its theater.

Roosevelt was exceptionally good at theater because he understood that visibility creates political capacity. A dramatic inspection, a publicized arbitration, a trust suit, a conservation trip, a naval gesture—each could turn an administrative choice into a national story about energetic government.

His personality became part of the office.

That had a democratic appeal. Voters could see a president acting rather than waiting.

It also carried a constitutional risk. An energetic executive can treat obstacles as evidence of insufficient energy rather than as restraints with their own legitimacy.

Roosevelt's stewardship theory is attractive when the president protects a forest or confronts corporate power. It becomes more troubling when the same self-confidence operates in another country's sovereignty.

The office cannot be given only the examples we admire.

That is the chapter's deepest connection between domestic reform and foreign expansion.

Roosevelt believed in national capacity.

He believed government should be strong enough to discipline private concentrations of power at home and foreign disorder abroad. He believed expertise and executive action could solve problems that older institutions handled too slowly. He believed the United States had grown into a great power and should behave like one.

The benefits and dangers came from the same source.

Remove Roosevelt and the United States remains industrial, expansionist, and increasingly global. McKinley's empire does not disappear. The canal still has overwhelming strategic logic. Antitrust law still exists. Conservation movements still grow. The presidency still expands over the twentieth century.

But the model loses its most vivid early operator.

A more cautious president might have relied more on Congress, moved more slowly against trusts, intervened less personally in labor disputes, treated Latin American sovereignty with greater restraint, or lacked the political energy to make conservation a central federal project.

The United States would still go abroad.

Roosevelt helped decide what kind of country would arrive when it got there.

It would call itself a republic.

It would speak the language of order, civilization, stewardship, and public interest.

It would regulate corporations, preserve forests, build a canal, mediate a war, and reserve the right to intervene in its neighbors.

The contradictions were not temporary.

They became part of American power.
