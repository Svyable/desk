# 2006–2015: Steve Jobs — The Country Moves Into the Phone

The smartphone did not invent distraction.

It industrialized access to it.

It also industrialized access to almost everything else.

A map.

A camera.

A newspaper.

A bank.

A taxi dispatcher.

A television.

A music library.

A workplace.

A classroom.

A dating service.

A storefront.

A protest network.

A family group chat.

A political rally.

A million strangers.

By the end of the decade from 2006 to 2015, Americans increasingly carried all of these institutions in one object.

Steve Jobs did not invent the smartphone.

That sentence matters as much here as the same sentence did with Henry Ford and the automobile.

IBM had demonstrated an early smartphone in the 1990s. BlackBerry made mobile email indispensable to executives. Palm built personal digital assistants. Nokia dominated global mobile phones. Microsoft developed mobile operating systems. Cellular networks, semiconductor advances, lithium-ion batteries, capacitive touchscreens, digital cameras, GPS, web standards, wireless chips, and countless component suppliers made the modern device possible.

Nor did Jobs build the iPhone alone.

Apple's engineers, designers, software teams, operations staff, component suppliers, wireless partners, and manufacturing network turned a demanding product vision into hardware shipped at global scale. Jonathan Ive and his design team were central. Scott Forstall led important software work. Tony Fadell had helped make the iPod and participated in early iPhone development. Tim Cook built much of the operational machine that could source and manufacture Apple's products at extraordinary scale.

The iPhone was a system achievement.

Jobs's leverage was choosing what the system should become before consumers had fully articulated the demand.

That is a rare kind of power.

Most successful companies become excellent at asking customers what they want.

Jobs's Apple often tried to notice what customers were tolerating.

In 2006, mobile phones were already widespread in the United States. People texted, called, took basic photos, checked email on some devices, and accessed stripped-down web services. BlackBerry's physical keyboard had become an icon of professional mobile communication.

The problem was fragmentation.

A phone did some things.

An iPod did music.

A digital camera did photos better.

A laptop did the real internet.

A GPS unit handled driving directions.

A desktop computer remained the place where digital life became fully capable.

Apple's 2007 iPhone launch attacked that separation.

The company presented the device as three things at once: a phone, an iPod, and an internet communicator.

The deeper move was that the categories began collapsing after the announcement.

The phone was no longer one device among several.

It was becoming the device into which other devices would disappear.

That is why the touchscreen mattered.

Physical buttons are excellent when the task is stable.

A keyboard stays a keyboard.

A number pad stays a number pad.

A touchscreen can become whatever the software requires.

The interface therefore became programmable.

When an application opened, the device could redraw itself around the application's needs.

That gave software much more control over the physical experience of the machine.

The phone became a shape-shifter.

Jobs's insistence on a large multi-touch display, finger-driven interface, and full web browser helped turn this possibility into a consumer expectation.

Again, none of the underlying ideas was completely unprecedented.

The achievement was integration at the right moment.

A technology becomes transformative when several curves cross.

Processors become fast enough.

Screens become good enough.

Battery life becomes tolerable.

Networks become fast enough.

Manufacturing becomes scalable.

Software becomes flexible enough.

Consumers become accustomed enough to digital life.

Then one product assembles the pieces into a form that makes the old categories look clumsy.

That was the iPhone's historical moment.

The first version was limited by later standards. It lacked many features users would soon consider basic. Its carrier arrangement in the United States constrained adoption. It was expensive. Mobile web speeds were slow. The App Store did not launch until 2008.

The App Store may have mattered even more than the original device.

The first iPhone was a product.

The App Store helped turn it into a platform.

Third-party developers could build software for a standardized pocket computer with sensors, connectivity, a touchscreen, and an expanding installed base. Consumers could discover and install applications through a central marketplace. Payment and distribution became easier. Small software teams could reach millions of users without negotiating shelf space or physical distribution.

The phone became an economy.

This is where Jobs's chapter connects directly to Gates's.

Microsoft had shown how valuable it could be to own the layer where other people's software runs.

Apple extended that lesson into a more controlled system.

It owned the device, operating system, application marketplace, and crucial rules governing distribution.

The integration produced extraordinary consistency and security benefits.

It also gave Apple private regulatory power over developers.

Which apps could be distributed?

What payment systems could they use?

What percentage would Apple collect?

What kinds of content were acceptable?

What access could apps have to device functions?

These were not merely product decisions.

As the platform became infrastructure, they became market-governance decisions.

The smartphone age concentrated private rulemaking inside a consumer object.

Google's Android provided the great counter-model.

Android spread across many hardware manufacturers and became the other dominant smartphone platform. Its existence is crucial because Jobs did not monopolize the smartphone revolution. Samsung, Google, Qualcomm, carriers, Chinese manufacturers, and a vast global supply chain shaped the market.

If Jobs disappears, smartphones still arrive.

That is the strongest objection to his selection.

The technology was too ripe.

What may change is the form and timing of mass adoption.

Before the iPhone, mobile devices were often designed around the assumptions of phone companies, enterprise email, physical keyboards, styluses, or simplified mobile web experiences.

Apple made the full internet in a touch-first pocket computer the reference point.

Competitors adjusted rapidly.

The direction of the industry changed because consumers had seen a new baseline.

That is acceleration through expectation.

Once people experience the better interface, the old one becomes older faster.

Pew's surveys captured the social shift.

By 2013, more than half of American adults owned smartphones. The iPhone and Android had become the dominant platforms. By 2014, Pew described the smartphone as central to the leap in mobile internet access.

Those adoption numbers matter less as gadget history than as institutional history.

When more than half the adult population carries a networked computer, every institution starts redesigning itself around that fact.

Retail changes.

A store competes with the phone in the shopper's hand.

Media changes.

The morning newspaper becomes a continuous feed.

Photography changes.

The camera is always present, and billions of ordinary moments become documents.

Transportation changes.

A ride can be requested, priced, tracked, and paid for through software.

Dating changes.

Location, profiles, and matching move courtship into platforms.

Banking changes.

Balances, transfers, checks, markets, and payments move into pockets.

Work changes.

Email never quite leaves.

Politics changes.

A speech can be recorded by anyone. A protest can organize through group messaging and social platforms. A lie can travel at the same speed as a fact. A candidate can bypass news organizations and reach supporters directly.

Family life changes.

The person in another state becomes constantly available.

The person across the dinner table becomes constantly interruptible.

The same infrastructure expands connection and competes with presence.

That contradiction defines the smartphone age.

The device made many things easier.

It made not using them harder.

A technology becomes socially compulsory long before any law requires it.

Employers expect messages.

Schools distribute information digitally.

Restaurants move menus online.

Banks reduce branches.

Government agencies assume internet access.

Friends coordinate through apps.

Tickets become QR codes.

Two-factor authentication arrives by phone.

The object that begins as convenience becomes the key to systems that increasingly assume ownership.

That is why the phone is not merely a consumer product.

It is a citizenship layer.

People without reliable smartphones or data plans can face higher friction in ordinary life.

The digital divide therefore changes form.

Access is not simply whether a household has an internet connection.

It is the quality of device, data, speed, literacy, privacy, and compatibility available to the person expected to live through digital systems.

Jobs did not design these inequalities.

The platform he helped accelerate made them more consequential.

The phone also transformed attention into a market.

This development cannot be credited to Apple alone. Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, Google, advertising networks, game developers, app makers, and later TikTok built business models around engagement. Notification systems, infinite feeds, recommendation algorithms, likes, streaks, badges, autoplay, and personalized content competed for time.

The smartphone supplied the perfect delivery system because it was physically near the user almost all day.

The television had to wait for you to sit down.

The phone came with you.

That changes the economics of persuasion.

An advertiser no longer buys only a place in a magazine or a thirty-second slot in a program.

Platforms can observe behavior, infer interest, personalize content, test responses, and deliver messages repeatedly to individuals.

Political campaigns can do the same.

The next chapter on Donald Trump is difficult to imagine without this infrastructure.

The permanent campaign requires a permanent screen.

Jobs's decade therefore changes not only technology but the conditions of politics.

The smartphone reduces the distance between public event and personal reaction.

A police shooting can be recorded and distributed before officials release a statement.

A rumor can become national before verification catches up.

A president can provoke millions of responses with one post.

A citizen can participate directly in public conversation and also become vulnerable to manipulation at unprecedented scale.

The gatekeepers weaken.

The platforms strengthen.

That is not automatically democratization.

Removing one kind of intermediary often creates another.

The newspaper editor loses power.

The recommendation algorithm gains it.

The television scheduler loses power.

The app store and feed gain it.

The local retailer loses some power.

The marketplace platform gains it.

The cab dispatcher loses power.

The ride-hailing platform gains it.

Digitization does not abolish gates.

It relocates them.

Jobs's personal style made this relocation look like simplicity.

Apple was famous for removing visible complexity. Few ports. Few buttons. Few product lines. Controlled interfaces. Carefully staged launches. Technology was presented not as machinery that required expertise but as something intuitive enough to disappear into experience.

This was more than aesthetics.

Ease of use expands markets.

A computer that requires configuration belongs to enthusiasts.

A device that appears to explain itself can reach everyone.

That was Jobs's gift as much as product judgment: he understood that adoption is often limited by embarrassment and friction as much as capability.

People do not want to feel stupid in front of their tools.

Apple made advanced computing feel less like computing.

The cost was control.

When a company hides complexity, it also decides which complexity the user is allowed to access.

Power users complained about closed systems, locked-down software, limited customization, nonreplaceable components, and Apple's control over distribution. The same design discipline that made devices approachable could make them restrictive.

Again, the benefit and cost came from the same source.

Integration.

This pattern appears repeatedly in the book.

Hamilton's financial system made national credit possible by centralizing assumptions.

Marshall's Court made constitutional rules more national by strengthening judicial authority.

Edison's electrical system made light useful by integrating generation and distribution.

Gates made computing easier by standardizing the desktop.

Jobs made mobile computing easier by controlling the stack.

Coordination creates value.

Whoever supplies coordination acquires power.

Who else could win 2006–2015?

Barack Obama is the strongest political rival. He became the first Black president, responded to the global financial crisis, enacted the Affordable Care Act, transformed the Democratic coalition, used executive authority aggressively, and represented a profound symbolic change in American political life. The financial crisis itself also makes Federal Reserve chair Ben Bernanke a plausible institutional choice; his crisis response helped prevent a collapse of the financial system on a scale that could have produced a second Great Depression.

Mark Zuckerberg changed social life and political communication through Facebook. Larry Page and Sergey Brin made Google central to information and advertising. Jeff Bezos changed retail, logistics, cloud computing, and corporate scale. Elon Musk helped make electric vehicles and private spaceflight commercially significant, though his larger political and cultural leverage comes later. Satoshi Nakamoto, an unidentified person or group, created Bitcoin and launched a new monetary and technological movement.

Jobs wins because the phone became the interface through which many of these other transformations reached ordinary people.

Facebook mattered more because it was in the pocket.

Google mattered more because search and maps were in the pocket.

Amazon mattered more because the store was in the pocket.

Obama's campaign innovations mattered more because supporters and media lived through networked devices.

Uber, Instagram, WhatsApp, mobile banking, streaming, and countless later services depended on the fact that a capable, sensor-rich, always-connected computer had become ordinary.

The iPhone was not alone in making that happen.

It made the form legible.

Remove Jobs and Apple may still produce phones under another leader. The iPod had already built mobile-device expertise. Competitors were moving toward touchscreens. Android still emerges. Mobile internet still expands as networks improve.

But product timing can redirect ecosystems.

A weaker first iPhone delays the App Store. A more carrier-controlled design preserves telecom gatekeeping longer. A less convincing touchscreen slows abandonment of keyboards. A fragmented application market delays developer concentration around mobile platforms. A less ambitious Apple gives Google, Microsoft, Nokia, BlackBerry, or Samsung more time to define the standard.

The smartphone revolution still comes.

America gets there by another road.

The result may be more open.

It may be more fragmented.

It may be less elegant.

It may also avoid some of the private platform power Apple helped normalize—or merely place that power in someone else's hands.

The counterfactual is uncertain.

The transformation is not.

By 2015, the phone was no longer a phone.

It was where the country kept moving.
