# Evidence Ledger

## Class 1 — verified / documented / strongly replicated

- Many animal taxa use geomagnetic information in orientation or navigation under controlled conditions.
- Migratory birds possess a light-dependent magnetic compass with characteristic behavioral constraints.
- Radical-pair chemistry can be magnetically sensitive in weak fields under appropriate conditions.
- European-robin CRY4 exhibits magnetic-sensitive photochemistry in vitro; mutations identify an electron-transfer chain relevant to the effect.
- Magnetotactic bacteria biologically construct magnetite chains that couple cells to magnetic fields.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- CRY4 is a leading candidate for the avian radical-pair receptor but has not been closed from molecule through neural circuit to migratory behavior.
- Specific RF noise bands can disrupt avian magnetic orientation; cross-laboratory mechanistic details still need standardized replication.
- Some vertebrates may employ more than one magnetic receptor mechanism for compass versus map functions.
- Earth-strength field rotations may evoke repeatable human EEG responses.

## Class 3 — disputed / unconfirmed

- A particular iron-rich structure in the pigeon beak is the definitive magnetite receptor.
- Humans possess a consciously accessible magnetic compass.
- One specific radical pair and one cryptochrome isoform fully explain avian magnetoreception.
- Everyday electromagnetic exposure produces broad human pathology through magnetoreception mechanisms.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis / open conjecture

- Navigation systems may routinely combine a radical-pair directional compass with a ferromagnetic intensity/polarity map sensor.
- Electromagnetic pollution may be an ecologically important sensory pollutant for some migratory species.
- Comparative evolution of cryptochrome magnetic sensitivity may map quantitatively onto migratory ecology.
- Humans may retain a vestigial magnetosensory pathway without conscious magnetic qualia.
