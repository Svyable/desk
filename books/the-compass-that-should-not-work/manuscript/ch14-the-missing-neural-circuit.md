# The Missing Neural Circuit

A receptor mechanism is not a sense until the nervous system reads it.

That sentence is where a great deal of magnetoreception writing becomes less satisfying than it first appears. The physics can be elegant. A radical pair can be magnetically sensitive. A cryptochrome can show field-dependent chemistry in vitro. A bird can orient under one magnetic condition and fail under another. Those facts matter. They still leave an entire animal between the molecule and the migratory decision.

Vision became a mature sensory science because the chain closes. Photons alter retinal molecules. Photoreceptors change membrane potentials. Retinal circuits transform the signal. Axons project to identifiable brain targets. Neurons encode edges, motion, color and spatial relationships. Lesions and genetic defects produce specific perceptual deficits. The organism's behavior can be connected back through the chain.

Magnetoreception is not there yet.

The encouraging part is that the gap is narrower than the phrase **mystery sense** suggests.

One of the most important anatomical results arrived in 2007, when Dominik Heyers and colleagues traced a pathway linking the retina of migratory birds to a forebrain region called Cluster N through the thalamofugal visual pathway. The paper combined neuronal tracing with activity-dependent ZENK expression during magnetic orientation. It did not identify a magnetoreceptor molecule. What it did was less glamorous and more useful: it placed two structures already associated with magnetic-compass behavior inside an ordinary visual circuit. Source: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0000937

That result changes the form of the question.

If a light-dependent magnetic compass is real, the signal does not necessarily travel through a dedicated nerve labeled **magnetic**. It may enter through visual hardware that already exists. The receptor event could modulate a retinal signal before the brain ever has a chance to call it anything separate from vision.

This is the origin of the phrase that birds may in some sense “see” the magnetic field. The phrase is memorable enough to become dangerous. No experiment has interviewed a robin about phenomenology. The visual-pathway evidence supports a neural architecture in which magnetic information is processed inside visual circuitry. It does not tell us whether a bird experiences a dim pattern, a color shift, a moving texture, a change in contrast, no conscious sensation at all, or something with no human analog.

The circuit nevertheless gives the radical-pair hypothesis a place to live.

The 2007 tracing work showed that retinal input and Cluster N are linked through restricted regions of the visual thalamus. Cluster N itself is part of the visual wulst, a forebrain structure involved in visual processing. The activation pattern was especially strong in night-migratory songbirds under conditions associated with magnetic orientation. That matters because a sensory theory gains credibility when anatomy, ecology and behavior point toward the same subsystem.

Then came a harder test.

In 2009 Manuela Zapka and colleagues lesioned Cluster N in European robins. The birds could still orient using star and sun compasses, but they lost the ability to perform normal magnetic-compass orientation. Cutting the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve, by contrast, did not abolish the compass behavior tested in that experiment. The paper's title stated the result plainly: **“Visual but not trigeminal mediation of magnetic compass information in a migratory bird.”** Source: https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08528

That is much stronger than an activation map.

An activation map can show that a region participates in a task without proving it is necessary. A lesion adds necessity. The bird retains other orientation systems while the magnetic compass fails. That pattern makes Cluster N difficult to dismiss as generic arousal, locomotion or night vision alone.

But it still does not close the receptor-to-neuron chain.

Cluster N is downstream. A visual thalamic pathway is downstream. Even a retinal requirement is downstream if the specific magnetically sensitive cell and molecule are unknown. The crucial missing step is still the first biological transduction event: which retinal cell changes its output when the geomagnetic field changes, by what molecular reaction, and with what sign and magnitude?

Cryptochrome 4 is a leading molecular candidate because purified European-robin CRY4 shows magnetic sensitivity under laboratory conditions and because cryptochromes occur in avian retina. That is important molecular evidence. It is not yet the equivalent of identifying rhodopsin in a rod cell and tracing its downstream response.

The difficulty is that **expression is not transduction**.

A protein can be present in the correct organ, at the correct time, with the correct chemistry, and still fail to be the receptor that matters in vivo. The cell has to orient the protein appropriately. The radical-pair lifetime has to fall into the right range. The reaction products have to influence a signaling pathway. The resulting change must be large enough, after noise, to alter neural output. The circuit must preserve the directional information rather than average it away.

Each step can fail while the previous step remains true.

There are hints at the retinal level. Work on European-robin retina has reported magnetic-field-dependent changes in electroretinogram responses under blue-light stimulation, with effects that were not spatially uniform across the retina. A 2022 paper described stronger effects in the nasal retinal quadrant and argued that retinal non-homogeneity could matter for magnetoreception. This kind of result is useful because a radical-pair compass almost certainly needs anatomical structure. A completely isotropic soup of freely tumbling magnetosensitive molecules would not automatically encode field direction for the animal. Source: https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4409/11/19/3056

The field should treat that evidence as a clue, not a finished map. Electroretinography measures aggregate electrical responses. It does not identify the exact cell type producing the magnetic modulation, and spatial asymmetry can arise for many reasons unrelated to a dedicated compass architecture. Still, it moves the problem from pure chemistry toward physiology.

The most satisfying experiment would make the chain causal at every level.

Start with a candidate retinal cell expressing CRY4. Measure its output while rotating an Earth-strength magnetic field under tightly controlled light. Then perturb CRY4 specifically in that cell—ideally with a manipulation that changes the predicted radical chemistry without broadly damaging vision. The magnetic modulation should disappear. Restore the functional molecule and it should return.

That is only the first floor.

The same experiment should record from the next circuit element in the visual thalamus and from Cluster N. The neural response should transform in a predictable way as the field rotates. If the receptor encodes angle as a spatial modulation across the retina, the downstream code should preserve enough of that geometry to recover orientation. If the encoding is based on intensity or some other parameter, the neural representation should say so.

Finally, the animal should behave.

Molecular perturbation, retinal physiology, central neural coding and orientation should fail together and recover together.

That is the standard the field should want because it is difficult to fake with a single hidden confound.

The trigeminal system complicates the story in a productive way.

The 2009 Cluster N lesion study showed that the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve was not required for the light-dependent magnetic compass behavior measured in European robins. That finding is sometimes simplified into “the trigeminal hypothesis was disproved.” It was not.

In 2010 Henrik Mouritsen and colleagues reported that changes in magnetic field activated neurons in the trigeminal brainstem complex of European robins and that sectioning the ophthalmic branch V1 reduced that activation. The result suggests that V1 carries some kind of magnetic information to the brain. Source: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0907068107

Put the two findings together and a more interesting architecture appears.

The visual/Cluster N system may mediate a magnetic **compass**—direction relative to field geometry—while trigeminal pathways may carry other magnetic variables useful for a **map**, such as intensity, gradients or local anomalies. That division is not proven, but it is biologically plausible and consistent with the broader distinction between compass and map functions discussed earlier in this book.

This is a good example of why the field's old mechanism wars were too simple.

Radical pairs and magnetite do not have to be mutually exclusive explanations for one monolithic “magnetic sense.” A migrating animal has several navigation problems. It needs heading, position, calibration, error correction and context. Evolution can assign different physical detectors to different tasks.

The nervous system is where those tasks must eventually meet.

A bird leaving northern Europe for Africa does not navigate by a molecular reaction. It navigates by integrating multiple signals over time: stars, sun, polarized light, odors, landmarks, wind, geomagnetic direction and perhaps geomagnetic map information. The neural problem is therefore not only **where is magnetic information encoded?** It is **where is magnetic information compared with everything else?**

Cluster N may be close to the compass side of that integration, but it is unlikely to be the whole navigation system. Lesion results show necessity for one kind of orientation behavior, not ownership of migration.

This distinction also protects the field from a familiar mistake in neuroscience: locating a function because a region lights up.

A brain area can be necessary for a task because it supplies one representation among many. A region can also be activated because the task recruits attention, memory or sensory conflict. Immediate-early-gene maps such as ZENK are powerful precisely because they reveal candidate circuits during behavior, but they are not a substitute for cell-type-specific recording and perturbation.

The modern toolkit makes those stronger experiments imaginable.

Single-cell transcriptomics could identify rare retinal cell populations enriched for candidate cryptochromes and relevant signaling partners. Spatial transcriptomics could show whether those cells have the orientation and topography a directional compass would require. Viral tracing could map their downstream projections. Calcium imaging or high-density electrophysiology could ask whether neural populations encode field azimuth, inclination or intensity. Genome editing could alter specific radical-pair-relevant residues rather than deleting an entire protein with many other functions.

The last point is essential.

A crude knockout can create a persuasive failure for the wrong reason. Cryptochromes are biologically busy proteins. If a manipulation disrupts circadian regulation, development, visual function or cell health, a navigation deficit proves little about magnetoreception. The ideal perturbation changes the magnetic chemistry while leaving the rest of the protein's work intact.

That is why the 2021 CRY4 biophysics matters beyond showing sensitivity. Structural and kinetic work can identify residues and radical pathways whose alteration should change magnetic response in a specific direction. Those predictions can be exported into the animal. A good molecular theory gives neurobiology something falsifiable to perturb.

The same logic applies to radiofrequency disruption.

Behavioral experiments showing that weak broadband electromagnetic noise can disrupt magnetic orientation are often cited as support for a radical-pair mechanism. If the effect is truly acting on spin chemistry, then a neural circuit downstream of the receptor should lose its magnetic tuning under the same RF conditions while ordinary visual responses remain intact. That is a much sharper test than asking only whether the bird points north.

The missing circuit can therefore organize several literatures that currently sit beside one another.

- **Behavior** says the bird has a magnetic compass.
- **Spin chemistry** says weak fields can alter radical-pair reaction outcomes.
- **CRY4 biophysics** supplies a plausible magnetically sensitive molecule.
- **Anatomy** links retina to a magnetically relevant forebrain region through a visual pathway.
- **Lesions** show Cluster N is necessary for magnetic compass orientation under tested conditions.
- **Trigeminal activation** suggests a second magnetic channel may carry different information.

The gaps are now specific.

Which retinal cell? Which cryptochrome or molecular complex? What photochemical state? What transduction partner? What firing-rate or population code? What central integration site? Which variables belong to the compass and which to the map?

This is no longer a vague mystery. It is a list of missing wires.

That is scientific progress even before the final mechanism is known.

There is a second reason to insist on the neural circuit: it prevents **quantum** from becoming an explanatory endpoint.

Suppose a radical-pair reaction is conclusively shown to change yield by a tiny fraction under Earth-strength fields. That would be beautiful physics. It would still not establish a sense. Biological amplification has to turn that difference into a robust neural signal while the retina is simultaneously processing real photons, eye movements, visual scenes, metabolic noise and temperature fluctuations.

The nervous system is not an afterthought bolted onto quantum biology. It is the test of whether the quantum effect matters to the animal.

The field's most convincing future paper may therefore look less exotic than the headlines expect. It may contain no new quantum phenomenon at all. It may identify a retinal cell, a synapse, a thalamic projection and a population of neurons whose tuning rotates with the field. It may show that a single targeted molecular change erases the tuning and that rescue restores both neural response and migratory orientation.

That would be enough.

The missing circuit is not an embarrassment to be hidden behind the phrase **magnetic sense**.

It is the part of the problem now sharp enough to solve.
