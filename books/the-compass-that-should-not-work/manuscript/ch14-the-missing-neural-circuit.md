# The Missing Neural Circuit

A receptor mechanism is not a sense until the nervous system reads it.

This is where magnetoreception remains frustratingly incomplete. Candidate cryptochromes can be found in retinae. Magnetic-sensitive photochemistry can be measured in vitro. Neural activation patterns associated with magnetic stimulation have been reported in birds and other animals. Trigeminal pathways have been implicated in some magnetic behaviors. Yet the clean receptor-to-neuron chain familiar from vision or hearing remains unfinished.

For a radical-pair compass, the encoding problem is especially interesting. If molecular orientation in retinal cells modulates reaction yield as a function of field direction, the animal might receive a spatial pattern superimposed on vision—a magnetic modulation rather than a separate compass dial. Models have imagined such visual patterns, but subjective reconstructions should not be mistaken for demonstrated avian experience.

The anatomical program is straightforward to state and hard to execute. Identify the receptor cell. Manipulate the candidate molecule without wrecking unrelated function. Record a neural signal that changes with magnetic field according to the molecular prediction. Trace the circuit. Show that targeted disruption abolishes the relevant behavior and rescue restores it.

Genetic tools may finally make that sequence possible in tractable species, though migratory birds are not the easiest experimental organisms. Comparative work can help: if a magnetic receptor architecture is conserved, model systems may expose the circuit even when their ecology differs.

The missing circuit is not an embarrassment to be hidden. It is the scientific opportunity. Fields mature when mysteries become bottlenecks specific enough that a graduate student can attack one synapse.
