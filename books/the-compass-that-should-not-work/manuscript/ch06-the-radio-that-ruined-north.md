# The Radio That Ruined North

The most theatrical magnetoreception experiment involves no magnet strong enough to impress anyone. It involves electromagnetic noise.

At Oldenburg, European robins tested in unscreened wooden huts failed to orient magnetically under the local anthropogenic electromagnetic environment. When the huts were electrically grounded and shielded with aluminum, magnetic orientation returned. Remove the grounding or deliberately add broadband noise, and the compass failed again. The reported disruptive band extended through low radiofrequencies, and the tests were conducted double blind.

This finding matters because weak radiofrequency disruption was predicted as a discriminating feature of spin-chemical magnetoreception. A tiny oscillating field can perturb spin dynamics even when it lacks the energy to heat tissue appreciably. The effect therefore sits in a peculiar evidentiary position: behavior appears vulnerable to a physically weak environmental perturbation whose relevance is more natural under a radical-pair model than under an ordinary magnetic torque picture.

It is not a free pass. Radiofrequency experiments in animal behavior are difficult, and the broader literature contains failures and disagreements about frequency, intensity and reproducibility. The right response is to treat the Oldenburg work as a high-value constraint that deserves standardized multicenter replication.

The urban-noise result also raises an ecological question almost nobody planned to ask. If migratory animals use a quantum-sensitive compass, technological environments might interfere with a sensory modality we barely recognize. That is not the same as saying everyday radio exposure is dangerous to humans. It is a species-specific sensory question, and field intensities below thermal safety thresholds can still matter to a specialized receptor if the mechanism is nonthermal.

This distinction is worth defending. Bioelectromagnetics is crowded with overclaiming. A demonstrated behavioral perturbation in robins cannot be generalized into vague claims that all radiofrequency radiation causes biological harm. Mechanism, dose, frequency, waveform and organism matter.

The better move is narrower and stranger: electromagnetic pollution may include forms of sensory pollution. We already accept light pollution and acoustic pollution because we share those senses. Magnetic pollution feels less intuitive only because our own experience provides no obvious magnetic qualia.
