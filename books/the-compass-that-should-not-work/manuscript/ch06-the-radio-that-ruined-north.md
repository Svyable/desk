# The Radio That Ruined North

The experiment began by refusing to work.

Between 2004 and 2006, European robins tested in wooden huts on the University of Oldenburg campus would not do the thing decades of migratory-bird experiments said they should do. In darkness, deprived of the celestial cues they normally use alongside magnetism, the birds failed to orient in their expected migratory direction. The local geomagnetic field was there. The birds were healthy. The classic funnel apparatus was familiar. North had apparently gone missing.

The useful clue was embarrassingly architectural. The researchers lined the huts with aluminum sheeting and grounded it. That did little to the static geomagnetic field but strongly attenuated the time-varying electromagnetic clutter inside. The robins' magnetic orientation returned. Disconnect the grounding and the compass failed again. Add broadband electromagnetic noise inside the screened hut and it failed again. Move the experiment to a rural site outside Oldenburg, where the ambient noise was much lower, and the birds could orient without the shield.

By the time Svenja Engels and colleagues published the work in *Nature* in 2014, the accidental nuisance had become the independent variable. Their grounded screens reduced noise from roughly 50 kHz to 5 MHz by about two orders of magnitude, and the final tests were run double blind. That is a much stranger story than "radio waves confuse birds." A piece of laboratory infrastructure had been quietly deciding whether a known sensory behavior appeared to exist.

It was also tempting to make the result cleaner than it was. Radical-pair models had given physicists a reason to care about weak oscillating fields: spin dynamics can in principle be perturbed by radiofrequency fields far too weak to heat tissue. Earlier bird experiments had reported disruption near frequencies associated with electron-spin precession, which looked attractively mechanism-specific. The Oldenburg broadband result seemed to join that picture.

Then the frequency story became awkward.

In 2016 Susanne Schwarze and colleagues moved the robins into a purpose-built electromagnetically quiet laboratory and compared weak broadband noise, spanning roughly 2 kHz to 9 MHz, with several substantially stronger narrow-band fields. The narrow-band tests included the free-electron Larmor frequency in the local geomagnetic field, twice that frequency, 1.315 MHz, and 50 Hz. None of those stronger narrow-band fields disrupted the birds' compass under their conditions. The much weaker broadband noise did.

That does not erase the radical-pair hypothesis. It makes the easy version of the story harder. A narrow resonance at one theoretically attractive frequency would have been a lovely fingerprint. Broadband susceptibility is less obliging. It leaves questions about which frequencies matter, whether several frequencies act together, whether the relevant spin chemistry has broader response structure than simplified models predict, and whether behavioral thresholds depend on details of light, season, apparatus, species, or field geometry.

The result is therefore stronger as a constraint than as a slogan. Whatever mechanism a robin uses must survive the fact that very weak broadband noise can abolish orientation in some controlled settings while stronger narrow-band fields that look mechanistically seductive can fail to do so. A useful theory has to predict that asymmetry rather than merely accommodate it afterward.

There is an ecological consequence, but it needs the same discipline. The 2014 paper showed a local laboratory and field-site effect, not that radio infrastructure is causing a population-level migration crisis. Wild birds are not funnel-tested under overcast skies with every other cue removed. They have stars, sunsets, landmarks, odors, learned routes, flockmates, and redundant sensory systems. A compass can be degraded without a migration failing.

Still, the category is worth keeping. We call artificial night light "light pollution" because we can see what has been added. We call traffic and aircraft "noise pollution" because we can hear it. A migratory bird may inhabit another sensory environment laid over the same city, one in which a switching power supply or other electronic device contributes not heat or sound but confusion.

That possibility should not be inflated into a general human-health claim. The robins are interesting precisely because they may possess a specialized receptor exquisitely sensitive to field structure that most human tissues do not use for anything. Exposure limits designed around heating or established human effects answer a different question from whether a specialized animal compass can be perturbed below those limits.

What began as a failed experiment in a wooden hut therefore became two experiments at once. One asks how a bird extracts direction from a field of only tens of microtesla. The other asks how often our instruments, buildings and cities alter biological worlds we do not share strongly enough to notice.
