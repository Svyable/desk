# Turtles, Fish, Insects

Birds monopolize the poetry of magnetoreception, but they do not own the phenomenon.

Sea turtles are an especially severe test of inherited navigation. Hatchlings enter an ocean circulation system they have never seen. Experiments exposing young turtles to magnetic fields that mimic geographically distinct regions have produced orientation changes appropriate to those regions, supporting the idea that components of the geomagnetic field can function as positional cues. The animal is not merely pointing north. It is responding as if magnetic geography carries meaning.

Fish add other versions. Salmon navigate at oceanic scales and then use additional cues as they approach natal rivers. Elasmobranchs possess extraordinary electroreception, complicating magnetic interpretation because motion through Earth's field can induce electric fields. In these animals, a 'magnetic' sense may partly exploit electrodynamics through an existing sensory system.

Insects widen the design space again. Magnetic effects have been reported in butterflies, ants and other taxa, with mechanisms ranging from cryptochrome-dependent responses to iron-based hypotheses. The quality and replication strength vary sharply by species and assay.

The lesson is not that magnetoreception is universal in one form. It is that Earth's field is a persistent environmental regularity available for evolutionary capture. Different lineages can solve the detection problem differently, just as vision independently recruits different optical arrangements while obeying the same physics.

This comparative breadth is useful against overfitting. A theory tuned to one bird experiment should predict something in another lineage or explicitly explain why it does not apply. Cryptochrome mechanisms may be favored in light-dependent compasses; magnetite or induction may be better in darkness or underwater. Ecology becomes a constraint on molecular theory.

The planet supplies the signal. Evolution decides what instrument is worth building.
