# What Else Biology Hides

Magnetoreception is valuable beyond navigation because it demonstrates how a real biological capability can remain mechanistically obscure for decades even after behavior is persuasive.

The lesson should be used carefully. It does not imply that every reported anomalous human ability conceals an undiscovered receptor. Magnetoreception earned seriousness because experiments could manipulate a known external variable—the magnetic field—and observe lawful changes in behavior. Mechanistic hypotheses then made additional predictions.

That structure is portable. Claims about weak electric sensing, infrared sensitivity, infrasound, polarization vision, chemical gradients or other poorly appreciated channels should begin by identifying an external variable that can be independently manipulated. If no variable can be isolated, the claim remains much harder to convert into physiology.

The field also reminds us that sensory worlds are species-specific. A shark's electroreception, a bee's ultraviolet vision and a bird's magnetic compass are not paranormal from the animal's point of view. They are ordinary interfaces to physical variables humans happen not to perceive directly.

The intellectually risky move is not to declare unknown senses impossible. The equally risky move is to declare every intuition a hidden sense. Magnetoreception offers the middle path: start with behavior, manipulate the environment, derive mechanism-specific predictions, and refuse to skip anatomy.
