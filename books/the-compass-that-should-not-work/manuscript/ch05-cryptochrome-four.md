# Cryptochrome Four

In 2021 a collaboration working on cryptochrome 4 from the European robin produced one of the cleanest pieces of molecular evidence in the field. The purified protein's photochemistry was magnetically sensitive in vitro. Robin CRY4 showed greater magnetic sensitivity than homologous proteins from two non-migratory birds, chicken and pigeon. Site-specific mutations implicated a chain of four tryptophans involved in electron transfer and radical-pair formation.

That result is class-one evidence for a narrow statement: avian CRY4 can possess magnetic-field-sensitive photochemistry under laboratory conditions, and the robin protein studied had properties consistent with the radical-pair compass hypothesis. It is not class-one evidence that CRY4 is the receptor a robin actually uses in flight.

The missing links are revealing. The protein must be expressed in the right cells, oriented in a way that preserves directional information, coupled to signaling partners, and connected to a neural circuit. Other cryptochromes are also expressed in avian retinae. Localization studies have shifted candidates over time. A molecular winner declared too early could become another attractive detour.

Still, the 2021 experiment changed the texture of the argument. Before it, cryptochrome was a plausible host chosen because its chemistry fit. After it, at least one candidate protein from a migratory species had directly measured magnetic sensitivity and a mutationally tractable mechanism.

The comparative result is especially interesting. Greater sensitivity in a migratory species hints at evolutionary tuning, though the sample of species is far too small to treat that pattern as proof of adaptation. A strong next experiment is comparative rather than rhetorical: measure CRY4 variants across many migratory and non-migratory lineages, control for phylogeny, connect molecular sensitivity to behavioral thresholds, and test whether sequence changes repeatedly track navigational ecology.

If that relationship survives, magnetoreception becomes one of the rare places where a quantum-chemical parameter could be followed all the way from protein sequence to planetary-scale behavior. A bird crossing continents would then be carrying a molecular instrument whose useful signal begins with spin dynamics measured in nanoseconds.
