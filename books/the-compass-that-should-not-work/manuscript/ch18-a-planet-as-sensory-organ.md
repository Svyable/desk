# A Planet as Sensory Organ

A migrating robin carries a piece of the planet in its nervous system.

Not literally. The phrase is useful because geomagnetic navigation converts a planetary-scale field into cellular chemistry. Dynamo action in Earth's outer core produces a field extending far into space. That field intersects a protein in a retina, or a magnetic particle in tissue, and becomes a behavioral decision measured in centimeters inside a cage. The scale collapse is the marvel.

It also changes how we think about environment. An environment is not merely temperature, food and terrain. It is whatever variables an organism can measure. Polarized skylight is habitat if an insect uses it. Electric fields are habitat if a fish uses them. Geomagnetic geometry is habitat if migration depends on it.

This broader sensory ecology matters as human technology fills the planet with signals evolution did not anticipate. We already know artificial light changes behavior. We know noise does. Magnetoreception raises the possibility that electromagnetic environments can alter information channels in species whose receptor physics differs radically from ours.

The scientific posture should remain narrow. The evidence does not justify generalized claims of electromagnetic toxicity. It justifies studying whether specific artificial spectra interfere with specific magnetic behaviors, with replication and field ecology strong enough to estimate real-world impact.

The deeper implication is epistemic. Nature has already built instruments that exploit effects humans once had difficulty imagining as biologically useful. That fact should make us neither gullible nor smug. It should make us experimental.

The compass that should not work is not an argument against physics. It is what happens when biology learns physics more patiently than we do.
