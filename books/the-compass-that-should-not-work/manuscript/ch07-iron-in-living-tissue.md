# Iron in Living Tissue

The rival mechanism begins with a mineral, not a photon.

Magnetite is ferrimagnetic iron oxide. Biology knows how to make it. Magnetotactic bacteria construct chains of magnetosome crystals that align the cell with magnetic fields. In larger animals, biogenic magnetic minerals have been reported in tissues implicated in navigation. A sufficiently constrained magnetite particle can exert mechanical force or torque on a membrane or cytoskeletal element when the external field changes.

This mechanism has a brute physical appeal. No delicate radical-pair lifetime is needed. A particle with many coherently aligned magnetic moments can amplify the field before thermal noise gets its vote. It can also in principle detect polarity and intensity differently from an inclination-only radical-pair compass.

The problem is anatomy and contamination. Iron is common. Magnetic particles from environmental contamination can masquerade as biological structures. Early reports of magnetite-rich cells in the upper beak of pigeons were interpreted as candidate receptors, but later work challenged the identity of those cells and found that iron-rich structures could be macrophages rather than specialized sensory neurons. A beautiful particle becomes irrelevant if it sits in the wrong cell.

This history is a model of how to handle anomalous biology. The failure of a specific anatomical claim does not erase the physical plausibility of magnetite transduction elsewhere. Nor does finding magnetite prove sensory function. Every stage needs its own evidence: mineral identity, biological origin, cellular localization, mechanical coupling, innervation, neural response and behavior.

Magnetotactic bacteria provide the existence proof that organisms can build magnetic material with exquisite control. They do not prove vertebrate magnetoreception uses the same trick. The interesting possibility is convergent engineering: biology may repeatedly recruit iron minerals because they offer a robust route from weak fields to mechanical force.

If radical-pair chemistry is the elegant compass, magnetite is the stubborn hardware. A mature theory of navigation may need both.
