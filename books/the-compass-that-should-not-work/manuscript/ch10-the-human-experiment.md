# The Human Experiment

Human magnetoreception is where a respectable animal-sensory literature walks into a room carrying decades of bad memories.

People have claimed for a long time that humans can sense north. The claims have arrived through blindfolded orientation tests, dowsing-adjacent demonstrations, anecdotes about direction finding, weak behavioral effects and experiments difficult to reproduce. That history matters because it changes the burden of proof. A positive result in a robin enters a field with a mature behavioral tradition. A positive result in a human enters a field where expectation, subtle cues and analytical flexibility have already produced several false starts.

The right response is not automatic disbelief. It is better experimental architecture.

A useful place to begin is with a negative result.

In 2002 a blinded study tested fifty adults while researchers altered controlled components of the Earth's magnetic field and recorded EEG and subjective responses. The experiment manipulated field magnitude, inclination and direction under conditions designed to remove obvious cues. The published conclusion was overwhelmingly negative. The authors did not find convincing EEG or conscious responses to the geomagnetic manipulations. Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1388245702001864

That paper should remain in the story because a field becomes distorted when later positive results are described as though nobody had ever looked carefully before.

Seventeen years later, a different experiment produced a more provocative result.

In 2019 Connie Wang, Joseph Kirschvink and colleagues reported that specific rotations of Earth-strength magnetic fields produced repeatable changes in human EEG alpha-band activity. Participants sat in a shielded experimental chamber surrounded by orthogonal coils while the magnetic field was rotated around the head. The subjects were in darkness and were not asked to consciously identify magnetic direction. The dependent measure was neural rather than verbal: changes in the 8–13 hertz alpha rhythm. Source: https://doi.org/10.1523/ENEURO.0483-18.2019

The effect was not simply “field changed, brain changed.”

That is the most interesting part.

The strongest alpha-event-related desynchronization appeared under particular horizontal rotations when the vertical component of the magnetic field pointed downward, matching the ordinary geomagnetic configuration of the Northern Hemisphere location where the participants lived. When the vertical component was inverted, otherwise similar rotations did not produce the same response. The authors also reported polarity sensitivity.

Those asymmetries matter because artifacts are often less choosy than biology.

A coil system can click. Current can induce vibration. Electronics can leak noise. Electrodes can pick up electromagnetic transients. If every physical rotation of the field produced the same EEG perturbation, the artifact problem would be severe. A response that depends on static field configuration and polarity is harder to explain with simple induction alone.

The authors explicitly used those dependencies to argue against two candidate explanations. First, electrical induction depends on field change rather than the sign of a static component, so the polarity dependence argued against a simple induced-current artifact or receptor. Second, a canonical radical-pair compass is axial rather than polarity-sensitive: reversing the field along the same axis should not produce the directional asymmetry they observed. They therefore argued that a ferromagnetic transduction mechanism, perhaps involving biogenic magnetite or a related magnetic material, remained compatible with the result.

That mechanistic inference is important and should be kept separate from the broader claim.

The experiment supports, if replicated, **neural discrimination of certain geomagnetic changes**. It does not by itself identify the receptor. It does not establish a useful human compass. It does not establish conscious perception. It does not establish that the effect influences navigation outside the chamber.

Those are four different claims.

The paper also improved its own credibility by making data and analysis resources available. That does not make the result true, but it changes the social structure around the result. Other researchers can inspect the analysis rather than arguing from a graph in a press release. The underlying paper and data links are available through eNeuro: https://www.eneuro.org/content/6/2/ENEURO.0483-18.2019

A strange thing then happened to the human-magnetoreception story.

Instead of a clean direct replication of the EEG result, another group reported a different positive phenomenon with a different implied mechanism.

In 2022 Kwon-Seok Chae and colleagues published a behavioral study in *Scientific Reports* arguing that male participants showed magnetic orientation under a two-alternative forced-choice paradigm. Participants sat in a rotary apparatus and were tested under manipulated geomagnetic conditions. The reported orientation depended on light reaching the eyes, especially blue wavelengths. Radiofrequency fields near a proposed resonance condition altered the behavioral effect. Reversing the vertical geomagnetic component also changed orientation. Source: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-12460-6

On the surface, this sounds like confirmation that humans possess a magnetic sense.

Mechanistically, it is almost the opposite of the 2019 EEG story.

The 2019 result was polarity-sensitive in a way the authors argued was inconsistent with a canonical radical-pair compass and more compatible with ferromagnetic transduction. The 2022 result was light-dependent and radiofrequency-sensitive in a way its authors interpreted as evidence for a radical-pair-like quantum mechanism.

That disagreement is not an inconvenience to be edited out. It is the most useful fact in the human literature.

If both effects are real, humans could in principle possess more than one magnetic pathway, just as birds may combine visually mediated compass information with trigeminal magnetic input. But that is an expensive hypothesis. Before adding a second hidden human magnetic system, the field has to show that each reported effect reproduces independently under the same protocol.

The alternative is simpler: one or both results are fragile.

This is why the word **replication** needs precision. A later paper showing some response to a magnetic field is not a direct replication of an earlier paper if it measures a different behavior, uses a different apparatus, depends on different stimulus parameters and implies a different receptor mechanism.

The 2022 study therefore broadens the case for taking human magnetoreception seriously without settling the 2019 finding.

The older 2002 null makes the situation even more interesting because it suggests parameter specificity matters enormously—or that the positive effects are not robust.

A useful comparison is to look at what each experiment asked the nervous system to reveal.

The 2002 work searched broadly across altered geomagnetic parameters and EEG measures and found little convincing evidence. The 2019 work used stimulus conditions shaped by what animal magnetoreception had taught researchers about biologically relevant field rotations and focused on alpha-band event-related desynchronization. The 2022 work used a behavioral forced-choice task with light and radiofrequency manipulations.

Those are not interchangeable assays.

A sensory pathway can produce a neural response without generating a conscious report. A person can possess a weak behavioral bias that does not appear in an EEG metric chosen in advance. Or analytical degrees of freedom can create an apparent response in one assay that disappears when another laboratory repeats it.

The field needs an experiment designed to discriminate those possibilities, not merely another positive paper.

The first rule should be preregistration.

Before data collection, the investigators should specify the magnetic stimuli, timing, EEG channels, frequency bands, artifact rejection, behavioral endpoints and statistical contrasts that count as confirmation. The original 2019 effect is specific enough to support this. A direct replication should reproduce the downwards-field horizontal rotation that produced the strongest alpha response, the reversed vertical configuration that did not, and sham conditions where coil currents are arranged so participants experience no corresponding field rotation.

The second rule should be engineering independence.

The magnetics need to be validated by people who are not invested in the biological result. Acoustic microphones, accelerometers, current monitors and field probes should record every trial. A successful human magnetoreception experiment should be able to show not only that the field changed, but that all plausible nonmagnetic cues failed to covary with the condition.

The third rule should be multicenter replication.

EEG effects are notorious for analysis flexibility and individual variability. A single laboratory can discover an effect. It should not be asked to certify its universality. A registered protocol run in several independent laboratories would do more for human magnetoreception than another decade of conceptually adjacent experiments.

The fourth rule should be cross-assay prediction.

If the 2019 EEG response and 2022 behavioral response reflect the same underlying magnetic sense, individual differences might correlate. A person with a strong alpha desynchronization should, under some theory, be more likely to show magnetic orientation behavior. If no relationship exists, that result would not kill either effect, but it would weaken claims that the experiments are observing one shared system.

The fifth rule should be mechanism-specific perturbation.

The two leading human claims make different physical predictions. A ferromagnetic receptor should care about polarity and static field properties in ways a canonical radical-pair compass does not. A radical-pair mechanism should show characteristic dependence on light and possibly weak radiofrequency perturbations. The experiment should therefore cross those variables rather than test them in separate literatures.

A single protocol could include:

- field rotations with normal and inverted vertical components;
- polarity reversals along the same field axis;
- darkness, blue light and long-wavelength light;
- carefully chosen radiofrequency perturbations with sham frequencies;
- simultaneous EEG and forced-choice orientation behavior;
- blinded trial control and independent magnetic engineering logs.

The result matrix would be far more informative than a simple positive or negative.

Suppose alpha desynchronization survives polarity reversal but disappears in darkness. That would contradict the original mechanistic reading. Suppose the behavioral orientation effect survives long-wavelength light and ignores RF perturbation. That would undermine the 2022 radical-pair interpretation. Suppose the EEG effect replicates while conscious behavior remains at chance. Then humans may retain a nonconscious magnetic input with little or no navigational use.

That last possibility deserves more attention.

A sense does not have to enter awareness to influence the nervous system.

The human body monitors blood pressure, carbon dioxide, gut distension, immune signaling and vestibular variables without presenting each measurement as a clean conscious object. A vestigial magnetic pathway could modulate neural activity while producing no recognizable sensation. Evolution does not erase every ancestral detector when its behavioral importance declines. Circuits can be repurposed, weakened or left below awareness.

But **vestigial** can become a storytelling loophole if used carelessly.

If an effect fails behavioral tests, it is tempting to say the sense exists but is unconscious. If it fails EEG tests, one can say the neural code is elsewhere. If it fails replication, one can say the population differs. A hypothesis that retreats to a new invisible level after every null cannot lose.

The human magnetoreception program therefore needs explicit defeat conditions.

If several high-powered independent laboratories fail to reproduce the 2019 alpha response under matched magnetic geometry and artifact controls, confidence in that specific effect should fall sharply.

If preregistered laboratories cannot reproduce the 2022 forced-choice orientation effect with the same light and radiofrequency dependencies, confidence in that behavioral claim should fall sharply.

If positive responses appear but change arbitrarily with apparatus and analysis, the burden shifts toward artifact.

If responses replicate but the proposed mechanism-specific signatures do not, the phenomenon may survive while the mechanism changes.

That separation between **phenomenon** and **mechanism** is the most important discipline in this chapter.

The 2019 investigators reported a phenomenon and proposed ferromagnetism as the remaining plausible transduction class under their tests. The 2022 investigators reported a different phenomenon and proposed a light-dependent magnetic-resonance mechanism. Neither proposal inherits certainty merely because the experiment produced a statistically significant effect.

There is another reason to be strict here: human magnetoreception is unusually easy to recruit into claims it does not support.

A weak neural response to Earth-strength field rotation says nothing by itself about electromagnetic hypersensitivity, health effects from Wi-Fi, geomagnetic control of mood, psychic navigation or therapeutic magnets. Those are separate claims with different exposure regimes and evidentiary burdens.

The existence of one magnetic receptor would not validate every magnetic story.

Birds are the best warning. Avian magnetoreception is strongly supported behaviorally, yet even there receptor identity and neural coding remain incomplete. A genuine sensory phenomenon can coexist with decades of mechanistic uncertainty. Humans, where the basic phenomenon itself is less secure, deserve even more caution.

The most interesting claim remains modest.

The human nervous system **may** retain measurable responses to geomagnetic information under particular conditions. There is one carefully designed EEG report supporting that possibility, one later behavioral study supporting a different kind of magnetic response, and an older controlled EEG study that was largely negative. The mechanisms proposed by the positive studies do not line up neatly.

That is not proof of a hidden human compass.

It is enough to justify a serious replication program.

The worst outcome would be to turn one EEG paper and one behavioral paper into evidence that humans possess suppressed navigational powers. The second-worst would be to ignore both because the conclusion feels implausible.

The right move is expensive boredom: preregister, reproduce the field geometry, instrument every artifact channel, run enough people, share the raw data, and repeat the work in laboratories that have no emotional stake in the answer.

If the effect survives that treatment, the consequence is not mystical.

It would mean ordinary human experience is an incomplete inventory of what the nervous system measures.

If it disappears, that result would be useful too.

A frontier becomes science when the experiment is allowed to make the mystery smaller.
