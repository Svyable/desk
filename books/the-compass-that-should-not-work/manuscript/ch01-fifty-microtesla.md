# Fifty Microtesla

The first mistake is to call Earth's field strong because a compass needle moves. The needle is a macroscopic conspiracy: trillions upon trillions of aligned magnetic moments, a low-friction pivot, and enough time to settle. A molecule in warm tissue gets no such luxury.

At mid-latitudes the geomagnetic field is typically on the order of a few tens of microtesla. That number should be kept physically humiliating. The magnetic interaction energy of a single electron spin in such a field is far below ordinary thermal energy at body temperature. If one insists on imagining a tiny bar magnet inside every molecule, the problem looks hopeless.

Biology often beats this kind of argument by refusing the premise. It does not need a field to overpower thermal agitation in equilibrium. It can use a field to bias reaction pathways in a system already driven far from equilibrium by photons and metabolism. A tiny change in chemical yield can be amplified by receptors, neural circuits and repeated sampling. The right comparison is not always magnetic energy versus kT; sometimes it is whether a field perturbs the timing or branching of a reaction enough that a downstream system can integrate the difference.

This distinction matters because weak-field biology attracts bad reasoning from both directions. Enthusiasts treat any biological response as permission to invoke exotic quantum control. Skeptics sometimes wave the thermal scale as if it forbids all magnetic sensitivity. Both shortcuts are lazy. Nature is full of detectors whose input energy is small because the detector is not passive.

The radical-pair mechanism belongs in this loophole. Photoexcitation creates correlated electron spins whose singlet-triplet interconversion can depend on magnetic interactions. The magnetic field need not supply the reaction's energy. It only needs to alter the branching probabilities before decoherence and chemistry erase the spin information.

That gives the field an honest opening. It does not give us a bird yet. Between a field-sensitive chemical reaction and navigation lies protein orientation, retinal anatomy, signal transduction, neural representation, calibration, learning and behavior. Most of the book lives in that gap.

The empirical discipline begins here: never let the weakness of the field become an argument by itself, and never let the existence of a quantum-sensitive chemical pathway become a finished sensory organ. The interesting science is the chain between them.
