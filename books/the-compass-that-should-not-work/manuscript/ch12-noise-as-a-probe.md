# Noise as a Probe

Interference is usually treated as a nuisance. In magnetoreception it can become spectroscopy performed on an intact animal.

If a bird's compass fails under particular oscillating magnetic fields, the frequencies and amplitudes that disrupt orientation constrain the internal spin dynamics. Different radical-pair lifetimes and hyperfine structures predict different susceptibility. In principle, behavior can reveal something about a molecular reaction that is otherwise hidden inside tissue.

This is an extraordinary inversion. The animal is not merely the endpoint of a mechanistic study; it becomes a detector for the mechanism's internal timescale.

The danger is equally extraordinary. Electromagnetic environments are hard to characterize. Harmonics, gradients, coil heating, vibration and unintended electric fields can create confounds. A spectacular low-intensity effect deserves stronger engineering documentation, not weaker, because the claimed coupling is subtle.

The best future experiments should therefore publish complete spectra of ambient and applied fields, coil geometry, calibration uncertainties, sham-switching behavior and blinded analysis. Replication should deliberately vary laboratory construction and geographic background noise. If an effect survives those changes with the predicted frequency dependence, the mechanistic inference becomes much stronger.

Noise can also be ecological data. Urban environments generate broad electromagnetic spectra alien to the evolutionary history of migratory species. If some frequency bands interfere with magnetic navigation, conservation biology may eventually need to consider electromagnetic sensory habitats alongside light and sound.

That claim remains conditional. The existing bird literature justifies careful field studies, not sweeping regulation. But the conceptual shift is already useful: a human technology can be biologically significant without depositing toxins or heating tissue if it overlaps a specialized information channel.
