# A Sense We Cannot Imagine

Human language describes senses by borrowing from senses we already possess. Bats 'see' with sound. Snakes 'see' heat. Electric fish 'feel' electric fields. These metaphors are probably wrong in detail, but we have no neutral vocabulary for qualia outside our own inventory.

Magnetoreception sharpens the problem. If a bird's magnetic compass is coupled to retinal processing, magnetic direction may alter a visual pattern. If an iron-based receptor feeds somatosensory pathways, it may feel more like pressure or balance. If the information is processed unconsciously, there may be no distinct experience at all.

This matters because human introspection is often smuggled into biological plausibility. People ask why they cannot 'feel' magnetic north and infer that such a sense must be exotic. But we do not consciously experience many variables our nervous system continuously estimates: blood pressure, vestibular acceleration components, pupil control, immune signaling. Consciousness is not the measurement ledger of the body.

The disputed human EEG result becomes more interesting in this light. A repeatable cortical response without conscious awareness would not be paradoxical. It would fit a large class of sensory processing that never becomes reportable experience.

For anomalous-cognition research, magnetoreception supplies a methodological warning. Hidden processing is possible, but it must be demonstrated through controlled perturbation, not inferred from anecdote. The fact that organisms possess unsuspected senses does not validate every claimed intuition. It tells us to design better experiments around specific physical channels.

Wonder survives discipline. In fact it improves.
