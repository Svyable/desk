# Quantum Without Mysticism

Quantum biology is a phrase with an unfortunate public relations problem. Every chemical bond is quantum mechanical, so the phrase can be trivial. At the other extreme it becomes a permission slip for claims about consciousness or healing that have no experimental bridge from quantum dynamics to biology.

Magnetoreception offers a better standard. The radical-pair proposal is interesting because a specifically quantum variable—electron spin coherence and singlet-triplet dynamics—may influence a classical biological output under Earth-strength fields. The hypothesis makes quantitative predictions about field amplitude, orientation, radical lifetime and radiofrequency perturbation.

That is what earns the word quantum. Not mystery. Constraint.

The system also exposes a misconception about decoherence. Warm, wet biology does not need to preserve arbitrary quantum states for seconds. A chemical reaction can exploit coherent spin evolution for microseconds or less, then irreversibly amplify a small yield difference. The relevant question is whether coherence lasts long enough for the mechanism, not whether an organism behaves like a quantum computer.

There are still difficult theoretical issues. The radical pair must be sufficiently long-lived and oriented. Hyperfine couplings determine directional sensitivity. Reaction kinetics and environmental relaxation matter. Proposals invoking additional radicals or superoxide attempt to improve sensitivity but introduce their own relaxation and biochemical constraints.

The discipline should be ruthless about these details. A model that produces a compass only by choosing biologically impossible parameters is not a mechanism. A protein experiment performed at fields far above geomagnetic strength does not automatically solve the natural problem. A behavioral disruption that fits one frequency prediction should survive independent replication.

Quantum biology becomes scientifically valuable when it loses its exemption from ordinary standards.
