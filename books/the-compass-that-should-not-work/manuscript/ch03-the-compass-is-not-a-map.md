# The Compass Is Not a Map

The phrase magnetic sense hides at least two different problems. A compass answers direction. A map supplies position. Animals appear capable, in different species and contexts, of using geomagnetic information for both.

Earth's field varies geographically in intensity, inclination and declination. Those gradients create a coarse coordinate system. Sea turtles exposed experimentally to magnetic fields corresponding to different points along migratory routes can show directionally appropriate responses, suggesting inherited sensitivity to regional magnetic signatures. Salmon and other migrants have been studied for analogous magnetic-map abilities.

The distinction matters mechanistically. A radical-pair compass optimized for direction need not be the same receptor that measures field intensity for map position. A magnetite-based sensor could be well suited to intensity or polarity while a light-dependent retinal mechanism supplies directional inclination. Evolution has no obligation to reward theoretical elegance with a single receptor.

This immediately complicates knockout logic. If one perturbation destroys one magnetic behavior but not another, that may not mean the experiment failed. It may mean the animal has multiple magnetic channels. Conversely, a behavioral deficit after damaging one tissue does not prove that tissue is the receptor if the manipulation also disrupts motivation, vision or vestibular function.

A useful book about anomalous sensing should resist the hunger for one secret organ. Human senses themselves are composite. Balance integrates vestibular organs, vision and proprioception. Spatial orientation is not a receptor but a negotiation among systems. Magnetic navigation may be similarly distributed.

This possibility also helps reconcile arguments that otherwise look mutually exclusive. Radical-pair researchers and magnetite researchers have often been framed as competitors for the one true magnetic sense. The more biologically plausible answer may be untidy coexistence. One mechanism could provide a compass, another a map, and both could be calibrated against stars, polarized light, odors and landmarks.

The experimental demand then changes. Rather than ask whether an animal 'has magnetoreception,' ask what magnetic variable is available to which behavior, under which sensory conditions, through which anatomy, at what precision. That sentence is less glamorous. It is also how a mystery becomes physiology.
