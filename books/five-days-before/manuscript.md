# FIVE DAYS BEFORE

## The satellite that found an earthquake signal nobody can use

**Sven Hardy Benson**

# 1. Five Days

The awkward number is five.

Not five seconds, which would belong to earthquake early warning: the ground has already ruptured somewhere, instruments have caught the first waves, and software is racing the more destructive shaking toward a city. Not five years, which would belong to hazard maps, insurance tables, building codes, and the slow arithmetic of living above faults.

Five days.

In 2017 Rui Yan, Michel Parrot and Jean-Louis Pinçon published a statistical study using measurements from DEMETER, a French microsatellite that had spent six years circling Earth and sampling the ionosphere. They compared ion densities measured near earthquakes with background measurements taken under similar conditions. After removing aftershocks and separating day from night, they reported a nighttime effect for earthquakes larger than magnitude 5. The anomaly appeared most strongly about five days before the earthquakes and within roughly 200 kilometres of their epicentres.

There is a sentence available here that I am not going to write.

It is the sentence in which a satellite saw earthquakes coming.

The paper itself did not earn that sentence. Its authors said the perturbations could be seen statistically and that ion-density measurements alone could not be used for earthquake forecasting. That qualification is not a little disclaimer attached to an otherwise spectacular result. It is the subject.

Because the signal was not nothing.

That is what makes this case worth following.

DEMETER was not a backyard magnetometer attached to a prophecy. The satellite was a serious scientific instrument. Its name expanded to Detection of Electro-Magnetic Emissions Transmitted from Earthquake Regions, which is unusually candid naming for a spacecraft. It measured electric and magnetic fields, plasma parameters and energetic particles from low Earth orbit. Researchers could accumulate years of observations rather than wait beside one fault for one earthquake. They could compare thousands of events. They could ask whether a pattern survived averaging.

Some patterns did.

A 2009 study by František Němec and colleagues examined more than three and a half years of DEMETER observations and reported a very small but statistically significant reduction in very-low-frequency wave intensity before earthquakes. The decrease occurred at night, at frequencies around 1.7 kilohertz, during the few hours before the main shock. A later analysis using almost the full 6.5-year mission and about 8,400 earthquakes again reported roughly two decibels of attenuation in that band.

The effect is small enough that the adjective matters. Statistically significant does not mean conspicuous. It means a pattern in an ensemble of measurements was unlikely, under the authors' statistical model, to have arisen from the background they used for comparison.

This distinction becomes viciously important in earthquake science.

An ionosphere is not graph paper. It is being struck by sunlight, stirred by geomagnetic storms, driven by tides and planetary waves, seeded by lightning, altered by seasons, latitude and local time, and contaminated from below by human transmitters and electrical infrastructure. A researcher looking for an earthquake-related disturbance is not searching an empty room for a dropped coin. The room is already full of coins falling down the stairs.

And earthquakes are not rare enough to make coincidences difficult.

Every day the planet produces seismic events. Give an analyst enough earthquakes, enough stations, enough frequencies, enough time windows, enough spatial radii, enough definitions of anomaly and enough choices for constructing a background, and something will happen before something else. This is not an accusation of fraud. It is the geometry of retrospective search.

The problem gets worse because the physical hypothesis is broad. A fault preparing to rupture might change stress in rock. Stress might move charge carriers, fluids or gases. Those might alter near-surface electric fields or atmospheric ionization. The atmosphere might communicate some disturbance upward. The ionosphere might respond. A satellite might then measure density, electric field, magnetic field, wave intensity or energetic particles differently than expected.

There are plausible verbs all the way up the chain.

There is not yet a demonstrated operational chain.

That gap has swallowed decades of earthquake-prediction research.

The United States Geological Survey states the current institutional position without much romance: neither USGS nor other scientists have successfully predicted a major earthquake, and convincing electromagnetic precursors have not been demonstrated despite decades of work. USGS researchers have repeatedly reexamined famous electromagnetic claims. In some cases the anomaly survives as an observation but loses its proposed cause. In others it disappears when the background is handled differently.

One 2021 reassessment is almost too perfect for this book. Researchers revisited ultra-low-frequency magnetic pulses that had been reported before two California earthquakes near Alum Rock. They could reproduce an increase in pulse counts before the 2007 event at one station. So far, so interesting. But the number of pulses depended on arbitrary detection thresholds. A second nearby station did not show matching pulses. The counts rose during peak human-activity hours. The authors concluded that local cultural noise was the likely source.

The anomaly existed.

The earthquake explanation did not survive.

That sentence should stay in reach throughout what follows. An observation can be genuine while its interpretation fails. A statistical association can survive while its utility fails. A physical mechanism can be plausible while the proposed measurement channel is useless. A predictor can perform better than chance in a selected retrospective dataset and still be worthless once asked to issue alarms into the future.

These are different failures. Collapsing them into belief or disbelief is how anomalous fields become stupid.

The opposite error is just as easy. Skepticism can become a compression algorithm. A field accumulates false alarms, noisy measurements, excitable press coverage, methodological disputes and a few spectacularly bad claims. Eventually the entire subject is stored under one label: debunked. That label saves time. It can also destroy information.

DEMETER prevents the easy version of that move because some of its published results are not stories about one strange night. They are statistical results across large datasets. If they are artifacts, the artifact must be identified at the level of the analysis. If they are real associations, their physical origin still has to be established. If their physical origin is seismic, their predictive value still has to be demonstrated. Each conditional is a separate door.

There is a useful cruelty in forcing the argument through all of them.

Consider the five-day ion-density result. Suppose, for the moment, that the reported association is exactly right. Nighttime ion density really does become statistically unusual several days before some earthquakes above magnitude 5.

A mayor still cannot evacuate a city.

To do that, somebody needs an alarm rule. What measurement crosses what threshold? How large an area is warned? How long does the alarm remain active? Which magnitude range counts? What happens when solar or geomagnetic activity disturbs the same variable? What is the false-alarm rate over every quiet day on which no damaging earthquake follows? How many earthquakes arrive without the signal? Does the rule work in a region, season and year that were not used to invent it? Does it survive after the analyst loses the privilege of knowing where and when the earthquake occurred?

Retrospective precursor research begins with an earthquake and looks backward.

Prediction begins with Tuesday.

Tuesday is much harder.

On Tuesday the algorithm does not know that a magnitude 6.4 earthquake will occur on Saturday at 03:17. It receives a planet. It has to decide whether the ionosphere above one region is sufficiently abnormal to issue a forecast while geomagnetic conditions change, thunderstorms fire, transmitters switch, seasons turn, and other seismic zones remain quiet. It must do this every Tuesday, including the thousands of Tuesdays that do not become stories.

This is where base rates enter and ruin the party.

A diagnostic can be impressively sensitive and still produce mostly false alarms when the event it seeks is rare in the relevant time-place window. Medicine lives with this problem. Security screening lives with it. Fraud detection lives with it. Earthquake prediction would live with an especially punishing version because the social cost of an alarm is not a second laboratory test. It can mean closing schools, stopping trains, moving hospital patients, shutting factories, evacuating buildings and teaching a population whether the next warning deserves belief.

The cost of missing an earthquake is terrible. The cost of crying earthquake often enough is also real. Eventually people stop moving.

This is why the distinction between a precursor and a predictor is not semantic fussiness. A precursor is something associated with a process before an event. A predictor is an information source that improves a forecast under defined conditions. The first can exist without the second.

There is another possibility, less comfortable for both camps.

The Earth may produce genuine pre-rupture electromagnetic or atmospheric effects that are physically real and practically nonunique. Rock can change before failure without doing so in a way that identifies which stressed volume will become a damaging earthquake. Fault systems are not laboratory coupons loaded until they snap on command. Many patches creep, slip, fracture, transfer stress, move fluids and produce small earthquakes without cascading into catastrophe. A measurable preparation process might therefore be real but diagnostically weak.

That would be a fascinating scientific result and a disappointing warning system.

Science has room for both sentences at once.

The book begins there because earthquake prediction has spent a century making people choose too early. Either the planet whispers before it breaks or prediction is impossible. Either electromagnetic anomalies are the suppressed frontier or they are noise. Either a strange light in the sky is a precursor or it is folklore. Either a satellite correlation is evidence of a mechanism or it is data dredging.

The actual landscape is meaner.

In 2004 France launched a spacecraft whose mission explicitly included searching the ionosphere for earthquake-related electromagnetic phenomena. It flew until 2010. Researchers used the resulting archive to report several small statistical associations. Other researchers, including USGS scientists, have shown that famous electromagnetic and ionospheric precursor claims can collapse under alternative background models, comparison stations, or ordinary human noise. The institutional conclusion remains that reliable electromagnetic precursors have not been demonstrated.

All of those statements can be true.

The temptation is to resolve the contradiction by picking the statement one already likes. I want to keep the contradiction alive long enough to measure it.

Five days before an earthquake is an extraordinary place for a signal to appear.

Five days before an earthquake is also exactly where a noisy planet can fool you.

The rest of this book is about learning the difference before anyone rings the alarm.

# 2. A Satellite Named for the Hypothesis

DEMETER's institutional origin, mission design, orbit, instruments, observing geometry and what the spacecraft could and could not sample. The chapter reconstructs why a national space agency considered seismo-electromagnetic coupling worth a dedicated microsatellite without treating mission approval as evidence that the hypothesis was correct.

# 3. The Planet Before the Earthquake

What physically changes in rock before rupture? Stress, microfracture, fluids, electrokinetic effects, piezomagnetism, radon, charge-carrier hypotheses and the severe scale problem between a laboratory specimen and a crustal fault.

# 4. Getting a Signal Through Sixty Kilometres of Weather

The lithosphere-atmosphere-ionosphere coupling problem. A proposed crustal signal must survive conductivity, turbulence, meteorology, lightning and atmospheric chemistry before becoming an ionospheric measurement. Mechanism claims are separated from phenomenological correlations.

# 5. The Ionosphere Is Already Busy

Solar forcing, geomagnetic storms, lightning, transmitters, seasons, local time, latitude and ordinary plasma variability. This chapter builds the background model before permitting an earthquake anomaly to exist.

# 6. Two Decibels

Close reconstruction of the DEMETER VLF statistical studies: event selection, frequencies, spatial windows, local-time dependence, effect size, controls and what “statistically significant” means when the measured change is small.

# 7. The Five-Day Result

The ion-density literature, especially superposed-epoch studies reporting pre-seismic effects days before M>5 earthquakes. The chapter distinguishes population-level association from event-level discrimination.

# 8. Begin With Tuesday

Retrospective analysis versus prospective forecasting. Alarm rules, denominators, false alarms, missed detections, base rates, calibration and the requirement that a predictor operate before the target event is known.

# 9. The Signal at Rush Hour

The Alum Rock ULF pulse claim and its reassessment as likely cultural noise. The chapter follows an anomaly that remains measurable while changing causal identity.

# 10. A Curve Can Invent a Precursor

The Tohoku TEC controversy and the sensitivity of inferred pre-earthquake enhancements to reference-curve choices. Synthetic-data demonstrations show how a postseismic feature can leak backward through detrending.

# 11. Parkfield Was Waiting for Us

The unusually instrumented 2004 Parkfield earthquake as a negative case. Magnetic and electric observations failed to deliver the expected precursors, putting pressure on mechanisms that explain positives only after the fact.

# 12. Lights

Earthquake lights, historical reports and proposed charge-carrier mechanisms. The chapter separates documented luminous phenomena, candidate physics, witness-report problems and the leap from “can occur near earthquakes” to “predicts earthquakes.”

# 13. The Volcano Control

DEMETER also found ionospheric disturbances associated with volcanic activity. If multiple geophysical processes perturb similar observables, the mechanism may be real while specificity collapses. Volcanoes become a control rather than another marvel.

# 14. When China Builds the Next Satellite

CSES and subsequent seismo-electromagnetic observing programs. Continued state investment is evidence that the measurement problem remains scientifically interesting, not evidence that operational earthquake prediction has been achieved.

# 15. The Cost of Being Right Too Often

Decision theory for warnings: evacuation, infrastructure shutdown, public trust, false-alarm tolerance and asymmetric loss. A weak signal is evaluated by what decisions it improves, not by whether its p-value survives.

# 16. The Experiment Nobody Has Run Long Enough

Design for a prospective, preregistered, multi-year test: frozen features, global quiet-time denominators, geomagnetic and meteorological controls, independent holdout regions, explicit alarm windows, public timestamping and scoring before earthquakes occur.

# 17. What Would Change My Mind

Competing models receive explicit defeat conditions. The chapter asks what evidence would force a skeptic to accept a useful precursor and what evidence should force a proponent to abandon a favored channel.

# 18. Saturday

Return to the five-day interval with the retrospective privilege removed. The ending distinguishes early warning after rupture, probabilistic hazard forecasting, scientifically real precursory physics and reliable short-term prediction. They are four different achievements, and civilization benefits from refusing to pretend otherwise.
