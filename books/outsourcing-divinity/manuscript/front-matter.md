## Author's Note

This book uses religious language deliberately and carefully. It does not argue that artificial intelligence is divine, conscious, spiritually authoritative, or a literal replacement for God. Nor does it reduce religion to primitive outsourcing of thought.

The subject is a recurring human move: placing judgment, interpretation, prediction, permission, legitimacy, memory, absolution, or meaning into an authority outside the individual. Religions have developed sophisticated and often competing accounts of when such mediation is legitimate. States, markets, professions, bureaucracies, and technical systems have developed their own. Artificial intelligence matters because a single conversational interface can now perform pieces of many of those roles at once.

Where the manuscript discusses theology, the claim belongs to a tradition and is identified as such rather than presented as a universal religious view. Where it discusses AI behavior, documented research and primary governance materials are preferred to speculation. Where it moves from evidence into philosophy, cultural interpretation, or forecasts about possible futures, the argument is presented as argument.

The machine can help us think. The question is whether we remember which decisions still belong to the people who must live with them.
