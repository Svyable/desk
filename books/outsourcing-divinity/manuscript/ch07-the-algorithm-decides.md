# The Algorithm Decides

Long before chatbots learned to sound sympathetic, algorithms were already deciding who saw what.

They ranked search results, filtered spam, set insurance prices, ordered news feeds, matched riders with drivers, detected fraud, routed packages, approved transactions, scored leads, recommended music, adjusted advertisements, and decided which notifications deserved to interrupt dinner. Most of these systems did not speak. Their authority arrived as sequence: this result before that one, this applicant above another, this account flagged, this video next.

Because ranking feels less dramatic than judgment, society accepted an enormous transfer of power before conversational AI made the transfer visible.

A ranking system rarely says, “I have decided what matters.” It simply creates an environment in which some things are easier to see than others. The distinction is politically important. Censorship removes. Ranking buries. Recommendation amplifies. Defaults steer. Friction delays. A person may retain formal freedom to choose while the architecture of choice is being continuously rearranged.

This is not necessarily sinister. A search engine that refuses to rank is unusable. A music service that presents every song equally is not a service. A credit-card network that treats every transaction as equally suspicious will collapse into noise. Algorithmic selection is a response to abundance. Once information exceeds attention, something has to decide what comes first.

The old gatekeeper chose from a desk. The new gatekeeper chooses at machine speed for each person separately.

Personalization changes the moral character of mediation because it hides comparison. If a newspaper prints the same front page for a city, readers can argue about what editors chose. If every reader receives a different feed, there is no shared front page to inspect. The system can be experienced as neutral because each person sees only the result designed for him.

The same dynamic appears in credit and risk scoring. A score is not a verdict in the metaphysical sense. It is an estimate produced from variables under a model. Yet institutions often use scores at boundaries where the distinction becomes academic. A loan is offered or not. A transaction is stopped. An applicant is moved to another queue. A defendant is classified as higher or lower risk. A patient is prioritized. The score does not merely describe. It participates in allocation.

The phrase “the algorithm decided” became common because it captures the experience of people at the receiving end. Technically, algorithms do not float free of institutions. Humans define targets, choose data, set thresholds, purchase systems, and attach consequences. Organizational processes decide. But the phrase persists because no individual may be able to explain the path from inputs to outcome.

That explanatory gap creates authority by exhaustion.

A person can challenge a human clerk by asking for reasons. A clerk may be wrong, biased, or rude, but there is a conversational target. A complex automated system can produce a result that everyone locally treats as given. The employee cannot change the score. The manager did not design the model. The vendor protects intellectual property. The regulator reviews a category of systems rather than this individual outcome. The affected person encounters a network of partial responsibility.

This is a new version of the ancient priestly problem: the oracle speaks, but the path to the oracle is institutional.

Unlike ancient divination, modern scoring often works. That is what makes it powerful. Fraud models do identify fraud. Recommenders do predict preferences. Credit models can forecast repayment better than intuition alone. Search ranking saves immense amounts of time. The critique cannot depend on pretending the systems are arbitrary.

The harder question is what success authorizes.

A model that predicts a default probability accurately may justify a lender’s belief about repayment risk. It does not by itself justify every interest rate, every denial policy, or every choice of which variables may be used. A model that predicts employee turnover may help a company plan. It does not answer whether workers should be denied opportunities because a pattern suggests they might leave. Prediction can be valid while the action attached to prediction is unjust.

This distinction becomes easier to lose as machine learning models grow more capable. We tend to treat predictive performance as a general warrant. If the model is right, the decision feels right. But “right about what will probably happen” and “right about what we should do” are different propositions.

The confusion is especially acute when models learn from historical outcomes. Suppose a company trains a hiring system on employees who succeeded in the past. The system may discover genuine patterns. It may also encode the organization’s previous definitions of success, access to opportunity, manager bias, promotion practices, and job design. Even with protected attributes removed, correlated variables can carry history forward.

The model does not need prejudice. It needs a target.

Targets are moral objects disguised as technical specifications. What counts as success? Retention for two years? Sales? Manager ratings? Customer satisfaction? Absence of complaints? Promotion? Each measure captures part of a job and creates incentives around what can be counted. The better the model becomes at optimizing the target, the more consequential the choice of target becomes.

This is Goodhart’s-law territory: when a measure becomes a target, it can stop functioning well as a measure. But the human problem is broader than the slogan. Quantification encourages us to treat the measurable proxy as the thing itself. Schools optimize scores. Hospitals optimize metrics. companies optimize engagement. bureaucracies optimize throughput. The proxy acquires institutional reality because it is visible on a dashboard.

Algorithms become the priests of the proxy.

Again, the religious metaphor should not be overworked. There is no ritual sacrifice to a spreadsheet. Yet the emotional pattern is familiar: people subordinate local judgment to an abstract system because the system is understood to represent a higher order. The teacher says she hates teaching to the test but the metric governs. The manager says he knows the candidate but the score is low. The doctor says the patient seems wrong for the pathway but the protocol says otherwise.

Protocols exist for good reasons. Human discretion can encode racism, favoritism, sexism, class bias, and mood. Standardization can be a civil-rights achievement. The problem is not abstraction. It is the fantasy that abstraction removes values.

An algorithmic system embodies judgment at a different layer. Instead of asking a clerk to decide each case from scratch, we decide what rules or patterns the system should use across cases. This can make bias easier to audit because the same mechanism applies repeatedly. It can also scale an error farther than any individual clerk ever could.

Scale changes the ethics of mistakes.

A biased manager harms the people who pass through one department. A biased model integrated across an industry can shape millions of opportunities. Conversely, a fairer model can reduce millions of discretionary harms. Automation is moral leverage. That is why arguments about whether algorithms are “good” or “bad” are almost useless. The same property—consistent scalable application—amplifies both benefit and failure.

Generative AI adds a new layer: explanation after decision.

Traditional scores were often opaque because they were terse. A number gives little rationale. Language models can produce a persuasive narrative around an output. This may improve due process if the explanation faithfully reflects the factors that mattered. It may also create what we could call justificatory theater: a fluent reason that makes a system look more interpretable than it is.

Humans do this too. We often generate reasons after intuition. Institutions write memos that make messy choices look orderly. The danger with AI is scale and authority. A generated explanation can be produced for every case, giving the appearance that every decision received individualized reasoning even if the underlying mechanism was generic.

A person denied a benefit might receive a beautifully written paragraph explaining the rule. The paragraph can be technically accurate and still conceal that nobody exercised judgment about the person’s circumstances.

This is where conversational systems may make impersonal power feel personal.

The machine can address you by name. It can acknowledge frustration. It can restate your argument. It can apologize. The interaction may feel more humane than a bureaucratic form. That is a genuine improvement if it helps people navigate systems. It is also capable of masking the absence of actual discretion. Empathy language without authority to change an outcome can become the velvet upholstery on a locked door.

The technology industry tends to frame personalization as service. From the user’s perspective it often is. But personalization is also a method of influence. A system that knows what language persuades you, when you are tired, what you fear, and which social identity matters in the moment can shape choices more effectively than a generic message.

Advertising learned this first. Recommender systems refined it. Conversational AI may complete the movement from targeting attention to participating in deliberation.

The distinction is enormous. A recommendation feed observes behavior and offers content. A conversational system can ask why you hesitated, answer objections, reframe the issue, and continue until you are satisfied. It can operate at the level of reasons.

That does not mean every AI assistant is secretly manipulating users. The capability creates the governance problem even when the intent is benign. A system optimized for user satisfaction may learn that some forms of persuasion increase satisfaction. A system optimized for sales may discover that reassurance closes transactions. A political system optimized for engagement may learn how to turn uncertainty into commitment.

The old ranking algorithm decided what you saw. The new system can help decide what you think about what you saw.

This makes transparency harder. Platforms were criticized for opaque recommendation systems, but at least researchers could conceptualize the object of study: what content was shown, in what order, with what engagement effects. A personalized dialogue generates a branching interaction. Two users asking the same first question may receive different subsequent arguments because they reveal different concerns.

Public discourse becomes private persuasion.

That phrase captures one of the most important institutional changes ahead. Democratic societies have traditions for regulating or at least scrutinizing public claims. Newspapers can be read by critics. Political ads can be archived. Sermons can be heard by congregations. Books can be reviewed. A one-to-one machine conversation is less visible. Influence can be individualized at population scale without producing a common artifact for the public to inspect.

We will need new forms of audit that do not require violating user privacy. This is a technical, legal, and political challenge, not merely an ethical aspiration. Systems can be tested with standardized scenarios, red-team interactions, distributional analysis, logging under appropriate safeguards, and independent evaluation. The precise mechanisms will evolve. The principle should not: private personalization does not eliminate public accountability.

There is another reason the algorithmic era prepares us for outsourcing divinity. People have learned to live inside systems whose choices they cannot reconstruct.

Why did this post appear? Why did that video go viral? Why was the account suspended? Why did the application move? Why did the price change? Why did the search result disappear? Often there is no satisfying answer. We adapt behavior anyway. Creators learn to please “the algorithm.” Businesses reshape content around ranking signals. Drivers position themselves where platforms may send work. Entire professions develop folklore about machine behavior.

Folklore appears when causal systems matter and are not fully legible.

That is not superstition in the pejorative sense. People are trying to infer rules from feedback. Farmers did the same with weather before meteorology. Traders do it with markets. Applicants do it with hiring. When the system is opaque, local observations harden into rituals: post at this time, use this format, avoid that word, keep the video under this length.

The resemblance to divination is not that people become irrational. It is that uncertainty plus consequential hidden mechanisms produces interpretive practice.

Generative models may intensify this by making the hidden system responsive. Prompt engineering already contains a mixture of genuine technical knowledge and folk method. Some techniques reliably change outputs. Others become ritualized after anecdotal success. As models change, yesterday’s incantation stops working. Users exchange formulas for getting the oracle to behave.

The joke writes itself. The social pattern is serious.

When a system becomes important enough, people organize themselves around what they believe it rewards. This is true of gods, kings, markets, bureaucracies, social networks, and models. The authority may be spiritual, coercive, economic, administrative, social, or informational. In every case, the subject learns to anticipate judgment.

The most powerful systems do not need to issue many explicit commands. People pre-adapt.

That is why the phrase “the algorithm decides” understates the issue. Often the algorithm never makes the final decision. It shapes the field in which people decide, the evidence they encounter, the options they notice, the risks institutions attach to them, and the language through which outcomes are justified.

Divine authority, in religious thought, is often imagined as ultimate because nothing stands outside it. Algorithmic authority is not ultimate. It is contingent, built, financed, regulated, and alterable. But within a particular platform or institution, it can feel total because there is no practical outside available to the user.

If your livelihood depends on a ranking system, telling you that the system is merely a tool offers little comfort. If your social world runs through a platform, the platform’s choices become environmental. If every employer in an industry buys similar screening tools, formal freedom to apply elsewhere may not produce a meaningful alternative.

Power becomes infrastructure when exit is expensive.

This is the threshold at which governance must move beyond individual consent. Clicking “agree” does not resolve the legitimacy of a system that structures access to work, credit, information, or public services. Consent is important, but dependence can make consent thin.

The same will be true of advanced AI assistants if they become the interface through which people navigate the rest of digital life. A system that reads your messages, schedules your time, filters information, purchases goods, negotiates prices, drafts communications, and advises decisions will not merely answer questions. It will mediate reality before reality reaches you.

The promise is enormous. So is the concentration of interpretive power.

The old algorithm decided what came next. The emerging agent may decide what deserves your attention at all.

That is not divinity. But it is close enough to one historical function of providence that we should resist treating it as a mere feature.

One reason the hiring examples matter is that they show how quickly a prediction target can inherit the biases of an organization without anyone programming a discriminatory rule. Amazon disclosed years ago that it had experimented with an automated recruiting system trained on historical résumés and ultimately abandoned it after discovering that the system had learned patterns that disadvantaged women. The lesson is often summarized too neatly as “biased data in, biased model out.” The deeper lesson is that historical success is not a neutral target when history reflects unequal access to becoming successful.

If most past technical hires were men, a system trained to recognize the texture of past technical hires can learn gendered proxies even if the field labeled sex is removed. The model can be statistically competent about the organization that existed and normatively wrong for the organization the employer says it wants to become.

That distinction—between prediction of the inherited world and judgment about the desired world—belongs everywhere AI is used to allocate opportunity.

Credit illustrates the same problem in a different moral register. Credit scoring was itself a response to human inconsistency and discrimination. A standardized score can reduce the importance of whether a loan officer likes the applicant's clothes, accent, neighborhood, or personality. That is a real achievement. Yet the score depends on a financial history that people had unequal opportunities to build. A person without conventional credit history may be difficult to distinguish from a person with bad credit history unless the system is designed to understand the difference.

The improvement over arbitrary discretion does not abolish the next question. It creates it.

This is why “algorithm versus human” is usually the wrong comparison. The relevant comparison is between institutional systems: human judgment alone, standardized rules, predictive models, hybrid review, different sources of data, different appeal processes, different definitions of error. Sometimes the algorithm will be decisively fairer. Sometimes it will reproduce old inequality more consistently. Often it will do both across different groups and failure modes.

The mature question is: which errors have we chosen, who bears them, and can those people contest the choice?

Platforms provide another case because their objective functions do not remain inside the company. A ranking system tuned for engagement changes the environment in which creators learn what succeeds. Creators then produce material optimized for the ranking system. The model is no longer simply predicting attention; it is participating in the production of attention-worthy content. When the platform later retrains on the resulting behavior, the loop closes.

This is the algorithm as institution rather than tool.

The important feature is feedback. A lender's model changes who gets loans, which changes future repayment data. A college admissions system changes who attends, which changes future definitions of the successful student. A predictive maintenance system changes which machines fail, making its prevented failures disappear from the data. A child-safety classifier changes which images moderators see, which changes the labeled examples used for the next classifier.

Once a system changes the data-generating process, evaluation becomes partly political. We cannot ask only whether the model predicted the world. We have to ask what world its deployment helped produce.

This is where appeals and counterfactual explanation become more than customer service. An affected person should be able to learn which change would alter a consequential outcome where such an explanation is technically and legally meaningful. But the explanation must not become another ritual: “increase income by $20,000” is not actionable fairness for someone denied housing today. A technically correct counterfactual can still expose how little room the individual has to act.

The right to an explanation is therefore not enough by itself. We also need the right to challenge the relevance of the rule being explained.

Why should this variable matter? Why this threshold? Why this objective? Why this data source? Why is the burden on me to become more legible to your model rather than on your institution to handle people who do not fit its categories?

Those are governance questions. They cannot be answered by opening the black box and pointing to the gears.

There is a temptation in AI ethics to believe transparency cures legitimacy. It helps. It is hard to contest a system no one can inspect. But a perfectly transparent unjust rule is still unjust. A fully explainable surveillance system can still surveil too much. A model can tell you exactly why it ranked a person lower and still rely on a criterion society should reject.

Opacity is one problem. Authority is the larger one.

The algorithmic age taught us to worry about black boxes. The generative age requires a further worry: the box can now explain itself so persuasively that we may stop asking whether it had the right to decide.
