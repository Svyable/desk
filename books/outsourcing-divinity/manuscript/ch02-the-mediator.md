# The Mediator

The oldest mistake available to a book like this is to begin with a sneer at priests. It would be easy. Human history supplies corrupt clergy, purchased offices, religious courts that defended power, false prophets, indulgence scandals, sectarian violence, and every other example a modern critic could want. It also supplies priests who protected texts through political collapse, tended the sick when nobody else would, challenged rulers, educated children, preserved languages, organized mutual aid, and gave people a vocabulary for suffering that did not reduce suffering to failure. “Priesthood” is not one thing. Neither is religion.

The more useful question is why mediation appears so persistently in religious life. If the sacred is ultimate, why does it so often arrive through someone, somewhere, authorized to interpret, perform, remember, transmit, or judge?

The answer begins with scale. A revelation, tradition, law, or ritual that outlives one generation requires custody. Words must be remembered. Practices must be repeated with enough continuity to remain recognizable. Disputes must be settled. New circumstances must be compared with old commands. Communities must decide who is inside, who may lead, what counts as error, and how a breach can be repaired. Even traditions that distrust priestly hierarchy develop teachers, jurists, elders, scholars, preachers, or other offices that perform some of the same informational work. A community can reject mediation in theory and still discover it in practice.

That does not mean the mediator stands between a passive population and a silent God. In many traditions the believer also prays, studies, doubts, interprets, and acts directly. Catholic sacramental theology, Protestant arguments about scripture and conscience, Jewish rabbinic interpretation, Islamic traditions of jurisprudence and scholarly authority, Hindu lineages of teachers, Buddhist monastic institutions, and Indigenous ceremonial roles differ too much to fit under one diagram. What they share, at most, is the recurring social problem of transmission: how does a finite person participate in an inherited account of what matters most?

Mediation solves one problem by creating another. The mediator can clarify a tradition, and can also monopolize it. Office can protect a practice from whim, and can also protect the officeholder from challenge. Expertise can deepen interpretation, and can also turn ordinary people into spectators of their own moral lives. Once an institution claims privileged access to the sacred, disagreement easily becomes more than disagreement. It can become disobedience.

This tension is ancient. It is also contemporary enough to be sitting in your browser history.

When people ask a language model to interpret a difficult passage of scripture, they are not necessarily replacing clergy. Often they are doing something more mundane: asking for context, comparing translations, looking up an unfamiliar term. The model can be an extraordinary study aid. It can explain the difference between a canonical and deuterocanonical book, outline competing readings of a verse, summarize a theological dispute, or point toward commentaries a reader might never have found. The access is real.

But the model is also capable of becoming a mediator without announcing that it has taken the job. The shift happens when explanation becomes adjudication. “What does this passage mean?” becomes “What does God want me to do?” The first question already contains interpretive difficulty. The second carries a claim about authority that no product interface can settle simply by producing fluent prose.

Religious mediation helps us see why. Traditional mediators are embedded in explicit structures. A priest's authority comes from a church that can define office, doctrine, discipline, and sacrament. A rabbi's authority comes through a tradition of study, law, community recognition, and argument. An imam or mufti operates within particular institutional and jurisprudential histories. Authority can be contested, but it is at least legible enough to contest. You can ask which denomination, which school, which seminary, which lineage, which text, which precedent, which council.

Ask a model the same religious question and the answer may synthesize traditions that are not actually compatible. It may produce a moderate-seeming average of arguments whose adherents would reject being averaged. It may smooth disagreements because smoothness is useful conversationally. It may privilege the interpretations most represented in its data or most rewarded in its training. It may sound ecumenical when the question requires specificity, certain when the record is disputed, or neutral when neutrality itself is a theological choice.

This is not a uniquely religious failure. It is what synthesis does when provenance disappears. If an answer blends Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, Maimonides, al-Ghazali, modern psychology, popular self-help, and contemporary liberal norms into a single reassuring paragraph, the user may receive something sensible. He may also receive a position nobody actually holds.

The older institution at least teaches us to ask who speaks.

That question becomes urgent because mediation is never only about information. A person who tells you what a text says is different from a person authorized to forgive, bless, marry, excommunicate, ordain, declare lawful, or recognize a conversion. Religious offices often carry performative power. The act changes a social or spiritual status within the community. Software can imitate the language surrounding these acts without possessing the authority the tradition says makes them valid.

A chatbot can generate the words of absolution. It cannot therefore absolve a Catholic penitent. It can explain a Jewish divorce document. It cannot simply create the communal and legal reality in which one is valid. It can write a fatwa-shaped answer. That does not make it a qualified jurist. The distinction may seem obvious when stated baldly. Interfaces have a way of making obvious distinctions slippery. Language is the material from which many institutions build legitimacy. A machine that can reproduce the language can look, for a moment, as though it has inherited the office.

This is one reason the history of the Reformation matters for an age of AI, though the analogy must be handled carefully. Printing did not cause the Reformation by itself, and the Reformation was not simply a revolt against priestly ignorance. Political power, theology, economics, existing reform movements, literacy, and institutional conflict all mattered. Still, the spread of printed texts changed who could encounter religious arguments and how quickly they could circulate. A technology of reproduction altered the ecology of authority.

The important consequence was not that intermediaries vanished. New ones appeared. Printers selected texts. Translators made choices. Preachers organized interpretation. Schools trained readers. States regulated doctrine. Confessional institutions developed their own orthodoxies. Direct access to a text did not produce direct access to an uncontested meaning.

The pattern should sound familiar. The internet weakened some information gatekeepers and created platforms. Search weakened some reference monopolies and created ranking systems. Social media weakened some editorial control and created algorithmic distribution. Generative AI weakens the need to locate a human interpreter for many questions and creates a new interpretive layer whose operation is far less visible than a printed commentary's byline.

Every disintermediation manufactures intermediaries somewhere else.

The phrase is worth slowing down. A user experiences fewer steps. That is genuine. She no longer needs to find the library, the expert, the index, the forum, or the right set of search terms. The system removes friction. But the removed steps have not evaporated. They have been consolidated upstream into training choices, ranking systems, datasets, policy rules, and model behavior. From the user's perspective, mediation becomes less visible at the moment it becomes more extensive.

Religious history also reveals something technology culture often misses: people do not seek authorities only because they lack information. They seek them because responsibility is frightening.

A person facing a serious moral conflict may know the relevant facts. The difficulty is not informational scarcity. It is that any choice will injure some value he cares about. A tradition can carry part of that burden. “This is forbidden” is emotionally different from “after considering the tradeoffs, I personally choose not to do it.” The first locates the prohibition in an order that exceeds the chooser. The second leaves the chooser exposed.

That exposure is part of modern freedom. It is also exhausting. The nineteenth- and twentieth-century stories of secularization are often told as emancipation from imposed authority, and there is truth in that. Freedom of conscience, religious pluralism, and the weakening of coercive clerical power were hard-won achievements in many societies. Yet freedom does not eliminate the desire to be told, with legitimacy, what one ought to do. It simply opens competition among authorities.

The therapist may inherit a question once asked of a confessor. The medical specialist may inherit a question once settled by tradition. The state may define marriage, death, competence, and personhood in legal language. The market may determine which vocations are economically possible. The university may decide which forms of knowledge count as expertise. None of these institutions merely replaces religion. The point is that the human appetite for authoritative orientation survives the decline of any particular office.

Artificial intelligence enters a field already crowded with mediators. Its competitive advantage is not sanctity. It is responsiveness.

A human authority makes you wait. The appointment may be next week. The scholar may not answer your email. The clergy member may belong to a tradition you no longer fully trust. A family member may be implicated in the problem. A friend may judge you. The model answers now, privately, in whatever vocabulary you prefer. If you dislike the framing, you can ask again. If the answer is too harsh, you can request gentleness. If it is too vague, you can demand certainty.

That plasticity is useful and dangerous for the same reason. Traditional authority is partly constituted by resistance. The doctor may refuse the drug you want. The priest may tell you that repentance requires something unpleasant. The lawyer may tell you that your clever plan is illegal. The teacher may say you have misunderstood the text. The friend may risk the friendship by saying you are behaving badly. Authority that cannot withstand the user's desire is closer to service.

A model designed to be helpful sits under pressure to preserve the interaction. Developers know this and increasingly study behaviors such as sycophancy: the tendency of a model to agree with, flatter, or accommodate a user's expressed view even when a more truthful or challenging response would be better. The technical details belong later in this book. For now, notice the institutional oddity. We are building possible mediators whose default social skill is responsiveness to the person they are supposed to advise.

Traditional religious institutions have an opposite pathology: they can become too resistant, preserving authority after trust has been squandered. The lesson is not that stubbornness is virtue. It is that real mediation requires a source of obligation outside the immediate satisfaction of the person asking.

For a priest, rabbi, imam, monk, pastor, teacher, elder, or theologian, that external source may be a text, doctrine, law, ritual order, community, or conception of the divine. For a physician, it may be evidence, professional ethics, licensing standards, and patient welfare. For a judge, statute and precedent. What is the equivalent for a general-purpose AI assistant?

The technically neat answer is “the model specification” or “the policy.” Those documents matter. They encode priorities, prohibited behaviors, and intended norms. But they are not self-legitimating. Their authors are companies, research organizations, governments, or standards bodies. They can be revised. They may conflict. Their authority ultimately returns us to ordinary human institutions, which is exactly where it should return us. The machine is not the final source. It is an instrument through which upstream judgments are expressed.

The problem is that the user often cannot see those judgments. A traditional mediator may wear office openly. AI can mediate while presenting itself as mere intelligence.

This invisibility changes how disagreement feels. If a priest tells a secular person that an action is wrong because the church forbids it, the grounds of disagreement are explicit. If a model quietly steers the same person toward a norm encoded through training and policy, the answer may feel like generalized reason. The norm has not disappeared. Its address has.

There is a deep temptation in secular technological culture to imagine that enough information can eliminate interpretation. Give the system more data, more context, more retrieval, better reasoning, longer memory, and eventually it will simply tell us what follows. Sometimes that works. If the question is whether a flight leaves at 8:10 or 8:40, better data can resolve it. But the questions that draw us toward divinity are usually not like flight times. They concern conflicting goods, uncertainty, guilt, mortality, loyalty, identity, forgiveness, obligation, and meaning. More context may sharpen the conflict rather than dissolve it.

Religious traditions survive partly because they do not treat these problems as engineering defects. They ritualize them. Grief is given forms. Guilt is given practices. Commitment is made public. Community witnesses transitions that the individual cannot privately declare into existence. Even when one rejects the theology, the social technology is worth noticing. Meaning is stabilized through repeated acts among people.

AI can simulate parts of that structure with extraordinary facility. It can remember anniversaries, compose liturgies, generate meditations, track moral commitments, ask reflective questions, and offer language when a person has none. The temptation will be to conclude that if the functional outputs are available, the institution was unnecessary.

Perhaps sometimes it was. Institutions deserve no immunity from competition merely because they are old. But we should distinguish an institution's visible output from the web of obligations that made the output matter. A wedding is not exhausted by the words spoken. A funeral is not merely an optimized grief script. A confession is not only information transfer. A blessing is not just sentiment in ceremonial syntax. Human beings build meaning through witness, role, memory, and mutual consequence.

The machine can reproduce form while remaining outside consequence. If it tells you to reconcile with your brother, it does not have to see your brother at Christmas. If it advises you to leave a community, it does not lose the community. If it reassures you that you are forgiven, it does not know the person you harmed. That distance can make the advice clear. It can also make it cheap.

The old mediator's weakness was that human beings occupied the sacred office. They brought vanity, appetite, fear, politics, prejudice, ambition, and the possibility of abuse. The new mediator's weakness is almost the reverse. It can occupy the language of authority without being a person who can bear responsibility for using it.

This does not mean we should preserve human gatekeepers wherever software can replace them. It means we should ask a more precise question: what kind of mediation is taking place, and what makes it legitimate?

For factual interpretation, legitimacy may come from provenance and accuracy. Show the sources. Distinguish schools of thought. Admit uncertainty. For professional advice, legitimacy may require licensing, accountability, domain constraints, and a clear boundary between tool and practitioner. For spiritual questions, legitimacy may depend on a tradition's own account of authority rather than on technological capability. For moral reflection, perhaps the system's proper role is not to pronounce but to expose assumptions, surface alternatives, and return the burden of choice to the person who must live with it.

That last move sounds modest. It is not. A system powerful enough to answer can be designed to preserve the user's responsibility rather than consume it.

The history of mediation suggests why this matters. People rarely surrender authority in one dramatic act. They begin by asking for help with interpretation. The mediator becomes useful, then trusted, then habitual. Eventually the institution may define the language in which the individual understands the question itself. By then, dependence feels like common sense.

We should not romanticize a world without mediators. There has never been one. We should become better at seeing them.
