# The New Priesthood

The people closest to powerful AI systems occupy an uncomfortable social position. They know more than the public, often less than the public imagines, and far less than anyone would like.

An engineer can understand the architecture, training process, evaluation suite, and known failure modes of a model without being able to explain why one particular internal representation formed or why one response appeared instead of another. A safety researcher can identify categories of risk without knowing how a future system will behave outside tests. An executive can see deployment data unavailable to outsiders while remaining dependent on technical teams for interpretation. A regulator may possess legal authority and less technical context than the company being regulated.

This asymmetry creates something that looks, from a distance, like priesthood: a small class interprets an opaque power for everyone else.

The analogy is tempting and dangerous. AI researchers are not clergy. A laboratory is not a church. Model weights are not sacred mysteries. Most engineers would be horrified to be treated as spiritual authorities, and many spend considerable effort making systems more measurable and understandable. The point is structural. When consequential systems cannot be directly inspected by most people, societies depend on interpreters.

The interpreters acquire authority whether they ask for it or not.

Every technological field has experts, but AI carries an unusual combination of technical opacity and general social reach. A specialist in turbine design may possess knowledge the public lacks, yet the turbine’s role is comparatively bounded. A frontier model can be used in education, medicine, law, defense, finance, intimate relationships, media, software, research, and government. The experts explaining it are therefore asked questions that outrun their technical training.

How dangerous is this capability?

Will it transform employment?

Could it undermine democracy?

Is the system conscious?

Should children use it?

Will it improve science?

Could it become uncontrollable?

What rules should govern it?

Technical knowledge is relevant to all of these questions. It is sufficient for none.

This is where a new priesthood can form accidentally. Society asks a technologist to interpret the machine and begins treating the technologist as an interpreter of the future.

The mechanism is familiar from previous technologies. Nuclear physicists became public figures in debates that included strategy, ethics, geopolitics, and human survival. Economists move from analyzing systems to advising governments on choices that embed values. Epidemiologists become public authorities during a pandemic because the technical facts matter urgently, even though policy requires judgments beyond epidemiology.

The transition is understandable. If one group possesses scarce knowledge, people ask it broader questions. The responsible expert marks the boundary. The irresponsible expert enjoys the expansion.

AI makes boundary marking difficult because capability itself is contested. Companies possess private evaluations. Researchers disagree about what benchmarks measure. Systems change quickly. External investigators may have limited access. Marketing rewards dramatic claims. Fundraising rewards scale. Safety advocacy can reward alarming scenarios. Skepticism can reward contrarian confidence. Every side has incentives.

Under these conditions, public understanding depends heavily on trust in institutions rather than direct verification.

That is not automatically bad. Scientific and technical civilization cannot operate without institutional trust. But trust should be earned through practices that make error discoverable: documentation, independent evaluation, reproducibility where possible, incident reporting, whistleblower protection, external research access, clear limitations, and a willingness to revise claims.

Mystique is the enemy.

The technology industry sometimes cultivates mystique even while speaking the language of science. Product launches announce capabilities with cinematic restraint. Executives make sweeping predictions about the future of intelligence. Model names imply generations, reasoning, agents, or intelligence levels. Researchers use benchmark tables that most users cannot interpret. The public receives a mixture of careful technical claims and civilizational rhetoric.

That mixture can make laboratories feel like temples where tomorrow is already visible to the initiated.

The rhetoric cuts both directions. Utopian narratives portray model builders as stewards of abundance, curing disease, democratizing education, accelerating discovery, and perhaps solving problems that politics has failed to solve. Catastrophic narratives portray the same builders as custodians of a force capable of ending human control. In either version, the laboratory sits near the center of history.

Sometimes it may. The capabilities are consequential enough to justify serious attention. The danger is that claims about importance become claims to authority.

If a company says its technology could transform civilization, society should not respond by giving the company more unilateral power over the transformation.

This sounds obvious. Yet a subtle logic pushes the other way. The systems are technically complex, therefore experts must govern them. The leading experts work at the companies building them. Therefore the builders must have unusual standing in deciding how they are governed.

Expert participation is necessary. Builder sovereignty is not.

A pharmaceutical company knows its drug better than most regulators before approval. That knowledge does not make the company the sole authority on acceptable evidence, labeling, or market access. Aviation manufacturers possess extraordinary expertise; public safety still requires independent investigation and oversight. Financial institutions understand their systems; societies learned repeatedly that self-assessed risk is not enough.

AI should not be exempt from the institutional lessons of every other powerful industry merely because the technology is new.

The priesthood analogy becomes more literal around interpretability. Deep learning systems can be studied at many levels: architecture, training data, input-output behavior, intermediate activations, learned features, causal interventions, evaluations, red-team results, and more. Researchers are developing increasingly sophisticated techniques for understanding what models represent and how behaviors arise. This work is important precisely because the systems are not transparent in the ordinary software sense.

A conventional program can often be traced through explicit instructions written by programmers. A learned model contains behavior distributed across parameters shaped through training. The creators design the process that produces the model without manually specifying every resulting rule.

That gap creates a category of knowledge that sounds theological if described carelessly: the creators do not fully understand the creation.

We should resist the drama. Human beings routinely create systems whose aggregate behavior exceeds individual comprehension: economies, cities, legal codes, ecosystems altered by agriculture, even large software stacks. Partial understanding does not imply magic. It does imply limits.

The problem comes when public rhetoric oscillates between “it is just math” and “it may become godlike,” depending on which framing is convenient.

If critics raise concerns about agency, companies may emphasize that the system is a statistical model. If investors ask about opportunity, presentations may emphasize emergent reasoning and extraordinary capability. If regulators ask for explanation, technical complexity becomes a reason to defer. If customers ask for reliability, benchmark scores become evidence of maturity.

No single framing is always false. Their strategic alternation can obscure accountability.

A trustworthy institution should be able to say what the system is for the purpose of the decision at hand. Is it a product subject to consumer protection? A model subject to evaluation? Infrastructure subject to resilience requirements? A communications medium? A high-risk decision system? A research artifact? Different uses may require different law.

Calling the system “intelligence” does not settle its institutional category.

The new priesthood is also cultural. A relatively small technical community develops vocabulary that outsiders must learn: scaling, inference, alignment, fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, context windows, hallucination, embeddings, evals, mechanistic interpretability, chain-of-thought, agents. Specialized vocabulary is normal and necessary. It becomes exclusionary when jargon is used to convert value disputes into technical inevitabilities.

Suppose a company says deployment is necessary because “the scaling curve supports it.” That may be evidence about capability or cost. It does not answer whether deployment should occur under current safeguards. Suppose critics say a model is “unaligned.” That may refer to a measurable behavioral problem or to a broad philosophical fear. The same word can carry different levels of claim.

Translation is therefore a governance function.

Journalists, independent researchers, civil-society organizations, academics, standards bodies, and technically literate public officials matter because no single interpretive class should monopolize the story of the machine. Healthy societies build competing interpreters.

Religious history offers a useful analogy here. The translation of sacred texts into vernacular languages shifted authority partly because ordinary people could encounter texts more directly. It did not eliminate interpretation. It widened the field of interpreters.

AI needs its vernacularization.

That does not mean publishing every dangerous technical detail without judgment or forcing companies to disclose legitimate trade secrets. It means the public should have ways to understand capabilities, limitations, incidents, and governance choices that do not depend entirely on corporate narration.

Independent evaluations are one such mechanism. So are standardized reporting, incident databases, academic access, protected whistleblowing, and adversarial audits. None produces perfect transparency. Together they reduce dependence on revelation from the builder.

The public also needs epistemic discipline. Outsiders can romanticize independence. A researcher with no internal access may be free of company incentives and missing crucial information. A journalist may identify institutional conflicts and misunderstand a technical result. A regulator may be independent and under-resourced. Competing interpreters are not automatically correct.

The point is contestability.

Authority becomes dangerous when there is no legitimate path to challenge it.

This is why corporate “trust us” messaging fails even when the company contains honorable people. Trust in high-stakes institutions should not require a judgment that leaders are saints. Governance exists so decent behavior does not depend on sainthood.

The same is true inside laboratories. An engineer may notice a safety problem. Can she stop deployment? A policy team may object to a feature. Does revenue override them? A red team may find a dangerous capability. Who sees the result? A researcher may disagree with leadership’s public characterization. Is dissent protected?

Internal governance determines whether expertise can resist power.

Priesthoods historically faced similar questions: can a lower-ranking member challenge hierarchy? Who interprets doctrine? What happens when the institution’s interest conflicts with its professed values? The comparison is not meant to equate sins. It reminds us that expertise without institutional independence can become decoration.

AI companies increasingly publish principles, constitutions, model specifications, safety frameworks, preparedness plans, and governance structures. These documents can be meaningful constraints. They can also become liturgy: public statements recited while operational decisions follow another logic.

The difference is testability.

A principle that says safety matters should produce observable consequences when safety conflicts with schedule or revenue. A commitment to transparency should survive embarrassing results. A claim that a board has oversight should be matched by authority, information, and independence. Governance becomes real when it can change an outcome.

This is also where religion offers a surprisingly secular insight: institutions are shaped by what they worship.

Strip the word of theology for a moment and ask what receives ultimate priority when priorities collide. Growth? Safety? shareholder value? scientific discovery? national advantage? user welfare? prestige? access? A company can list many values. Crisis reveals hierarchy.

There is no shame in having commercial objectives. Companies need revenue. The problem is pretending commercial structure disappears because the product is intellectually grand.

The new priesthood may therefore be less a class of engineers than a broader alliance of institutions that interpret intelligence for society: laboratories, cloud providers, chip companies, universities, governments, media, investors, standards bodies, and security agencies. Each sees part of the system. Their interactions decide what becomes normal.

This network can provide checks if interests differ. It can also produce shared assumptions. If every actor benefits from rapid AI adoption, the debate may concern how to adopt rather than whether a particular use should exist.

A mature society needs institutions capable of saying no to a capability that works.

That is rarer than opposing a capability that fails. Failure supplies its own argument. Success creates constituencies.

Consider facial recognition, predictive policing, biometric inference, automated persuasion, or workplace surveillance. A system can become more accurate and therefore more controversial, not less, because increased accuracy makes uses feasible that were previously impractical. Technical progress can intensify the governance question.

AI labs are naturally rewarded for making systems work. Society needs other institutions tasked with deciding what working systems should be allowed to do.

This is not anti-innovation. Roads need brakes because cars move. Medicine needs ethics because treatments work. Finance needs regulation because credit is useful and leverage is powerful. The need for limits is often evidence that a technology matters.

What we need is less a replacement priesthood than an epistemic separation of powers.

The builder knows things the regulator does not. The deployer knows things the builder does not. The teacher, physician, lawyer, soldier, or caseworker knows what happens when the system meets a particular practice. The affected user knows failure from the receiving end. An independent evaluator can test claims without carrying the same commercial incentives. A journalist can connect incidents that each institution sees as isolated. A court can ask a different question altogether: not whether the system performs well, but whether its use is lawful and whether the person affected received a fair process.

No one of these viewpoints deserves supremacy merely because it is independent. Their value lies in being differently situated.

This is what separation of powers does at its best. It does not assume legislators are wise and judges foolish, or courts virtuous and executives corrupt. It assumes concentrated authority creates blind spots and gives institutions different powers, incentives, information, and ways to interrupt one another.

AI governance needs the epistemic version.

A model card written by the builder can say what was tested. It should not be the only account of what the model can do. An external benchmark can expose a weakness. It should not become scripture either. A red team can find adversarial failures without knowing ordinary user behavior. Incident reports can reveal failures in deployment that pre-release testing never imagined. People harmed by the system may understand something essential before they possess the technical vocabulary to prove it.

The architecture works when these kinds of knowledge can collide without one side dismissing the others as illegitimate.

This requires standing, not just voice.

A whistleblower who may speak but cannot stop an unsafe process has voice. A safety team whose sign-off is optional has voice. An external researcher allowed to publish after the product ships has voice. Standing means the objection enters a process capable of changing the decision.

That distinction matters because modern institutions are very good at collecting feedback. Feedback is not governance. A comment box is not an appeal. A listening session is not a veto. A red-team report is not a safety constraint if the deployment decision can ignore it without consequence.

The same problem appears in public transparency. Companies can release large quantities of information and still control the categories through which the public sees the system. Thirty benchmark scores may reveal less than one well-documented failure in a domain that matters. A glossy safety report can be factually correct and still leave the reader unable to tell which unresolved issue almost delayed launch.

This is why claims about frontier systems need grammar.

Measured: the model scored this result under these conditions.

Observed: users behaved this way in deployment.

Inferred: we think this mechanism explains the behavior.

Forecast: we expect a future capability or social effect.

Normative: we believe deployment is worth the tradeoff.

Those sentences can all appear in the same report and carry radically different warrant. The public debate becomes confused when a measured benchmark result is used to support a forecast, and the forecast is then treated as a reason for a normative conclusion, without the transitions being named.

Religious institutions learned to distinguish scripture from commentary, doctrine from speculation, office from personal opinion—imperfectly, unevenly, and after enormous conflict. Technical institutions need comparable humility about the status of their own utterances.

There is another reason to distribute interpretation. Frontier capability claims can become self-fulfilling socially even when they remain uncertain technically. If employers believe coding is nearly automated, they may change hiring. If schools believe writing assistance is universal, they may change assessment. If investors believe a model will transform an industry, capital flows before the transformation is proved. The claim begins altering the environment in which the claim will later be judged.

Builders therefore possess a kind of agenda-setting power merely by describing what they think they have built.

That power should not be censored into silence. It should be counterweighted by institutions capable of asking different questions.

Not only: What can the model do?

Also: Compared with what? For whom? Under what conditions? With what failure distribution? Who bears the error? What changes after deployment? What claim here is measurement and what claim is aspiration?

This is not bureaucratic suspicion for its own sake. It is how a society turns extraordinary private expertise into public knowledge without demanding that citizens become machine-learning researchers.

A civilization cannot personally inspect every bridge, drug, airplane, bank, reactor, and model. It can build institutions so that no single interpreter has to be holy.

That is the opposite of priesthood.

The priesthood metaphor should therefore end by turning on itself. The goal is not to identify the new priests and replace them with better priests. It is to avoid constructing an epistemic order in which any small group has to be believed because nobody else can see.

Powerful AI will always require expertise. The democratic question is whether expertise becomes a service within accountable institutions or a source of authority above them.

The engineer should be able to say, “Here is what the model does, here is what we know, here is what we do not know, and here is the evidence.”

She should not have to say what humanity ought to become.

That part was never an engineering requirement.
