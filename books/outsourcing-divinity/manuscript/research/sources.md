# Research Source Ledger — Outsourcing Divinity

This is the working source record for *Outsourcing Divinity*. It supports factual claims and provides direct paths back to primary or authoritative material. It is not a list of authorities whose conclusions the book adopts wholesale. The manuscript distinguishes source-backed facts from analogy, philosophical interpretation, and speculation.

## Source hierarchy

1. Primary texts and official institutional materials for what an institution, author, law, or framework says.
2. Original peer-reviewed or preprint research for empirical claims about AI behavior.
3. Durable scholarly reference works for historical, philosophical, and theological orientation.
4. High-quality reporting only where the reporting itself is needed to document a contemporary event.

Claims about living AI systems should be rechecked immediately before publication. Theology is attributed to particular traditions rather than flattened into a universal religious position.

## Chapter 1 — The Oracle in the Box

- UNESCO, **Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence** (adopted November 2021). Use: human dignity, responsibility/accountability, transparency, and the principle that AI should not displace ultimate human responsibility. https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/recommendation
- NIST, **Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)**, NIST AI 100-1 (2023). Use: governance, risk management, trustworthiness characteristics, and the Govern/Map/Measure/Manage framework. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1
- Plato, **Euthyphro**. Use: historical framing for the Euthyphro problem; cite a scholarly edition in production if a quotation is introduced. No extended quotation is used in the draft.

## Chapter 2 — The Mediator

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, **Religious Authority** and related entries on philosophy of religion and testimony. Use: orientation to the diversity of claims about religious authority; not evidence for a single cross-religious theory. https://plato.stanford.edu/
- Encyclopaedia Britannica / standard histories of the Reformation and printing should be replaced in final notes by specialist histories if specific causal claims are expanded. The draft intentionally states that printing did not by itself cause the Reformation.
- Vatican/Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and Dicastery for Culture and Education, **Antiqua et nova: Note on the Relationship Between Artificial Intelligence and Human Intelligence**, 28 January 2025. Use: a specific Catholic institutional account of AI as a human artifact and of human intelligence/dignity; not presented as a universal Christian view. https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2025/01/28/250128b.html

## Chapter 3 — The Written God

- Plato, **Phaedrus**. Use: classical background to philosophical concerns about writing and memory. Any final direct quotation requires edition-level citation.
- Elizabeth L. Eisenstein, **The Printing Press as an Agent of Change** (Cambridge University Press, 1979). Use: scholarly background on printing and institutional change; the draft avoids claiming a single-cause theory of the Reformation.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, entries on **Hermeneutics** and **Testimony**. Use: orientation to interpretation, linguistic mediation, and the epistemology of relying on others. https://plato.stanford.edu/

## Chapter 4 — The State Above Us

- Max Weber, **Economy and Society**. Use: background on bureaucracy, legitimate authority, and rule-bound administration; consult publication edition before adding quotations.
- UNESCO, **Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence**. Use: human oversight and ultimate human responsibility. https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/recommendation
- NIST, **AI RMF 1.0**. Use: organizational governance and accountability. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1

## Chapter 5 — The Market Knows

- F. A. Hayek, **“The Use of Knowledge in Society,”** *American Economic Review* 35(4), 1945, 519–530. Use: dispersed knowledge and the coordinating function of prices. https://www.aeaweb.org/aer/top20/35.4.519-530.pdf
- Frank H. Knight, **Risk, Uncertainty and Profit** (1921). Use: conceptual background distinguishing measurable risk from deeper uncertainty.
- For prediction-market claims in later editing, prefer original market-design research and the actual contract rules of any named market rather than treating market prices as pure probabilities.

## Chapter 6 — The Expert Problem

- Alvin I. Goldman, **“Experts: Which Ones Should You Trust?”** *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 63(1), 2001. Use: epistemic dependence and the problem of choosing among experts.
- NIST, **AI RMF 1.0**. Use: trustworthiness as a system-level governance and risk-management problem rather than a property inferred from one benchmark. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1
- Raja Parasuraman and Dietrich H. Manzey, work on **automation bias/complacency**. Use: background to human over-reliance on automated aids; final edition should cite the specific paper used for any quantitative claim.

## Chapter 7 — The Algorithm Decides

- Solon Barocas and Andrew D. Selbst, **“Big Data's Disparate Impact,”** *California Law Review* 104 (2016). Use: how apparently neutral data-driven systems can reproduce patterns of disadvantage.
- Andrew D. Selbst et al., **“Fairness and Abstraction in Sociotechnical Systems,”** FAT* / FAccT 2019. Use: limits of treating fairness as a property of a model abstracted from deployment context. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3287560.3287598
- NIST, **AI RMF 1.0**. Use: transparency, explainability, fairness with harmful bias managed, and human-AI interaction. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1

## Chapter 8 — The Confessor

- Joseph Weizenbaum, **“ELIZA—A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine,”** *Communications of the ACM* 9(1), 1966, 36–45. Use: the original ELIZA system, scripts including DOCTOR, and Weizenbaum's discussion of human-computer conversation. https://doi.org/10.1145/365153.365168
- Joseph Weizenbaum, **Computer Power and Human Reason** (W. H. Freeman, 1976). Use: historical context for Weizenbaum's later ethical concerns about computational judgment.
- Helen Nissenbaum, **Privacy in Context** (Stanford University Press, 2010). Use: contextual integrity as background for why disclosure to one relationship or context is not equivalent to unrestricted reuse.

## Chapter 9 — The Machine That Agrees

- Mrinank Sharma et al., **“Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models,”** arXiv:2310.13548 (2023). Use: empirical evidence that human-feedback-trained assistants can match user beliefs over truthful answers and that preference signals can reward sycophantic behavior. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548
- Related contemporary sycophancy and model-behavior research should be refreshed immediately before publication because model behavior changes quickly.

## Chapter 10 — Moral Machines

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, entries on **Consequentialism**, **Deontological Ethics**, **Virtue Ethics**, and **Moral Responsibility**. Use: orientation to major traditions of moral reasoning; the draft does not claim they exhaust moral philosophy. https://plato.stanford.edu/
- UNESCO, **Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence**. Use: human rights, dignity, oversight, responsibility, and accountability as a global governance framework. https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/recommendation

## Chapter 11 — Prayer Without Transcendence

- Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and Dicastery for Culture and Education, **Antiqua et nova**, 28 January 2025. Use: one authoritative Catholic treatment distinguishing artificial systems from the integral human intelligence of embodied persons and discussing responsible AI use. https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2025/01/28/250128b.html
- For claims about Jewish, Islamic, Buddhist, Hindu, or other religious traditions in final copyediting, use tradition-specific primary or specialist sources. The draft intentionally avoids attributing a single theory of prayer or authority to “religion.”

## Chapter 12 — Manufactured Providence

- Daniel Kahneman, Paul Slovic, and Amos Tversky (eds.), **Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases** (Cambridge University Press, 1982). Use: historical background on judgment under uncertainty and retrospective/heuristic errors.
- Robert K. Merton, **“The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy,”** *Antioch Review* 8(2), 1948. Use: conceptual background for predictions or beliefs that help generate their own confirmation.
- For predictive systems in consequential domains, pair model-performance evidence with research on interventions and feedback loops rather than assuming prediction is causally passive.

## Chapter 13 — The New Priesthood

- NIST, **AI RMF 1.0** and associated Playbook. Use: independent evaluation, organizational governance, and operational risk management. https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework
- NIST, **AI RMF Playbook**. Use: the four operational functions Govern, Map, Measure, Manage and the idea that the framework is contextual rather than a one-size-fits-all checklist. https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/nist-ai-rmf-playbook
- The chapter's “priesthood” language is explicitly structural analogy, not a claim that engineers constitute clergy.

## Chapter 14 — The Idol That Works

- *Antiqua et nova* (2025). Use: a contemporary Catholic source on human intelligence, dignity, technological responsibility, and the limits of equating artificial and human intelligence. https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2025/01/28/250128b.html
- Shoshana Zuboff, **The Age of Surveillance Capitalism** (PublicAffairs, 2019). Use: background to economic incentives around behavioral data and prediction; specific empirical claims should be independently verified where possible.
- Data-center resource claims should be sourced to operators, grid/water authorities, or peer-reviewed lifecycle work if quantitative figures are added. The current draft stays qualitative.

## Chapter 15 — Who Gets to Say No?

- UNESCO, **Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence**. Use: responsibility, accountability, auditability, human oversight and determination, and human-rights framing. https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/recommendation

- NIST, **Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile**, NIST AI 600-1 (2024). Use in final technical review for generative-AI-specific risk practices. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1

## Chapter 16 — The Right to Remain Human

- UNESCO, **Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence**. Use: centrality of human rights and human dignity in AI governance. https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/recommendation
- *Antiqua et nova* (2025). Use: a specifically Catholic account of human dignity and embodied, relational intelligence in contrast to computational performance. https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2025/01/28/250128b.html
- Final education claims should be checked against current learning-science research on generative AI rather than relying on early adoption anecdotes.

## Chapter 17 — Taking Back Judgment

- NIST, **AI RMF 1.0**. Use: lifecycle governance, measurement, management, and the need to address risks in context. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1
- UNESCO, **Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence**. Use: preservation of human responsibility and accountability. https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/recommendation
- Research on automation bias and human factors should be used for any stronger empirical claim about rubber-stamping, skill decay, or override behavior.

## Chapter 18 — No God in the Machine

- This chapter synthesizes the book's argument. Its factual foundations are the sources above; its conclusion about delegation, responsibility, and human agency is philosophical interpretation rather than an empirical finding.
- Claims about possible future machine consciousness remain explicitly conditional. The manuscript does not assert that current AI systems are conscious or that future systems cannot be.

## Pre-publication verification queue

Before release:

1. Re-run all time-sensitive AI behavior claims against current model research.
2. Replace any broad history reference with edition-level or specialist scholarship where copyediting introduces specific dates, quotations, or causal claims.
3. Verify every direct quotation against the cited edition and permissions policy.
4. Add page/section locators for print endnotes.
5. Check every URL and DOI.
6. Review tradition-specific theological claims with sources from that tradition rather than relying on cross-tradition generalization.
7. Review high-stakes AI governance passages against the then-current versions of NIST and other relevant frameworks.
8. Keep documented fact, credible interpretation, and speculation visibly distinct in the final manuscript.
