# The Expert Problem

The modern person is told to think for himself and spends most of the day depending on people he has never met.

The plane stays in the air because engineers, inspectors, manufacturers, pilots, air-traffic controllers, software developers, weather forecasters, and maintenance crews did work the passenger cannot personally verify. The medicine in the cabinet depends on chemistry, clinical trials, manufacturing standards, regulatory review, supply chains, and prescribing judgment. The phone assumes physics most users could not derive. The clean water in the tap is an institutional achievement disguised as a utility bill.

There is no embarrassment in this. Expertise is one of the ways a civilization escapes the limits of individual life. The problem begins when we confuse necessary dependence with simple obedience.

Expertise occupies an awkward position in democratic culture because it is both indispensable and unequal. A cardiologist really does know things about a failing heart that most patients do not. A structural engineer knows things about load paths that voters cannot settle by referendum. A virologist, an actuary, a cryptographer, a trial lawyer, and a materials scientist all possess domain knowledge that took years to acquire. Pretending every opinion is equal in every domain is not freedom. It is a refusal to notice competence.

Yet experts are human, institutions have incentives, disciplines develop blind spots, and credentialed communities can become overconfident. Expertise answers some questions better than others. The epidemiologist can estimate transmission dynamics; she cannot derive the politically acceptable cost of closing schools from epidemiology alone. The engineer can estimate a bridge’s safety margin; he cannot decide by engineering which neighborhood should bear the disruption of construction. The economist can model an expected effect; the model does not decide which distribution of gains and losses is just.

The expert problem is therefore not whether to trust experts. We have no choice but to trust expertise somewhere. The problem is knowing where the expertise ends.

Modern institutions often blur that boundary because technical language has a useful authority. A choice framed as “evidence based” sounds different from a choice framed as “we prefer these values under these constraints.” The first can be defended with citations. The second must survive politics. It is tempting to smuggle the second into the first.

Artificial intelligence magnifies this temptation because it can perform expert language without holding an expert office.

A model can explain renal physiology, draft a contract clause, discuss medieval theology, critique a statistical method, and write software in one session. The range is unprecedented. It destabilizes a social cue people have used for centuries: narrowness. Human experts are usually experts in something. Their limitation is visible in the shape of their career. A brilliant tax lawyer may be useless at oncology. A physicist does not receive automatic authority in constitutional law. The model moves across those boundaries without changing seats.

That breadth can create the impression of a generalized expert: not a cardiologist or historian but an intelligence that simply knows.

This impression is reinforced by tone. Expert prose often carries markers of caution, qualification, technical vocabulary, and structured explanation. Models reproduce those markers fluently. The surface resemblance can be so strong that a user may forget to ask whether the answer came from reliable source retrieval, statistical pattern completion, stale training material, or a fabricated citation.

The risk is not unique to naive users. Professionals can over-trust systems when the output falls just outside their own competence. A physician may recognize a medical hallucination but accept a legal one. A lawyer may challenge the legal analysis and trust the cybersecurity advice. The better educated the user, the more domains exist in which he knows enough to formulate a sophisticated question and not enough to detect a sophisticated error.

This creates a new version of an old social problem: borrowed authority.

A policymaker cites the scientist. The scientist relies on the statistician. The statistician relies on a data pipeline. The data pipeline depends on classifications made by institutions in the field. Each link may be reasonable. By the time the conclusion reaches the public, the chain has been compressed into “the evidence shows.” The phrase can be accurate. It can also conceal uncertainty accumulated at every step.

AI compresses the chain further. The model can turn a complicated literature into a paragraph without forcing the user to see the chain at all.

The obvious response is citation. In consequential uses, models should expose sources and make verification easier. That is necessary and insufficient. Citations solve provenance better than they solve judgment. Two experts can read the same literature and disagree about study quality, external validity, mechanism, confounding, or what the evidence warrants in a new context. A system can cite impeccably and still choose a frame that narrows the decision prematurely.

Science itself is not a warehouse of conclusions. It is a set of practices for making some kinds of claims more accountable to evidence. Those practices include measurement, replication, criticism, peer review, methodological dispute, revision, and the possibility that a favored result fails. Institutional science often falls short of its ideals, but the ideals matter because they make error correctable.

The public usually encounters the endpoint, not the process. A recommendation arrives after committees, papers, revisions, and arguments. The process can therefore look priestly: specialized language, credentialed interpreters, controlled institutions, and conclusions issued to people who cannot independently reproduce the work.

Some critics exploit this resemblance to dismiss expertise as merely another authority system. That is a serious mistake. A medical claim that survives randomized trials is not equivalent to a doctrinal claim because both are communicated by institutions. Methods matter. Evidence matters. The fact that every knowledge system has social structures does not make every knowledge claim equally reliable.

Still, the resemblance tells us something useful about trust. Evidence does not travel by itself. People trust communities that produce, review, and communicate evidence. When those communities lose legitimacy, better data may not repair the damage because the crisis is partly institutional.

AI companies will face the same problem on compressed timescales. A model can be extraordinarily capable and still lose trust after visible failures, policy reversals, data controversies, manipulative behavior, or opaque decisions. Trust will not be restored merely by announcing a higher benchmark score. Users will ask, often inarticulately, whether the institution behind the model deserves authority.

This is why “trustworthy AI” can be a misleading phrase if treated as a property of the artifact alone. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework is more careful than the slogan: it treats trustworthiness characteristics across design, deployment, use, measurement, and governance. Reliability, safety, transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness do not emerge from a single model weight. They depend on the system around the model.

Expertise works similarly. We trust a surgeon not because human brains are intrinsically trustworthy, but because training, licensing, norms, hospitals, malpractice law, peer review, records, and professional reputation create a structure around judgment. The structure fails sometimes. It also gives failure an address.

What is the address of a model’s bad advice?

The user may have clicked through terms of service. The developer may say the system is not a substitute for professional advice. The professional may say he relied on a tool approved by the organization. The organization may say the vendor met procurement requirements. The vendor may say the model output depends on user context. Responsibility can dissolve into a perfectly documented chain.

This is the expert problem after automation: we can distribute cognition more easily than accountability.

The temptation is to solve this by keeping a human expert “in the loop.” As with government, that phrase can conceal more than it clarifies. If a radiologist reviews an AI-highlighted image, what exactly is the division of labor? Does the machine draw attention to anomalies the radiologist might miss, or does it anchor the radiologist on its own interpretation? Can the human meaningfully review hundreds of machine suggestions under time pressure? Is disagreement recorded? Does the institution examine cases where humans overrode the tool and were right?

The quality of the partnership matters more than the ceremonial presence of a licensed person.

There is also a subtler threat to expertise: deskilling.

If a system reliably performs a cognitive task, institutions will rationally reduce the time humans spend learning that task. Pilots do not navigate by sextant on ordinary flights. Accountants no longer do every calculation by hand. Programmers rely on compilers rather than writing machine code. This is progress. Nobody should preserve obsolete difficulty merely to keep skills alive.

But some skills are not isolated functions. They are training grounds for judgment.

A young lawyer learns to read cases not only to extract rules but to notice how facts alter doctrine. A physician learns differential diagnosis not only to name diseases but to build a sense of which symptoms are urgent, which absences matter, and how uncertainty feels before the answer arrives. A historian learns archival work not only to collect facts but to become suspicious of clean narratives. If AI performs the intermediate tasks, novices may reach decisions faster while developing weaker internal models of the domain.

This is not inevitable. Good tools can accelerate apprenticeship. A model can challenge a student, generate counterexamples, explain mistakes, and expose more cases than a human mentor could provide. The question is whether the system is used to practice judgment or to bypass the conditions under which judgment forms.

That distinction becomes critical because expertise is not just information possession. Much of it is knowing when the formal rule does not fit.

Experienced nurses notice patients who “do not look right” before a vital sign crosses a threshold. Skilled mechanics hear patterns that novices cannot name. Judges learn that two cases with similar facts may differ in a legally consequential detail. Editors know when a grammatically perfect sentence is dead. These forms of tacit judgment are difficult to specify because they were built through repeated contact with reality.

Models may eventually acquire functional analogues through data and multimodal training. The argument does not depend on assuming a permanent human monopoly on tacit skill. It depends on recognizing that expertise is produced through feedback. If human practitioners stop receiving certain forms of feedback because models mediate the work, the human skill distribution will change.

Then oversight may become circular. We keep humans to supervise machines while training fewer humans capable of independent supervision.

Aviation offers a warning without supplying a simple analogy. Automation has made flying enormously capable and, in many contexts, safer. It has also generated decades of concern about mode awareness, automation surprises, and the maintenance of manual skills. The lesson is not “automation bad.” It is that a system can improve average performance while changing the shape of rare failure.

The same could happen across cognitive professions. A model may reduce routine diagnostic errors while creating rare cascades when everyone trusts the same mistaken recommendation. It may improve contract drafting while causing subtle legal errors to propagate across thousands of documents. It may improve research summaries while homogenizing which papers get noticed.

Expert institutions need a concept of cognitive resilience: enough independent competence remains in the system to recognize and recover from correlated machine error.

This is expensive. Redundancy always looks wasteful before it is needed. Two pilots, second opinions, appellate courts, peer review, independent audits, adversarial testing, and backup systems all consume resources to preserve disagreement. Efficiency culture tends to attack these layers because they duplicate work.

Divinity, in its strongest theological sense, would not need a second opinion. Human systems do.

That sentence marks an important boundary in this book. The more a technology is described with quasi-divine metaphors—omniscience, superintelligence, godlike capability—the easier it becomes to treat redundancy as temporary. Why maintain slow human review if the system is better? Why preserve competing institutions if one model consistently outperforms them? Why teach a skill the machine already has?

The answer is not sentimentality. It is that performance is not the same thing as legitimacy, and reliability is not the same thing as infallibility.

Experts themselves should understand this. The strongest expert cultures are not those that demand deference as a status privilege. They make the basis of deference inspectable. They distinguish established findings from live disputes. They tell outsiders what would change their minds. They create methods for criticism by peers and channels for correction after error.

AI systems should be built to encourage the same habits. A good system in a high-stakes domain should not merely sound authoritative. It should expose uncertainty, identify the scope of its competence, preserve sources, distinguish evidence from recommendation, and make dissent operationally possible.

This may reduce the feeling of magic. Good.

Magic is a poor design target for institutions that will influence medicine, law, science, finance, education, and government.

There is another reason to resist generalized machine authority. Expertise is relational in ways that benchmarks do not capture. A doctor does not merely know medicine. She has duties to a patient. A lawyer has duties to a client and court. A researcher has obligations to methods and professional norms. A therapist has boundaries around confidentiality and care. These roles can fail morally, but the obligations define what the expertise is for.

A general-purpose model has no single fiduciary. The user may assume it is “on my side,” while the system is governed by a provider’s safety policy, business model, legal obligations, and broader social constraints. Those may be appropriate. But the relationship is not the same as hiring a professional whose role is explicitly constituted around your interests.

The ambiguity can be productive. A model should not blindly serve a user who wants help harming someone. But ambiguity about loyalty is still ambiguity about authority. If the system refuses, users deserve some account of why. If it advises, they deserve to know whether the advice reflects professional norms, public sources, company policy, or generalized conversational judgment.

The expert problem is ultimately a problem of boundaries. We need people who know more than we do. We need institutions that can act on specialized knowledge. We need technologies that extend those capacities. We also need reliable ways to tell when specialized knowledge has crossed into a domain where evidence cannot decide the value question.

AI will make that boundary harder to see because it can continue speaking smoothly after expertise runs out.

A human expert often reveals the edge by becoming uncomfortable: “That’s not really my field.” The machine needs an engineered equivalent of intellectual embarrassment.

Without it, we risk creating a universal consultant whose greatest credential is that it never has to admit it went to the wrong school.
