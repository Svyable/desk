# The State Above Us

A king who says “because I command it” and a clerk who says “because the system says so” appear to belong to different moral universes. One wears authority theatrically. The other hides inside procedure. Yet both reveal a recurring feature of organized life: eventually a decision must become binding even after argument has failed.

Political authority is the machinery we build for that moment.

The theological history of sovereignty is complicated enough to punish slogans. European doctrines of divine right did not simply mean that every monarch could do anything he pleased because God liked kings. Medieval and early modern arguments about kingship, church, natural law, estates, custom, resistance, and divine order were contested and internally diverse. Other civilizations developed different accounts of legitimate rule. The useful point is smaller: political systems have repeatedly anchored human commands in something presented as higher than ordinary preference.

Sometimes that higher source was God. Sometimes ancestry, conquest, cosmic order, law, nation, constitution, people, reason, history, revolution, race, class, or the general will. The vocabulary changed. The structural need did not. Power wants an answer to the child’s question: says who?

Modern constitutional government gives a sophisticated answer. The official may command because an office was created by law, the law was enacted under constitutional procedures, the constitution claims authority through a political community, and the whole arrangement is constrained by rights, courts, elections, federalism, norms, and the possibility of amendment. No single person is the sovereign in the old theatrical sense. Authority is distributed through an architecture.

That distribution is one of modernity’s great achievements. It also makes power easier to misunderstand. When responsibility is spread across statutes, agencies, contractors, databases, guidelines, administrative rules, software systems, and appeals processes, no one person appears to have decided. The citizen encounters the state as a form letter.

Bureaucracy is often mocked for exactly this impersonality. The mockery misses why impersonality was attractive. A rule-bound administration can be less arbitrary than personal favor. The ideal clerk does not care whose cousin you are. A benefit should be awarded because you meet the criteria, not because you charmed the official. A building permit should depend on standards rather than tribute. The whole point is to replace personal whim with procedure.

But procedure can acquire a strange moral aura. Once a process is formalized, people begin treating its output as though the process itself justified the result. The application was denied because the rule required it. The sentence was imposed because the table produced it. The family was removed because the classification said they did not qualify. Each sentence may describe what happened accurately. None answers whether the rule was just, the table appropriate, or the classification legitimate.

The distinction between legality and justice is ancient. Automation makes it newly easy to forget.

Governments were among the earliest institutions to understand the appeal of reducing judgment to categories. States count because states act. Censuses, tax rolls, property records, military registries, criminal files, public-health statistics, and eligibility systems turn messy populations into legible administrative objects. This can enable public goods at enormous scale. It can also make people disappear inside the categories used to manage them.

The twentieth century showed both possibilities with terrifying clarity. Statistical administration supported vaccination programs, pensions, infrastructure, and social insurance. The same capacities for registration and classification could be conscripted into persecution, exclusion, and mass violence. Data do not contain their own politics. A precise administrative system can be efficiently humane or efficiently cruel.

AI inherits this history, whether product designers think in those terms or not. A model used by a public agency does not enter a neutral space. It enters an institution already full of delegated authority. If it helps summarize case files, prioritize inspections, identify fraud, allocate services, draft decisions, or estimate risk, it becomes part of the chain by which the state says yes and no.

The argument for using it can be strong. Human decision makers are inconsistent. They get tired. They carry biases. They overlook relevant records. Administrative backlogs can keep deserving people waiting months or years. A system that helps a caseworker find information or flags inconsistent treatment may make government more fair, not less.

The danger begins when assistance hardens into deference. An official who receives a score, ranking, or model-generated recommendation does not encounter it as one more opinion. Quantification carries institutional weight. The number appears to have been computed rather than asserted. A generated explanation may sound more complete than the fragmentary human record. The official still possesses discretion on paper while psychologically moving toward ratification.

This is a familiar phenomenon in automation research: people can over-rely on automated outputs even when they retain formal authority. The machine does not need legal sovereignty to exercise practical power. It only needs to become the answer people are punished for ignoring.

Imagine an agency where an employee can follow the model recommendation and later say the approved tool supported the decision. To reject the recommendation, she must write a justification, gather extra evidence, and perhaps explain herself to a supervisor. The nominal rule says a human remains in the loop. The incentive structure says the human is there to approve.

“Human in the loop” can therefore become ceremonial, like a signature placed on a document whose substance was decided elsewhere.

This matters because contemporary AI governance often emphasizes human oversight, and rightly so. UNESCO’s ethics recommendation says member states should ensure that AI systems do not displace ultimate human responsibility and accountability. NIST’s risk framework treats governance as an active organizational function rather than a stamp placed on a finished system. These principles are valuable precisely because merely placing a person near the machine does not create responsibility.

Responsibility requires authority to disagree.

That means the human needs enough information to understand what the system did, enough time to review it, enough institutional permission to override it, and enough protection that disagreement is not career suicide. Oversight without capacity is theater.

Religious language helps illuminate the danger because political systems have long borrowed sacred forms. The ruler’s body, oath, seal, crown, constitution, flag, courtroom ritual, inauguration, memorial, and public ceremony all transform power into legitimacy. Modern democracies may reject divine right while retaining ritual because legitimacy cannot be reduced to force. People need to believe that power is exercised through a rightful process.

Algorithmic government introduces a new ritual object: the neutral system.

Neutrality is one of the most powerful claims an institution can make. It says that the decision was not personal. In many settings, that is exactly what justice demands. Yet “not personal” is not the same as neutral. A model’s target variable, training data, threshold, error tolerance, and deployment context all express choices. Which errors are acceptable? Which outcomes matter? Which historical patterns count as signal rather than injustice? Those are political questions even when implemented mathematically.

The risk is a secular version of sacralization. Once the system becomes officially designated as objective, criticism of its output can be treated as ignorance of the method. The system stands above the citizen because the citizen cannot see inside it. Expertise and secrecy combine. The person affected may know that the result is wrong without being able to state the technical reason it was produced.

Older authority systems faced this problem too. A sovereign could insist that only the sovereign understood affairs of state. A priestly institution could insist that only trained clergy could interpret doctrine. A bureaucracy could insist that only administrators understood the rule. Specialized knowledge is often real; pretending otherwise is populist theater. But specialization becomes domination when affected people are denied a meaningful language of appeal.

A healthy system therefore needs two kinds of legibility. The administrator needs to see the person well enough to avoid treating a category as the whole human being. The person needs to see the administration well enough to challenge what it has done.

AI can improve the first form. A model can surface overlooked context in a large case file, translate documents, identify relevant precedent, or help an official understand a complicated history. It can also damage it by compressing a life into a summary that becomes more influential than the record from which it was made.

The summary problem deserves attention. Anyone who has been summarized by another person knows the unease. A page of complicated motives and events becomes three bullet points. Something true is retained; something essential may vanish. In bureaucratic systems, the summary often becomes more real than the source because it is what decision makers actually read.

Generative AI makes summary cheap enough to become universal. Every long file can produce an executive version. Every meeting can become minutes. Every citizen history can become a case narrative. The gain in administrative capacity will be enormous. So will the power of whatever decides what is salient.

The issue is not hallucination alone. Suppose the summary is factually accurate. It can still be normatively distorted. A system instructed to summarize a welfare case for fraud risk will notice different facts than a system instructed to summarize barriers to eligibility. Both can be faithful to the record. Their frames serve different institutional purposes.

There is no unframed state.

This is where the language of outsourcing becomes useful again. We do not merely outsource calculations to administrative systems. We can outsource salience: what deserves attention. We can outsource suspicion: who looks risky. We can outsource consistency: which cases resemble one another. We can outsource justification: the language used to explain a decision. By the time a human signs at the end, much of the moral work may already have been structured.

The solution cannot be to demand that every public decision be made from scratch by one fully autonomous official. That would reintroduce arbitrariness and destroy administrative capacity. The point of institutions is precisely to constrain personal whim. The challenge is to design delegation without creating an altar to procedure.

Constitutional systems offer a model. Good constitutional design does not assume officials will always be wise. It distributes power, creates review, establishes rights, makes some decisions harder, and preserves channels for contest. It expects fallibility.

AI governance should begin from the same anthropology. Models will be wrong. Developers will be wrong. agencies will be wrong. users will be wrong. The question is not how to build a system so good that disagreement becomes irrational. The question is how to preserve legitimate disagreement after the system becomes very good.

That sounds less glamorous than building artificial general intelligence. It is also more immediate.

Consider a future in which public agencies use highly capable models to draft benefit determinations, tax notices, immigration analyses, licensing decisions, and regulatory findings. The model may be more consistent than most staff and able to retrieve more law than any individual. Citizens may benefit from faster service. The same capability could quietly produce a new kind of sovereignty: not the machine ruling in its own name, but institutions increasingly unable to imagine acting against the machine’s recommendation.

No coup is required. Authority can migrate through workflow.

The migration may be welcomed because it solves real problems. Legislatures pass vague laws because compromise requires ambiguity. Agencies must interpret them. Courts create precedent. Staff turn precedent into guidance. Vendors encode guidance into systems. Models learn from the accumulated record. Eventually the model can answer a question with an authority no individual actor possesses. It seems to know what the institution means.

Then the institution begins learning what it means from the model.

This feedback loop is especially dangerous when automated systems train on past decisions that were themselves influenced by earlier systems. Institutional judgment can become self-referential. Yesterday’s policy shapes today’s data, which shapes tomorrow’s model, which validates the policy because the model finds the expected pattern. A bureaucracy can turn its own history into evidence of necessity.

Religious traditions have names for the problem of an institution mistaking itself for its source. Political theory has names too: absolutism, authoritarianism, bureaucratic domination, technocracy. AI does not create these temptations. It gives them a new infrastructure.

The liberal democratic answer, at its best, is that no institution gets to be final everywhere. Courts review agencies. Legislatures can change laws. Elections change officials. Rights limit majorities. Journalism investigates. Civil society organizes. Federal structures create competing jurisdictions. None works perfectly. Their friction is partly the point.

A machine optimized for coherence can find this friction inefficient. So can administrators. A centralized model could harmonize rules, standardize decisions, eliminate duplication, and reduce variance. Every one of those outcomes may be desirable in a particular setting. Taken as a political ideal, they should make us nervous.

Human freedom often survives in the gap between systems.

The person denied by one institution can appeal to another. The local rule can differ from the national default. The dissenting judge can write an opinion that becomes law decades later. The bureaucrat can exercise discretion. The journalist can publish the document. The citizen can insist that a category does not fit.

If AI becomes the shared interpretive layer across all these institutions, formal separation may remain while cognitive separation shrinks. The same models, data providers, cloud infrastructure, and policy templates could quietly standardize how different parts of society understand evidence and risk.

That possibility deserves more attention than fantasies of a robot emperor. Power rarely needs a throne if it can become infrastructure.

The state teaches us that legitimate authority requires more than correct answers. It requires a chain of authorization, constraints on power, rights for the affected, procedures for appeal, and a place where responsibility finally lands. These features can look inefficient because they are designed for a world in which rulers and systems are fallible.

AI will tempt us to believe that enough accuracy makes some of those protections obsolete. A model that is right 99 percent of the time will create enormous pressure to treat the remaining dissenter as the error. Yet constitutionalism was not invented only for average cases. Its moral significance appears precisely when an individual stands against a confident system.

The machine does not need to claim divine right. It only needs us to stop asking who authorized the answer, who can reverse it, and who remains responsible when everyone followed the procedure.
