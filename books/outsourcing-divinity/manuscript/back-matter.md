# Back Matter

## About the Book

*Outsourcing Divinity* asks what happens when artificial intelligence begins performing functions human beings have historically placed in authorities larger than themselves: interpretation, prediction, judgment, confession, moral permission, memory, and the settlement of uncertainty.

It is not a book about machines literally becoming gods. It is about the human habit of granting authority before we have finished asking where that authority came from, what it is good at, where it stops, and who remains responsible when it is wrong.

The book moves through priesthood, scripture, sovereignty, bureaucracy, markets, expertise, algorithms, intimate disclosure, moral advice, spiritual practice, prediction, AI laboratories, institutional governance, and the meaning of human agency. Its argument is deliberately double-sided. Delegation is not a civilizational failure; civilization depends on it. The question is how to gain the power of collective and machine intelligence without turning delegation into abdication.

## Notes and Sources

The manuscript was researched chapter by chapter using a source ledger maintained alongside the book in `research/sources.md`.

The source hierarchy favors primary texts, official institutional materials, original research, and durable scholarly references. Historical and theological analogies are treated as analogies rather than identity claims. A source showing that one tradition or scholar holds a view is not used to imply universal agreement. Claims about contemporary AI systems are separated from forecasts about future systems.

The manuscript deliberately preserves several category boundaries: prediction is not providence; performance is not legitimacy; language about an experience is not evidence of having the experience; synthesis is not adjudication; and technological capability is not, by itself, moral permission.

Some frameworks and technical facts will change after this draft. Time-sensitive claims should therefore be rechecked during copyediting and again immediately before publication. The research ledger records canonical or authoritative URLs so the final edition can convert the working notes into endnotes without relying on uncited recollection.

## Acknowledgments

This book owes an intellectual debt to people who take authority seriously enough to examine its limits: theologians who distinguish faith from institutional convenience; historians who make easy analogies harder; philosophers who refuse to let moral vocabulary hide unresolved premises; scientists and engineers who document what systems can and cannot do; researchers who test models for uncomfortable behavior rather than merely impressive capability; public servants who build appeal and accountability into procedure; and critics willing to ask who benefits when a system becomes inevitable.

The argument also depends on traditions that disagree with one another. Those disagreements are part of the evidence. A book about authority would fail its own test if it converted centuries of religious, philosophical, political, and scientific dispute into one synthetic voice.

Any remaining errors of fact or interpretation belong to the author.

## About the Author

Sven Hardy Benson writes about technology, markets, science, institutions, and human judgment. His work is interested in the moment when useful abstractions begin governing the people who made them, and in the harder question of what should remain human when machines become better at things humans once mistook for uniquely their own.

He is also the author of *Leveraging Luck: An Optimistic Guide to Life*.
