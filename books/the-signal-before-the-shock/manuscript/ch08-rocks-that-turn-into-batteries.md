# Chapter 8 — Rocks That Turn Into Batteries

Friedemann Freund spent years arguing that ordinary-looking rocks may hide electrically active defects. Under stress, in his model, peroxy defects can break and generate mobile positive charge carriers—often described as positive holes—that propagate through rock, potentially producing surface potentials, air ionization and other phenomena invoked in earthquake precursor discussions.

It is an attractive mechanism because it attempts to join several otherwise disconnected reports. Electrical currents in crust could create magnetic anomalies. Charge reaching the surface could alter atmospheric ionization. Chemical reactions could affect gases. One process might climb from rock to sky.

That elegance is exactly why it must be attacked.

The first question is laboratory reproducibility: what rocks, stresses, temperatures and water contents produce the effect, with what current density and lifetime? The second is scaling: can a centimeter-scale laboratory specimen be extrapolated to a heterogeneous fault volume kilometers across? The third is transport: do charge carriers survive grain boundaries, fluids, fractures and realistic conductivity structures? The fourth is field amplitude: after geometric spreading and conductive attenuation, what should a magnetometer or atmospheric sensor actually measure at a specified distance?

A mechanism earns scientific value when it makes the anomaly harder to invent after the fact.

Suppose the rock-electrification model predicts a characteristic sign, spectrum and spatial gradient for a ground electric field. Then stations can be built to test it. If instead every possible field configuration can be explained by unknown geology, the mechanism becomes decorative.

This chapter deliberately refuses to use laboratory rock electricity as proof of earthquake precursors. Many materials emit charge under stress. Triboelectric and fracture electrification are real. The leap is from material behavior to a robust pre-rupture geophysical signal. Scale is where frontier mechanisms go to die.

But scale can be calculated.

A serious program would measure candidate lithologies under triaxial stress with pore fluids, map the generated electrical current, determine effective source terms, insert those into realistic conductivity models derived from magnetotellurics, and predict field strengths at surface arrays. Those predictions should be made before looking at earthquake windows.

If the predicted field is ten thousand times smaller than observed anomalies, either the model is wrong or the anomalies are not from that mechanism. If predicted fields are above detection thresholds but never appear during well-instrumented ruptures, the mechanism loses relevance. If a particular polarization and spatial decay repeatedly appears before rupture and not during controls, the problem changes.

Mechanisms are not stories about how a signal *could* happen.

They are machines for generating numbers that can embarrass us.
