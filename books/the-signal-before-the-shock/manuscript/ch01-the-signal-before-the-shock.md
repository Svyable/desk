# Chapter 1 — The Signal Before the Shock

At 5:04 in the afternoon on October 17, 1989, the Santa Cruz Mountains moved. The earthquake lasted seconds. The argument about what happened in the weeks and hours before it has lasted decades.

Near Corralitos, roughly seven kilometers from the epicentral region, a low-frequency magnetic monitoring system had been recording the local field. In 1990 Antony Fraser-Smith and colleagues reported a sequence that, read forward from the paper and backward from the earthquake, is almost too good. A narrow-band signal appeared around September 12. Noise rose on October 5. A dip appeared about a day before the event. Then, about three hours before the Loma Prieta earthquake, activity at the low end of the instrument's range climbed to an exceptional level. The authors used the careful verb *may*. These features, they wrote, may have been magnetic precursors.

It is a beautiful story because the clock appears to count down.

Years later, when Jeremy Thomas, Jeffrey Love and Malcolm Johnston examined the longer Corralitos record, instrument logs, comparison stations and geomagnetic activity, the countdown became much less beautiful. Baseline noise levels changed at other times. The sensor system had problems. Independent analyses found that some relevant magnetic disturbance was regional rather than local. Their conclusion was blunt: the famous precursor was not earthquake physics but instrumental malfunction.

This is not merely a debunking episode. It is a model of the entire subject.

A precursor experiment contains at least three clocks. There is the earthquake clock, which we know perfectly only after rupture begins. There is the instrument clock, which includes maintenance, gain shifts, temperature cycles, telemetry interruptions, battery aging and undocumented insults. And there is the world clock: solar rotation, magnetic storms, daily ionospheric cycles, weather systems, human electrical loads, tides, seasons. Retrospective precursor hunting has an almost irresistible tendency to align all three clocks to the earthquake and then call whatever is nearby unusual.

The Earth provides no blank control week.

That is why an anomaly is not yet information. If a magnetic excursion occurs five days before a magnitude seven earthquake, we need to know how often similar excursions occur without earthquakes, how many earthquakes occur without excursions, how many station-days were searched, how the five-day window was chosen, whether the same filter was used before looking, and whether a solar disturbance would have produced the same excursion over a larger area. The photograph of the anomaly is the numerator. Prediction lives in the denominator.

The word *prediction* makes this harder because it smuggles in a public promise. In everyday speech a prediction has a place, time and magnitude. Seismology has had much greater success with probabilistic forecasting—rates over regions and windows—and with early warning, which detects rupture after it starts and sends alerts ahead of damaging waves. These are not semantic distinctions. One is an inference before nucleation, another a probability distribution over future seismicity, and the third a race against wave propagation after the event has begun.

The precursor field repeatedly slides among them.

A laboratory result showing that stressed rock can produce an electrical effect does not predict an earthquake. A statistically elevated ion-density anomaly five days before a class of earthquakes is not a deterministic alarm. A radon transient correlated with a later earthquake does not establish that the transient was caused by preseismic strain. A machine-learning classifier that identifies earthquakes from a dataset assembled around known earthquakes may know the archive rather than the future.

Still, there is a reason the question returns. Earthquakes are not magic discontinuities. Stress accumulates. Fluids move. Cracks nucleate. Frictional conditions evolve. If some rupture processes have extended preparatory phases, it is physically reasonable to ask whether any coupled variable escapes the fault zone before the seismic cascade becomes obvious. The claim becomes extraordinary only when one jumps from that modest physical possibility to a usable warning system.

In 2025, a paper in *Geophysical Research Letters* made the problem newly interesting by doing something precursor studies should have been doing all along: asking whether non-seismic anomalies add forecast information beyond an established seismicity model. The study incorporated ultralow-frequency magnetic and carbon-monoxide anomalies into a temporal Epidemic-Type Aftershock Sequence model around Japan's Kakioka station. The authors reported a large probability gain and used randomized anomaly times as a significance test. That is not proof of a precursor. The sample, station geography, anomaly definitions and retrospective fitting still matter enormously. But the architecture of the question is better. The anomalies must compete against a model that already knows earthquakes cluster.

That is the hinge of this book.

For half a century the precursor debate has often been staged as believers versus skeptics. That is useless. The useful split is between experiments that can lose and experiments that cannot. A claim that can always explain a missing precursor by changing distance, magnitude threshold, depth, geology, weather or coupling is not a forecast method. A skeptic who dismisses every anomaly because prediction has historically failed is also not doing an experiment.

The field needs a trap.

Not a trap for proponents. A trap for the phenomenon.

Specify the sensors. Specify the controls. Specify the confounders. Freeze the transforms. Define the alarm. Publish it. Then let the Earth decide.
