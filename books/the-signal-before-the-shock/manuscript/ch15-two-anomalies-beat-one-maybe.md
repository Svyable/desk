# Chapter 15 — Two Anomalies Beat One—Maybe

Multiple evidence streams feel safer than one. This instinct can be disastrously wrong.

If radon and atmospheric ionization are linked by the same weather system, they are not independent votes. If TEC and satellite ion density are both driven by geomagnetic activity, combining them can double-count the Sun. If two ULF stations share grid noise, coincidence is not geological replication.

The 2025 Kakioka analysis is interesting because it combines ULF magnetic and carbon monoxide anomalies as external excitations in an earthquake forecast model. The reported probability gain is large. The temptation is to say two precursors corroborate each other.

The right question is causal independence.

CO can have anthropogenic and meteorological sources. ULF magnetic activity has geomagnetic and instrumental sources. If the two measurements share a confounder, their joint occurrence may be less impressive than it appears. If they arise through separate crustal pathways predicted by the same stress evolution, joint occurrence could be more informative.

A multimodal precursor network should therefore be built like a forensic case, not a sensor shopping list. Each modality gets an explicit causal graph: proposed seismic path, known confounders, expected spatial scale, lag distribution and failure modes. Combining modalities should occur only after covariance under non-earthquake conditions is measured.

There is a beautiful possibility here. Different signals might encode different pieces of the preparatory process. Strain could alter permeability first, changing gas transport. Electrical effects might emerge later as damage localizes. Microseismicity could accelerate near failure. A sequence across modalities could outperform any single threshold.

There is also an ugly possibility: the sequence is what analysts see after sorting noise around a known rupture.

Prospective timestamping decides which world we are in.
