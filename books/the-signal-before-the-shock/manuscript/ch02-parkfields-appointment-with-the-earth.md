# Chapter 2 — Parkfield's Appointment With the Earth

Parkfield is a small place that acquired an appointment with geology.

A sequence of moderate earthquakes had occurred on the central San Andreas Fault near the town. By the 1980s the recurrence looked regular enough to tempt a prediction experiment: another magnitude-six-class event was expected within a specified window. The location was instrumented with the seriousness normally reserved for a patient whose disease is expected to declare itself soon. Strainmeters, creepmeters, seismic stations, geodetic instruments and other systems were placed where they could catch the fault preparing to rupture.

The appointment was missed.

USGS reviews later described the original probability calculation as oversimplified. James Savage's 1993 critique was sharper: the celebrated 95 percent statement depended on statistical choices, including omission of the 1934 event from a sequence because it did not fit the apparent regularity. The problem was not that recurrence models are forbidden. The problem was that a small historical sequence had been promoted into a clock.

There is a scene here worth holding. Scientists had built perhaps the best earthquake observatory in the world around a forecast that was aging badly. In October 1992 a magnitude 4.7 earthquake triggered a formal alert; a 72-hour public warning assigned a substantial chance of the expected magnitude 6 event. Nothing followed. The instruments continued recording.

Then, on September 28, 2004, the magnitude 6 Parkfield earthquake finally arrived.

This should have been the dream case for precursor science. The fault had been watched densely and continuously. No one needed to reconstruct whether an instrument happened to be operating. The earthquake occurred inside the net built for it. Yet Bakun and colleagues concluded in *Nature* that the event showed no obvious precursors and reinforced the judgment that reliable short-term prediction was not then achievable.

Parkfield is often told as the death of earthquake prediction. That is too theatrical. It is more useful as a lesson in experimental geometry.

A retrospective event study asks: what changed before this earthquake? Parkfield allowed something closer to the inverse: given years of high-quality observations, what changes were unusual enough, specific enough and timely enough to have generated a useful alarm before the event? That inversion is brutal because the quiet years stay in the denominator.

This matters for every exotic precursor claim. Suppose a ground electric signal occurs twice during the month before a large earthquake. Without the preceding years we can make the two signals look prophetic. With the preceding years we may discover hundreds of similar signals. Or we may discover none, in which case the claim gets interesting. The density of non-events determines the meaning of the event.

Parkfield also separates two kinds of ignorance that are routinely confused. One is ignorance of *when rupture will occur*. Another is ignorance of *what the preparatory process is doing*. A fault can exhibit measurable creep, microseismicity, fluid-pressure changes and stress transfer without providing a deterministic countdown. Conversely, absence of a simple deterministic precursor does not imply absence of preparatory physics. It may mean the relevant variables are spatially localized, below sensor thresholds, nonunique, or controlled by a nucleation process whose final transition is intrinsically hard to time.

This is why the phrase 'earthquake prediction failed' can become lazy. What failed at Parkfield was a particular temporal model and the hope for clear short-term observables in that experiment. That result should narrow hypotheses, not prohibit questions.

The right descendant of Parkfield is not another town waiting for a scheduled earthquake. It is a distributed test designed around forecast skill. A precursor method must issue many prospective probabilities, not one heroic date. It must be compared with a baseline seismicity model. Its false alarms must be expensive in the score. Its silence before earthquakes must be expensive too.

Parkfield taught seismology to instrument uncertainty.

The precursor field still has to learn to count it.
