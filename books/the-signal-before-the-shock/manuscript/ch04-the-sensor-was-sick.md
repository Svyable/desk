# Chapter 4 — The Sensor Was Sick

The most damaging sentence in the Loma Prieta precursor literature is not 'there was no signal.' It is 'the sensor system malfunctioned.'

Thomas, Love and Johnston reexamined twenty-one months of Corralitos data, from January 1989 through October 1990, and the operational logbook. That longer window is the difference between a miracle and maintenance. They found baseline changes not only during the alleged precursor period but at other times. Comparison with other magnetic data weakened the local-source interpretation. Their conclusion was that the anomalous magnetic noise was an artefact of sensor-system malfunction.

Campbell approached parts of the claim through regional magnetic activity and similarly argued that relevant signals were consistent with natural geomagnetic disturbance rather than an earthquake source.

Two skeptical mechanisms therefore competed: sick instrument and ordinary space physics. That is good science. A negative conclusion does not require one immaculate replacement story. It requires showing that the extraordinary interpretation is not identified by the data.

This point is often missed in anomaly debates. Critics are pressured to explain every wiggle. They do not need to. If a dataset cannot distinguish crustal current from sensor drift or regional geomagnetic noise, the inference is underdetermined. 'Unknown cause' is allowed. It is much stronger than pretending unknown means seismic.

Sensor pathology deserves more respect in frontier science. Drift is not random white noise. It has structure. Batteries age. contacts oxidize. amplifiers saturate. temperature coefficients follow the day. A damaged instrument can generate persistent regimes, sudden jumps and apparent precursors because failure itself has temporal dynamics. These signatures can look more purposeful than Gaussian noise.

A modern precursor network should therefore treat engineering telemetry as scientific data. Every magnetometer should record internal temperatures, supply voltages and calibration states. Stations should periodically inject known signals. Redundant colocated sensors should permit disagreement analysis. Hardware changes should be versioned like software. Raw data should be immutable, and every derived anomaly should carry the complete transformation recipe.

There is an irony here. The more exotic the hypothesized phenomenon, the more boring the instrument record must become. A laboratory looking for a fifth force obsesses over thermal gradients. A gravitational-wave observatory catalogs environmental couplings with almost paranoid precision. Earthquake precursor work, because its signal is uncontrolled and rare, needs at least that level of suspicion.

A fault will not repeat an earthquake because a magnetometer was under calibration.

That is why the network must be designed to learn from the days when nothing dramatic happens. Faults, instruments and the magnetosphere all produce nonstationarity. We need enough ordinary time to model each.

Corralitos should be taught to every anomaly hunter for one reason: an instrument can have a life story more predictive of its output than the catastrophe beside it.
