# Chapter 3 — Corralitos and the Magnetometer That Became a Witness

Scientific instruments are supposed to be less impressionable than people. This is true only if their biographies are preserved.

The Corralitos magnetometer became famous because it was close to Loma Prieta and because its record contained an apparent escalation before rupture. In the compressed form repeated for years, the coincidence looked like a rare natural experiment. A sensor sat seven kilometers away. It registered unusual ultralow-frequency behavior. Then the earthquake happened.

Distance creates persuasion. Seven kilometers is close enough that a reader begins inventing coupling mechanisms before asking what the instrument was doing on ordinary days.

The original Fraser-Smith paper deserves to be read fairly. It reported two monitoring systems spanning a broad frequency range. The higher-frequency system at Stanford did not show precursor activity the authors could identify. The ULF system at Corralitos did. This asymmetry is important. It demonstrates that the authors did not simply declare every channel anomalous. It also makes the ULF channel carry almost all of the extraordinary burden.

What physical process could generate a magnetic signal before rupture? Candidate ideas include electrokinetic currents from fluid movement, piezomagnetic effects in magnetic minerals, stress-activated charge carriers, microfracturing and currents associated with crustal conductivity changes. Each mechanism predicts different spectra, spatial decay, polarization, relation to geology and timing. 'Electromagnetic precursor' is not a mechanism. It is a filing cabinet.

One problem with iconic event papers is that the anomaly gradually becomes detached from those physical obligations. The Corralitos trace is reproduced as a shape. A shape can survive long after its proposed origin has failed.

To test a magnetic precursor, one needs stations arranged so that local crustal signals and regional magnetospheric signals can be distinguished. A true local source should have a spatial gradient or phase behavior unlike a large-scale geomagnetic disturbance. One also needs instrument health channels: temperatures, power, calibration injections, gain state, maintenance records. A crustal signal should not know when a cable was touched.

The original event was scientifically valuable precisely because later investigators could recover enough of this context to attack it. The attack is the experiment continuing.

The Corralitos episode therefore belongs in the book not as evidence that magnetic precursors exist, and not merely as evidence that they do not. It is evidence that a celebrated precursor can be manufactured by the interaction of a real earthquake with an insufficiently modeled measurement system.

There is a deeper epistemic trap here. Once an earthquake occurs, every nearby instrument becomes a witness. But instruments do not testify. They output voltage. The conversion from voltage to 'precursor' passes through a chain of assumptions about calibration, background, source localization, filtering and coincidence windows. If one link is retrospective, the whole testimony changes.

Imagine the opposite history. Suppose the Corralitos ULF rise had occurred exactly as recorded, but Loma Prieta had not happened for six months. Would the rise have been published as a discovery? Probably not. It might have been logged as an equipment problem or a geomagnetic disturbance. The earthquake did not change the voltage. It changed the category.

That is the basic disease of precursor research: outcomes rename prior observations.

The cure is not skepticism as temperament. It is prospective naming.
