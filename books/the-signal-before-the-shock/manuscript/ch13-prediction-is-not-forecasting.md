# Chapter 13 — Prediction Is Not Forecasting

Earthquake prediction failed partly because the public noun was too crisp for the science underneath it.

A deterministic prediction says, in effect: a damaging earthquake will occur here, within this time window, around this magnitude. A probabilistic forecast says the rate has changed. Early warning says rupture has already begun elsewhere and shaking is traveling toward you. Hazard maps speak in years or decades. These products answer different questions.

A non-seismic precursor is most plausibly useful first as a forecast modifier.

Suppose an established seismicity model assigns a 0.1 percent probability of a target earthquake in a region over the next three days. A validated anomaly might raise that to 0.5 percent. That would be a fivefold probability gain and still mean 99.5 percent chance of no target earthquake. Calling such a signal a 'prediction' would guarantee public misunderstanding.

This is where ETAS matters. Epidemic-Type Aftershock Sequence models capture the fact that earthquakes trigger earthquakes. If precursor researchers compare their anomaly model only with a constant-rate baseline, ordinary seismic clustering can masquerade as new information. The proper question is whether ULF, gas, TEC, radon or other measurements improve the probability forecast *after* the model already knows about recent earthquakes.

A 2025 GRL paper by Zhang and colleagues did exactly this for ULF magnetic and carbon monoxide anomaly times around Japan's Kakioka station. The authors reported that adding those external excitations to a temporal ETAS model improved forecast performance for their dataset and used randomized anomaly times as a check against overfitting.

The result is provocative because it moves the field into a scored competition. But it is not yet the finish. Retrospective anomaly definitions can still leak information. One station region is not a global result. Model flexibility must be charged. Out-of-sample prospective performance is the test.

Still, this is the direction.

Do not ask whether the anomaly predicts the earthquake. Ask how much it moves the probability distribution, and whether that movement improves a proper score on future data.

A forecast can be modest and still be revolutionary.
