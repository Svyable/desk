# Chapter 18 — What Would Count

After all the lights, gases, satellite maps and frightened animals, the question is embarrassingly plain.

What would count?

Not a striking anomaly before a famous earthquake. We have those.

Not a mechanism that can produce electricity in a laboratory. We have those too.

Not a p-value computed after selecting an earthquake-centered window. Not an accuracy percentage from a balanced dataset in which earthquakes are artificially common. Not a classifier that saw the future during normalization. Not a correlation that disappears when the geomagnetic field is plotted on the same axis. Not a satellite map cropped around the epicenter after rupture.

What would count is a locked forecast becoming better before anyone knows the earthquake will happen.

The improvement need not be cinematic. A model that moves probability from 0.1 percent to 0.3 percent at the right times and places, repeatedly, may teach more physics than a perfect-looking one-off precursor. What matters is calibration and information gain. The forecast should become sharper when the proposed physics is active and remain quiet when its confounders are active.

A second criterion is transportability. A signal discovered in Japanese volcanic geology may not transfer unchanged to California, but a physical mechanism should tell us why. Geology can alter amplitude without granting infinite flexibility. Predictions must scale with conductivity, rock type, depth, stress state or whatever variables the mechanism claims.

A third criterion is causal leverage. Can we identify a sequence that distinguishes competing mechanisms? Does a radon anomaly precede an electric anomaly as predicted by a permeability-charge model? Does a satellite effect occur at magnetic conjugate points? Do local magnetic gradients reject magnetospheric forcing? Each discriminator removes a story.

And then there is the public question.

Even a real precursor may be operationally weak. Earthquake warnings have costs. Evacuations kill economic activity and can kill people. False alarms erode trust. A forecast system must therefore separate scientific detectability from decision usefulness. A one-percent probability gain may be priceless for understanding fault physics and useless for closing a city.

That distinction protects the science from its own history.

The precursor field became notorious because it promised the social product before earning the physical one. The repair should proceed in reverse. First establish a reproducible, prospective information increment. Then identify mechanism. Then determine whether the increment supports any decision better than existing hazard, forecast and early-warning systems.

There is a possibility that the answer will remain no. Perhaps nucleation is too localized, too variable or too fast. Perhaps every proposed electromagnetic signal is below the natural background. Perhaps gas changes occur only in special hydrogeology. Perhaps satellite statistics are selection artefacts. A well-designed twenty-year network could spend its life proving that the Earth does not whisper in any way we can use.

That would not be failure.

Failure is spending another forty years collecting anomalies that already know where the earthquake was.
