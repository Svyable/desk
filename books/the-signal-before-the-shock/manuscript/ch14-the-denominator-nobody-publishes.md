# Chapter 14 — The Denominator Nobody Publishes

The literature likes earthquakes because earthquakes give papers endings.

A sensor did something odd. Three days later, magnitude 7.2. Figure 4 receives a vertical red line. The anomaly becomes a precursor.

Missing from the figure are the 2,417 other days.

This denominator problem is not a minor statistical correction. It determines whether the observation contains information. Let A be a defined anomaly and E a target earthquake. Most case studies estimate something resembling P(A|E): anomalies are reported before earthquakes. Operational forecasting needs P(E|A): given an anomaly today, what is the probability of an earthquake? Bayes' theorem connects them through the base rate. When E is rare, P(E|A) can remain tiny even if P(A|E) is respectable.

False alarms are therefore not an embarrassment to be discussed qualitatively. They are the dataset.

Every precursor archive should store alarm-minutes, not just event windows. If an algorithm produces an alert state for 20 percent of time, catching 30 percent of earthquakes may be useless. If it alerts 0.1 percent of time and catches 20 percent, that is very different.

Spatial exposure matters too. A network with one sensor near a future epicenter may appear impressive if that station twitches. But if a thousand identical stations twitch every day, the event-centered selection is meaningless. Count station-hours, searchable cells, frequency bands and tested windows.

Multiple testing belongs in the denominator. If researchers inspect magnetics, radon, TEC, infrared, groundwater, CO, ozone and animal behavior across dozens of lags, one channel will look prophetic by chance. The correct penalty may be impossible to reconstruct after exploratory work. This is another reason for held-out prospective scoring.

The field does not need fewer anomalies.

It needs more boring data.
