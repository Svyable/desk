# Chapter 7 — DEMETER's Statistical Ghost

France launched a satellite called DEMETER in 2004. Its name expanded to Detection of Electro-Magnetic Emissions Transmitted from Earthquake Regions. A whole spacecraft carried the hypothesis in its acronym.

That fact is sometimes presented as evidence of establishment interest, as though a funded mission authenticates a phenomenon. It does not. Governments and space agencies fund uncertain questions precisely because the answer is not known.

DEMETER operated in low Earth orbit until 2010 and measured electromagnetic waves and plasma parameters. Its archive allowed something event anecdotes could not: statistical comparison across many earthquakes and many satellite passes.

A 2017 *Journal of Geophysical Research: Space Physics* analysis used a superposed-epoch approach on ion-density observations. After attempting to control for location and observation conditions and removing aftershock-associated data, the authors reported statistically detectable nighttime ion-density perturbations about five days before earthquakes above magnitude 5 and within roughly 200 kilometers. They also reported the expected post-seismic effect. Importantly, they cautioned that the perturbations were statistical and that ion density alone could not be used for earthquake forecasting.

This is exactly the kind of result that should make both camps uncomfortable.

For enthusiasts it is weaker than an alarm: the effect emerges after stacking many events. For skeptics it is harder to dismiss than a single famous trace because the analysis uses a mission-scale archive and control comparisons.

But superposed-epoch methods have their own demons. Earthquakes are not uniformly distributed in latitude, local time or geomagnetic conditions. Satellite sampling is structured by orbit. Declustering choices matter. Multiple plasma variables and windows create multiplicity. A statistically significant mean departure does not automatically translate into useful predictive value for an individual future event.

The key inversion is again necessary. Instead of asking 'what does the ionosphere do before earthquakes?', ask 'given today's ionospheric measurements, how much should tomorrow's earthquake probability change?' The latter forces prevalence into the calculation. Earthquakes are rare. Even a marker with respectable sensitivity and specificity can produce mostly false alarms when deployed continuously.

DEMETER's most valuable legacy may therefore be not a precursor detection but a dataset on which prediction architecture can be rehearsed. Train on early mission years. Lock the algorithm. Predict later years. Compare against matched non-earthquake windows and a seismic baseline. Penalize every alarm.

The ghost becomes interesting only when it has to knock before we know which house will fall.
