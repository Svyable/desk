# The 2025 Split

On January 10, 2025, the Intelligence Community published a document that should have ended the habit of speaking about its Havana judgment in the singular.

Most components still assessed that a foreign adversary was very unlikely to be responsible for the reported anomalous health incidents. Five components placed substantial weight on intelligence reporting that continued to point away from adversary involvement, investigations of key incidents, and review of foreign research and weapons development. They also judged it very unlikely that a foreign actor had used a novel weapon or prototype device even against a subset of personnel.

Then the assessment recorded dissent.

One component judged there was a roughly even chance that a foreign actor had used a novel weapon or prototype to harm a small, undetermined subset of U.S. personnel or dependents. Another judged there was a roughly even chance that a foreign actor had developed a device capable of harming such a subset, while still considering actual deployment unlikely. Both judgments were low confidence.

The capability question split further. Five components continued to judge it very unlikely that a foreign actor possessed a pulsed-RF weapon or prototype capable of producing the reported symptoms and sensory phenomena. One component now assessed it likely that a foreign actor had an RF antipersonnel capability and that it could cause biological effects consistent with some AHI symptoms. Another assessed roughly even odds of such a capability.

The shifts were based on new reporting about foreign directed-energy research and weapons development.

This is a remarkable public intelligence document because it preserves disagreement at the exact point where public narratives usually flatten it.

The majority says: after years of collection, no compelling link to a foreign actor has emerged.

The dissent says: our estimate of what foreign actors may be technically able to do has changed enough that a small subset cannot be dismissed as easily as before.

Both can be true.

The document also says that the medical evidence does not show a consistent set of physical injuries among the reporting population. It cites the 2024 NIH research. Again, this does not require the conclusion that all reports were psychogenic or trivial. It means the syndrome does not behave like one uniform lesion.

The 2025 assessment is therefore a lesson in Bayesian discomfort.

Suppose your prior belief that an adversary has a relevant RF capability rises. That should increase the probability assigned to an attack explanation for otherwise compatible cases. But suppose at the same time years of collection fail to connect an adversary to any incident, and medical studies fail to identify a common injury pattern. Those observations push the other way.

There is no rule saying one side must win completely.

The posterior can remain low overall while rising for a narrow subset.

Public argument dislikes this because it does not create a stable tribe. People who were convinced of Russian attacks can point to the shifted capability judgments. People convinced there was no weapon can point to the dominant attribution judgment. Each side can quote the same document accurately while misrepresenting its architecture.

The interesting work is in the architecture.

Intelligence assessments combine different evidence streams that are not publicly visible. Signals intelligence, human reporting, foreign technical intelligence, incident investigations, medical research, and scientific feasibility analyses carry different uncertainties. Agencies also have different collection access and analytic cultures. A component with better visibility into foreign technical programs may update capability before another component does. A component with stronger counterintelligence collection may weigh the absence of operational linkage more heavily.

Confidence levels matter too.

Low-confidence dissent should not be ignored, but it should not be read like a laboratory result. In intelligence language, low confidence signals limited, questionable, or fragmented information, or significant analytic uncertainty. A roughly even chance at low confidence is not a secret way of saying fifty percent measured in a dataset. It is a disciplined expression of uncertainty.

That nuance is often erased when assessments enter politics.

Victims and advocates may understandably prefer the minority judgment because it preserves an external cause. Agencies facing criticism may prefer the majority judgment because it reduces the likelihood of a hostile campaign they failed to stop. Political actors can select whichever fragment supports prior commitments. Journalists need a headline.

The document resists all of them by being internally plural.

There is another reason the split matters. It tells us that foreign RF research did not stop being an intelligence target when the broad Havana attribution weakened. The assessment says the IC would continue collecting on foreign weapons programs and supporting U.S.-government-affiliated laboratory work on whether RF signals can cause bioeffects consistent with reported AHIs. It notes that most historical results showed no harmful bioeffects, while more recent limited studies had produced mixed preliminary results. The IC chose to evaluate published findings in the assessment and defer unpublished government research until completion.

This is exactly the evidence posture a serious frontier program should adopt.

Do the experiments.

Do not announce preliminary laboratory work as operational proof.

Keep collecting on adversary capability.

Do not convert capability into incident attribution without linkage.

Update minority judgments when evidence changes.

Preserve the majority when contrary evidence has not crossed its threshold.

The assessment also lists scenarios under which its conclusions could be wrong: a covert intelligence activity that evaded detection; a scientific breakthrough that bypassed Western assumptions; critical symptoms hidden among common ones; or early natural events that later inspired an adversary to develop a harassment device.

Those scenarios are not endorsements. They are red-team escape routes.

An intelligence product becomes stronger when it tells the reader how it could fail.

The 2025 split does not solve Havana.

It does something more useful for this book.

It makes uncertainty legible.
