# The Moscow Signal

For years, the United States Embassy in Moscow was being illuminated by microwave radiation.

That sentence has survived into the Havana era because it sounds like a prequel. It is safer to treat it as its own problem first.

American officials became aware of an unusual radiofrequency signal directed at the Chancery in the 1950s. Later declassified diplomatic records show repeated U.S. measurements and protests. By the late 1960s the matter had a bureaucratic vocabulary: the Technically Unidentified Moscow Signal, or TUMS. The source, purpose, exact health implications, and appropriate diplomatic response remained unsettled even while the fact of directed electromagnetic illumination became difficult to dispute.

The most important detail is not that the Soviets used microwaves. It is that the U.S. government knew the building was being irradiated and still did not know why.

That epistemic condition lasted a long time.

In June 1967, Secretary of State Dean Rusk raised the electromagnetic signal with Soviet Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko. Ambassador Llewellyn Thompson's record says Rusk asked that the activity stop. Soviet officials denied a special beam existed and suggested local industrial sources. American officials had directional measurements that they believed contradicted that explanation. The diplomatic exchange therefore reached an uncomfortable plateau: one side had an instrumented phenomenon; the other denied the interpretation; neither side had an agreed purpose.

Years later, as signal characteristics and exposure levels changed, concern sharpened. A January 1976 Embassy cable summarized U.S. expert measurements of two microwave beams above one gigahertz, arriving from different directions and sometimes operating for long periods each day. Measured radiation densities inside parts of the Chancery and some apartments were reported in the range of roughly one to fifteen microwatts per square centimeter. The cable said U.S. medical experts considered prolonged exposure at measured levels a potential health hazard.

Another State Department fact sheet later told employees that no causal relationship had been established between the microwave transmissions and health problems among past or present personnel. Those statements can coexist. A potential hazard is not an observed causal effect.

The policy problem was made uglier by secrecy. For years, many Embassy employees were not fully informed about the signal. Officials worried about health, intelligence collection, diplomacy, and leaks at the same time. The records show a government trying to decide whether disclosure would protect its people, panic them, compromise countermeasures, embarrass negotiations, or reveal what the United States knew about Soviet surveillance techniques.

This is where the Moscow Signal becomes more than historical scenery.

It is a clean example of a national-security phenomenon in which exposure is real but meaning is unstable.

The Soviets may have been trying to interfere with American electronic intelligence collection. They may have been powering or interrogating covert listening devices in the building. They may have had multiple purposes over time. Some U.S. officials worried that biological effects could themselves be intentional. The declassified record does not establish a definitive Soviet health-attack mission.

That distinction tends to disappear in retrospective comparison with Havana. Once a later controversy exists, earlier anomalies become evidence in its direction. Moscow gets remembered as proof that the Soviet Union had already attacked diplomats with microwaves. The archive is more interesting because it refuses that convenience.

The electromagnetic exposure was documented.

The health concern was documented.

The intent remained uncertain.

The same archive also demonstrates why governments take uncertain mechanisms seriously. If a foreign power is deliberately directing energy at an embassy, the responsible response is not to wait until peer-reviewed epidemiology settles every endpoint. Security organizations have to investigate capability and motive under uncertainty. That imperative can look paranoid from outside and negligent if ignored.

The problem becomes especially difficult when scientific literatures differ across countries. Soviet exposure standards for microwave radiation were historically more conservative than U.S. standards in some regimes, and Soviet researchers reported biological effects at low intensities that Western investigators often disputed or failed to replicate. American officials reading that literature had to decide whether it reflected a genuine scientific lead, methodological differences, ideological science, or intelligence-significant knowledge.

That uncertainty helped produce Project PANDORA.

Before getting there, the signal itself deserves another pass.

Microwatts per square centimeter are not numbers that explain a biological outcome by themselves. Frequency matters. Modulation matters. duty cycle matters. Exposure duration matters. Spatial distribution inside a building matters. A beam measured on a wall does not map directly onto absorbed dose in a brain. The Moscow measurements were also revised over time, and early U.S. estimates sometimes overstated intensity relative to later assessments.

This is an important warning for any historical reconstruction based on declassified summaries. Intelligence records are not omniscient notes from the future. They contain provisional measurements, institutional arguments, mistaken assumptions, and corrections. A document can be authentic and wrong.

The 1965 ARPA memorandum that helped motivate biological research is a good example. Samuel Koslov considered whether low-level exposure could produce neurophysiological effects and cited Soviet work. He also acknowledged that detailed study of the Moscow signal did not give that hypothesis a high probability. Later review found that some initial comparisons to Soviet safety limits had been overstated. Yet the uncertainty was sufficient to justify controlled experiments.

This is what real anomalous research looks like inside government. It is not a secret priesthood guarding a conclusion. It is often a procurement chain built around uncertainty somebody important cannot afford to ignore.

The Moscow Signal also reveals how technical countermeasures can become entangled with diplomacy. Declassified material from 1976 suggests negotiations over microwave emissions were linked to intelligence equipment on embassy rooftops. One interpretation was that the Soviet signal responded to U.S. collection activities. If so, the beam was part of an electronic contest, not primarily a medical weapon. But because the human body occupied the same building as the antennas, motive did not erase exposure.

That sentence should remain available when we reach Havana.

A signal can have one operational purpose and another biological consequence.

Conversely, an illness can arise in a place where a hostile signal exists without being caused by that signal.

Confounding is not a laboratory inconvenience here. It is the architecture of espionage.

Embassies are full of transmitters, countermeasures, bugs, shielding, stressed personnel, classified work, adversary surveillance, and political incentives to hide some truths while revealing others. They are terrible epidemiological laboratories and irresistible intelligence laboratories.

Moscow taught the U.S. government that an adversary could illuminate a diplomatic building with microwaves for years while leaving purpose ambiguous.

It did not teach what later writers wanted it to have taught.

That is why it belongs in the book.
