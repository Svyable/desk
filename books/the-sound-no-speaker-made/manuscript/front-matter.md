# The Sound No Speaker Made

## Microwave Hearing, the Moscow Signal, and the Physics Beneath Havana Syndrome

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There is a class of sound that does not require a loudspeaker, a vibrating wall, or an audible wave traveling through the air before it reaches you. A short pulse of radiofrequency energy can be absorbed in the head, create an extremely small and rapid temperature rise, launch a pressure wave through tissue, and stimulate the auditory system. The temperature change can be too small to feel as heat. The sound can seem to originate inside or just behind the skull.

This is not a theory invented to explain Havana Syndrome. The effect was reported and studied decades earlier. Allan H. Frey described human perception of pulsed microwave fields in the early 1960s. Kenneth Foster and Edward Finch showed in 1974 that pulsed microwaves can create thermoacoustic transients in water strong enough to account for the clicks people heard. James C. Lin and others developed the thermoelastic account and measured thresholds across frequencies and exposure geometries. The effect became unusual mainly because most people never encounter it.

Then, in 2016, American personnel in Havana began reporting abrupt episodes involving sound or pressure followed in some cases by dizziness, headache, cognitive difficulty, sleep disturbance, tinnitus, and other symptoms. A diplomatic crisis became a medical problem and then an intelligence problem. The phrase *Havana Syndrome* arrived before the mechanism did.

The public argument quickly collapsed several different questions into one. Can radiofrequency energy produce an auditory sensation without ordinary airborne sound? Yes. Can pulsed RF produce harmful biological effects under some exposure conditions? In principle and under sufficiently intense exposures, yes; the exact regime matters. Does either fact prove that an adversary used a directed RF device against U.S. personnel? No. Does the failure to identify such a device prove that every reported incident had a conventional cause? Also no.

Those separations are the reason for this book.

The story begins before Havana, in a radar field and in a U.S. Embassy in Moscow that Soviet transmitters illuminated with microwave energy for years. The United States knew about the Moscow Signal, worried about health effects, argued with Soviet officials, and secretly funded Project PANDORA to study whether low-level electromagnetic exposure could affect physiology or behavior. The declassified record is both stranger and less conclusive than later memory. Monkeys were exposed. Blood studies were hidden behind cover language. Meetings entertained hypotheses that ranged from sensible to premature. By 1969 the PANDORA board still judged the evidence insufficient to draw conclusions about human behavioral effects.

Half a century later, Havana reopened many of the same categories: a real sensory phenomenon; an uncertain dose; a geopolitical adversary with relevant research; symptoms that did not sort neatly into one clinical syndrome; and a government trying to distinguish what was physically possible from what had actually happened.

The official record itself became divided. A 2020 National Academies committee judged directed pulsed radiofrequency energy the most plausible mechanism among those it considered for the distinctive acute cases. A 2022 Intelligence Community expert panel said pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly radiofrequency energy, plausibly explained the core characteristics of a subset of anomalous health incidents. In 2023, most intelligence agencies assessed that a foreign adversary was very unlikely to be responsible for the reported incidents. In January 2025, that overall judgment remained, but two components shifted on foreign capability: one assessed it likely that a foreign actor possessed an RF antipersonnel capability able to cause biological effects consistent with some reported symptoms; another saw roughly even odds of such a capability. Neither shift established use.

Meanwhile, NIH's 2024 studies found no consistent MRI-detectable brain injury or biological abnormality separating AHI participants from matched controls. That result did not prove nothing happened. It narrowed what could responsibly be claimed about persistent measurable injury.

This is an awkward evidentiary shape. It does not reward anyone who arrives needing a single answer.

The book therefore treats four categories separately. Verified facts include the microwave auditory effect, the Moscow Signal, Project PANDORA, the reported AHI cases, official investigations, and published studies. Credible but incomplete evidence includes certain acute case descriptions, technical feasibility arguments, and intelligence judgments about foreign RF research. Disputed claims include specific weapon attributions, alleged operators, individual device descriptions, and assertions of a single syndrome without independent causal confirmation. Speculative synthesis includes the possibility that a small subset of cases involved a directed stimulus while the broader reporting population accumulated heterogeneous causes.

The point is not agnosticism for its own sake. The point is to preserve the shape of the evidence long enough to learn from it.

A sound can be real without a speaker.

A mechanism can be real without a weapon.

A weapon can be possible without having been used.

And an intelligence failure can consist not only of missing an attack, but of forcing unlike events into the same name.
