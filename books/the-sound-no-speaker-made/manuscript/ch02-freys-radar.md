# Frey's Radar

Allan Frey did not begin with Havana, spies, or a weapon. He began with people who worked around radar.

That origin matters. Technologies often reveal biological effects accidentally because the people closest to the machinery encounter exposure regimes no laboratory ethics committee would design as a first experiment. Radar technicians, radio operators, industrial workers, pilots, and medical-device users become the first witnesses to odd interactions between engineered fields and bodies. The anecdotes are noisy. They are also where some real phenomena first become visible.

Frey's early work is remembered now because the effect took his name, but the important move was methodological. Reports of sounds near pulsed radar could have been dismissed as equipment noise, electrical arcing, mechanical vibration, expectation, or folklore passed among operators. Instead, the phenomenon was brought into controlled exposure.

In a laboratory, an odd story has to acquire knobs.

Change the pulse. Change the power. Move the subject. Alter the frequency. Ask whether the sensation tracks the electromagnetic stimulus when ordinary acoustics are controlled. Determine a threshold. See whether people with impaired conventional hearing respond differently. A reproducible effect is an anecdote that has survived enough knobs.

Frey reported that humans could perceive pulsed radiofrequency energy as auditory sensations. The finding entered a field that was still trying to understand what non-ionizing radiation could do biologically. The distinction between ionizing and non-ionizing energy is foundational: microwaves do not carry photons energetic enough to break chemical bonds by the same mechanism as X-rays or gamma rays. That does not make them inert. Radiofrequency fields deposit energy, induce currents, and heat tissue. The interesting question is what patterns of exposure create which effects.

Early bioelectromagnetics was vulnerable to two opposite errors. One was to assume that if an exposure did not ionize molecules, it could not matter. The other was to treat every reported symptom near an antenna as evidence of a novel interaction. Microwave hearing survived because it could be tied to a concrete transduction pathway.

The radar context also teaches a lesson about averages. Safety limits for radiofrequency exposure often focus on time-averaged energy deposition because tissue heating is a major established hazard at sufficiently high levels. Pulsed systems complicate intuition. A radar can produce enormous instantaneous peak power while maintaining a much lower average power because the pulse occupies only a tiny fraction of time. For thermal injury, average and local specific absorption remain central. For a thermoelastic auditory transient, energy per pulse and rise time can become unusually important.

This is not exotic mathematics. It is bookkeeping across timescales.

Imagine depositing the same total energy in a cup of water over an hour or in a brief pulse. The final bulk temperature increase can be similar, but the mechanical response during deposition is not. Rapid local heating does not give the material time to expand gently. The expansion launches stress. In tissue, that stress becomes sound.

Frey's radar therefore sits at the boundary between two engineering cultures. The radio engineer thinks in carrier frequency, pulse width, antenna gain, duty cycle, and field strength. The auditory physiologist thinks in pressure, cochlear mechanics, neural thresholds, and perceived pitch. The effect only becomes intelligible when the two descriptions are allowed to meet.

This interdisciplinary seam is precisely where later intelligence analysis struggled. A security officer hears “microwave” and thinks transmitter. A physician hears dizziness and thinks vestibular disorder. An acoustician hears a recording and looks for a source in air. A neurologist sees cognitive complaints and asks about injury. A counterintelligence analyst asks who had motive and access. Each discipline can be competent while the total question remains misassembled.

The history of microwave hearing warns against choosing one discipline too early.

Experiments after Frey clarified the mechanism. Foster and Finch's 1974 work showed that pulsed microwaves generated acoustic transients in water. The result was elegantly deflationary. A phenomenon that sounded like direct electromagnetic stimulation of the nervous system could be explained substantially through ordinary thermoelastic acoustics. Lin's analyses in the 1970s compared radiation pressure, electrostrictive forces, and thermal expansion, finding the thermal mechanism capable of masking the others. Later animal studies recorded auditory-system responses to microwave pulses and showed behavior consistent with the energy-per-pulse predictions.

The effect became less mystical as evidence accumulated.

That is how frontier science is supposed to age.

The most useful extraordinary phenomena are not those that remain forever beyond explanation. They are those that enter the laboratory strange and leave with constraints. Microwave hearing did not disappear when the mechanism became mundane. It became stronger evidence because anyone proposing it in a new context inherited the mechanism's requirements.

There is another detail worth holding. Audible perception is not injury. The threshold at which a person notices a click is not automatically a threshold for neurological damage. The two outcomes involve different questions. A sensory event can be startling and unpleasant without leaving structural injury. Conversely, exposures capable of causing tissue damage need not announce themselves with the same perceptual signature.

Public discussion of Havana often used the reported sound as though it were a dosimeter. It is not. The fact that someone heard or felt something does not by itself tell us the absorbed energy, pulse structure, distance, or tissue endpoint. A click can establish that some physical or perceptual event occurred. It cannot reconstruct a transmitter.

Frey's radar gives us a useful control because the source in the experiments was known. When the exposure apparatus is in front of you, threshold measurements mean something. The antenna geometry can be measured. The pulse can be recorded. The subject can be moved. Sham exposures can be used. The experiment can be repeated.

An alleged covert exposure removes nearly all of that support.

The source is missing. The waveform is unknown. The range may be uncertain. The first measurement often occurs after the event. Symptoms are gathered under stress. Witnesses talk. Media descriptions alter expectations. Security investigations and medical care proceed simultaneously. The result is a reconstruction problem in which the most important independent variable was never recorded.

That does not make investigation futile. It changes what counts as strong evidence.

Instrumented detection during an event would matter enormously. A reproducible spatial boundary would matter. A measured RF transient synchronized with acute symptoms would matter. A device recovered with a relevant emission profile would matter. Independent exposure biomarkers with known dose-response behavior would matter.

Without those, the Frey effect remains what it has always been: a demonstrated interaction between pulsed electromagnetic energy and hearing.

The fact is strong.

The historical inference is not included.
