# The Click Inside the Skull

The first useful fact is small enough to disappear inside the argument.

A pulse of microwave energy can make a person hear a click.

Not because the microwave carrier frequency is somehow audible. It is not. Human hearing lives in the rough neighborhood of tens of hertz to tens of kilohertz; the electromagnetic fields used in microwave-hearing experiments can occupy hundreds of megahertz or gigahertz. The ear is not decoding a radio station in the ordinary sense. Something else happens between absorption and perception.

The effect is usually called the microwave auditory effect or the Frey effect, after Allan H. Frey, who studied it in humans in the early 1960s. Subjects exposed to suitable pulsed fields reported clicks, buzzes, knocks, or related brief auditory sensations. The apparent source could feel internal or near the head. The phenomenon was peculiar enough to invite poor explanations, but it proved stubborn enough to become a legitimate bioelectromagnetic problem.

There is an easy mistake here. Once a phenomenon acquires a name, later readers assume the name contains the mechanism. It does not. “Microwave hearing” describes the experience and the stimulus class. The difficult work was figuring out how energy that did not arrive as audible sound ended up exciting an auditory pathway.

The eventual answer is almost offensively mechanical.

Tissue absorbs a short burst of electromagnetic energy. Absorption produces a tiny, rapid temperature increase. The material expands. Because the heating happens quickly, the expansion creates a pressure transient. That pressure wave can propagate through the head and reach the cochlea. The auditory system then does what it normally does with pressure waves: it converts mechanical motion into neural activity.

The microwave does not have to cook the head. In the auditory regime, the relevant temperature increment can be minuscule. The key variable is the rapidity and spatial pattern of energy deposition, not a consciously perceptible rise in average temperature. Kenneth Foster and Edward Finch demonstrated the central thermoacoustic principle in 1974 by generating acoustic transients in water with pulsed microwaves. The measured pressures were adequate to explain the reported clicks. James Lin's later modeling compared candidate transduction mechanisms and found thermoelastic stress sufficient to dominate the alternatives under typical conditions.

This matters because the phrase “energy weapon” encourages the wrong mental picture. We imagine a beam powerful enough to burn, then ask how anyone could fail to notice such heating. Microwave hearing lives in a different part of the parameter space. A rapid pulse can be perceptually efficient while depositing a modest average amount of energy. Peak power, pulse width, repetition, frequency, geometry, absorption pattern, skull dimensions, and hearing sensitivity all matter.

The effect also forces an odd distinction between sensation and source. Under ordinary circumstances, hearing tells us that pressure variations have traveled through air from a vibrating object. The auditory system is useful partly because the geometry of those waves carries information about location. With microwave hearing, the pressure wave can be generated within tissue. The apparent location does not have to correspond to an external acoustic emitter.

That can be deeply disorienting without being mysterious in the supernatural sense.

The same distinction appears in bone conduction. Put a vibrating transducer against the skull and sound reaches the cochlea through a pathway that bypasses the usual airborne route. Anyone who has used a bone-conduction headset has encountered an auditory experience whose physical path differs from the default expectation. Microwave hearing is stranger because the initial coupling is electromagnetic and can be contactless, but by the time the cochlea receives the relevant stimulus, ordinary mechanics has reentered the story.

The literature established several useful boundaries. Thresholds depend strongly on energy per pulse. Experiments in the 1990s using radiofrequency coils for magnetic resonance found human auditory responses over a broad frequency range, with localized exposures requiring lower total pulse energy than homogeneous head exposures. A 1996 radar-hazard review called microwave hearing the only well-established specific effect in realistic pulsed-microwave exposure situations and noted threshold energy densities in sensitive individuals on the order of tens of millijoules per square meter per pulse. Those numbers are not a weapon recipe. They are reminders that a phenomenon can be experimentally accessible without requiring science-fiction physics.

This should have simplified the later Havana argument. It did the opposite.

When diplomats reported hearing strange sounds, some commentators treated any mention of microwaves as evidence of fringe thinking. The Frey effect made that dismissal untenable. A person can indeed perceive a sound-like event from pulsed RF exposure. Other commentators made the inverse mistake: because microwave hearing is real, a microwave attack must explain the incidents. That inference is equally bad.

A mechanism is a bridge between two states. It is not a historical witness.

Suppose a window shatters. We know stones can shatter windows. We may even find laboratory data describing the energy required. That does not tell us whether a stone, a pressure wave, thermal stress, a manufacturing flaw, or deliberate vandalism broke this particular pane. The more precisely we understand the mechanics, the less excuse we have for confusing possibility with attribution.

Microwave hearing gives the Havana problem a known physical anchor. It tells us that one reported feature—an abrupt localized auditory sensation—does not require an ordinary acoustic source. It also tells us something about expected dependencies. Pulse structure should matter. Energy deposition in the head should matter. Frequency and geometry should influence thresholds. Hearing pathways should be involved. A proposed exposure must satisfy those constraints rather than merely using the word *microwave*.

That last sentence is important because public debate about frontier mechanisms often becomes qualitatively physical. A beam could do this. A pulse might do that. A classified device could be stronger. Perhaps. But physics becomes useful when it reduces freedom.

If microwave hearing is invoked, the proposed signal has to produce enough rapid absorbed energy to launch an acoustic transient. If injury is invoked, the relevant biological endpoint needs a plausible dose and pathway. If directionality is invoked, the aperture, range, propagation environment, and targeting geometry matter. If concealment is invoked, unwanted emissions and detectability become part of the system problem.

Each additional claim makes the device harder, not easier, to imagine responsibly.

There is also a cultural reason the Frey effect became such volatile evidence. Most physical facts that enter national-security disputes are familiar. Radar exists. Lasers exist. Nerve agents exist. When an unfamiliar but real effect enters the conversation, it can be used rhetorically by both sides. Skeptics hear the unfamiliarity and classify it with impossible claims. Believers hear the experimental validation and borrow that legitimacy for unvalidated extensions.

The phenomenon itself sits patiently between them.

A short pulse enters tissue. A tiny amount of heat appears quickly. Tissue expands. A pressure wave forms. The cochlea responds.

No telepathy. No secret Soviet neuroscience required. No proof of Havana.

Just a click inside the skull.

The rest of the book begins with the discipline of leaving it there until another piece of evidence earns the next step.
