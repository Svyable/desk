# Havana, 2016

The first reports from Havana did not arrive as a clean new disease.

They arrived as episodes.

U.S. government personnel and family members described abrupt sensory experiences—sound, pressure, vibration, sometimes a feeling of directionality—followed in some cases by headache, dizziness, nausea, tinnitus, hearing complaints, visual difficulty, sleep disturbance, cognitive problems, imbalance, and other symptoms. The first known cluster emerged among personnel in Cuba in late 2016. The reports eventually spread to China, Europe, Washington, and other locations. The name *Havana Syndrome* escaped into public use before investigators had established either a syndrome or a cause.

The original Havana cases were compelling partly because of their timing and occupational setting. These were diplomats and intelligence personnel in a country where the United States had an obvious adversarial relationship with local and foreign intelligence services. Some people described a sudden event localized to a room or area. Some said the sensation stopped when they moved. The pattern invited an external source.

It also invited retrospective compression.

Later cases did not all look like the early cases. Some lacked the sudden auditory or pressure onset. Some involved chronic symptoms that overlap with migraine, vestibular disorders, persistent postural-perceptual dizziness, traumatic stress, infection, medication effects, sleep disruption, and many other conditions. A case category designed for security reporting began absorbing events that might not share one mechanism.

This is the first place where the book has to resist the ordinary seduction of a mystery. A mystery becomes narratively satisfying when every clue belongs to one hidden cause. Real surveillance datasets do not owe us that economy.

The early medical literature gave the reports seriousness. In 2018, a University of Pennsylvania team published a JAMA case series describing 21 U.S. government personnel who had reported directional audible and sensory phenomena in Havana. The authors documented persistent cognitive, vestibular, oculomotor, sleep, auditory, and headache complaints, with objective abnormalities in several domains. Fourteen of the 21 were held from work at the time of multidisciplinary evaluation. The investigators described a constellation of neurological manifestations without a history of blunt head trauma.

A case series can establish that patients were symptomatic and that clinicians found abnormalities. It cannot by itself establish a common cause.

There was no randomized exposure. No known transmitter. No waveform. No pre-event baseline for most measures. Referral and selection were shaped by the very phenomenon under investigation. These limitations were not an accusation against the clinicians. They were properties of the evidence.

The distinction became more important after 2019, when a larger imaging analysis from the Penn group reported group differences in brain structure and functional connectivity. Headlines hardened around brain injury. Yet the imaging findings were exploratory, involved many comparisons, and were difficult to interpret mechanistically. In 2024, NIH researchers using a larger, carefully matched cohort and highly reproducible MRI protocol did not replicate a consistent structural or functional brain signature.

The history therefore did not move from ignorance to proof. It moved from an alarming clinical pattern to a more complicated phenotype.

That is what often happens when a rare event receives sustained scrutiny.

The first cohort is assembled because the cases look similar. Later surveillance widens. Controls improve. Follow-up stretches. Some findings become stronger; others dissolve. The category splits.

Havana also became entangled with policy unusually quickly. The State Department had to protect employees. The CIA had to investigate whether officers were being targeted. Congress demanded answers and benefits. The Department of Defense inherited care obligations. Intelligence agencies searched for adversary capabilities. The same person could simultaneously be a patient, witness, security subject, compensation claimant, and source of intelligence.

Those roles create different definitions of enough evidence.

A clinician may treat disabling dizziness without knowing the cause. A counterintelligence officer may investigate a foreign service based on circumstantial indicators far below scientific proof. Congress may create benefits because uncertainty should not become a barrier to care. A physicist may reject a proposed mechanism because the exposure geometry is underspecified. None of those actors is necessarily contradicting the others.

Public argument tended to make them contradict one another anyway.

The sudden-onset cases remained the most interesting for an external-stimulus hypothesis because they included sensory phenomena that seemed temporally linked to symptom onset. The 2022 IC expert panel explicitly narrowed its analysis to a subset whose features it considered distinctive. That narrowing is critical. The panel was not saying pulsed RF explained every person ever counted under the AHI label. It was asking whether a mechanism could plausibly produce the core characteristics of a smaller class.

This is how one rescues signal from an expanding category.

Suppose a hundred reports include ten abrupt, spatially localized sensory events followed immediately by a similar acute symptom pattern, while ninety involve heterogeneous symptoms without the same onset. Treating all hundred as one syndrome can dilute evidence for the ten and create false coherence among the ninety. Splitting the category can simultaneously strengthen and weaken the external-source hypothesis: stronger for the distinctive subset, weaker as a universal explanation.

The January 2025 intelligence assessment essentially lived inside that tension. Most IC components continued to judge foreign-adversary responsibility very unlikely for the events reported as possible AHIs. Years of collection had not tied a foreign actor to a specific event. Yet two components moved toward greater belief in foreign RF capability, based on new reporting about directed-energy research. One component saw roughly even odds that a foreign actor had used a novel device against a small undetermined subset; another saw roughly even odds that a capability existed that could have produced some experiences, while still judging actual deployment unlikely.

The disagreement is narrower than the headlines made it sound.

No component publicly identified a device.

No component publicly identified an operator at a specific incident.

No component said the whole AHI population had a single RF cause.

The split concerned capability and a small subset.

That makes Havana a better book subject than a solved case would have been. The evidence has matured enough to rule out some lazy claims but not enough to close every path.

It is no longer responsible to say the government concluded nothing happened. Personnel experienced real symptoms, and official bodies continue to investigate a small number of events and foreign capabilities. It is also no longer responsible to say a directed-energy attack has been established. The intelligence community's dominant judgment points away from foreign causation, and medical studies have not found a common persistent injury signature.

The mystery survives in a smaller room.

That room is where this book will stay.
