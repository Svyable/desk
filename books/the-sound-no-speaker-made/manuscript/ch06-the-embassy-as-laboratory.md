# The Embassy as Laboratory

An embassy is a bad place to do science and a good place to discover that your assumptions were incomplete.

The U.S. Embassy in Moscow contained nearly every variable an epidemiologist would prefer to separate. Personnel rotated in and out. Families lived nearby. Stress was unusual and persistent. The building hosted intelligence activity, diplomatic work, communications equipment, countermeasures, and adversary surveillance. Exposure to the Moscow Signal varied by room, time, source direction, and changes in Soviet transmission. Medical records were collected under administrative and political pressure. Some people knew about the signal; others did not for years.

Then illness occurred, because illness always occurs in a population.

The moment officials suspected a possible environmental hazard, ordinary disease became evidence-shaped.

This is not an accusation of hysteria. It is a statistical fact. If a workforce is large enough and observed long enough, cancers, headaches, miscarriages, fatigue, blood abnormalities, dizziness, and neurological complaints will appear. The investigative task is to determine whether their rates, timing, types, and exposure relationships differ from expectation.

That sounds simple until the denominator becomes classified.

Who exactly was exposed? At what intensity? For how long? Which rooms? Which years? What other RF sources existed? Which health outcomes were being searched for before the signal became known, and which were noticed afterward? Were comparison groups matched for posting, occupation, age, and stress? How many outcomes were examined before one appeared unusual?

These questions are not skeptical decoration. They are the machinery that keeps coincidence from becoming causation.

The Moscow records show officials struggling with them. Medical consultants examined Embassy personnel. State Department communications alternated between concern and reassurance as measurements and interpretations changed. In 1977, State Department testimony reported no observed harmful health effects among former personnel while noting a small blood-composition difference in one group that appeared not to be clinically harmful and resolved after departure. Earlier internal statements had treated prolonged exposure as a potential hazard.

The contradiction is less dramatic when the verbs are kept intact.

Potential hazard.

Observed effect.

Causal relationship.

These are different levels of claim.

A risk assessment can reasonably recommend reducing an uncontrolled foreign exposure without proving injury. A physician can reassure a patient that no causal disease pattern has been demonstrated while still supporting precaution. Intelligence officials can treat the same signal as hostile technical activity even if it is biologically harmless.

This separation is one of the great lost skills in public controversy.

When Havana arrived, the categories collapsed again. Personnel reported genuine symptoms. Some cases had sudden sensory onsets. Physicians identified vestibular and cognitive abnormalities in early cohorts. Journalists reported the possibility of directed energy. Intelligence agencies investigated foreign actors. Soon any criticism of the weapon hypothesis could sound like criticism of the patients, and any acknowledgment of patient suffering could be recruited as support for the weapon hypothesis.

The Moscow archive shows why those moves are unnecessary.

A person can be ill and the suspected exposure can be innocent of the illness.

A hostile exposure can occur and fail to cause illness.

A hostile exposure can cause a sensory event without causing persistent structural injury.

Different people in one named episode can have different causes.

These possibilities are difficult to communicate because institutions prefer one operational category. Benefits programs need eligibility rules. Counterintelligence investigations need case definitions. Medical studies need inclusion criteria. Congressional committees need a subject. The media needs a noun.

“Havana Syndrome” did enormous organizational work before anyone knew whether syndrome was the right word.

Moscow had the same administrative pressure but fewer cameras. The government had to decide whether to evacuate personnel, modify the building, confront Soviet officials, continue intelligence operations, and disclose the exposure. Every decision created incentives around the scientific interpretation. If the signal was dangerous, prior nondisclosure looked negligent. If it was harmless, aggressive diplomatic protest risked appearing theatrical. If it was part of an intelligence contest, public technical detail could expose U.S. capabilities.

Science entered a room already full of consequences.

This is why the phrase *follow the evidence* can become naïve in intelligence medicine. Evidence does not arrive untouched. Case definitions determine which incidents enter the dataset. Security classification determines which scientists can inspect contextual information. Medical confidentiality limits sharing. Diplomatic sensitivity affects release. People who feel harmed may have compensation claims. Agencies have reputational stakes in earlier judgments. Adversaries can seed disinformation into precisely these seams.

None of this means objective inquiry is impossible. It means the architecture of inquiry needs to be explicit.

The best studies create controls that resemble the affected population in the boring ways that matter. NIH's 2024 AHI work improved on earlier comparisons by including matched healthy controls, including government employees with similar assignments who did not report AHIs. The imaging protocol emphasized reproducibility. The negative result—no significant group differences in structural or functional MRI measures after correction for multiple comparisons—therefore carried real weight.

It did not retroactively prove that no external event occurred.

That restraint is important. Absence of a persistent MRI signature months later cannot exclude every transient physiological insult. But it does challenge narratives of a common severe structural brain injury across the studied cohort.

The Embassy as laboratory teaches a broader lesson about environmental mysteries. The most dangerous evidence is often the evidence collected after everyone knows what they are looking for. Retrospective awareness changes reporting, testing, and attention. A symptom that would once have been coded as migraine or vertigo may be routed into a special program. That can improve care. It can also create a surveillance artifact.

Prospective instrumentation is therefore disproportionately valuable.

If diplomats in high-risk posts carry environmental sensors before an incident, the signal question changes. If baseline vestibular and auditory data exist before deployment, the medical comparison improves. If RF detectors log wideband events continuously rather than being brought in days later, the source hypothesis becomes testable. If case definitions are set before politically salient clusters emerge, selection becomes less vulnerable to narrative pressure.

The 2022 IC expert panel recommended better data, biomarkers, detectors, clinical measurements, and devices to aid research. That recommendation can be read as a confession of the central problem: by the time the most disputed cases were investigated, the decisive measurements often no longer existed.

Moscow had been irradiated for years before the biological question was organized properly.

Havana produced symptoms before a sensor network existed.

The common failure is not necessarily that governments ignored exotic physics.

It is that they were forced to reconstruct an exposure after the moment capable of proving it had passed.
