# The Expert Panel

In 2022, the Intelligence Community did something unusually useful: it separated mechanism from attribution.

The distinction should have been routine. It was not.

The CIA and Office of the Director of National Intelligence convened an expert panel of scientists, physicians, and engineers to examine possible causal mechanisms for anomalous health incidents. The panel reviewed more than a thousand classified documents along with incident reports, scientific literature, and other material. Its declassified summary presented six findings.

The first was that reported signs and symptoms were genuine and compelling.

The second was that a subset of cases could not be easily explained by known environmental or medical conditions and might involve external stimuli.

The third was the finding that transformed public debate: pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly in the radiofrequency range, plausibly explained the core characteristics of that subset, although information gaps remained.

Ultrasound was judged plausible only in close-access scenarios. Several other proposed mechanisms were considered implausible for the core characteristics in the absence of additional factors.

The panel did not identify an attacker.

It did not say it had detected a weapon.

It did not say all AHIs shared one cause.

It did not convert microwave hearing into proof of injury.

The report is interesting precisely because it stops short.

Mechanism assessment asks whether a physical process could produce an observed pattern under plausible conditions. Attribution asks who did it, if anyone. Forensic identification asks what device or source produced a particular event. Epidemiology asks whether a population shares a causal exposure. These are different jobs.

The panel narrowed its job to the first.

That allowed it to take pulsed RF seriously without making an intelligence claim the evidence could not support.

Why did pulsed RF survive the filter?

Partly because there is known physics for rapid energy deposition producing unusual sensory effects. Microwave hearing demonstrates that RF can create an abrupt auditory perception without ordinary external sound. Directed RF can be spatially selective relative to many environmental exposures. Pulse structure allows high peak power with lower average energy. Bioeffects from sufficiently intense RF exposure are established in other contexts, even if the exact symptom constellation remained uncertain.

The panel also reportedly considered features such as abrupt onset, location specificity, and sensations that seemed directional. A hypothetical external source should explain those better than a diffuse environmental toxin or ordinary infectious process.

But the same features that make pulsed RF attractive also make it testable.

A directed source has geometry.

A pulse has a waveform.

An emitter has power and an aperture.

Propagation through walls and windows depends on frequency and materials.

Tissue absorption depends on frequency and orientation.

A biological effect should have some dose relation.

The absence of those measurements in most incidents created the panel's information gaps.

This is where “plausible” is often misheard as “probable.” Scientists use plausibility to mean a mechanism is consistent enough with known principles to remain under consideration. Intelligence analysts use confidence language differently. Neither convention maps neatly onto everyday speech.

The panel's work therefore became ammunition for opposite claims. Advocates said the intelligence community had confirmed directed energy. Skeptics pointed to later attribution assessments and said the panel had been superseded. Both compress distinct questions.

The 2023 Intelligence Community assessment concluded that most agencies judged foreign-adversary involvement very unlikely. That was an attribution judgment based on years of collection, investigation, foreign-weapons analysis, and medical evidence. It did not repeal thermoelasticity.

The January 2025 assessment made the separation even clearer. Five components still judged it very unlikely that a foreign actor had an RF capability capable of producing AHI-like symptoms. But two components shifted because they evaluated new reporting as showing progress in foreign directed-energy programs. One now judged such an RF antipersonnel capability likely; another saw roughly even odds. Yet the overall judgment still pointed away from adversary responsibility for reported incidents.

This is not institutional incoherence.

It is the natural shape of uncertain evidence when capability and use are scored independently.

A country can possess a weapon it did not use.

A mechanism can be physically plausible while intelligence collection finds no operator.

A patient can have genuine symptoms while no common biomarker appears.

The public desire for one verdict is the incoherent part.

The expert panel also made recommendations that deserve more attention than its headline mechanism finding. It called for improved data collection, biomarkers, detectors, communications, clinical measurements, biological-effects research, and research devices. In other words, the experts were telling the government that future incidents needed to be instrumented before the evidence evaporated.

That is the least glamorous and most consequential conclusion.

If a future case occurs in a sensor-rich environment and no RF transient is detected across an adequate band, the RF hypothesis weakens sharply for that event. If a transient is measured with spatial and temporal correlation, the investigation moves into an entirely different evidentiary class. If biomarkers tied to a known exposure regime emerge, retrospective medicine becomes stronger. If nothing reproducible appears after years of prospective monitoring, the prior should fall.

Science advances when anomalies are forced to leave traces.

The panel did not solve Havana.

It described what a solution would need to measure.
