# Heat Too Small to Feel

The mechanism depends on an insultingly small temperature change.

That is one reason people misunderstand it.

When ordinary language says microwave, ordinary memory supplies an oven. A microwave oven heats food because water-rich material absorbs electromagnetic energy over time. The association is so strong that proposals involving radiofrequency biological effects are often sorted into two categories: either the exposure cooks tissue or nothing happens.

Thermoelastic hearing occupies the gap.

A short pulse deposits energy faster than the heated volume can mechanically relax. The local temperature rises by a tiny amount. Thermal expansion creates a pressure pulse. The pressure pulse travels through tissue. The cochlea is extraordinarily sensitive to mechanical disturbance, so the pressure need not resemble the destructive wave one might associate with blast injury.

The useful quantity is not “did the head get hot?” but how much energy was absorbed, where, and how quickly.

This distinction is familiar in other domains. A camera flash can deliver intense light for a fraction of a second without heating a room. A hammer can deliver a high peak force while transferring modest total energy. A radar can emit high peak power with a low duty cycle. Biological response depends on the timescale and geometry of coupling.

Foster and Finch's 1974 experiment reduced the mystery by moving from heads to water. If a pressure transient appeared in water exposed to pulsed microwaves, one did not need to posit direct electrical decoding by the brain. The material itself was acting as the first acoustic transducer.

The head is more complicated than a tank of water. It contains bone, cerebrospinal fluid, brain tissue, vasculature, air cavities, and structures with different acoustic impedances. Electromagnetic absorption is frequency-dependent and spatially uneven. Reflections and interference shape the acoustic field. Yet the central mechanism survives this complexity because it does not depend on a miraculous tissue receptor. It depends on bulk physical properties shared by ordinary materials.

That makes microwave hearing both less exotic and more relevant to weapon speculation.

If a sensation can be produced without large bulk heating, then “no burns were observed” does not rule out every RF exposure. But moving from perception to injury requires a second argument. One must show how the hypothesized waveform could create the reported biological effects without producing other expected consequences.

The 2022 Intelligence Community experts confronted exactly this problem. Their declassified summary did not say that pulsed electromagnetic energy had been proven as the cause of anomalous health incidents. It said that pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly in the radiofrequency range, plausibly explained the core characteristics of a subset of cases, with information gaps. They judged ultrasound plausible only in close-access scenarios and considered several other mechanisms implausible for the core characteristics.

Plausibility here is a technical filter. It means the physics and known bioeffects do not immediately disqualify the mechanism. It is not the same as a forensic match.

The difference can feel frustrating because intelligence questions usually demand actors and verbs: Russia did X; a device caused Y; an officer was targeted. Physics often returns constraints instead. A source capable of producing a specific field at a target must have enough power, an appropriate aperture, and line-of-sight or propagation conditions. A pulsed exposure must deposit energy in the relevant tissue volumes. A waveform that creates auditory transients has characteristic dependencies. A system intended to be covert must manage sidelobes, reflections, operator access, and detectability.

These constraints are gifts. They make the hypothesis expensive.

The more effects a single device is asked to explain, the more expensive it becomes. It must account for localized sounds, pressure sensations, vestibular symptoms, headaches, cognitive effects, occasional reports of directional boundaries, differences among rooms and locations, and the absence of a consistent persistent biomarker in later cohorts. Some of those features may not belong to one mechanism at all.

This is where syndrome naming becomes dangerous. A syndrome can be clinically useful as a pattern without implying one cause. But in public language, a single name encourages a single story. Once hundreds of heterogeneous events across countries and years are placed under one label, the mechanism must either stretch to include everything or the label has to loosen.

The 2025 intelligence assessment loosened it. Most components still judged foreign-adversary causation very unlikely across the reported AHI population. At the same time, two components shifted their view of foreign RF capability based on new reporting about directed-energy research. One assessed it likely that a foreign actor had an RF antipersonnel capability capable of biological effects consistent with some reported symptoms. Another saw roughly even odds of a capability that could account for some experiences. Those judgments were explicitly separated from deployment.

That split is scientifically healthy even if institutionally awkward.

Capability is not use.

Use is not causation.

Causation in one event is not causation across the category.

The phrase “heat too small to feel” also helps correct another misconception: that nonthermal means nonphysical. Bioelectromagnetic literature sometimes uses *nonthermal* loosely to mean effects that do not arise from bulk temperature elevation. But thermoelastic hearing is produced by heating in a literal microscopic or local sense. The temperature change is simply tiny and fast. Calling the experience nonthermal can obscure the mechanism.

Words become treacherous near thresholds.

The same is true for safety standards. Occupational RF standards are designed around established hazards and practical exposure metrics. An exposure below a time-averaged thermal limit can still contain pulse structure relevant to perception. That does not mean safety standards are useless or that ordinary wireless devices are secretly producing hidden neurological effects. It means a standard answers the hazard question it was designed to answer.

Radar engineers learned this because pulsed systems forced attention to peak quantities. Medical MRI researchers encountered related auditory responses from pulsed RF coils at lower carrier frequencies. Experiments using localized coils found auditory thresholds at lower total pulse energies than broad homogeneous exposures, a reminder that geometry matters as much as headline power.

Geometry is where covert-device stories become engineering stories.

At range, a beam spreads. Walls reflect and attenuate differently by material and frequency. Windows transmit some bands better than others. Near-field and far-field behavior differ. Human heads are not point targets. A source outside a building may have to illuminate more than the intended person. A source inside a building sacrifices standoff. Direction-finding equipment can detect emissions if anyone is looking with the right bandwidth at the right time.

A hypothetical attacker can optimize around some of these constraints. Classified programs exist precisely to exploit engineering margins. But classification cannot remove the margins.

The mature question is therefore not whether enough power can be imagined. Enough power can nearly always be imagined if the device is allowed to become large, close, obvious, and inefficient. The question is whether the complete mission geometry remains plausible after source, range, waveform, concealment, targeting, and effect are specified together.

That is harder.

The first three chapters of this book have stayed with an effect that can be reproduced in the laboratory. This is deliberate. Before entering Moscow and Havana, it is useful to know what secure ground feels like.

A pulse is delivered.

A tiny temperature rise occurs.

A pressure wave is generated.

A subject reports a click.

The apparatus is known.

History will take the apparatus away.
