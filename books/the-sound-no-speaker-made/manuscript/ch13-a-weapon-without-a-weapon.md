# A Weapon Without a Weapon

The strangest object in the Havana story is the weapon nobody has publicly produced.

That absence should neither end the inquiry nor be waved away.

A covert device capable of producing acute sensory phenomena and biological effects at useful standoff distances would be an intelligence target of obvious value. If such a system were used repeatedly against American personnel across multiple countries, investigators would expect some combination of emissions intercepts, procurement traces, test histories, foreign technical reporting, operator communications, captured hardware, defectors, human-source reporting, or pattern evidence linking incidents to an actor.

The public Intelligence Community assessments say that years of targeting, collection, and investigation have not produced compelling intelligence tying a foreign actor to a specific reported AHI. That is one of the strongest pieces of evidence against a broad campaign.

It is not equivalent to a proof of nonexistence.

Intelligence services miss programs. Compartmentalization works. Foreign laboratories can run small prototypes without the industrial footprint of an aircraft fleet. A device used rarely by a disciplined service could avoid detection, especially if investigators initially lacked the right collection requirements.

But every concession has to be priced.

A hypothesis that survives only by assuming perfect secrecy, perfect operational discipline, perfect technical reliability, no recoverable emissions, no insider reporting, no captured components, and no intelligence penetration becomes increasingly expensive. Secrecy is not magic. It is a system with failure modes.

This is where historical comparison helps. The Soviet microwave illumination of the U.S. Embassy in Moscow was secretive, but the signal itself was measurable. Project PANDORA was classified, but it generated contracts, minutes, experiments, and eventually declassified records. U.S. foreign-materiel exploitation programs remained hidden for years, yet aircraft had to be flown, maintained, crashed, and staffed. Project Pluto built facilities that later became environmental-cleanup sites.

Real secret technology leaves metabolism.

A compact RF device could leave less than a nuclear ramjet. That changes the expected residue, not the principle.

The 2025 assessment is revealing because two IC components shifted their view of foreign capability without claiming a recovered weapon. Their change was based on intelligence reporting they interpreted as evidence of progress in foreign directed-energy research. One component judged it likely that a foreign actor had an RF antipersonnel capability able to cause biological effects consistent with some AHI symptoms. Another judged roughly even odds that a foreign actor had a capability that could account for some experiences. The majority remained more skeptical.

This is what evidence looks like before hardware reaches the table.

Analysts may know about research programs through classified collection while lacking proof that a specific device exists in operational form. Laboratory progress can shift a capability estimate. Capability estimates can alter the prior probability assigned to incident reports. Yet incident attribution still requires linkage.

Public debate often skips this middle layer.

If the intelligence community says a capability may exist, believers hear confirmation of attacks. If the community says no attacks are linked, skeptics hear confirmation that no capability exists. The 2025 document says neither.

A weapon system has at least three lives.

There is the physical principle.

There is the engineered capability.

There is operational use.

Microwave hearing occupies the first category securely. Some forms of RF injury and directed-energy effects occupy the first and, in known military systems for other purposes, portions of the second. A covert antipersonnel system designed around AHI-like effects remains publicly unverified as a specific device. Operational use against the reported personnel remains unproven and judged unlikely by most IC components.

This layered model matters because engineering difficulty changes sharply between categories.

Producing microwave hearing in a laboratory is straightforward enough to have been demonstrated for decades. Producing a repeatable auditory sensation at a chosen person through a wall from a concealed source is a harder system problem. Producing disabling neurological symptoms without obvious heating or other signatures is harder still. Doing so covertly across variable rooms and distances while avoiding detection adds another layer. Operating globally against intelligence targets without human or signals intelligence exposure adds yet another.

The phrase *directed energy weapon* compresses all of that into two words.

A serious book has to decompress it.

One useful comparison is radar. Radar proves that high-power pulsed RF can be generated, directed, and propagated over distance. It does not prove an antipersonnel effect at a desired dose. Another comparison is active-denial technology, which proves that millimeter-wave energy can be directed at humans to create intense superficial heating and pain. It does not reproduce the reported AHI pattern and operates through a known, readily perceptible thermal mechanism. A third comparison is microwave hearing, which proves that pulsed RF can create an internal auditory sensation at lower average heating. It does not prove persistent injury.

Each existing technology solves a different portion of the hypothetical device.

The unknown system must assemble the relevant portions without inheriting all of their disadvantages.

That is possible in principle more often than it is possible in procurement.

The difference between a laboratory effect and a field weapon is usually found in the boring nouns: power supply, antenna, cooling, aiming, waveform generation, packaging, range, duty cycle, safety, operator training, reliability, and intelligence signature.

A covert weapon adds another noun: deniability.

Deniability can shape design. An effect with no obvious projectile, residue, or conventional acoustic source is attractive precisely because victims may struggle to prove exposure. But a deniable effect is not necessarily an undetectable emitter. Electromagnetic energy can be measured. A sufficiently instrumented target environment changes the contest.

That is why the absence of prospective detection is such a loss in the historical AHI cases. Investigators were often trying to answer a hardware question after the alleged hardware had left.

The correct response is not to fill the missing waveform with imagination.

It is to build the next room differently.
