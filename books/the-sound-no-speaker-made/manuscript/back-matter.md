# Back Matter

## Evidence note

This manuscript treats microwave hearing as an established bioelectromagnetic effect and the Moscow Signal, Project PANDORA, AHI investigations, and official Intelligence Community assessments as documented history. It does not treat those facts as proof that a foreign actor used a directed-energy device against U.S. personnel.

## Chronology

- **1950s** — U.S. personnel detect electromagnetic illumination of the U.S. Embassy in Moscow; the phenomenon later becomes known as the Moscow Signal or TUMS.
- **Early 1960s** — Allan H. Frey publishes experimental work on human perception of pulsed microwave energy.
- **1965** — ARPA initiates Project PANDORA to investigate possible biological and behavioral effects relevant to the Moscow Signal.
- **1967** — Secretary of State Dean Rusk raises the Moscow microwave signal with Soviet Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko.
- **1969** — PANDORA meeting records state there is insufficient evidence to draw conclusions about effects of the Moscow Signal on human behavior.
- **1974** — Foster and Finch publish thermoacoustic evidence explaining microwave hearing through rapid thermal expansion.
- **1975–1976** — Intensified concern over Moscow microwave exposure produces renewed U.S.-Soviet protests, medical review, and eventual briefings of Embassy personnel.
- **1977** — ARPA leadership tells Congress it does not foresee DARPA development of microwave weapons aimed at altering nervous-system function or behavior.
- **Late 2016** — U.S. government personnel in Havana begin reporting abrupt sensory experiences and neurological symptoms.
- **2018** — JAMA publishes the first major clinical case series of 21 Havana personnel. JASON analyzes associated recordings and judges famous examples biological/mechanical, including a cricket match.
- **2019** — Penn investigators publish exploratory advanced neuroimaging group differences in a Havana cohort.
- **2020** — National Academies concludes directed pulsed radiofrequency energy is the most plausible mechanism among those considered for distinctive acute cases, while emphasizing uncertainty.
- **2022** — ODNI/CIA expert panel says pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly RF, plausibly explains the core characteristics of a subset of AHIs.
- **March 2023** — Most IC agencies assess foreign-adversary responsibility for reported AHIs as very unlikely.
- **March 2024** — NIH/JAMA studies find no consistent MRI-detectable brain injury or broad biological abnormality distinguishing AHI participants from matched controls.
- **July 2024** — GAO reports on DOD care access and implementation for AHI patients.
- **January 10, 2025** — Updated IC assessment retains the overall judgment against foreign attribution but records two components shifting toward greater belief in foreign RF capability and possible involvement in a small subset.

## What would change the book

A publicly documented device tied by chain of custody, emissions evidence, operator records, and incident-specific telemetry to one or more reported AHIs would move the central attribution question from disputed to established for those cases. Replicated controlled research showing an RF exposure regime that reliably reproduces the distinctive acute and persistent symptom pattern would materially strengthen mechanism plausibility. Conversely, long-duration prospective monitoring with adequate RF instrumentation across high-risk posts that repeatedly captures acute AHI reports without relevant emissions would weaken directed-RF explanations. A robust common biomarker with a known exposure-dose relationship would also reorganize the medical evidence.
