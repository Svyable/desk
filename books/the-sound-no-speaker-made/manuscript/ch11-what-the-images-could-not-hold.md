# What the Images Could Not Hold

Brain images are dangerously photogenic evidence.

A colored map of statistical differences looks like a picture of injury even when it is nothing of the kind.

In 2019, researchers at the University of Pennsylvania published an advanced neuroimaging study of forty U.S. government personnel who had reported symptoms after possible exposure to directional phenomena in Havana. The analysis found group differences in several MRI-derived measures, including tissue volumes and functional connectivity. The authors were cautious about clinical interpretation, but the public story hardened quickly: Havana had left a mark on the brain.

The study was important.

It was also exploratory.

Neuroimaging produces enormous numbers of potential measurements. Analysts choose preprocessing pipelines, regions, connectivity metrics, correction methods, and comparison groups. Small differences can reach statistical significance without mapping cleanly onto pathology. Scanner differences, motion, demographics, prior health, and multiple comparisons can matter. A group-level difference does not automatically identify a lesion in an individual.

This is not a criticism unique to Havana. It is the ordinary difficulty of modern neuroimaging.

The 2024 NIH work changed the evidentiary balance because it was explicitly designed around reproducibility. Eighty-one AHI participants and forty-eight age- and sex-matched controls underwent structural and functional MRI, with many participants rescanned. The imaging metrics were highly reproducible within individuals. After correction for multiple comparisons, the researchers found no significant differences between the AHI group and controls in brain structure or function. The study did not replicate the earlier findings.

This creates a temptation to reverse the old headline.

No brain injury.

That is too strong.

NIH itself emphasized that lack of a persistent MRI-detectable difference does not exclude a transient adverse event. A physiological insult can occur without leaving a long-term structural signature visible to current imaging. Migraine can be severe without producing a diagnostic MRI lesion. Vestibular dysfunction can persist through network adaptation. Functional neurological symptoms can be disabling without gross structural damage.

What the NIH result does undermine is the claim that the studied AHI population shares a consistent, persistent, MRI-detectable brain injury pattern.

That narrower conclusion is powerful enough.

The imaging story reveals a recurrent failure mode in anomaly research: a technically sophisticated measurement can become less epistemically secure as its visualization becomes more persuasive. The prettier the image, the easier it is to forget the statistical chain between raw signal and colored region.

A brain map is not a photograph of causation.

It is a model output.

The same lesson applies to thermal cameras in UAP cases, spectral plots in materials claims, satellite maps in earthquake precursor studies, and anomaly heatmaps in machine learning. Instrumentation increases rigor only when the measurement model is understood.

Havana's neuroimaging sequence also teaches something about replication under political pressure. The 2019 findings were not fraudulent because a later study failed to reproduce them. Exploratory work often generates signals that later shrink. The correct scientific response is not accusation but updating.

That sounds easier than it is when patients' legitimacy has been attached to the finding.

If a person has been told that imaging shows brain changes, a later null result can feel like erasure. Advocates may fear that agencies will use negative studies to deny benefits. Skeptics may overread the null as proof of psychogenic origin. Scientists then inherit a moral burden that their methods cannot discharge.

The answer is to detach care from causal certainty.

Congress did this in part through laws providing treatment and compensation pathways for affected personnel. GAO's 2024 review focused on access to care and Department of Defense implementation rather than requiring a settled mechanism first. That is administratively sensible. People can deserve treatment even when the causal model remains unresolved.

Once care no longer depends on proving a weapon, science can breathe.

The 2024 NIH papers also improved the control problem. Some healthy controls had similar government employment and overseas assignments. This matters because occupational selection, stress, travel, sleep patterns, and health surveillance can distinguish diplomatic or intelligence personnel from the general population. A control group of ordinary local volunteers may make occupational differences look like exposure effects.

Matching is never perfect.

The AHI group was heterogeneous in geography and timing. Many were scanned weeks or months after onset. Some biological effects, if they occurred, could have resolved. Exposure status was based on reported incidents rather than a measured dose. There was no laboratory-confirmed attack group because no such exposure record existed.

These limitations mean the NIH study cannot answer the weapon question directly.

It answers a medical one: in this cohort, with these methods, researchers did not find a consistent persistent biological or MRI signature distinguishing participants from controls.

That result should constrain every future theory.

A proposed mechanism for the broader cohort should either predict little persistent MRI change or explain why the relevant subset was diluted in the sample. A theory that requires widespread structural brain injury now carries a larger evidentiary debt.

This is how null results make science stronger.

They remove comfortable stories.
