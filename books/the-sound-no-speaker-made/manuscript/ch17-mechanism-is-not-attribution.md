# Mechanism Is Not Attribution

A courtroom and a physics laboratory ask different questions of the same broken window.

The physicist can tell you what impact energy fractures the glass.

The forensic examiner can identify residue and trajectory.

The detective needs a person, motive, opportunity, and evidence linking that person to the act.

Havana spent years confusing those jobs.

The microwave auditory effect answers a mechanism question. It establishes that pulsed radiofrequency energy can produce an auditory sensation through a known thermoelastic pathway. The 2022 IC expert panel went farther and judged pulsed electromagnetic energy a plausible mechanism for the core characteristics of a subset of AHIs.

Neither finding identifies an attacker.

The 2023 and 2025 Intelligence Community assessments concentrated on attribution and capability. Most components judged foreign involvement very unlikely. Their reasoning included years of collection that failed to connect adversaries to reported incidents, investigations that found conventional explanations for some cases, assessments of foreign programs, and medical evidence that did not support a common injury syndrome.

Two components in 2025 became more open to foreign RF capability and possible use in a small subset.

That is where the broken-window analogy becomes useful again.

If you learn that a suspect owns a slingshot, the probability that they broke a window may rise. It does not rise to one. If forensic evidence places them elsewhere, ownership matters less. If a stone matching their ammunition is found inside the room, it matters more.

Capability changes priors.

Linkage changes attribution.

The Havana debate repeatedly treated one as a substitute for the other.

This is common in intelligence history because capabilities are often easier to classify than incidents. Analysts can know that an adversary has a system without knowing who used it on Tuesday. Conversely, a suspicious incident can occur before analysts know the relevant capability exists. The two streams meet imperfectly.

The most famous example is technological surprise. Before the Soviet atomic test in 1949, U.S. estimates of timing were uncertain. After the test, capability was no longer hypothetical. But possession of an atomic bomb did not prove Soviet responsibility for every unexplained explosion thereafter.

The same logic applies at smaller scale.

A foreign RF antipersonnel capability, if it exists, would matter greatly for force protection. It would justify detectors, medical protocols, counterintelligence collection, and diplomatic concern. Those preparations do not require proving that Havana was caused by the device.

This is an underappreciated escape from the political trap.

Governments can defend against a plausible capability while remaining skeptical of historical attribution.

They can care for symptomatic personnel without certifying one causal story.

They can fund bioeffects research without announcing a secret weapon.

They can investigate adversary programs without treating every anomaly as hostile action.

These separations reduce the incentive to overclaim.

The evidentiary matrix in this book is built around that idea. Class one contains facts whose existence is well documented: microwave hearing, Moscow microwave illumination, PANDORA, published AHI clinical studies, official assessments. Class two contains credible but incomplete material: acute witness descriptions, expert-panel feasibility judgments, intelligence reporting summarized without public details, and some mechanism proposals. Class three contains disputed allegations: named foreign operators, specific devices, broad global attack campaigns, and claims of a single injury mechanism. Class four contains synthesis: perhaps a small subset involved external stimulation while the broader category accumulated heterogeneous causes; perhaps early natural or medical cases altered reporting enough to hide later hostile experimentation; perhaps none involved a weapon at all.

The classes are not a ladder toward belief.

They are a way to keep verbs attached to evidence.

Documented.

Reported.

Assessed.

Alleged.

Speculated.

Those verbs do more work than adjectives like extraordinary or credible.

Attribution also needs to account for adversary incentives. A state capable of a deniable RF harassment device might value it precisely because proof is difficult. It could disrupt personnel, create fear, test countermeasures, or produce intelligence costs disproportionate to physical injury. On the other hand, repeatedly using such a device against diplomats risks escalation and discovery for uncertain strategic gain. The motive is plausible but not self-evident.

Friendly institutions have incentives too. An agency that failed to protect officers may prefer natural explanations. An agency accused of dismissing injured personnel may prefer an external adversary. Contractors can benefit from threat research. Advocates can benefit from a coherent cause for compensation and recognition. Skeptics gain status by puncturing official panic. Journalists gain attention from both revelation and debunking.

None of these incentives decides the facts.

They tell us where to demand independent evidence.

The book's central argument can now be stated without drama.

Havana is not a test of whether you believe in microwave weapons.

It is a test of whether you can keep mechanism, capability, incident causation, and actor attribution separate long enough to discover that the evidence for each is different.
