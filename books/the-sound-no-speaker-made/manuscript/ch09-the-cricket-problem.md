# The Cricket Problem

The cricket is funny, which is unfortunate.

Humor changes evidentiary weight in public memory. A mysterious intelligence case involving diplomats, Russia, microwaves, and brain symptoms sounds grave. A cricket sounds ridiculous. Once the insect entered the story, it became tempting to treat the entire affair as an institutional embarrassment.

That is bad science for the same reason treating the microwave auditory effect as proof of attack is bad science.

The cricket explains what it explains.

JASON's acoustic analysis was strong for the recordings it examined. The periodic structure, spectral characteristics, and temporal pattern matched a biological source, particularly the Indies short-tailed cricket. The finding undercut claims that the recorded audio itself was direct evidence of an exotic acoustic or electromagnetic weapon.

It did not explain every reported medical event.

The distinction matters because causal chains often contain irrelevant coincidences. A smoke alarm can beep during a heart attack. The beep is real, recorded, temporally associated, and not causal. The more dramatic the event, the more likely witnesses and investigators are to bind simultaneous sensations into one narrative.

In Havana, the acoustic clue had another complication: some early patients reportedly experienced the sound or pressure as directional or localized. A natural insect call inside a reflective room can produce strong spatial variation through interference, resonance, and ordinary acoustics. JASON considered room acoustics and biological sources. That makes an insect explanation more capable than the punch line suggests.

But again, capability is not case closure.

There were reports without recordings. Reports without obvious sound. Reports in other countries. Reports whose onset descriptions differed. A cricket species in Cuba cannot explain an event in Vienna simply because both were eventually filed as AHI.

The category had outgrown the clue.

This is a common failure in anomaly research. A skeptical explanation succeeds on one famous case and is then promoted into a universal solvent. Balloons explain some UAP reports, therefore every unresolved observation is a balloon. Sleep paralysis explains some abduction experiences, therefore every extraordinary encounter belongs to sleep paralysis. Misidentified animals explain some cryptozoological sightings, therefore every trace is misidentification. The error mirrors credulity: both sides want one cause.

A mature anomaly program should celebrate pluralism at the level of mechanism.

Some reports are insects.

Some are migraine.

Some are vestibular disease.

Some may be functional neurological disorders or persistent postural-perceptual dizziness.

Some may involve environmental exposures.

Some may remain unresolved because the decisive evidence was never collected.

A very small subset could, in principle, involve deliberate external stimulation without forcing that explanation onto the rest.

That last possibility is not a concession to mystery. It is how heterogeneous surveillance categories behave.

The 2025 IC assessment explicitly recognized this structure. Most components assessed foreign involvement was very unlikely overall. Yet the document preserved scenarios under which a small number of cases could have involved a foreign actor, and two components shifted toward greater belief in foreign RF capability. The disagreement did not resurrect the cricket recording as weapon evidence. It moved elsewhere, into intelligence reporting about adversary research and the scientific possibility of RF bioeffects.

The cricket therefore performs a second service: it teaches us not to mistake an evidentiary correction for a moral judgment about witnesses.

People can sincerely report a frightening sound and be wrong about its source. That does not make them dishonest. Perceptual source identification is difficult under normal conditions and worse under stress. The State Department's and intelligence community's obligation to care for symptomatic personnel does not depend on whether every source attribution is correct.

This should be obvious in medicine. A patient can misattribute chest pain to indigestion and still have a myocardial infarction. A patient can fear cancer and have a benign lesion while still deserving evaluation. Cause and care are separable.

National security makes that separation harder because error carries status implications. If an intelligence officer says a foreign service attacked them, a natural explanation can feel like professional invalidation. If an agency accepts every attribution to avoid invalidating personnel, it corrupts its own analytic process.

The only stable position is procedural respect.

Treat the report seriously.

Do not pre-decide the mechanism.

Instrument the environment.

Use matched controls.

Preserve chain of custody.

Score hypotheses against predictions.

Change the conclusion when evidence changes.

That sounds bureaucratic because it is. Good anomaly research is often a triumph of boring procedure over charismatic interpretation.

The cricket also raises an adversarial possibility that the JASON report itself noted in broad form: a visible clue can distract from an invisible mechanism. Intelligence services use deception. A harmless sound could be deliberately introduced to confuse reporting or mask another action. But such a scenario adds actors, intent, and coordination. It should not be adopted merely because it saves a preferred hypothesis from disconfirmation.

A deception hypothesis becomes serious when independent evidence points toward deception.

Without that, it is narrative insurance.

This is one of the most useful tests in controversial research: does the hypothesis become more elaborate exactly when evidence moves against it? If every failed prediction produces another invisible layer, the model is protecting itself rather than learning.

The cricket should be allowed to kill some stories.

It should not be allowed to kill people’s symptoms.

Those are different tasks.
