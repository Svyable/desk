# The First Patients

The first twenty-one patients carried more than their own symptoms.

They carried the shape of the future category.

The 2018 JAMA report from the University of Pennsylvania was medically consequential because it gave a formal clinical description to what had been, until then, a collection of security reports and diplomatic rumors. The cohort included U.S. government personnel evaluated after reporting directional audible or sensory phenomena in Havana. Many described persistent cognitive, vestibular, visual, auditory, sleep, and headache symptoms. Clinicians documented abnormalities in several domains and recommended rehabilitation for some patients.

The paper did not identify an exposure mechanism.

That sentence was technically clear and culturally weak.

Once a case series appears in a prestigious medical journal, the public tends to read diagnosis backward into cause. If patients show neurological abnormalities, then something neurological must have injured them; if an injury occurred after a strange sound in Havana, then the sound-producing device caused it. Each step feels reasonable. Together they exceed the study.

The original clinicians faced a genuine problem. They were not conducting a clean etiological trial. They were trying to evaluate symptomatic people after unusual events. In that setting, the responsible first task is characterization: what hurts, what functions are impaired, what tests are abnormal, what rehabilitation is needed?

Etiology comes later.

This sequence matters because early clinical cohorts in novel syndromes are rarely representative. They are enriched for severe cases, unusual referrals, and people whose symptoms match the emerging definition. They often lack ideal controls. Test batteries are broad because doctors do not yet know which organ system matters. Broad testing increases the chance of finding abnormalities, especially when thresholds and normative comparisons vary across instruments.

None of that invalidates the findings.

It changes the inference.

The distinction between patient-level abnormality and syndrome-level biomarker is especially important. A person can have a real vestibular deficit. Another can have hearing loss. Another can have migraine. If those findings do not recur in a consistent pattern across a well-defined exposed group compared with controls, they do not establish a common injury mechanism.

The 2024 NIH studies were designed in part to address this problem. More than eighty people who had reported AHIs were compared with matched healthy controls, including government personnel with similar overseas work. Participants underwent clinical, auditory, vestibular, visual, neuropsychological, blood biomarker, and advanced MRI assessments. Researchers found no significant group differences in most objective measures and no consistent MRI-detectable brain injury signature.

The NIH team did find substantial symptom burden. Participants reported more fatigue, post-traumatic stress, depression, and other difficulties. A notable fraction met criteria for functional neurological disorders or significant somatic symptoms, often persistent postural-perceptual dizziness.

That finding is easy to mishandle.

Functional neurological disorder does not mean fabricated symptoms. It describes dysfunction in nervous-system operation without the structural lesion model people often expect. Symptoms can be disabling. They can arise after illness, injury, stress, sensory disruption, or other triggers. PPPD similarly involves persistent dizziness and unsteadiness shaped by visual and postural processing. These diagnoses do not tell us that an original external stimulus never occurred.

They do, however, provide mechanisms by which symptoms can persist after a transient trigger is gone.

That is important because one of the hardest Havana questions is temporal. Some people described an abrupt event lasting seconds or minutes and then experienced symptoms for months or years. A proposed causal mechanism has to account not only for the onset but for persistence. Structural injury is one route. Maladaptive sensory processing, migraine sensitization, autonomic changes, sleep disruption, trauma responses, and functional neurological mechanisms are others.

The public debate preferred a binary: brain injury or mass psychogenic illness.

The patients did not fit politely into either box.

The term *mass psychogenic illness* also carries rhetorical baggage that exceeds its scientific use. Socially mediated symptoms are real symptoms. Expectation, threat perception, group communication, and attention can alter physiology and perception. But invoking psychogenic mechanisms as a universal explanation before investigating environmental causes is as premature as invoking a secret weapon.

The 2022 IC expert panel took a careful position: psychosocial factors alone could not account for the core characteristics of the subset it analyzed, although they could contribute to some incidents or long-term symptoms. NIH later found functional and psychological features in a broader AHI cohort without establishing that these explained the initial events.

The studies are not necessarily contradictory because they asked different questions of different populations.

This is a recurring problem in the literature. The word *AHI* can refer to a sharp, unusual, spatially localized onset case; a later self-reported chronic syndrome; a security report screened into a government program; or a research participant whose inclusion criteria differ from another study's. If readers ignore those definitions, evidence appears to swing wildly.

Good science begins by asking who is in the denominator.

The first twenty-one patients were never the whole phenomenon.

They were the first formal medical window into it.

What came through that window was real suffering, objective findings in a small referral cohort, and an urgent need for care.

What did not come through was a weapon.
