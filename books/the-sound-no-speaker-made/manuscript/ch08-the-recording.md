# The Recording

A recording feels like evidence because it can be replayed.

That property is dangerously persuasive.

During the Havana investigation, audio associated with some reported incidents circulated among investigators and eventually the public. The sound was high-pitched, rhythmic, and insect-like. To anyone looking for a weapon, the recording appeared to offer what the medical files could not: a captured trace of the event itself.

But a microphone records pressure waves in air.

That sentence rearranges the whole problem.

If a person experiences microwave hearing through a thermoelastic pressure wave generated inside the head, an ordinary phone microphone sitting nearby should not necessarily record the same internal auditory event. The microphone and the cochlea are sampling different physical channels. A sound file can therefore be temporally associated with an episode without being the causal stimulus.

This seems obvious in retrospect. It was not obvious in the public argument, where “the Havana sound” became attached to “Havana Syndrome” as though the recording were an acoustic fingerprint of the illness.

JASON, the long-running scientific advisory group, was asked by the State Department to analyze recordings. A declassified version of its 2018 report concluded that the recorded sounds were most likely mechanical or biological rather than electromagnetic in origin. In the best-known examples, the acoustic signature matched the Indies short-tailed cricket. JASON also concluded that no plausible single source of radiofrequency or sonic energy could account simultaneously for the recorded audio and the reported medical effects.

That result was widely summarized as “crickets caused Havana Syndrome.”

It said something narrower.

The recorded sound was likely a cricket.

That does not establish that the cricket caused the symptoms.

It also does not establish that no unrecorded external stimulus occurred at the same time.

JASON itself preserved that distinction. The possibility of deception or an unrelated injury mechanism could not be ruled out merely because the microphone captured an insect.

The recording therefore became a beautiful demonstration of evidentiary entanglement.

A witness hears a disturbing sound during an episode. The witness records the sound. Investigators later identify the recording as biological. Does that weaken the witness? It weakens the claim that the recorded airborne sound was the exotic causal agent. It may also suggest that at least some perceived novelty came from an unfamiliar natural sound. But it does not automatically adjudicate every symptom or every other case.

This is where ordinary source criticism enters physics.

What exactly does an instrument measure?

A phone microphone measures acoustic pressure over a limited bandwidth through its own automatic gain control and compression. It does not measure radiofrequency field strength. It does not measure ultrasound outside its passband reliably. It does not measure absorbed dose in tissue. It does not preserve phase or amplitude with laboratory calibration. The file is evidence about sound in the room, not about every kind of energy present.

Investigators should have been delighted by the cricket result because it removed one false clue.

Public argument was not built to reward subtraction.

For proponents of an attack hypothesis, the cricket finding could be dismissed as irrelevant because microwave hearing might not record acoustically. For skeptics, the cricket became a totalizing explanation. Both reactions moved too quickly. A good investigation uses the recording to eliminate hypotheses that require the recorded sound itself to carry the injurious energy. Then it asks what remains.

This produces an uncomfortable possibility: two things can happen at once.

A person can hear an insect and experience dizziness for another reason.

A person can hear an insect during a panic response and later associate the sound with the onset.

An adversary could theoretically use an unrelated stimulus while an ambient sound is recorded.

A person could experience an internal microwave-auditory sensation while a microphone separately records the environment.

The evidentiary burden differs for each scenario.

The simplest explanations should receive preference when they account for the facts without additional machinery. If a recording matches a cricket, the acoustic source should be treated as a cricket absent contrary evidence. Adding a hidden weapon solely to preserve an exciting narrative is not analysis.

But simplicity is not a prohibition against multiple causes when the dataset is already heterogeneous. A syndrome category containing many different events may require several ordinary explanations plus, perhaps, a residue of genuinely unexplained cases.

This is one reason the phrase *the cause of Havana Syndrome* became scientifically corrosive. It presupposed a unitary object.

The recording also illustrates how sensory testimony works. Humans are not microphones. Perception localizes, interprets, and predicts. An unfamiliar cricket call reflected inside a room can feel electronic. A standing wave can make a sound seem spatially bounded. Movement can alter loudness quickly. The brain tries to infer source from incomplete cues and sometimes produces a confident but wrong model.

That is not a defect unique to frightened diplomats. It is normal perception.

The same principle applies in the opposite direction. A genuine internal auditory event caused by pulsed RF might be described using familiar sound words—click, buzz, chirp—even though no equivalent acoustic waveform exists in the air. Testimony preserves experience, not a calibrated transfer function.

This makes retrospective engineering from adjectives hazardous.

A witness says “beam.” Did they observe a beam, infer directionality from where symptoms stopped, or simply use the nearest word? A witness says “pressure.” Was there measurable acoustic overpressure, vestibular sensation, headache onset, ear fullness, or an internal feeling? A witness says “high-pitched.” Was that an airborne tone or an interpreted internal transient?

Every noun needs unpacking before it becomes a design parameter.

The JASON report did one valuable thing beyond identifying insects: it refused to let the recording carry more causal weight than the recording could support.

That should be the model.

Evidence is strongest when it is allowed to stay small.
