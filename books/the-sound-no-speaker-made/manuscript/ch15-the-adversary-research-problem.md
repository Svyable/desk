# The Adversary Research Problem

A government does not need to believe a scientific claim in order to worry that an adversary believes it.

This is one of the oldest distortions in classified research.

During the Cold War, American agencies studied Soviet work on parapsychology, psychotronics, microwave bioeffects, and other unusual subjects partly because Soviet institutions appeared to be studying them. The intelligence question was not identical to the scientific question. Even a technically misguided adversary can spend money, develop equipment, train operators, and create operational risk.

This recursive structure can keep weak fields alive for decades.

Country A hears that Country B is studying a phenomenon.

Country A funds a small program to assess the threat.

Country B learns that Country A has funded a program.

The existence of the program becomes evidence that the phenomenon must be important.

Neither side needs a decisive experimental result for the loop to continue.

Project PANDORA had elements of this dynamic. American officials read Soviet literature suggesting biological effects from low-level microwave exposure while the U.S. Embassy in Moscow was actually being illuminated by Soviet microwave beams. Investigating the intersection was rational. But the existence of the investigation later became part of the mythology of hidden microwave weapons even though the program's results were inconclusive.

The Havana case inherited the same adversary-research problem at a more advanced technological baseline.

Russia, China, the United States, and other states indisputably research high-power microwaves, electronic warfare, radar, directed energy, counterelectronics, and nonlethal effects. These fields have legitimate military applications that do not require exotic neurobiology. A foreign technical program can therefore be real while the specific antipersonnel application remains uncertain.

The 2025 IC assessment says new reporting about foreign directed-energy research was sufficient for two components to revise their capability judgments. That statement is deliberately nonspecific in public. It does not identify the country, program, waveform, effect, or maturity. The classification gap is where speculation breeds.

A responsible manuscript should not fill that gap with names borrowed from rumor.

Instead, ask what technical milestones would matter.

Can a foreign laboratory generate the required peak power in a portable system?

Can it direct the energy through realistic building materials?

Can it produce repeatable effects in animal models or humans under ethical or unethical testing regimes?

Can the effect be separated from ordinary heating?

Can the system aim at one person without exposing others?

Can it operate without obvious RF detection?

Can it be packaged for clandestine deployment?

Those are capability indicators.

They are also collection requirements.

This is where intelligence and science should reinforce each other. Scientific mechanism defines what to look for in foreign programs. Intelligence reporting identifies parameters worth reproducing in controlled laboratories. Neither should be allowed to substitute for the other.

A danger appears when classified reporting cannot be externally challenged. Suppose an intelligence source reports that a foreign institute achieved a remarkable bioeffect. Analysts may know the source has access to the institute but not understand the experimental controls. The source may faithfully report what foreign scientists believe, while the science itself is wrong. A classified human report can be excellent intelligence about a bad experiment.

This distinction is essential in frontier technology assessment.

States classify claims, not truth.

A Top Secret document can contain an incorrect technical interpretation. A compartment can protect a source without improving the measurement. A high-ranking official can repeat a contractor's claim and add authority without adding replication.

The opposite error is to dismiss classified concern because public literature is weak. A covert military program may have equipment and data not available to academic researchers. Intelligence agencies exist partly because open literature does not contain everything an adversary knows.

There is no shortcut.

The proper response is triangulation.

Look for convergent technical reporting from independent sources. Look for procurement consistent with the claimed capability. Look for facilities, test ranges, antennas, power systems, safety procedures, patents as evidence of interest rather than efficacy, scientific publications, personnel movement, accident reports, and doctrine. Then ask whether the implied system obeys known physics.

A real program should cast more than one shadow.

This is where Havana remains weak in public. The 2025 assessment acknowledges reporting sufficient to move some capability estimates, but the public record still lacks a named foreign system with demonstrated AHI-like effects and an operational chain tied to specific incidents.

That gap may be classification.

It may be absence.

The book cannot know which.

What it can do is refuse to convert the word *classified* into positive evidence.

The existence of hidden information widens uncertainty. It does not tell us which direction the hidden information points.
