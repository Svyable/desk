# A Wave from a Blast

The idea began with something embarrassingly ordinary: somebody blew up a reef and watched the water move.

That is a better beginning than the phrase *tsunami bomb*, which arrives already wearing a cheap suit. The phrase sounds retrofitted for television. It invites mushroom clouds underwater, secret laboratories, generals stroking maps of coastal cities. It encourages the reader to decide, before seeing a document, whether this is evidence of terrifying hidden competence or another military fantasy with a filing cabinet.

Project Seal deserves neither reaction yet.

In January 1944, Commander E. A. Gibson was thinking about waves produced during explosive work in the Pacific. The surviving account of Project Seal traces the investigation to Gibson's suggestion to New Zealand's chief of the general staff, Lieutenant General Sir Edward Puttick. The question was crude and therefore useful: if an underwater demolition could produce an unexpectedly large wave, could explosives be arranged deliberately to produce inundation?

Not a natural tsunami. Not tectonic rupture. A weapon made by coupling chemical explosive energy into water in the right place, at the right depth, with the right geometry.

There is an important difference between those sentences.

A natural tsunami is usually a problem of moving an obscene quantity of water through displacement of the seafloor or another large-scale disturbance. A bomb is very good at releasing energy quickly. Those facts do not imply that a bomb is good at making the particular long-period wave that travels across shallow water and runs up a coast with military usefulness. Energy is not enough. The energy has to go somewhere useful.

This became the problem inside the problem.

The wartime investigation that followed was not a single theatrical detonation. Contemporary and later records describe an extended experimental program in New Zealand waters, associated especially with engineer Thomas David James Leech, then dean of engineering at Auckland University College. Thousands of small explosive trials were conducted. Wave behavior was measured. Charge depth mattered. Arrangement mattered. Bottom shape mattered. The work continued long enough to leave behind something much more interesting than a rumor: an engineering argument.

The argument was conditional.

Later summaries have turned that conditional argument into the irresistible sentence that New Zealand and the United States “built a tsunami bomb.” That is too strong. Project Seal investigated whether arrays of explosions could generate militarily useful inundation waves. It produced experimental and theoretical work suggesting that sufficiently large, carefully arranged explosive charges might produce destructive local waves under favorable conditions. It did not produce an operational weapon demonstrated against a coast.

That distinction is the book.

Because the distinction is exactly where secret weapons become culturally dangerous. Once the existence of a classified program is established, the imagination tends to promote every investigated possibility into a capability. A file stamped secret proves secrecy. It does not prove success.

Project Seal gives us the rare luxury of being able to watch that promotion happen in reverse. The ridiculous-sounding thing was real. The strongest version of the ridiculous-sounding thing was not established. Between those poles lies the actual engineering.

## The first evidence problem

There are four different Project Seals, and they should not be allowed to impersonate one another.

The first is documentary. A wartime project existed. New Zealand personnel conducted explosive-wave research. Thomas Leech was central to it. American military interest existed. The work remained sensitive after the war. A formal report, *Project SEAL: The Generation of Waves by Means of Explosives*, was produced under the New Zealand Department of Scientific and Industrial Research and is catalogued as a 1950 publication.

The second is reported history. Journalists who examined released New Zealand government files in 1999 described correspondence showing continued American and British interest, including a 1946 visit by Karl Compton and discussion of sharing information from the Bikini atomic tests that might bear on Seal. These reports are useful, but wherever possible this book will return from the newspaper sentence to the underlying file.

The third is the legend. The legend says the Allies possessed, or nearly possessed, a weapon capable of manufacturing a ten-metre tsunami and erasing a coastal city. Numbers such as two million kilograms of explosive, eight kilometres offshore, or ten coordinated detonations recur in secondary accounts. Some may trace faithfully to Project Seal calculations. They still require provenance, assumptions and geometry before they become engineering conclusions.

The fourth is the modern extrapolation. If Project Seal was possible, perhaps later earthquakes or tsunamis were secretly weapons. That inference has no automatic standing. A historical program to study explosion-generated waves is evidence that people studied explosion-generated waves. It is not evidence that a particular later natural disaster was artificial.

The temptation to collapse these categories is enormous because Project Seal sits in a perfect narrative trap. It was secret. It concerned a spectacular weapon. It involved real military authorities. It overlapped the birth of the atomic age. It was later declassified. Every ingredient of a conspiracy story is present except the part conspiracy stories usually omit: the engineering losses.

Those losses are where we will spend our time.

## Whangaparaoa is not a movie set

The experimental site at Whangaparaoa, north of Auckland, matters because scale matters. Small explosions can reveal relationships without proving that those relationships extrapolate conveniently. Engineers do this constantly. A model is not fraudulent because it is small. It becomes fraudulent only when the path from model to full scale is smuggled past the reader.

Project Seal's researchers were trying to learn that path.

An underwater charge generates a violent local pressure field, a gas bubble, oscillations, jets, surface disturbance and waves. The military question is not whether water moves. Water obviously moves. The question is how much explosive energy can be transferred into a wave with the period, direction and spatial scale needed to produce damaging coastal run-up rather than a magnificent local splash.

The ocean is exceptionally good at making bad ideas expensive.

If the charge is wrong, energy goes into shock. If the depth is wrong, the surface response changes. If the explosions are badly spaced or timed, their contributions do not build the desired wave. If the seabed geometry is wrong, the wave evolves differently. If the continental shelf causes breaking before the wave reaches the target, energy is dissipated where nobody wanted it dissipated. If the required explosive mass becomes enormous, logistics begin competing with physics.

This is why “could it work?” is almost never one question in weapons development.

Could an explosion make a measurable wave? Yes.

Could an arrangement of explosions make a larger wave? Under some conditions, yes.

Could a sufficiently large engineered arrangement create damaging coastal inundation? That is a harder theoretical and experimental question.

Could a military force secretly deliver the required explosive mass to the required underwater geometry near an enemy coast, prepare or place it, synchronize it, survive countermeasures, and achieve a predictable enough effect to justify the operation over conventional alternatives? Now we are asking whether there is a weapon.

The distance between the first yes and the last yes is where secret-weapons mythology usually disappears.

## Halsey's enthusiasm

Secondary accounts of the released records describe Admiral William Halsey responding enthusiastically to early observations and asking New Zealand to continue development. The language attributed to his memorandum is striking because it treats artificial inundation as potentially useful in amphibious warfare.

That interest should be taken seriously without being mistaken for validation.

Military history is full of senior officers sponsoring investigations that failed. That is not evidence of stupidity. One function of wartime research is to spend relatively small amounts of money and expertise discovering whether an odd observation can be turned into an advantage before the enemy does. In 1944, with amphibious warfare consuming entire islands and the invasion of Japan looming as a conceivable future operation, an unconventional way to damage coastal defenses was not inherently absurd.

The absurdity, if any, had to be measured.

This is one reason Project Seal is more valuable than a story about a forgotten wonder weapon. It shows what institutional curiosity looks like before hindsight sorts the winners from the dead ends. Radar once sounded speculative. Guided missiles were immature. Atomic weapons were being built behind an unprecedented secrecy wall. Operational research was teaching militaries to quantify things they had previously handled by intuition. A proposal involving explosive-generated waves entered that ecosystem not as magic but as another physical question with possible tactical consequences.

The fact that the atomic bomb worked spectacularly does something unfair to every neighboring wartime project. It makes us assume that all secret programs were miniature Manhattan Projects waiting for sufficient funding. They were not. The Manhattan Project was exceptional partly because nature was unusually cooperative once the required fissile material and engineering were obtained. A nuclear chain reaction gives back enormous energy from a compact mass. Project Seal faced the opposite bargain: enormous conventional explosive mass had to persuade an enormous body of water to move in a particularly useful way.

That is a much meaner transaction.

## The number 3,700

The figure that follows Project Seal through modern retellings is approximately 3,700 experimental explosions over roughly seven months. It is a wonderful number for a headline because it converts a strange proposal into visible labor. Nobody sets off thousands of charges for a joke.

But the number can mislead in the other direction. Thousands of trials do not mean thousands of weapon tests. They mean an experimental campaign. A large number of small tests can indicate uncertainty, parameter exploration, calibration and the stubbornness of the phenomenon as readily as confidence.

A future chapter will reconstruct those trials as closely as the surviving records permit: charge sizes, positions, depth, wave measurements, experimental phases, instrumentation, failures and changes in theory. Until that reconstruction is complete, “3,700” belongs in the category of a repeatedly reported historical figure with strong documentary provenance to be page-indexed, not as a talisman proving success.

This evidentiary fussiness is not decorative. It is the only way to write responsibly about classified technology.

There is a recurring pathology in secret-history literature. A program's existence is denied or unknown for decades. Records emerge. The people who always suspected something feel vindicated. Vindication about existence then spills into vindication about performance. If the government really did test psychic spying, perhaps psychic spying worked. If it really did build a submarine-recovery ship behind a mining cover story, perhaps every later retrieval allegation inherits credibility. If it really did study a tsunami weapon, perhaps geophysical warfare is merely hidden engineering.

No.

The correct update is narrower and more interesting: governments sometimes investigate ideas that public common sense would classify as ridiculous. Therefore ridicule is poor evidence that a program did not exist. It tells us almost nothing about whether the program succeeded.

Project Seal lets us hold both thoughts at once.

## Bikini enters the story

After the war, the atomic bomb changed the scale of every weapons conversation. Released-file reporting describes Karl Compton, who was involved in assessing the American atomic tests, visiting New Zealand and expressing interest in Leech's deductions. A July 1946 communication reported in the New Zealand press said Compton was prepared to recommend that technical data from the tests relevant to Seal be made available to the New Zealand government for Leech's further study.

Again, the careful verb is *reported* until the underlying correspondence is indexed directly.

But the connection makes physical sense. Nuclear explosions offered a radically larger energy source. If Project Seal's central practical weakness was the mass of conventional explosive required, the arrival of nuclear weapons naturally reopened the question of explosion-generated water waves.

It also sharpened the distinction between energy and coupling.

A larger explosion is not automatically a proportionally better tsunami generator. Later theoretical and experimental work on explosion-generated waves emphasized inefficiencies and coastal propagation limits. The sea does not care that a weapon is historically important. It obeys geometry.

This is where Project Seal stops being an eccentric New Zealand footnote and becomes a book about a broader human error: confusing access to enormous energy with control over what that energy becomes.

We made this mistake repeatedly in the twentieth century. Nuclear explosives were proposed for excavation, canals, gas stimulation, harbor construction and planetary engineering. Sometimes the underlying physical effect was real. The question was whether the effect could be shaped, predicted, contained and made preferable to less theatrical alternatives.

Project Seal encountered that question early, with dynamite and wave gauges.

## What we can say now

At this stage the documentary floor is firm enough for a few statements and no more.

Project Seal existed. It investigated the generation of large waves by explosives during World War II. Thomas Leech and New Zealand institutions played central roles. American military interest was real. Experimental work was extensive enough to produce a substantial technical record and a later formal report. The program did not culminate in a publicly documented operational tsunami weapon or full-scale destructive coastal demonstration.

Claims beyond that floor will be graded.

If an original memorandum says a thing, we will say the memorandum says it. If a later participant remembered a thing, we will call it recollection. If a journalist reports a released file that we have not yet inspected, we will preserve that distance. If a modern author calculates that Seal would have worked, the assumptions enter the text with the conclusion. If someone points at a later tsunami and says *therefore*, the burden starts again from zero.

That may sound severe for a story this strange.

It is precisely because the story is strange that the bookkeeping matters.

The easiest Project Seal book would promise that the Allies secretly invented a doomsday machine. The second easiest would laugh at them for trying. Both versions waste the material.

The better story is about a weapon balanced on a narrow strip between absurdity and plausibility. Smart people saw a physical effect. A military institution asked whether it could be scaled. Engineers spent months making the water answer. The answer was not simply yes or no. It was a chain of conditions.

And conditions are where wonder weapons go to die.
