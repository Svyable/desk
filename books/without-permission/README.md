# Without Permission

## How the Army Turned American Cities Into Biological-Warfare Test Terrain

**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson

## Reader promise

This book reconstructs the U.S. Army's Cold War atmospheric dispersion tests over populated places without converting secrecy into a claim of mass poisoning. The documented fact is already strange enough: military researchers released tracers over American and Canadian communities to learn how biological-warfare aerosols could spread through cities. The harder questions are what residents were told, what planners knew about the tracers, what the experiments actually measured, and what later health reviews can and cannot establish.

## Thesis

The most consequential technology in these tests was not zinc cadmium sulfide. It was permission by substitution. A city could become a biological-warfare laboratory because planners replaced a pathogen with a tracer and then treated the ethical problem as though it had been replaced too.

That claim has boundaries. The National Research Council concluded in 1997 that estimated ZnCdS exposures from the Army tests were very unlikely to have caused illness, and found no evidence that the low exposures caused cancer. This book will not erase that finding. Nor will it imply that ZnCdS was itself a biological weapon. The subject is the institutional decision to conduct population-scale military aerosol experiments, the secrecy surrounding them, and the peculiar epistemic aftermath in which communities learned decades later that an experiment had occurred and then had to reconstruct their own exposure from government records.

## Chapter architecture

1. **A Line Across the City** — begin inside an urban release: a fluorescent powder, an ordinary street, and a military question invisible to the people underneath it.
2. **What a Biological Attack Looks Like in the Wind** — why the Chemical Corps needed empirical dispersion data rather than elegant meteorology alone.
3. **Safe Enough to Substitute** — ZnCdS chemistry, fluorescence, particle size, and the assumptions that made it an acceptable simulant.
4. **Operation LAC** — Large Area Coverage and the attempt to measure continental-scale aerosol behavior.
5. **St. Louis** — rooftop and mobile releases, housing geography, surviving records, and the later fight over what happened.
6. **Minneapolis** — another city, another geometry, and what the test documentation permits us to say.
7. **Corpus Christi** — coastal air, line sources, sampling networks, and military meteorology.
8. **Thirty-Two Places** — the national test map without turning it into a sensational catalog.
9. **The People Under the Plume** — consent, notice, public knowledge, and the difference between civilian exposure and human-subject experimentation.
10. **How Much Reached a Lung** — reconstructing concentration, duration, particle deposition, and uncertainty.
11. **Cadmium Is Not Zinc Cadmium Sulfide** — toxicology, solubility, bioavailability, and why chemical names can frighten more efficiently than dose calculations inform.
12. **The Missing Denominator** — incomplete records, uncertain individual location, sensitive populations, and the limits of retrospective epidemiology.
13. **1994** — public rediscovery, congressional pressure, Army risk assessments, and why late disclosure changes the meaning of reassurance.
14. **The Academy Recalculates the Plume** — the 1997 National Research Council review, its worst-case assumptions, findings, and unresolved data gaps.
15. **What the Army Was Actually Learning** — munitions requirements, strategic biological warfare, atmospheric transport, and the military value of civilian geography.
16. **A Simulant Is Still an Exposure** — ethics independent of demonstrated injury: risk transfer, knowledge asymmetry, and involuntary participation.
17. **Records Are Part of the Experiment** — why secrecy and archival fragmentation become a second exposure decades later.
18. **Without Permission** — what this history establishes, what it does not, and how democracies should think about low-risk experiments imposed on populations for high-stakes national-security knowledge.

## Evidence posture

### Class 1 — verified / documented

- The Army sponsored ZnCdS atmospheric dispersion tests during the 1950s and 1960s in numerous U.S. and Canadian urban and rural locations.
- The tests belonged to the U.S. biological-warfare research program and were intended to study aerosol dispersion and, among other purposes, estimate requirements for strategic BW use.
- ZnCdS was used as a fluorescent tracer/simulant rather than as a biological agent.
- The National Research Council's 1997 toxicological assessment concluded that the estimated exposures were unlikely to have produced adverse health effects and that lung-cancer risk, if any, was very low.
- EPA reviewers broadly agreed that the Army's no-health-threat conclusion was likely correct while criticizing aspects of how narrowly the conclusion and exposure questions were framed.

### Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- Exact individual exposure for residents cannot generally be reconstructed from a city name alone; release location, plume geometry, time, individual location and particle concentration matter.
- Historical documentation is uneven enough that a complete person-level exposure denominator may be impossible.
- Public understanding and notice varied by test and location and require test-specific archival reconstruction rather than one blanket claim of universal ignorance.

### Class 3 — disputed / not independently established

- Claims that the ZnCdS tests caused particular clusters of cancer or other disease are not established by the 1997 NRC review and must not be presented as demonstrated causation.
- Claims that every urban test secretly contained a live biological agent require test-specific evidence; the existence of simulant programs does not establish covert pathogen release in any particular event.
- Later allegations concerning additives or compositions must be evaluated against chain-of-custody and original material records rather than repeated because they are alarming.

### Class 4 — synthesis / open conjecture

The book's central synthesis is that **substitution solved the engineering problem more completely than it solved the ethical one**. Replacing a pathogen with a low-risk fluorescent tracer made large-scale field testing technically defensible. It did not automatically answer whether residents should have been informed or whether a military institution could legitimately appropriate civilian bodies and neighborhoods as measuring surfaces. That is an argument to be tested against the period's rules and records, not smuggled in as a historical fact.

## Research queue

- Build a release-by-release ledger from the NRC source bibliography and underlying Army reports.
- Separate Operation LAC from city-specific tracer studies rather than using LAC as a catch-all label.
- Reconstruct St. Louis claims from primary Army documents before narrating later allegations.
- Identify the contemporaneous toxicological basis for treating ZnCdS as safe and distinguish what was known then from what was learned later.
- Page-index the NRC exposure tables and identify the maximally exposed modeled locations.
- Trace 1990s congressional requests, Army risk assessments, EPA/CDC/ATSDR reviews, and public meetings.
- Locate period military doctrine describing strategic BW aerosol requirements so the military purpose is quoted from primary documentation rather than inferred.
- Build a notice/consent ledger for every major test discussed.
- Maintain a separate ledger for live-agent open-air tests elsewhere; do not merge them with ZnCdS simply because both belong to biological-warfare history.

## Defeat condition

If primary records show that meaningful contemporaneous public notice and consent were routine across the major populated-area tests, the title and central permission thesis must change. If new dose or epidemiological evidence overturns the NRC's health-risk conclusion, the health chapters must change with it. The book does not get to keep its argument by outrunning the record.
