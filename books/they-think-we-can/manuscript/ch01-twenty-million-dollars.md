# Chapter 1 — Twenty Million Dollars

The number is twenty million dollars.

It appears in a secret American report dated July 1972, the kind of number that acquires a second life because somebody typed it behind a classification marking.

The report is called **Controlled Offensive Behavior — USSR**. Its subject is not modest. The author, John D. LaMothe, is trying to describe Soviet work on methods of influencing human behavior. Drugs are in there. Sound. Light. Color. Odors. Sensory deprivation. Sleep. Electronic and magnetic fields. Hypnosis. Autosuggestion.

Then the list walks off the paved road.

Psychokinesis. Extrasensory perception. Astral projection. Dream state. Clairvoyance. Precognition.

The report says paranormal phenomena had generated enough Soviet excitement that, according to reporting it cites, the USSR had twenty or more centers studying the area in 1967 and an annual paranormal-research budget of approximately $20 million.

Twenty million dollars in 1967 sounds like a weapon.

It may not have been.

The first job of this book is to stop the sentence before it becomes one.

A budget estimate is not a capability. A research center is not a successful experiment. A classified American report describing a foreign claim is not an independently replicated foreign result. A list of topics is not a coherent program. And a report written to identify possible hostile methods has a professional reason to retain possibilities that a journal referee might discard.

None of that makes the document unimportant.

It makes it more important.

Because by 1972 an American defense-intelligence document was not merely asking whether paranormal phenomena existed. It was placing them inside the category of things an adversary might use.

That is a different epistemic machine.

Science asks, among other things: *What is true?*

Threat intelligence has to ask another question: *What happens if this is true and we ignore it?*

Those questions can rationally produce different thresholds for action.

If a laboratory claims a new particle with weak evidence, another laboratory may wait for better data. If an adversary is rumored to possess a way to collect information through walls, the cost of waiting could look enormous even when the probability is small. A few hundred thousand dollars spent checking the claim can be rational under uncertainty without anyone having crossed the much higher threshold required to say the phenomenon is real.

That is how a research program can be evidence of concern without being evidence of efficacy.

It is also how historians can get fooled.

## The classified adjective

Read **Controlled Offensive Behavior** as an artifact before reading it as a statement about nature.

The document had a customer. It had a mission. It had a threat frame. It had sources of uneven quality. It had a classification level. It had an institutional environment in which missing a Soviet capability could be career-defining in a way that entertaining an ultimately unproductive lead might not be.

The classification marking matters historically. It tells us something about circulation and sensitivity.

It does not improve an experiment.

This sounds obvious until you spend time in declassified anomaly archives. A secret document feels heavier than a public one. Redactions create a peculiar gravitational field. The reader can begin to supply the missing certainty. The fact that officials once restricted a report becomes confused with the proposition that officials had secret evidence validating its contents.

Sometimes they did have better evidence.

Sometimes they were protecting sources, methods, names, foreign relationships, collection capabilities, or simply the fact that they were interested.

Sometimes the secret was uncertainty.

The archive does not let us choose whichever possibility makes the better story.

So: twenty million dollars.

Where did it come from?

That is not a rhetorical question. It is a provenance problem.

The 1972 report attributes the figure rather than presenting an audited Soviet budget. The number therefore needs to be walked backward through its citation chain until we reach the closest available source: Soviet administrative records if they exist, named scientific institutions and appropriations if reconstructable, or at minimum the original Western report and its basis. Until then, the correct sentence is not *the Soviet Union spent $20 million a year on paranormal research in 1967.*

The correct sentence is narrower:

*A 1972 U.S. defense-intelligence report repeated reporting that estimated approximately $20 million in annual Soviet paranormal-research spending for 1967.*

Less cinematic.

Much more useful.

## The loop begins before we know it is a loop

The Cold War produced a special kind of technical fear. Both superpowers were secretive. Both had large scientific establishments. Both had demonstrated that apparently impossible engineering projects could become strategic facts with very little warning.

The atomic bomb was not folklore.

Sputnik was not folklore.

Ballistic missiles were not folklore.

The question *what if they are ahead?* had earned its place in government.

This is why it is too easy to mock the people who opened files on telepathy.

Imagine the analyst's problem without the benefit of hindsight. You receive translated Soviet papers and reports of laboratories investigating effects that, if real, would puncture ordinary assumptions about distance, shielding, secrecy, and human performance. Most of the evidence is bad. Some of it is difficult to evaluate because experimental detail is missing. Some researchers have legitimate scientific credentials. Soviet publication practices are opaque. The military relevance, if the effect exists, is obvious.

What probability of reality justifies a look?

Ten percent?

One percent?

A tenth of one percent?

The answer depends on the cost of the look and the cost of being wrong.

This is not an argument for psi. It is an argument against a lazy history of psi.

The interesting question is why institutions behave as they do under asymmetric uncertainty.

## 1975: the threat acquires a bibliography

By the middle of the decade, the concern had become more organized. A later declassified reference list preserves a chain of U.S. intelligence products: **Soviet and Czechoslovakian Parapsychology Research** from DIA in September 1975; **Soviet and East European Parapsychology Research** from CIA in April 1977; **Paraphysics R&D — Warsaw Pact** from DIA in March 1978. The same list sits beside American work from SRI and a CIA-contracted report on “Novel Biophysical Information Transfer Mechanisms.”

This is the archive beginning to fold over itself.

Foreign assessment and domestic experiment are no longer cleanly separable categories. The people assessing Soviet work know Americans are experimenting. American experimenters know Soviet work is part of the customer's concern. Papers, briefings, translated reports, rumors, contracts, and threat assessments circulate through overlapping communities.

Then, on 15 May 1978, the 902d Military Intelligence Group at Fort Meade requested a briefing on Soviet parapsychology research and the intelligence applications the Soviets might exploit. The reason was explicit: the group had to assess hostile intelligence collection threats against the United States.

Notice the grammar.

Not *demonstrated Soviet capability*.

Threat assessment.

A bureaucracy charged with comprehensive defense against hostile collection has a structural reason to ask about low-probability, high-consequence methods. That is exactly what we should want it to do.

But once the briefing exists, the briefing itself becomes a historical fact. Decades later someone can say: *The Army was being briefed on Soviet psychic spying.* True.

Then: *The Army took Soviet psychic spying seriously.* Maybe, depending on what “seriously” means.

Then: *The Soviets had a psychic-spying capability serious enough to alarm the Army.* Not established.

Three sentences. One document. Three different evidentiary burdens.

Most anomaly histories move between them too quickly.

## The submarine

There is a story that belongs in the center of this book because it is almost too perfect.

The submarine *Nautilus* supposedly participated in a telepathy experiment in which information was transmitted to a submerged vessel. The tale became famous in Western paranormal literature and traveled internationally. Soviet scientific discussion noticed it too.

A declassified U.S.-held translation of Soviet writing on ESP and thought transmission contains a wonderfully inconvenient sentence. The Soviet author discusses the *Nautilus* case, then warns that it is possible not everything reported about it was true and says more experiments and theoretical proof would be required before the phenomenon could be considered real.

Stop there.

A Western paranormal claim is being discussed by a Soviet scientist. Soviet interest can then be observed by Western intelligence. Western intelligence may cite Soviet interest as evidence that the subject deserves attention.

If we are careless, the claim has crossed an ocean and returned wearing a second lab coat.

This is the mechanism I want to find—or kill.

Not every Soviet program came from Western rumor. Not every American program came from Soviet threat reporting. The loop will be different for each case. But the *Nautilus* anecdote demonstrates the contamination problem in miniature: national provenance is not the same as evidentiary independence.

Two countries can repeat one story.

Two laboratories can share one methodological mistake.

Two intelligence services can amplify one rumor.

Two budgets can arise from one fear.

## The document that predicts success

In August 1984, DIA's Directorate for Scientific and Technical Intelligence issued a report titled **Psychoenergetics Research**. Its discussion of the Soviet Union is confident enough to deserve close reading. It says that, beginning in the early 1970s, Soviet review commissions had given considerable attention to psychoenergetics and that military support and high-level Party sanction should create an environment favorable to the field, likely affecting future achievements and facilitating novel intelligence and warfare applications.

“Should.”

“Most likely.”

“Potential.”

These are not embarrassing words. They are forecasting words.

The report is doing what intelligence analysis often has to do: estimate where a capability might go before the answer is experimentally settled.

But this creates another trap for the future reader. A prediction that a field *could* generate applications can later be quoted as if the applications existed when the prediction was written.

The distinction matters even more in secret technical history because failed programs disappear more easily than successful weapons. A classified concern can remain culturally alive long after the forecast that generated it has expired.

The Soviet Union collapsed in 1991.

The paranormal superweapon did not emerge from a warehouse.

That is evidence.

It is not complete evidence. Programs can fail, remain hidden, fragment, migrate, or be misunderstood. But a serious account must charge a forecast for what failed to happen afterward.

Prediction has a denominator too.

## The mirror-race hypothesis

Here is the book's dangerous proposition, stated in a form that can lose.

Some portion of the Cold War psychoenergetics race may have been **recursive threat amplification**. American institutions observed Soviet interest and treated that interest as a reason to investigate. Soviet institutions observed Western claims and programs and had their own reasons to investigate. Each side's investigation increased the apparent strategic seriousness visible to the other. Later observers then treated parallel state interest as independent convergence on an underlying phenomenon.

That model predicts archival fingerprints.

Foreign-threat language should appear in domestic program justifications.

Frequently repeated budget and capability claims should trace back to surprisingly few original sources.

Some “Soviet” evidence should itself depend on Western publications, demonstrations, visitors, or press stories.

Threat assessments should sometimes outrun demonstrated performance.

Program continuation should be explainable partly by uncertainty about the rival rather than by accumulating domestic experimental success.

And when the rival's system opens, the expected revolutionary operational capability should be difficult to find.

The model also has clear ways to fail.

If Soviet primary records show a large, independently motivated program with strong internally replicated results predating meaningful Western influence, the mirror account weakens.

If American funding decisions were driven overwhelmingly by domestic experimental evidence rather than foreign-threat concern, it weakens.

If separate national programs produced convergent, high-quality, prospectively specified effects under controls that eliminate shared methodological artifacts, the phrase *we were watching each other* becomes inadequate.

Good.

A book about intelligence should be capable of receiving bad intelligence about itself.

## The most ordinary explanation is not necessarily the smallest

There is another mistake available here: believing that if the paranormal mechanism disappears, the history becomes mundane.

It does not.

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that every spectacular psychoenergetic claim eventually fails under decisive controls. We would still be left with a serious phenomenon: technically sophisticated states spending years collecting, translating, classifying, briefing, contracting, experimenting, forecasting, and defending against a capability that did not exist.

That would not be a footnote about gullibility.

It would be a case study in how national-security systems reason when evidence is weak and surprise is expensive.

It would belong beside missile gaps, bomber gaps, deception operations, strategic warning failures, mirror imaging, threat inflation, and the politics of secret technical estimates.

And if a small anomalous effect *does* survive?

Then the feedback problem becomes more important, not less. Because a real but weak effect is exactly the kind of thing an arms race can inflate into a capability claim faster than engineering can make the capability true.

The archive can contain both signal and mirror.

That is why we are going back to the twenty million dollars.

I want the receipt.
