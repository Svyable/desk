# They Think We Can

## The Weird Arms Race Built From Other People's Fear

Sven Hardy Benson

---

There is a dangerous sentence in intelligence work:

**The other side is studying it.**

The sentence contains no claim that *it works*.

That omission rarely survives the briefing room.

A rival laboratory is photographed. A translated journal mentions telepathy. A military institute appears in a distribution list. Somebody estimates a budget. Somebody else puts the estimate in a classified report. Classification changes the social weight of the sentence even when it does not change the quality of the evidence beneath it. Then a domestic program is authorized because failing to investigate a potentially revolutionary adversary capability seems less defensible than spending a little money to find out.

Across the border, another analyst notices the program.

Now there are two programs.

Years later, a historian finds both and writes: *Remarkably, both superpowers independently took the phenomenon seriously.*

Maybe they did.

Or maybe each was looking at the other.

That difference is this book.

## Four evidence classes

The subject is unusually vulnerable to documentary optical illusion. A declassified document proves that a document existed. It may prove that an agency commissioned a study, circulated a claim, paid a contractor, convened a briefing, or worried about an adversary. It does not automatically prove the technical claim inside the document.

Accordingly, the manuscript uses four explicit classes.

**Class 1 — verified, declassified, or otherwise documented facts.** Examples include dated DIA, Army, CIA, and SRI records; their distribution and classification markings; contracts and tasking where recoverable; contemporaneous Soviet publications; and experimental papers whose methods and results can be inspected.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete reporting or testimony.** This includes technically informed participant recollections, foreign-source reporting whose original provenance cannot yet be recovered, estimates based on partially visible programs, and retrospective accounts that fit the record but are not independently complete.

**Class 3 — disputed allegations or claims without independent confirmation.** Spectacular Soviet psychotronic weapons, operational telepathic submarine communications, reliable psychokinesis, remote incapacitation, secret breakthroughs, and claims of enormous hidden budgets belong here unless primary evidence moves them.

**Class 4 — speculative synthesis and open conjecture.** The central mirror-race model belongs here at the beginning. It must earn promotion case by case. The mere fact that Soviet-interest reports preceded or accompanied U.S. funding does not establish causation. American investigators had domestic scientific, institutional, and personal motives too.

## What this book will not do

It will not use classification markings as scientific peer review.

It will not use a government budget as a replication.

It will not use the phrase *the Soviets believed* unless the evidence identifies which Soviets, when, on what basis, and with what degree of institutional authority.

It will not turn an intelligence officer's obligation to consider a threat into that officer's personal endorsement of the threat's physics.

It will not convert American anxiety about Soviet research into evidence that Soviet research succeeded.

And it will not rescue a skeptical story by assumption. A feedback loop is not the default explanation merely because it is elegant. If independently motivated Soviet and American programs produced strong, preregisterable, reproducible effects under improving controls, then convergence would be evidence worth taking seriously.

The archive has to decide how much of the loop was mirror and how much was signal.

## The stronger possibility

There is an uncomfortable reason to do this carefully.

Threat inflation and technological surprise are both real.

Intelligence services have overestimated adversaries. They have also underestimated them. Radar, ballistic missiles, nuclear weapons, stealth, cryptography, satellites, and cyber capabilities all punish the analyst who decides that an unfamiliar technical claim is ridiculous because it does not fit yesterday's model.

So the interesting problem is not credulity versus skepticism.

It is how to investigate a claim whose expected value may justify attention long before its evidence justifies belief.

That is a much stranger place to stand.
