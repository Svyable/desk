# Twenty-million-dollar provenance audit

This is a bounded research pass for *They Think We Can*. It follows the opening chapter's most important unresolved number one step farther backward and, unusually, finds a later DIA document auditing the inflation of that same claim.

## What the 1972 report actually says

John D. LaMothe's July 1972 DIA report, *Controlled Offensive Behavior — USSR*, says it had been reported that the Soviets had 20 or more centers studying paranormal phenomena in 1967 and that the annual 1967 paranormal-research budget was approximately $20 million. Later in Part II the report gives the range more carefully: over 12 million rubles ($13 million) and reportedly as high as $21 million.

Primary source: CIA FOIA copy of DIA ST-CS-01-169-72, July 1972:
https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00787R000100120001-9.pdf

The manuscript is already right not to treat this as an audited Soviet appropriation. But the provenance can now be made substantially sharper.

## DIA later audited the number

DIA's March 1978 *Paraphysics R&D — Warsaw Pact* contains a revealing reference note on the recurring budget claim. It identifies Sheila Ostrander and Lynn Schroeder's 1970 *Psychic Discoveries Behind the Iron Curtain* as stating that the USSR had twenty or more paranormal-research centers and an estimated 1967 budget of 12–20 million rubles ($13–20 million). Crucially, DIA says their own note attributes the estimate only to a **"Confidential Report, American Source."**

The 1978 DIA note then traces what happened next. It observes that later in the same book Ostrander and Schroeder drop the lower bound and write of an estimated 20 million rubles a year. DIA further notes that Douglas Dean later used essentially the same claim while stating $20 million a year and omitting "estimated," whereas Charles Tart selected the lower end of the range.

Primary source: DIA, *Paraphysics R&D — Warsaw Pact*, DST-1810S-202-78, 30 March 1978, references to Section VII, especially reference 3:
https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/dia/PARAPHYSICS_RD-WARSAW_PACT.pdf

A CIA-hosted copy/searchable release of the 1972 material also preserves the 12-million-ruble to $21-million range:
https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00792R000600360001-2.pdf

## Why this changes the opening

The book currently treats $20 million as a number whose original receipt has not yet been found. That remains true. But we can now say more about the route by which the number acquired authority.

The chain is not simply:

`Soviet budget → U.S. intelligence estimate`

The located record supports something closer to:

`unrecovered "Confidential Report, American Source" → 1970 popular book with a 12–20 million-ruble estimate → U.S. defense-intelligence reporting → later repetitions that select a bound, change currency, or drop "estimated"`

That is almost a laboratory demonstration of the book's thesis. The number does not merely cross institutions. Its uncertainty changes shape while it travels.

## Publication correction

Do not write that the 1972 report's $20 million figure was an independent U.S. intelligence estimate unless the underlying source is recovered. The later DIA audit points to a published Western intermediary and an unrecovered American source behind that intermediary.

Likewise, do not write that *Psychic Discoveries Behind the Iron Curtain* invented the number. Its own cited provenance, as summarized by DIA, is a "Confidential Report, American Source." The source behind that phrase remains the real research target.

The safest current formulation is:

> A 1972 DIA report repeated a Soviet paranormal-research budget claim that a later DIA source audit traced through a 1970 popular book to an unidentified "Confidential Report, American Source." As the claim circulated, later versions selected different ends of the original range and sometimes dropped the word "estimated."

That sentence is less mysterious than "the secret report said $20 million" and more consequential. It shows the reader the mutation rather than merely warning that mutation can happen.

## Strongest counterargument

A messy citation chain does not establish that the underlying budget estimate was false. The unidentified American source could have been based on excellent collection, Soviet administrative information, or a defensible aggregation of known institutions. A number can be badly cited and still be right.

The evidentiary conclusion therefore has to remain narrow: **the currently located public record does not establish an independent Soviet budget source for the figure, and later repetition cannot be counted as independent corroboration.** Recovering the original American report could materially strengthen or destroy the claim.

## Narrative opportunity

Chapter 1 ends, "I want the receipt." The 1978 DIA reference note gives the book a better turn than simply announcing that the receipt is missing. Six years after *Controlled Offensive Behavior*, another DIA report effectively performed part of the source criticism itself and found a popular book pointing backward to an unnamed American report.

The hunt has moved one node closer to the source while becoming stranger: an American intelligence report may have helped authorize concern using a number that the later American intelligence record traces through a popular book back to an unidentified American source.

If the underlying report is eventually recovered, the chapter should follow the chain all the way through rather than stopping at the irony.

## Next research target

Recover the "Confidential Report, American Source" cited by Ostrander and Schroeder. Search pre-1970 U.S. military/intelligence, RAND/ARPA, scientific-exchange, émigré/interview, and contractor reporting for the 12-million-ruble / 20-centers pair. Treat exact repetition of both quantities as a provenance marker, not independent confirmation.
