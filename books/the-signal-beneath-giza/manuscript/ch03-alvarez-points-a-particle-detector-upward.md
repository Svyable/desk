# Alvarez Points a Particle Detector Upward

Long before ScanPyramids, Luis Alvarez and an Egyptian-American team placed cosmic-ray equipment in Khafre's pyramid. Their 1970 Science paper, *Search for Hidden Chambers in the Pyramids*, is a useful antidote to the idea that advanced sensing at Giza began with viral imagery.

The trick was elegant. Muons rain through us continuously. Stone removes some of them. A sufficiently large void removes less stone from a muon's path, producing an excess from that direction. Instead of transmitting energy through the pyramid, the experiment borrowed particles supplied by the atmosphere.

The historical irony is delicious. Khafre—the pyramid beneath which enormous structures would be claimed in 2025—was already the subject of one of archaeology's earliest famous particle-physics surveys. The early experiment did not disclose the kind of hidden architecture later imagined.

That null is not eternal proof of absence. Detector acceptance, placement, exposure time and geometry constrain what can be excluded. But it gives us the first rule of this book: every extraordinary subsurface claim has to be compared with what independent instruments should already have been capable of noticing.
