# The Picture That Went Around the World

The rendering looked better than evidence usually looks. That was the first warning.

Eight vertical forms appeared to descend beneath Khafre's pyramid, each wrapped by a spiral. Far below, geometric chambers seemed to wait in the rock. Numbers attached to the story were almost architectural: shafts allegedly extending hundreds of metres, chambers tens of metres across, connections spreading beneath the plateau. By the time most people encountered the claim, they were not encountering measurements. They were encountering an interpretation rendered as an object.

The distinction matters because the March 2025 claims were associated with Corrado Malanga, Filippo Biondi and Armando Mei and described as results of synthetic-aperture-radar analysis. The dramatic Khafre claims did not arrive as a conventional peer-reviewed Khafre excavation or geophysical paper containing raw observations, calibration targets, uncertainty analysis and an independently reproducible inversion. Specialists in radar and Egyptian archaeology objected on exactly those grounds. Lawrence Conyers, whose career has involved archaeological radar, told AFP that radar attenuation makes the claimed extreme depths implausible. Egyptian archaeologists noted that no authorized investigation had produced such a discovery.

None of this proves that every anomaly in the underlying analysis is meaningless. It establishes the burden. A picture of an inversion is not a photograph underground. Every reconstruction has a chain: transmitted or ambient signal, interaction with matter, received signal, preprocessing, assumptions, inversion, segmentation, visualization, interpretation. The further one travels down that chain, the easier it becomes to forget where measurement ended.

Giza is an unusually dangerous place to forget.
