# Eight Shafts, Six Hundred Metres

The most spectacular 2025 Khafre claim is also, in one respect, the easiest to test.

Not because satellite imagery is easy to interpret.

Because the claimed structures are enormous.

The public March 2025 abstract describes **eight vertically aligned cylindrical structures**, arranged in two north–south rows, descending to a depth of approximately **648 metres**. It says the cylinders are hollow and wrapped by descending spiral pathways. At their lower end, the eight structures are said to merge into **two large cubic structures measuring about 80 metres per side**.

Then the public story grows again: deeper structures, connections beneath the plateau, kilometers of architecture.

Stop before that.

Take only the dimensions the claim actually gives and ask what physics they purchase.

The public abstract I can verify does **not** give a trustworthy cylinder diameter. That omission matters. A 5-metre-radius shaft and a 25-metre-radius shaft are not remotely the same geophysical target. Any calculation that quietly assigns a diameter from a rendering would manufacture precision the claim itself has not supplied.

But the two cubes are already enough for a useful order-of-magnitude test.

An 80-metre cube has a volume of

`80 × 80 × 80 = 512,000 m³`.

Two of them contain just over **1.0 million cubic metres**.

If those volumes are air-filled cavities replacing ordinary Giza limestone, the missing mass is enormous. Published gravity modelling of the Giza Plateau has used regional/topographic rock densities around **2,200–2,500 kg/m³**, while engineering measurements on pyramid limestone span roughly the low-2,000-kg/m³ range depending on porosity and material.

Using a density contrast of 2,200–2,500 kg/m³, two empty 80-metre cubes correspond to a rock-mass deficit of approximately **2.25–2.56 billion kilograms**.

That is roughly **2.3–2.6 million metric tonnes** of missing rock before adding a single cubic metre for the eight shafts, spiral structures, connecting passages or any larger claimed complex.

This is why the phrase `underground city` is almost a distraction.

The geometry itself has consequences.

## The gravity test

Gravity is indifferent to archaeology.

Remove mass from below a gravimeter and the local vertical gravitational acceleration becomes slightly smaller. A cavity therefore produces a negative gravity anomaly relative to the same geology without the cavity.

The exact anomaly from the alleged Khafre geometry requires the actual locations, depths, dimensions, fills and surrounding density structure. None of those are adequately specified in the public claim. The eight-cylinder diameter is especially important and should not be guessed.

Still, the two cubes permit a deliberately crude sanity check.

Treat their combined missing mass as if it were concentrated at a point roughly 648–690 metres below a surface station directly above it. This is not a survey model; it is a scale estimate. Newton gives

`g ≈ GM/r²`.

For a combined missing mass of about 2.25–2.56 × 10⁹ kg, that approximation gives a negative surface anomaly on the order of **30–40 microgals** if the two cavities project near the station and are mostly air-filled.

One microgal is `10⁻⁸ m/s²`.

Modern archaeological microgravity instruments work in exactly this unit. A recent Saqqara archaeology study used a CG-5 gravimeter with a nominal 1-microgal resolution and reported standard deviations below about 5 microgals. Reviews of archaeological microgravimetry describe cavity anomalies in the several-to-tens-of-microgal regime as measurable under suitable field conditions.

So the rough Khafre-cube prediction is not `too small for gravity to matter`.

It is large enough that gravity deserves to be one of the first independent tests.

That does **not** mean one could walk onto the plateau tomorrow, record -35 microgals and announce two chambers.

Gravity is an inverse problem.

Topography matters. The pyramids themselves matter. Bedrock density varies. Karst, fractures, sediment, groundwater and near-surface archaeological structures all perturb the field. Elevation errors matter because gravity changes with height. Instrument drift and tides must be corrected. A shallow small mass deficit can imitate part of the field of a deeper large one.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's gravity-method guidance makes the same general point: voids generate negative anomalies, but interpretation is non-unique without external constraints on geometry and density.

That is a reason to do the survey carefully, not a reason to skip it.

The claim has already supplied a geometry.

That converts gravity from an open-ended inversion into a prediction test.

Freeze the alleged shaft/cube coordinates before looking at a new gravity map. Forward-model the gravitational field those exact structures should produce under several plausible fills: air, water, sediment and partial collapse. Include the measured plateau topography and a geologically reasonable density model. Then ask whether the observed residual contains the predicted spatial pattern at the predicted amplitude.

The model can lose.

That is the point.

If the claimed two 80-metre cavities are absent from a gravity dataset whose noise and geological model are demonstrably capable of detecting their predicted signal, the deep-structure interpretation takes a direct hit independent of SAR.

If a matching anomaly appears, gravity still does not prove architecture. Natural density variation can produce gravity anomalies. It would mean the claim has survived one physically independent test and earned the next one.

## What if the chambers are water-filled?

The viral story has sometimes accumulated water, aquifer or machine-like interpretations around the deep geometry. Those are later interpretive layers, not established observations.

But fill state matters physically.

Water replaces part of the missing rock mass. A water-filled cavity in 2.2–2.5 t/m³ limestone has a density contrast closer to roughly 1.2–1.5 t/m³ than to the full rock-air contrast. The predicted anomaly drops, but it does not disappear. Under the same crude point-mass approximation, the two 80-metre volumes still sit in approximately the **high-teens to low-twenties of microgals** if they are water-filled rather than air-filled.

Again: order of magnitude, not a field inversion.

The useful feature is that different fill hypotheses predict different amplitudes while preserving similar broad spatial geometry.

Gravity becomes a constraint on both existence and bulk density contrast.

## The spoil problem

There is a second mass balance that receives less attention because it is not rendered in glowing colors.

Where did the rock go?

Two 80-metre cubes alone require removal of more than a million cubic metres of host material if they are excavated spaces.

That is before the shafts.

Because the verified public abstract does not state the shaft diameter, this chapter should refuse to compute their excavation volume. The correct response to missing geometry is `unknown`, not `choose a number that makes the illustration work`.

But any future claimant who supplies a diameter automatically supplies a spoil prediction:

`V_shaft = πr²L`.

Multiply by eight and by approximately 648 metres of length. Add the spiral galleries if their cross-sections are specified. Convert the result to mass using the same density range.

Then look for the archaeological consequences of moving that volume through an Old Kingdom landscape.

Spoil does not have to remain in one giant pile. It can be reused in construction, dispersed, dumped into quarries, moved along ramps or incorporated into landscape works. Erosion and later activity can modify it.

But millions of tonnes of excavated limestone cannot simply become epistemically weightless because the hypothesized architecture is underground.

An engineering claim predicts a waste stream.

## The seismic test

A 648-metre vertical artificial structure also changes elastic properties.

A void, rubble-filled zone, water-filled shaft and intact limestone transmit seismic waves differently. Large regular cavities can generate reflections, scattering, travel-time perturbations and resonance effects. The exact response depends strongly on geometry, coupling, fracture state and frequency, which is why `seismic anomaly` is not a one-line proof of a chamber.

But the same preregistration rule applies.

Use the claimed geometry to forward-model expected responses before fitting the observed field.

If the eight shafts are arranged in two parallel rows, the model predicts spatial periodicity. If they are hollow, water-filled or rubble-filled, each hypothesis predicts different impedance contrasts. If spiral galleries surround them, those features add structured heterogeneity rather than random limestone noise.

A seismic campaign designed after seeing the answer would be weak.

A seismic campaign whose target geometry is frozen in advance can be devastatingly informative.

## The electrical test

Electrical resistivity is already part of Giza's legitimate archaeological toolkit.

ERT has been used in the ScanPyramids North Face Corridor work, and earlier resistivity investigations were performed around Khafre and the plateau decades ago. Air, water, porous sediment and competent limestone have very different electrical behavior.

Depth is the problem.

Near-surface archaeological ERT is one thing. Resolving a detailed 80-metre chamber roughly six hundred metres down beneath a noisy, heterogeneous archaeological plateau is another. Electrode spacing, current penetration, survey geometry and inversion regularization limit what can be recovered.

That makes ERT less obviously decisive than gravity for the claimed deepest structures.

But it can constrain the shallower parts of the alleged system. If the eight deep shafts connect to structures immediately below the pyramid or plateau, those upper connections should be sought where electrical methods have better sensitivity.

A claim spanning from near surface to 648 metres creates multiple testable depth regimes.

## The muon problem

Muography is attractive because Giza already has a spectacular success story.

But depth cannot be ignored.

Muon radiography works because cosmic-ray muons arriving from the sky are attenuated by the amount of matter they traverse. A void inside a pyramid reduces the integrated density along particular trajectories and creates an excess relative to solid-stone expectation.

A structure hundreds of metres **below** ground is geometrically different. Surface cosmic muons must traverse a much larger rock overburden before reaching any detector positioned to sample relevant paths. Flux falls as material thickness rises, and detector placement becomes the entire experiment.

So `the shafts are huge; muons will see them` is not a valid shortcut.

The ScIDEP Khafre project provides an empirical platform for serious sensitivity calculations. Given detector area, exposure time, angular resolution, placement and rock overburden, one can ask which portions of the claimed geometry are actually testable by absorption muography and at what statistical power.

If some deep regions are inaccessible to practical muon geometries, say so.

The value of independent testing comes from honoring each instrument's limits.

## The cube is already enough

The strongest feature of the 2025 claim is not its antiquity.

It is its size.

Tiny unknown chambers can remain ambiguous because several methods sit near their detection limits. Two 80-metre cubes six hundred metres down, connected to eight gigantic shafts, are not asking for that indulgence.

The public claim predicts at least four classes of consequence:

1. a large missing-mass budget;
2. a structured gravity field;
3. elastic/electrical discontinuities whose detectability can be forward-modeled;
4. an excavation and spoil history commensurate with the alleged volume.

The next serious paper should not begin with another rendering.

It should publish the exact 3-D coordinates and dimensions of every claimed structure, including shaft diameters; provide the inversion uncertainty; and release a machine-readable geometry that independent groups can feed into gravity, seismic, electrical and muon forward models without asking the original authors how to interpret the picture.

Then make the hypotheses compete.

**H1:** the claimed artificial geometry exists approximately as published.

**H2:** some anomaly exists, but its geometry or origin is geological and materially different.

**H3:** the deep geometry is an inversion artifact and no corresponding density/elastic/electrical structure exists.

Every new instrument should report how likely its data are under all three.

This is the advantage of an extraordinary geometric claim.

It is not too extraordinary to test.

It is too large to be allowed to remain only an image.
