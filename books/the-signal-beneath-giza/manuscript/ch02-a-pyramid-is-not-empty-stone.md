# A Pyramid Is Not Empty Stone

A pyramid is already a subsurface problem before anyone looks beneath it. Its interior is a density field assembled from quarried blocks, joints, mortar, relieving spaces, corridors and chambers. Beneath it lies bedrock that was itself cut, leveled and incorporated into construction.

This makes the language of a 'scan' deceptive. There is no universal archaeological scanner. Ground-penetrating radar sends electromagnetic energy and reads reflections produced by contrasts in electromagnetic properties. Electrical resistivity asks how current travels through material. Ultrasound interrogates elastic propagation. Muography counts naturally occurring cosmic-ray muons surviving different thicknesses of matter. Satellite SAR is another instrument family again, exquisitely useful for surface deformation and terrain but not a magical X-ray of hundreds of metres of limestone.

Each method has a different depth-resolution tradeoff and a different collection of things that can impersonate a void. A fracture, moisture contrast, construction joint, lithological boundary or badly modeled surface can matter enormously to one technique and barely register in another.

The correct question is therefore never 'did radar see a chamber?' It is: what observable changed, and which physical models can produce that change?
