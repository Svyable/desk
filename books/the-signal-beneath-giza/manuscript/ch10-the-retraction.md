# The Retraction

On 10 August 2026 *Remote Sensing* retracted the 2022 Biondi–Malanga paper that had presented Synthetic Aperture Radar Doppler Tomography as a way to reconstruct the interior and subsurface of the Great Pyramid.

The wording matters.

The journal did not say merely that the subject had become controversial. It said that, after a reader raised methodological concerns, the Editorial Office and Editorial Board investigated and **confirmed serious methodological flaws and statistical errors that were deemed to undermine the scientific conclusions of the article**.

The authors disagreed with the retraction.

Those two facts belong beside each other. Retraction is a formal change in the status of a scientific paper, not an oracle. The journal concluded that the paper's conclusions no longer stood on an acceptable methodological/statistical foundation. The authors did not accept that judgment. A historian should record both. An investigator deciding how much evidentiary weight to place on the paper should also record that the burden has changed.

The retraction is especially important here because the 2022 article was not a disposable side paper. It was the published methodological ancestor of the later public story.

Its central move was audacious. Ordinary synthetic-aperture radar does not penetrate hundreds of metres through limestone and return a high-resolution architectural image. The authors acknowledged the penetration problem directly. Their proposed escape was to infer interior structure indirectly from tiny surface motions—micro-movements associated with background seismic excitation—measured through Doppler information in repeated COSMO-SkyMed SAR observations. In their description, the pyramid became effectively transparent in a vibration or micro-motion domain rather than because the radar beam itself physically illuminated deep internal walls.

That distinction is essential because public retellings routinely collapse it.

If somebody says a satellite radar 'saw' a shaft six hundred metres underground, a reader naturally imagines energy traveling down to the shaft and returning from it. That is not the proposed mechanism. The proposed chain is longer:

subsurface or internal structure influences mechanical response;

mechanical response influences surface micro-motion;

surface micro-motion alters a remotely measured Doppler signal;

an inversion algorithm maps that signal back into a three-dimensional structural interpretation.

Every arrow is a model.

That does not make the idea impossible. Seismology, medical imaging, gravitational inversion and muography all infer unseen structure indirectly. Indirect measurement is most of science.

But indirect measurement lives or dies by validation.

If an inversion claims to recover known corridors, chambers or other structures, those known targets become controls. If it predicts an unknown feature, a second physical method or direct inspection should eventually test it. Synthetic recovery tests should demonstrate what happens when realistic noise, alternative structures and nuisance motions are fed through the same pipeline. Statistical significance should be tied to a defined null model rather than to the visual persuasiveness of a reconstruction.

The public peer-review record from 2022 is instructive because the validation problem was visible before the retraction. One reviewer described the work as interesting and detailed but specifically noted that validation of the results was missing.

That sentence now reads differently.

Peer review is not certification. A paper can pass review with a limitation that later becomes central. Reviewers may judge a novel method worth publishing even when external validation remains incomplete, especially if the paper frames itself as an exploratory demonstration. Subsequent readers often forget the conditionality and cite publication itself as proof that the method works.

Four years later, the journal decided the problem was stronger than an ordinary limitation.

This book should not invent details the retraction notice does not provide. The notice does **not** publish a line-by-line forensic report explaining every statistical error, every methodological defect or exactly which figure fails under which alternative model. It does not supply the full internal investigation. Therefore the responsible claim is the one the record supports: the journal investigated reader-raised concerns, concluded that serious methodological flaws and statistical errors undermined the scientific conclusions, retracted the article, and recorded the authors' disagreement.

Anything more specific has to come from disclosed technical criticism, reproducible reanalysis or additional documentation.

That boundary is important because retractions attract mythology in both directions.

Supporters can say the paper was suppressed because it threatened archaeology.

Critics can say the retraction proves every later underground claim fraudulent.

Neither follows from the notice.

The first claim needs evidence of improper motive beyond the existence of an adverse editorial decision. The second confuses a failed evidentiary pillar with a universal disproof.

The correct effect is more mechanical.

Before 10 August 2026, somebody arguing for the later deep-Khafre structures could point backward to a peer-reviewed 2022 paper and say: the method already exists in the literature; it has been published; it has reconstructed pyramid interiors.

After 10 August, that move is no longer available in the same form.

The paper can still be studied historically. Its equations can still be tested. Its code or procedures, where available, can still be reproduced. A retracted method could in principle be rehabilitated by an independent group that implements it cleanly, preregisters the inversion, validates it on known geometry and obtains blind predictive success.

But the retracted paper itself cannot serve as established validation.

That matters even more because the sensational 2025 claim concerns **Khafre**, while the 2022 paper concerned **Khufu**. The objects, geometries and later claims are not interchangeable. A failure in the Khufu paper does not logically prove there is no deep structure beneath Khafre. What it changes is the status of the technique offered to infer that structure.

The claim must now stand without borrowed authority from the retracted article.

This is where the North Face Corridor becomes such a useful control case.

The corridor behind the chevron on Khufu's north face did not become persuasive because one image looked architectural. Muography localized an anomaly. Ground-penetrating radar, ultrasonic testing and electrical-resistivity tomography approached the region with different physical sensitivities. Endoscopy finally turned an inference into a directly observed space.

Each method had different failure modes.

That is what convergence means.

A SAR-derived reconstruction and another SAR-derived reconstruction produced through the same conceptual inversion are not independent in the same way, even if the satellite passes are different. If both depend on the same unvalidated mapping from surface micro-motion to deep geometry, their agreement can reproduce the same model error twice.

The retraction therefore makes the experimental design for Khafre clearer.

Do not argue about whether a rendering looks plausible.

Freeze the claimed geometry first.

The public claim describes enormous features: multiple near-vertical shafts said to extend hundreds of metres, spiral structures, large chambers, and a broader connected complex. Structures on that scale should not be allowed to exist only inside one inversion framework.

Calculate their predicted signatures under independent physics.

A void or low-density shaft changes local mass and therefore gravity, however slightly.

A large cavity or strong density contrast changes muon attenuation along suitable paths.

Mechanical discontinuities affect seismic-wave propagation.

Fluid-filled or resistivity-contrasting structures affect electrical measurements depending on depth, geometry and host rock.

Nearer features may affect conventional radar, though deep hundreds-of-metres claims exceed the useful penetration regime usually imagined for archaeological GPR in limestone.

The models should be published before measurements are interpreted. Not because preregistration is fashionable, but because inverse problems are generous. If analysts are allowed to alter geometry, thresholds, filters and priors after looking at the data, many landscapes can be made to confess.

Then include controls that embarrass the method.

Run the full pipeline over places on the plateau where geology and known archaeology predict no giant shafts.

Run it over known voids and known solid structures.

Blind the teams analyzing one modality to the reconstructions produced by the others.

Define in advance what degree of mismatch kills the eight-shaft model.

If the claimed features are genuinely hundreds of metres tall and architecturally regular, they should be easier to falsify than a small unknown chamber.

Their extravagance is an experimental advantage.

The retraction also changes how the cultural timeline should be written.

A viral claim can become cognitively permanent before the literature catches up. In 2025, renderings of immense structures beneath Khafre circulated globally. They were simple enough to remember: shafts, spirals, chambers, a city below the pyramids. In August 2026, the correction arrived in the least viral form imaginable—a retraction notice discussing methodological flaws and statistical errors in an older paper.

The first object is an image.

The second is metadata.

Images win that race unless somebody preserves the chronology.

So preserve it.

**2022:** a peer-reviewed SAR Doppler tomography paper claims high-resolution reconstruction of Khufu's interior and subsurface through micro-motion analysis. Its peer-review record includes an explicit concern that validation is missing.

**2025:** a much larger public claim is made about deep structures beneath Khafre, relying on related methodological ideas but extending them into geometry of a radically different scale.

**10 August 2026:** *Remote Sensing* retracts the 2022 article after an investigation, citing serious methodological flaws and statistical errors that undermine its scientific conclusions. The authors disagree.

That sequence does not tell us what is beneath Khafre.

It tells us what evidence no longer gets to answer the question for us.

The deep-structure claim remains experimentally accessible.

That is better than either faith or ridicule.
