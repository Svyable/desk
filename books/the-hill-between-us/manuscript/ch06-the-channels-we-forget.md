# Chapter 6 — The Channels We Forget

Close your eyes and the room does not become silent.

Put on headphones and the room does not become dark.

Stand behind a wall and the floor may still vibrate. Turn off the lights and a phone may still transmit. Say nothing and your face continues to move.

The phrase *no communication* is surprisingly difficult to engineer.

This matters whenever somebody claims information passed between people without ordinary sensory contact. Before looking for a new channel, we have to know how many old ones were still open.

Humans usually think about communication in categories shaped by conscious experience. We talk. We look. We touch. We smell. The categories feel separate because the experiences feel separate.

Physical signals are less cooperative.

A speaking voice produces airborne pressure waves, but it can also vibrate a table, floor, wall, or shared object. A moving person changes reflected light, generates sound below the level of conscious notice, disturbs air, produces heat, and changes the visual field. A phone emits light and sound while also communicating through radio. A held board carries forces between hands even when nobody labels those forces as touch.

If the experimental claim is that information crossed without a known channel, the investigator’s job is not to ask whether participants *noticed* a channel. It is to ask whether target information was physically available in one.

Vision is the first place to look because eyes extract more than people realize.

A target does not have to be shown directly. It can appear in a reflection. It can affect the gaze of someone who knows it. The orientation of a head can indicate where attention is directed. Pupil size, blinking, facial tension, and tiny shifts of posture can covary with surprise or expectation. A person reading candidate letters may linger differently on the right one. A facilitator may glance toward a region of the board before moving it.

Humans can learn these regularities without verbalizing them.

The classic caution comes from Clever Hans, the horse once believed able to perform arithmetic. Under investigation, Hans’s apparent answers were found to depend on subtle cues from human questioners who knew the answer. The important lesson is not that animals are stupid or observers dishonest. It is that a receiver can become exquisitely sensitive to signals a sender does not know they are sending.

A telepathy experiment in which the sender and receiver can see each other therefore begins with an enormous confound.

The appropriate response is not to declare every success visual cueing. It is to remove vision and see what survives.

Opaque barriers are better than asking the sender to “keep a neutral face.” Separate rooms are better still. Cameras can verify positioning, but live video should not be available to the participants unless the experiment is specifically testing visual influence. If a communication partner remains with the receiver, that person should be blind to the target too.

Sound is equally treacherous.

A room that feels quiet can contain useful information. Breathing changes with effort. Fingers tap. Clothing rustles. A person shifts weight. A computer fan changes load. A target presentation system may make different noises for different stimuli. An experimenter may turn a page or click a mouse at a predictable moment. Speech in another room can leak through vents or structure.

Masking requires more than headphones if the body can feel vibration.

Acoustic isolation and structural isolation are different engineering problems. Bone conduction can transmit sound through contact with surfaces. Low-frequency vibration can travel through floors. A shared table can become a mechanical channel. If a sender taps a foot while imagining a rhythm, the receiver may not report “I heard a tap” and yet timing information may be present.

Then there is smell.

Olfaction is unlikely to carry arbitrary words at the bandwidth imagined in strong telepathy claims, but humans and other animals emit volatile chemicals that vary with physiology and environment. Perfume, food, smoke, cleaning products, stress-related sweating, and room airflow can provide contextual information. Smell is slow, diffuse, and difficult to use for precise hidden targets, which makes it a poor candidate for rapid semantic transfer. Still, if the experimental question concerns emotional state or presence rather than a random number, chemical cues cannot be dismissed casually.

Heat is similar.

Infrared radiation and convection reveal that a warm organism is present. Skin temperature changes with autonomic state. Thermal cameras can detect vascular changes in the face. None of this resembles a known pathway for decoding another person’s sentence at a distance, but physiological-state experiments can be contaminated by shared thermal environment or direct contact.

The broader lesson is that the body leaks state through multiple physical variables simultaneously.

A person who knows a target becomes a target-dependent system.

Their eyes may change.

Their movements may change.

Their timing may change.

Their voice, breathing, and muscle tension may change.

Even when none of those changes intentionally encodes the answer, a practiced receiver may exploit correlations.

This is why “the sender never said anything” is weak evidence for telepathy.

Silence closes one channel.

Now consider technology.

Modern experiments live inside an invisible radio environment. Phones, watches, earbuds, tablets, Wi-Fi access points, Bluetooth devices, smart speakers, cameras, laptops, cloud services, messaging notifications, and networked lab equipment routinely move information through walls. A participant may have no intention to cheat and still receive a notification that changes timing. An experimenter’s device may sync a target file to another machine. A camera feed may be accessible in a control room. Shared documents may update automatically.

Extraordinary claims require embarrassingly ordinary cybersecurity.

Devices should be inventoried. Unnecessary radios should be disabled or removed. Target generation should occur on systems whose outputs cannot reach the receiver. Logs should record network activity. If a study is serious enough to investigate a new biological channel, it is serious enough to check whether Bluetooth was on.

Procedures can leak information without physics doing anything unusual.

Suppose a target list contains one hundred images, but researchers use them in the same order for every participant. A receiver who has encountered the protocol before can outperform chance without telepathy. Suppose randomization is performed with shuffled index cards that researchers can see. Facial reactions can leak. Suppose “hard” targets are unconsciously repeated after failures. Trial history becomes informative. Suppose only impressive trials are uploaded or discussed publicly. Selection creates an apparent effect after data collection even if no signal existed during it.

The channel can be statistical.

This is one of the least intuitive points in anomalous research. Information does not have to leak physically between sender and receiver if it leaks from the design into the answer distribution.

Imagine a researcher asks a participant to guess a number from one to ten but unconsciously favors seven. A receiver who knows human choice biases may outperform a true random baseline. If the target is generated by the researcher rather than by a secure random process, the receiver can “read the mind” by modeling human habits.

Close relationships make this easier.

Partners accumulate shared priors. A parent asked to think of a favorite vacation destination is not selecting from all locations on Earth uniformly. A child may know which place the parent is likely to choose. A spouse asked for a song may know the handful that matter. A nonspeaking person who has spent years listening to family conversations can know much more about likely answers than observers assume.

This can produce events that feel impossible because outsiders misestimate the size of the target space.

The correct denominator matters.

If somebody “guesses” the name *Grandma* when asked who a parent is thinking about, chance is not one divided by every human name. The context concentrates probability. If a person identifies one of four truly randomized unfamiliar images, the target space is cleaner.

The channel of prior knowledge is particularly relevant to the Hill.

A shared imagined landscape can accumulate through exposure that nobody remembers as exposure. Somebody describes a gate months earlier. Another person sees fan art. A parent repeats a phrase. A podcast episode names a feature. A practitioner asks a leading question. Later, two participants independently mention the gate and the match feels spontaneous.

Source memory is fragile.

We often remember content without remembering where we learned it. This is ordinary cognition, not deceit. A Hill study therefore has to distinguish community-shared features from truly novel experimental features introduced after isolation.

The most powerful targets are boring before the experiment.

A randomized pattern. A meaningless symbol. A strange object generated from components. A number with no personal significance. Such targets have little emotional richness, which may reduce whatever effect believers expect. But they also reduce inferential shortcuts.

A careful program can use both.

First test low-semantic targets that cleanly measure information transfer. Then, if an effect exists, test emotionally meaningful or place-based content to see whether ecological relevance changes the channel.

This avoids another common trap: interpreting semantic richness as evidence strength.

A receiver who writes a moving paragraph about a deceased relative may produce a powerful experience while giving statisticians almost nothing to score. A receiver who identifies 73 instead of one of ninety-nine alternatives is emotionally dull and informationally crisp.

Science often advances through dull targets.

The forgotten channels also include the body’s internal sense of timing.

Two people do not need to exchange target content to become synchronized. If they know a trial begins every thirty seconds, both nervous systems can anticipate the same moments. Heart rate, breathing, movement, and brain activity may align around the shared schedule. A hyperscanning analysis can then find inter-brain synchrony created by the experiment itself.

This is a profound caution.

A measured correlation between brains does not imply that one brain transmitted the target to the other. Both may be entrained to the same clock, prompt, video, music, task structure, or movement rhythm.

Researchers in hyperscanning increasingly emphasize this problem: genuine reciprocal coupling has to be distinguished from synchrony driven by shared sensory input, joint attention, and parallel motor activity.

The same logic applies outside brain imaging.

If two people’s heart rates rise simultaneously while watching the same suspenseful film, no heart-to-heart information channel is needed. The film is the common cause.

If two participants relax together after a familiar breathing exercise, correlated respiration is expected.

If a parent becomes tense when a hidden target appears and a child’s arousal changes seconds later, that is more interesting—but only if no ordinary cue connects them.

The forgotten-channel inventory therefore becomes an experimental checklist.

Light.

Sound.

Vibration.

Touch and shared objects.

Air movement and chemical cues.

Heat.

Radio and network communication.

Timing.

Prior knowledge.

Target predictability.

Experimenter behavior.

Facilitator behavior.

Post hoc scoring.

Data selection.

That list can feel deflationary, as though every interesting event is being buried under mundane possibilities.

The opposite is true.

Each closed channel increases the scientific value of what remains.

Suppose a receiver performs above chance with visual contact. Interesting.

Add an opaque barrier and the effect remains. More interesting.

Move the sender to an acoustically isolated room. Still there.

Remove networked devices. Still there.

Generate targets after isolation. Still there.

Ensure nobody with the receiver knows the answer. Still there.

Use arbitrary targets. Still there.

Predefine the analysis. Still there.

Replicate at another site. Still there.

At some point, “you missed a cue” stops being a satisfying explanation unless the critic can name a plausible cue consistent with the controls.

This is how skepticism becomes constructive. It makes the anomaly expensive.

An effect that pays that price earns attention.

The same process can reveal something subtler than telepathy.

Perhaps performance falls only when the familiar communication partner is physically absent, even though the partner never knows the target. That would suggest the partner contributes regulation rather than information. Researchers could then study autonomic state, movement variability, anxiety, attention, and motor control to understand why the person communicates better in that relationship.

Perhaps performance falls when the board is fixed but returns with a motorized board that reproduces the familiar movement pattern. Then board motion is an access variable.

Perhaps performance survives opaque visual barriers but fails under acoustic masking. A barely audible timing cue may be involved.

Perhaps performance depends on trial rhythm. The receiver may have learned sequence statistics.

Every ordinary explanation discovered this way is a scientific result.

There is no shame in finding a channel we forgot.

The shame would be in preferring mystery so strongly that we refuse to look.

Human perception evolved in an environment where information arrived redundantly. A predator could be heard, seen, smelled, and felt through vibration. A familiar person can be recognized by gait before the face is visible. A baby coordinates with a caregiver through voice, touch, smell, temperature, and movement. Our nervous systems were not designed around laboratory categories.

They combine signals.

That makes subtle communication powerful and experimental isolation difficult.

It also gives us a standard for the extraordinary.

To claim a new human information channel, we do not need to prove that every known channel is absent from the universe. We need to show that target-specific information remains when plausible known routes are controlled strongly enough that they cannot account for the observed performance.

That standard is demanding but reachable.

We know how to build separate rooms.

We know how to randomize targets.

We know how to blind researchers.

We know how to log devices.

We know how to track movement and gaze.

We know how to publish protocols before results.

The unknown part of telepathy is not an excuse to be casual about the known parts of experimentation.

Quite the opposite.

Before asking whether minds have a hidden channel, we should become experts in every channel the room already has.
