# Chapter 28 — The Adversarial Lab

The ideal telepathy experiment should make both sides slightly nervous.

Believers should worry that the effect might disappear.

Skeptics should worry that it might not.

If only one side is vulnerable, the study is probably performing a social function before it performs a scientific one.

That is why the most interesting laboratory for this subject is adversarial in the old sense of the word: not hostile, but structured around competing explanations that are allowed to win.

Imagine the first meeting.

On one side sit families and practitioners who believe they have observed target-specific mind-to-mind communication repeatedly. Some have years of experience with nonspeaking participants. They know which conditions make communication easier and which conditions cause motor access to deteriorate. They are wary of tests that remove every support until the person can no longer respond and then call the collapse disproof.

On the other side sit researchers who know the history of facilitated communication, ideomotor effects, sensory leakage, selective reporting, weak blinding, and failed extraordinary claims. They are wary of demonstrations in which the only people who know the target are standing beside the person producing the answer.

Both groups have information the other needs.

The experiment should be designed so neither group has to trust the other’s intentions after the data arrive.

That begins with the claim.

Not “telepathy exists.”

Not “the Hill is real.”

Not “these methods are invalid.”

Choose one sentence narrow enough to be killed.

For example:

A receiver whose output authorship has been independently demonstrated will identify which of four randomly selected targets is shown to an isolated sender at a rate above the preregistered chance model when no person or device accessible to the receiver contains the target identity.

That sentence is ugly compared with a podcast story.

It is beautiful compared with an argument.

Every major ambiguity has somewhere to attach.

Who is the receiver?

How was authorship demonstrated?

How are targets generated?

What counts as isolation?

Who can access target identity?

How is the response scored?

What rate counts as evidence?

What happens to incomplete trials?

The protocol can answer these before the first result exists.

Preregistration is useful because memory becomes unreliable once an exciting pattern appears. Researchers sincerely remember an analysis as obvious after seeing that it works. They discover that one exclusion rule feels more principled than another. A participant’s unusual response suddenly looks interpretable. A subgroup begins to seem theoretically special.

This flexibility is not fraud.

It is ordinary cognition under motivation.

A preregistered protocol reduces the number of decisions that can quietly change direction after the outcome is visible.

For a controversial claim, go further.

Use a Registered Report or an equivalent review-before-results process where possible. Outside experts evaluate the question and method before the target data are collected. If the design is sound, publication does not depend on whether the effect is positive.

That changes incentives.

A null result is no longer a failed paper.

A positive result is less vulnerable to the accusation that only successful studies escaped the file drawer.

Both sides gain something.

Adversarial collaboration adds another layer.

Before data collection, ask each side to write what pattern would make them update.

The believers might say: if independently authored receivers perform at chance across two adequately powered labs under conditions that preserve their validated access method, we will reduce confidence that the tested telepathic transfer occurs reliably under those conditions.

The skeptics might say: if those receivers exceed the preregistered threshold in two independent labs, with target-blind receiver staff, sender-absent controls at chance, and a clean leakage audit, we will accept that an anomalous target-specific transfer effect has been demonstrated even if no mechanism is known.

The statements should be public.

This prevents the goalposts from moving after the kick.

A skeptic who responds to a positive replication by inventing an unlimited sequence of unspecified confounds has stopped being falsifiable.

A believer who responds to a null result by declaring that skepticism itself blocked the signal has done the same.

Both positions may generate new hypotheses, but new hypotheses need new tests.

The original claim still gets its result.

The authorship gate deserves its own team.

Telepathy experiments involving spelling should not make the transfer study carry the burden of proving the response method at the same time. That creates a logical knot.

First, establish independent semantic control under conditions appropriate to the person.

This does not necessarily mean typing alone at a desk.

A participant may need positioning, motor stabilization, breaks, sensory accommodations, or an access partner. The crucial feature is that the person providing those supports cannot know the answer on the diagnostic trials.

Participant-only knowledge trials are the heart of the test.

If a person can reliably report information only they received while the partner remains blind, the method has passed a meaningful authorship criterion under those conditions.

Document exactly what supports remain.

Then use that validated configuration in the telepathy study.

This sequencing protects the participant from a cruel design error: removing a necessary motor support in the name of independence.

Independence of *information* matters more than theatrical independence of posture.

A person can need another human physically and still author their own words.

The lab needs to demonstrate which role the support is playing.

Target generation should be treated like a security system.

Use a random procedure whose output is not predictable from previous trials. Generate the target only after sender and receiver are isolated. Store the assignment in an immutable log. Reveal it only to the sender through a device or procedure that does not transmit target data elsewhere.

Nobody in the receiver environment should possess the target.

Not the facilitator.

Not the local experimenter.

Not the camera operator.

Not the person administering breaks.

Not the software rendering the receiver interface unless it absolutely must, and preferably it should not.

The receiver system can record a choice among labels that have no local mapping to the target identity until the trial is over.

This is information compartmentalization.

Banks use it.

Security teams use it.

Telepathy research should too.

A robust lab also assumes that electronics can betray the protocol.

Stimulus computers can emit electromagnetic side channels. Network traffic can reveal trial timing. Shared cloud services can synchronize metadata. Smart watches and phones can create notifications. Video systems can alter fan noise or status lights. A target image may differ in file size and therefore load time.

The leakage audit should begin before participants arrive.

Run the apparatus with empty rooms.

Can any sensor near the receiver classify the sender’s target above chance?

Record microphones, field sensors, network logs, room vibration, device power, and whatever else is relevant to the proposed controls. The goal is not to prove the universe silent.

It is to determine whether target information is physically available through an unintended route.

Sender-absent trials make the audit behavioral.

The receiver completes trials exactly as usual, but no sender sees the target. The software and researchers proceed through the same sequence. If accuracy remains high, the sender’s mind was unnecessary.

That is devastating for the telepathy interpretation and extremely helpful for finding the leak.

Sham-target trials test something slightly different. The system goes through the timing of a trial but no meaningful target is assigned. If receivers produce rich confident descriptions anyway, the study learns about response generation and expectancy.

Neither control humiliates the participant.

Humans generate experiences under uncertainty.

The protocol needs to know how often a compelling experience appears without target information.

Data handling should be designed for disappointment.

Every initiated trial gets an identifier before the response occurs.

Every exclusion must match a rule written in advance or be reported transparently as post hoc.

A participant who needs to stop is not penalized, but the missingness remains visible. If sessions end more often after difficult targets or suspected misses, ignoring those trials can bias the result.

Video or sensor failures should be logged whether they occur on hits or misses.

No trial should vanish because the story became inconvenient.

The same principle applies to participants.

If twenty people are tested and two perform spectacularly, it is tempting to declare them the true telepaths and treat the other eighteen as noise.

Perhaps they are genuinely different.

The next study should predict that difference before testing new data.

Use the original sample to generate a subgroup hypothesis, not to certify a subgroup retrospectively.

Then recruit or retest according to the prespecified marker.

Exceptional-individual research is legitimate.

Exceptional-individual storytelling is not enough.

A multiverse of analyses is another threat.

Telepathy data can be scored in many ways: exact accuracy, semantic similarity, judge rankings, physiological synchrony, time windows, confidence, selected subgroups, selected target classes, selected days. If researchers inspect all of them and report the strongest, chance can look intelligent.

Choose one primary outcome.

Everything else can be secondary or exploratory.

The primary analysis should be boring enough that independent statisticians can reproduce it from a table.

For a four-choice task, that might simply be the proportion correct across all valid trials compared with the preregistered chance model, with participant-level structure handled as specified in advance.

Rich Hill reports can have a separate blinded ranking endpoint.

Physiological analyses can test mechanism only after behavioral transfer has been established.

This hierarchy prevents a study from failing behaviorally and then surviving as a beautiful EEG plot.

Replication should be designed into the first study, not requested defensively afterward.

Two sites can share the protocol while keeping target generation and data storage independently auditable. The second site should not copy every incidental feature of the first; some procedural diversity is useful because it tests whether the effect depends on a hidden local confound.

But the primary claim and analysis must remain comparable.

An especially strong design could split the work into discovery and confirmation.

Site A runs a preregistered protocol and, if an effect appears, may explore which state, distance, or participant variables predict it.

Site B receives the confirmatory predictions before opening its own outcome data.

Site B then tests them without modification.

If both succeed, the result has survived two different forms of researcher freedom.

Data sharing requires care because participants are identifiable and potentially vulnerable. Raw video of nonspeaking people in intimate communication settings cannot simply be dumped online in the name of openness.

Transparency has to coexist with privacy.

Share de-identified trial-level outcomes, code, target-generation methods, preregistration, scoring rules, equipment specifications, and synthetic or masked examples sufficient for audit. Make controlled-access data available where ethically appropriate. Have independent auditors verify sensitive logs without publishing private material.

Open science is not a waiver of dignity.

It is a design problem.

Consent deserves unusual attention too.

Participants should know what a null result means and what it does not mean. The study is not testing whether they are intelligent, whether their inner life is real, or whether their family relationship is meaningful. It is testing a specific information-transfer claim under specific conditions.

They should also know what a positive result could bring: media attention, pressure to perform, intrusive requests, attempts to commercialize the phenomenon, and strangers projecting beliefs onto them.

The possibility of success creates ethical risks as surely as the possibility of failure.

A lab around dignity needs a publicity plan before the headline arrives.

No participant should be made responsible for convincing the world.

The team should communicate the statistical result, limitations, and next replication step. Families and participants can describe their experiences separately, but the scientific claim should remain narrower than the human story.

Adversarial laboratories are sometimes imagined as tense rooms where opponents inspect one another for cheating.

The better version is quieter.

People with different priors build a system neither can easily manipulate after the fact.

The believer contributes ecological validity: the task must preserve the conditions in which the participant can genuinely communicate.

The skeptic contributes threat modeling: the target cannot leak and the analysis cannot change after seeing the result.

The statistician contributes identifiability.

The engineer contributes instrumentation.

The disability expert contributes access and consent.

The participant contributes the only performance that ultimately matters.

No single perspective controls the study.

That distribution of control is a feature.

If the result is null, believers helped make the test fair.

If the result is positive, skeptics helped make it difficult to dismiss.

There is a peculiar generosity in designing an experiment that could embarrass you.

It means the phenomenon matters more than your position.

The telepathy debate has had plenty of demonstrations designed to persuade people already willing to be persuaded and critiques designed to reassure people already unconvinced.

The next useful experiment should be built for the person who expects the opposite result.

Then let the data make somebody uncomfortable.
