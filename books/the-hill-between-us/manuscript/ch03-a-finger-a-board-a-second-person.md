# Chapter 3 — A Finger, a Board, a Second Person

Put an alphabet on a piece of cardboard and the object looks almost too simple to contain a controversy.

Twenty-six letters. Perhaps numbers. Perhaps a space for punctuation. A finger approaches. A letter is touched. Then another. The sequence becomes a word.

The visible machinery is minimal.

That is precisely why the invisible machinery matters.

In spelling-based communication used by some nonspeaking autistic people, a communication partner may hold a board, position it, move it between selections, provide verbal prompts, regulate pace, redirect attention, or offer other forms of support. Techniques and practices vary across programs and individuals. Some involve no physical contact between the communication partner and the speller. Advocates often emphasize that absence of touch as a decisive difference from older Facilitated Communication.

It is a meaningful difference.

It is not the same as proving independent authorship.

A communication system can transmit influence without direct contact. That sentence is so ordinary outside this debate that it barely needs defense. A catcher moves a glove and the pitcher changes the throw. A person backing a car watches another person’s hands. A conductor does not touch the violinist. A teacher’s face can change a student’s answer. Two people carrying a table adjust one another continuously through the object between them.

A held letterboard is also an object between two nervous systems.

To understand why this matters, we need to stop thinking of pointing as a ballistic event in which a brain selects a target and a finger simply travels there.

Movement is closed-loop.

The nervous system prepares a movement based on an estimate of the body and environment. Vision supplies information about the target and hand. Proprioception supplies information about limb position. The movement begins. New sensory information arrives. The path can be corrected. The target itself may move. The hand may accelerate, slow, hover, overshoot, or redirect. Another person holding the target can change the geometry while all of this is happening.

What looks like a single choice may therefore contain a negotiation between trajectories.

This does not imply manipulation. It implies dynamics.

Imagine a board held upright at arm’s length. The speller begins moving toward a region. The partner, intentionally or not, makes a tiny compensatory movement to keep the board aligned with the hand. The speller corrects. The partner corrects again. Across hundreds of selections, the pair can learn a stable rhythm. The partner may begin anticipating where the hand is going. The speller may begin using the board’s motion as part of the targeting process.

At that point the dyad has acquired a skill.

The question is what information the skill can transmit and who contributes what.

A laboratory interested only in the final letter may miss the answer.

This is why the mechanics of the board deserve the same attention that sports science gives a bat, racket, or dance partner. Put motion trackers on the board and hand. Record gaze. Record the timing of verbal prompts. Measure the board’s translation and rotation before each selection. Ask whether board motion predicts the selected letter before the speller’s finger trajectory does. Ask whether the hand leads the board or the board leads the hand. Compare familiar and unfamiliar partners. Fix the board to a stand and see what changes. Randomly rotate or reposition the board between trials while preserving readability. Remove verbal prompts. Mask the partner’s face. Change one variable at a time.

Every manipulation reveals part of the control architecture.

The same approach has transformed other fields. Skilled behavior often turns out to be distributed across body and environment. A baseball outfielder need not calculate a projectile equation explicitly; perceptual-motor strategies can guide running by keeping certain visual relations stable. A person using a cane extends useful sensory information through the tool. A pianist’s timing depends on the mechanical response of the keyboard as well as the motor command. Tools become incorporated into control loops.

There is no reason a letterboard interaction should be less interesting.

Yet the history behind assisted communication makes this interest ethically charged.

Facilitated Communication became widely known as a method in which a facilitator physically supported or guided a disabled person’s hand or arm while the person typed or pointed. Controlled message-passing studies produced a devastating pattern: when the facilitator and the disabled person were shown different information, outputs often corresponded to what the facilitator had seen rather than what the purported communicator had seen. Professional bodies including the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association have described Facilitated Communication as discredited because authorship could not be reliably assigned to the disabled person and facilitator influence was demonstrated under controlled conditions.

The consequences were not merely academic.

Messages generated through facilitated communication included allegations of abuse and major personal decisions. When authorship is uncertain, a method can accidentally give one person’s agency the appearance of another’s.

That history should make modern researchers more careful, not more cynical.

Newer spelling methods deserve evaluation according to their actual mechanics rather than guilt by resemblance alone. A person pointing independently to a board held by somebody else is not mechanically identical to a person whose wrist is being supported. Eye-tracking can reveal anticipatory selection. High-speed motion capture can quantify trajectories. Modern experimental control can blind partners to targets and log every trial. The question is empirically approachable.

But the history also teaches a principle that cannot be wished away: sincere subjective certainty about authorship is not enough.

The facilitator in an ideomotor system does not have to feel like an author.

This is one of the hardest facts for humans to accept about ourselves. We like our actions to arrive with labels. *I meant to do that.* *I did not move it.* *I was only helping.* *They chose it.* Introspection feels like privileged access to causal truth.

It is not always.

Small movements can be generated without conscious awareness of their origin. Expectations can bias perception and action. People can coordinate through feedback loops in which no participant experiences themselves as controlling the whole result. The classic Ouija board is an obvious cultural example, but the underlying point extends far beyond novelty séances. Ideomotor effects reveal that imagined or expected outcomes can influence motor behavior without a person experiencing a deliberate decision to produce the effect.

In a communication setting, this possibility has to be investigated delicately because the stakes are radically different.

A letterboard is not a toy.

The person pointing may be trying to tell us something important. The partner may have invested years in learning how to support that person. Families may have reorganized expectations and relationships around the resulting communication. Entering this space with a triumphant demonstration of unconscious influence would be both scientifically crude and personally destructive.

The experiment has to be built to discover independence too.

One of the most interesting pieces of modern evidence comes from a 2020 *Scientific Reports* study led by Vikram Jaswal. Nine experienced nonspeaking autistic letterboard users wore head-mounted eye trackers while answering novel questions. They pointed without physical support; an assistant held the board. The researchers reported that participants usually looked at target letters before pointing to them, often by roughly half a second, and that timing patterns resembled features of fluent spelling. The authors argued that these patterns made simple facilitator cueing unlikely for those participants in those sessions.

The result matters because gaze can reveal planning before contact.

If the eyes land on a letter before the finger moves there, it is harder to tell a story in which the board holder simply steers the hand to a letter the user has not selected. A predictive gaze sequence across successive letters suggests that the speller is contributing meaningful target information.

The result does not end the authorship debate.

The study deliberately observed familiar communication in situ rather than conducting controlled message-passing trials. The assistant holding the board was not prevented from knowing the conversational context or answers in the way required to establish independent authorship of novel hidden information. Nine experienced users are also a narrow sample, not a basis for universal claims about every person using a similar method.

Both sentences can be true at once: the gaze evidence is worth taking seriously, and stronger authorship tests are still required.

A later systematic review published in 2026 searched specifically for studies of Rapid Prompting Method, Spelling to Communicate, Spellers Method, and related variants that used research designs capable of empirically demonstrating authorship. It found no studies meeting its preregistered inclusion criteria. The authors called this an “empty review” and argued that the absence of controlled authorship validation is clinically significant.

That result is sometimes summarized too aggressively.

“No qualifying authorship studies were found” is not identical to “every individual response has been proven false.” An empty evidence base does not magically supply evidence for the opposite claim. What it does is place the burden where it belongs: before these methods are used to support consequential claims, independent authorship needs direct validation.

The fairest scientific program would combine the strengths of both approaches.

Start with kinematics and gaze because they tell us how the interaction works.

Then add message passing because it tells us who has the information.

A participant might first spell familiar material under their usual setup while eye and hand movements are recorded. Researchers can quantify anticipatory gaze, movement variability, board motion, prompt timing, and partner behavior. Then hidden-information trials begin.

The participant sees a novel target while the board holder does not.

The board holder sees a novel target while the participant does not.

Both see the same target.

Neither sees a target.

The experimenter in the room sees none of them.

The conditions are randomized automatically. The answers are captured automatically. No researcher can decide mid-session which trials “count.”

Now the movement data become more informative.

Suppose anticipatory gaze remains strong when only the participant knows the target and the spelled answer is correct. That is compelling evidence that the participant is selecting the content.

Suppose board motion begins predicting target letters specifically when the board holder knows the target and the participant does not. That is evidence of partner influence.

Suppose performance is correct only when participant and partner share the target. Then the dyad may require common knowledge to stabilize a jointly produced response.

Suppose some participants pass cleanly and others do not. That may be the most plausible outcome of all, because people and training histories differ.

Methods are often discussed as though validity has to be universal.

But authorship is ultimately an individual capability under specified conditions.

A person may begin with substantial support and later gain independent control. Another may remain dependent on a particular board motion. A third may communicate independently on some tasks but become more susceptible to partner influence under fatigue or stress. A fourth may never establish reliable symbolic output through that interface.

A binary culture war hides these trajectories.

Measurement can reveal them.

This individual approach also changes the telepathy question.

If a particular participant has already demonstrated independent authorship using a fixed device while everyone else is blind to the target, then a later telepathy study involving that participant starts from much stronger ground. The research team no longer has to wonder whether a communication partner supplied the answer. The communication channel has been validated separately.

That separation is almost absurdly important.

Without it, a telepathy test can accidentally become a facilitator-reading test.

Consider a common demonstration structure. A parent secretly looks at a number. The nonspeaking person spells the number while working with someone who may know what the parent saw or may be in visual contact with the parent. The result feels astonishing because no number was spoken.

But “not spoken” is not the same as “not signaled.”

The parent’s face, body orientation, gaze, breathing, reaction to candidate answers, or timing can provide information. The communication partner can provide information. The testing host can provide information. If any person who knows the target interacts with the receiver, the experiment contains a conventional route.

Closing those routes is not an insult to the participants. It is how the demonstration becomes something nobody can explain away.

A strong test is almost boring to watch.

The target is generated by software after isolation. The sender sees it alone. Nobody with the receiver knows it. The receiver enters an answer on a fixed device or through a previously validated support arrangement. The answer is timestamped before the sender’s room opens. There is no dramatic facial reaction because the researchers do not yet know whether it is right. Hundreds of trials accumulate. The analysis script was written before the first result.

Boredom is protective.

It prevents a room from becoming part of the signal.

The phrase *the room* deserves emphasis because humans constantly leak state.

Ask somebody to choose one of two cups while you secretly hope they choose the left one. You may look at it longer. Your shoulders may turn toward it. Your voice may change when you describe it. If they begin reaching right, you may inhale or hesitate. None of these signals has to be large enough to notice consciously. A receiver who has spent years adapting to subtle social cues may exploit them better than either participant can report.

This suggests an intriguing possibility for some experiences labeled telepathy.

What if certain nonspeaking people are unusually dependent on, or unusually practiced at, extracting low-level embodied information from familiar partners? Not supernatural mind reading. Not ordinary explicit communication either. A learned sensitivity to microtiming, movement, arousal, and prediction built through thousands of hours of interaction.

Such a system could feel telepathic from the inside.

If the receiver regularly knows what a familiar person is about to do before the person speaks, the experience is one of access. The underlying computation does not announce itself. Brains do not display a source list beside an intuition.

This possibility is not an explanation we are entitled to assume. It is a hypothesis with its own predictions.

It should weaken when embodied channels are removed.

It should depend on familiarity.

Performance should fall when the knowledgeable person is replaced by a blind intermediary.

Eye, posture, board, and timing variables should contain decodable information about the target.

A machine-learning model trained on those variables might predict choices above chance, revealing a channel the participants did not recognize consciously.

That would be a remarkable finding.

It would also prepare us for the more extraordinary one.

If all measurable embodied channels contain no target information, the facilitator is blind, the device is fixed, the sender is isolated, randomization is secure, and the receiver still identifies hidden targets reproducibly, then the ordinary room has been emptied of explanations.

Only then do we earn the right to ask what else is in it.

A finger, a board, and a second person can form a communication system.

Before calling the system telepathic, we have to learn where the information enters.
