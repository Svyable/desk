# The Hill Between Us

*Nonspeaking Minds, Inter-Brain Synchrony, and the Science That Could Test Telepathy*

© 2026 Sven Hardy Benson. All Rights Reserved.

There is a peculiar problem in writing about a claim that most scientists do not accept and some families believe they have watched happen in front of them.

If you begin with ridicule, you stop looking. If you begin with belief, you may stop testing. Both reactions can feel decisive while avoiding the harder work.

This book begins somewhere else.

It begins with a report.

Some nonspeaking autistic people who communicate by pointing to letters have described experiences they interpret as direct mind-to-mind communication. In stories circulating through families, practitioners, podcasts, and the wider community around spelling-based communication, one image has acquired unusual power. It is called the Hill.

The descriptions vary, as descriptions of private experience always do. The Hill is spoken of as a place where people meet without physically traveling there, a shared mental geography associated with other nonspeaking people, sometimes encountered in states described as dreamlike, meditative, or simply different from ordinary waking attention. It can sound, depending on the listener, like evidence of another layer of reality, a metaphor that hardened into a place, a shared imaginative construction, a lucid-dream landscape, a social mythology, or the beginning of a scientific anomaly.

We do not yet know which of those it is.

That sentence matters more than it appears to.

A mystery should not be protected by vagueness. The more extraordinary a proposed phenomenon becomes, the more specific its mechanism must eventually become. If one brain can obtain information contained only in another brain, and no ordinary sensory or technological channel carries the relevant content, then there is a physical problem hiding inside the word *telepathy*. Something must encode information. Something must propagate or couple. Something in the receiving organism must respond differently depending on what was sent. Noise must be overcome. The process must have a range, a speed, a bandwidth, a failure pattern, and some condition under which it weakens.

Nature is full of communication systems that once would have sounded fantastical to somebody who lacked the instrument to detect them. Bats produce ultrasound and reconstruct space from returning echoes. Sharks respond to weak electric fields. Migratory animals use environmental information humans do not consciously sense. Plants exchange chemical signals. Microbes coordinate behavior through molecules. Human bodies produce electrical activity, magnetic fields, heat, pressure waves, volatile compounds, movement, and changes in the surrounding environment.

None of those facts proves telepathy. They do something more useful: they show what a mechanism looks like when it becomes science.

The field generated by a brain is not the same thing as a message. Synchrony between two nervous systems is not the same thing as one nervous system reading the other. A person anticipating another person’s movement is not evidence that distance has disappeared. A shared dream image is not evidence that information crossed between sleeping brains. The world contains many forms of coupling. Most do not carry sentences.

This book therefore makes an unusual bargain with both believers and skeptics.

To the person who believes something important is happening, the bargain is this: the claim will be taken seriously enough to be made vulnerable. We will not protect it with words like *energy*, *frequency*, *quantum*, or *connection* unless those words are attached to quantities and mechanisms that can be tested. We will ask what should happen when vision is removed, when sound is masked, when facilitators do not know the target, when sender and receiver are separated, when timing is disrupted, when electromagnetic shielding is introduced, when the sender is absent, and when neither participant knows whether a real trial is occurring.

To the person who thinks the entire subject is nonsense, the bargain is different: dismissal is not allowed to substitute for measurement. Nonspeaking people are not intellectual blank spaces. Motor control, speech, language, attention, sensory regulation, and cognition do not map neatly onto one another. The inability to produce reliable speech does not tell us, by itself, what a person understands. Some people can demonstrate competence through independent augmentative communication even when speech is limited. The history of disability contains enough confident underestimation to make humility a scientific virtue rather than a sentimental one.

There is also a harder complication. Many of the telepathy claims discussed around nonspeaking “spellers” are entangled with communication methods that themselves remain scientifically disputed. Facilitated Communication has a long history of controlled authorship tests showing facilitator influence. Newer approaches such as Rapid Prompting Method and Spelling to Communicate differ in technique, and some users point without physical contact, but controlled authorship evidence remains a central unresolved problem. A 2026 systematic review searching specifically for studies capable of empirically demonstrating authorship in RPM, S2C, and related methods found none that met its preregistered criteria. A 2020 eye-tracking study of nine experienced letterboard users, meanwhile, found gaze patterns that the authors interpreted as evidence of agency: participants often looked toward letters before pointing to them, and their timing resembled fluent spelling.

Those findings do not cancel each other out because they answer different questions.

The eye-tracking result is interesting evidence about motor planning and selection in a small, specific group. It was not a blinded message-passing test in which the facilitator was prevented from knowing the answer. The systematic review’s empty result is evidence of a validation gap; it is not proof that every letter produced by every user is facilitator-generated. The scientifically responsible position is less satisfying than a verdict: authorship must be established under conditions appropriate to the individual before claims derived from the content of the messages can carry much weight.

This distinction will recur throughout the book. We will separate three questions that are often collapsed into one.

First: **Who authored the output?**

Second: **What information did the author have access to?**

Third: **How did that information arrive?**

A person can author a message and still learn its content through an ordinary channel that the experiment failed to notice. A person can receive genuinely novel information while expressing it through an imperfect interface. A facilitator can unintentionally influence an answer while sincerely believing no influence occurred. A participant can be highly sensitive to timing, posture, gaze, breathing, or expectation without either person consciously recognizing those cues. Human interaction is dense with information that never becomes explicit.

That density is where the physiological part of this story begins.

Two people sitting together are not two sealed skulls. They are organisms coupled through light, sound, air, vibration, heat, movement, smell, shared objects, shared history, and predictions built across thousands of prior interactions. Their pupils change. Their breathing changes. Their hands prepare movements before the movements occur. Their brains continually forecast what comes next. During real interaction, researchers can measure correlations and temporal alignment between people’s behavior, autonomic activity, and neural signals.

That is not telepathy. But it is a warning against pretending that the only alternatives are “ordinary speech” and “paranormal transfer.” Between those poles lies an enormous territory of subtle biological communication.

We will walk through it carefully.

We will examine vision at the edge of awareness, anticipatory gaze, motor preparation, proprioception, micro-movements, autonomic synchrony, predictive processing, shared priors, interpersonal entrainment, and the neuroscience of inter-brain synchrony. We will ask how much information these processes could plausibly carry and, just as important, what kind. A nervous system may be excellent at inferring *when* another organism is about to act while having no route for reading the hidden name of a randomly selected picture.

Then we will move outward, toward the physical candidates people reach for when ordinary channels seem insufficient. Brains generate electromagnetic fields. That is measurable. The magnetic fields recorded outside the head are extraordinarily weak; magnetoencephalography requires exquisitely sensitive equipment, often in magnetically shielded environments, to detect them. The existence of a field therefore poses a question rather than supplying an answer. Could such a field carry useful information across ordinary distances? Is there a biological receptor capable of extracting it? What signal-to-noise problem would have to be solved? If electromagnetic coupling matters, should a well-designed shield change performance?

We will also visit the quantum temptation, because any book on unexplained mind-to-mind phenomena eventually encounters it. Quantum mechanics is not a synonym for unknown. Biological systems are quantum systems in the trivial sense that all matter obeys quantum physics. A useful quantum proposal must identify a state, interaction, coherence requirement, measurement process, and experimental prediction that differs from classical alternatives. Mystery does not become mechanism merely by moving to a smaller scale.

And then we will return to the Hill.

A shared place is scientifically interesting even if no telepathy exists. Human beings build common geographies out of stories all the time. Religious landscapes, fictional worlds, recurring dream locations, childhood maps, online spaces, and remembered rooms can become socially shared without being physically shared. Language can align spatial imagery. Repetition can stabilize landmarks. Communities can converge on names and features. Memory is reconstructive, and stories told after experiences can reshape the experiences themselves.

But those ordinary explanations are testable too.

Imagine that two people who claim to meet on the Hill are isolated before a session. A computer privately assigns one of them a novel landmark that has never appeared in the community’s vocabulary: perhaps a blue glass arch, a copper ladder descending into fog, or one object selected from a standardized bank designed to minimize semantic overlap. The other participant does not see the assignment. Facilitators do not see it. Researchers interacting with the receiver do not see it. The sender is asked, in whatever independent way is suitable, to attend to the landmark. Later, both participants provide reports before any discussion occurs. Independent judges who do not know the target score the receiver’s report against the real target and several decoys.

One match means little. Repeated performance above a preregistered threshold, across participants, targets, days, and independent laboratories would mean more. Sender-absent trials would reveal expectation effects. Delayed trials could test whether timing matters. Distance could be varied. Known sensory channels could be blocked one at a time. Physiological measurements could determine whether any successful trial has a detectable precursor. If a signal survived all of that, the scientific question would stop being *whether anyone should take this seriously* and become *what kind of thing are we looking at?*

That experiment has not established telepathy. It is a description of what taking the claim seriously would look like.

There is a second reason to write the book this way.

The people at the center of this debate deserve better than being turned into symbols. Nonspeaking autistic people are routinely recruited into arguments other people want to have: about intelligence, disability, parenting, neuroscience, education, spirituality, fraud, miracles, technology, and the limits of science. A person can disappear inside a claim made on their behalf.

No result in this book gets to decide whether a life is meaningful.

If an authorship test fails, the response should be to improve access to valid communication, not to withdraw respect. If a telepathy test fails, it does not prove the participant was foolish, deceptive, or empty. People can sincerely interpret coincidence, synchrony, intuition, dreams, and subtle cues in ways that later prove mistaken. Scientists do it too. If a test succeeds, the result should not be converted overnight into a mythology that outruns the evidence. One reproducible anomaly would be the beginning of a research program, not the end of one.

The Hill is therefore a useful name for the problem itself.

From one side, the slope looks like credulity. From the other, it looks like arrogance. At the top is a place where claims have to survive contact with instruments.

That is where we are going.
