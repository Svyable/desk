# Chapter 1 — The Hill

There is no road to the Hill.

That is the first difficulty.

A road can be walked twice. Its turns can be measured. A stranger can be given directions and arrive without knowing why the destination matters. A surveyor can disagree with a traveler and both can return with instruments. A hill in the physical world is generous that way. It does not require belief to have a slope.

The Hill described by some nonspeaking autistic people who use spelling-based communication is a different kind of place. Accounts circulating through the community around *The Telepathy Tapes* describe it as a shared mental location or plane where nonspeaking people may encounter one another without ordinary physical travel. In some tellings it is associated with unusual states of consciousness. In others it is simply spoken of as though its existence needs less explanation than outsiders imagine.

The claim is startling because it combines two mysteries that are easy to confuse.

The first is communication. A person who does not reliably use speech may point to letters, type, use a speech-generating device, gesture, sign, or communicate through other forms of augmentative and alternative communication. If the output is genuinely theirs, language that was difficult to express through speech can become visible another way. There is nothing paranormal about that. Human cognition has never been required to leave the body through the mouth.

The second mystery begins when the content of a message appears to contain information the person should not have obtained through ordinary means. A hidden number. A word seen by somebody else. A thought that was not spoken. An experience apparently shared by two people who were not together. The Hill belongs to this second category because, taken literally, it implies more than private imagination. It implies access to a common place or common information without the usual path between people.

That is a much larger claim.

It deserves a larger experiment.

The most useful thing about the Hill, scientifically, is not that it is dramatic. It is that a place has structure.

A vague feeling of connection is difficult to test. People feel understood by friends, partners, parents, children, therapists, teammates, animals, and occasionally strangers. We complete one another’s sentences. We reach for the phone before it rings and remember the successes more vividly than the thousands of times no call followed. We can read a face before we can explain what changed in it. A person can enter a room and detect tension without knowing whether posture, silence, gaze, breathing, or prior knowledge supplied the clue.

A place is different. Places contain relations.

If the Hill is experienced as a shared location, it should in principle have properties that can be compared without requiring anyone to settle the metaphysics first. Does it have stable landmarks? Do different people place those landmarks in similar relations? Are there routes? Boundaries? Repeated objects? Other people? Does the reported geography stay consistent over time? Do two people report the same novel change before discussing it? Can one person introduce information that a second person later reports under conditions that prevent ordinary transmission?

The word *place* creates handles.

This is where an investigation can begin without deciding what it will find.

Suppose two participants, A and B, both say they can meet on the Hill. On Monday, before either interacts with the research team, a computer selects one target from a large bank of unfamiliar objects and scenes. The target is not simply a common symbol like a tree, river, house, or sun. Those are too easy to produce independently. It might be an object whose features can be scored: a red spiral staircase standing in shallow water, a silver cube with a circular opening, a yellow canoe suspended vertically, a stone arch containing three blue lights. The actual target and several carefully matched decoys are generated in advance by code, logged, and cryptographically committed so nobody can quietly change the answer after seeing the reports.

Participant A receives the target under controlled conditions. Participant B does not. The people assisting B do not. The researchers who later collect B’s report do not. Before any contact between A and B, each records a description. If spelling support is used, the support arrangement must first have passed a separate authorship procedure appropriate to that individual. Ideally B produces the report independently on a stationary device. If that is not possible, the facilitator is blind to the target and the target pool.

Then independent judges, also blind, compare B’s description with the real target and the decoys.

A single striking match would be fascinating and scientifically weak.

Why weak? Because the world contains coincidence, flexible interpretation, accidental cueing, target leakage, scoring bias, selective memory, and the human ability to make a broad description feel precise after the answer is known. If the target is a silver cube and B writes *bright room*, somebody who wants the effect to exist can find a path between them. If B writes *blue* and one decoy contains blue, scoring can become a negotiation. If ten pairs attempt the experiment and one produces an extraordinary resemblance, the nine failures can disappear in retelling.

A good design makes disappointment visible.

Before data collection, the researchers define what counts as success. The number of trials is fixed. The scoring system is fixed. Exclusions are fixed. All trials are retained. The target bank is archived. Sender-absent trials are mixed in, so B sometimes goes through the same procedure when nobody received a target. On other trials, A sees a target but is instructed not to attend to it until later. Some trials use prerecorded sessions to separate the importance of real-time interaction from expectation. If distance is claimed not to matter, distance can be varied. If sleep or meditation is important, state can be measured rather than merely named.

This would not drain the Hill of meaning. It would give the claim somewhere to stand.

The objection often raised at this point is that laboratory conditions may destroy the phenomenon. Perhaps anxiety interferes. Perhaps unfamiliar people interfere. Perhaps the Hill is entered only in trust. Perhaps being watched changes the state. Perhaps the demand to perform is itself the wrong mental posture.

All of those possibilities can be respected without making the claim untestable.

Science routinely studies phenomena that are state-dependent. Sleep laboratories do not conclude that sleep is fictional because the first night in the lab is strange. Sexual physiology, social bonding, pain, meditation, skilled performance, animal behavior, and memory can all change under observation. Researchers respond by improving ecological validity, acclimating participants, using home measurements, comparing states, and measuring the conditions under which the effect appears.

If trust matters, measure trust. If familiarity matters, compare familiar and unfamiliar pairs. If a particular attentional state matters, identify behavioral or physiological markers associated with it. If the effect emerges only after twenty quiet minutes, preregister that interval. If the laboratory suppresses it entirely but home trials produce it, create home protocols with concealed targets and automated timing so experimental control travels to the participant rather than forcing the participant into an alien room.

A phenomenon can be fragile without being exempt from causality.

The Hill also raises a subtler question. What would it mean for two people to “meet” there?

Human beings share places without occupying the same coordinates in many ordinary ways. Two siblings can independently picture the kitchen of a childhood home that no longer exists. Readers can carry remarkably similar maps of a fictional school, spaceship, or city after reading the same book. Members of a religious tradition can imagine a sacred geography described across years of teaching. Online communities inhabit persistent spaces made of pixels and rules. Friends can invent a place in conversation and return to it as a private joke until it acquires emotional reality.

Dreams complicate the distinction further. People sometimes revisit locations that exist only in dreams. A recurring dream city can feel stable even when its geography would not survive a survey. Memory supplies continuity by reconstruction. We are not passive cameras replaying exact prior states; each act of remembering rebuilds from traces, expectations, schemas, and current context. A dream landscape can therefore feel like a place one has returned to even if details shift.

Social transmission can make private places converge.

One person mentions a hill. Another later dreams of one. A third hears both descriptions and supplies a tree. Over months the shared story acquires landmarks. Nobody needs to fabricate anything. Each person can sincerely experience the resulting imagery as personal and spontaneous because people are poor archivists of the origins of every component in memory. Ideas migrate through conversation, videos, posts, family stories, therapy sessions, community language, and repeated retellings.

That ordinary process is a serious competitor to a telepathic explanation of the Hill.

It is also testable.

Researchers could document the vocabulary and imagery circulating in the community before testing begins. Reports could be timestamped privately before participants hear one another’s descriptions. Novel landmarks could be introduced to one participant only after baseline maps have been collected. Pairs who have never met and do not share the same facilitator network could be compared with close friends. A landmark first reported by one person could be traced forward through the social network to see whether ordinary exposure predicts later appearances.

If the Hill is primarily a socially shared imaginative world, those transmission paths should matter.

If something stranger is happening, the most interesting data would be details that outrun those paths.

This distinction between *shared world* and *shared information* will matter throughout the book. People can share a world by receiving the same inputs. A stadium crowd can roar in synchrony because everyone sees the same goal. Two musicians can align timing because each hears the other. Two brains watching the same movie can show related patterns of activity because the movie drives both. A parent and child can settle into coordinated physiology through touch, gaze, routine, and emotion.

None of that requires one brain to transmit hidden semantic content directly to another.

To demonstrate something closer to telepathy, the receiver has to obtain information that is novel, specific, and unavailable through ordinary shared inputs.

The word *novel* matters. If two people already know the Hill is green, agreeing on green tells us little.

The word *specific* matters. If one person reports “a bright thing” and another was shown a yellow sun, the match has too much interpretive room.

The word *unavailable* matters most. If a facilitator, camera operator, parent, researcher, device, reflected surface, audio channel, prior conversation, or trial sequence can reveal the target, then the experiment has not isolated the phenomenon it claims to measure.

These requirements may sound severe. They are severe because humans are extraordinary cue detectors.

A dog can respond to a handler’s unconscious posture. A person can infer which elevator will arrive from the faint sound of machinery. A poker player can notice timing without knowing exactly what was noticed. A child can learn the answer a teacher expects from minute changes in voice. Skilled partners build predictive models of each other that make coordination look effortless.

Those abilities are not fake alternatives to telepathy. They are remarkable forms of biological intelligence in their own right.

Consider what happens before somebody speaks a familiar sentence. Their chest prepares air. Their face and gaze shift. Their posture changes. A conversational partner already knows the topic, the relationship, the grammar, and thousands of probabilities about what words tend to follow. The receiving brain does not wait for a complete acoustic package. It predicts and updates continuously.

Now replace ordinary speech with a letterboard interaction involving people who have practiced together for months or years. One person is pointing. Another may be holding or moving the board, prompting pace, offering encouragement, repositioning the surface, tracking spelling, and anticipating likely continuations. Even without touch, the system is richly coupled. Tiny regularities can become useful. Neither person needs to know consciously which regularities are doing the work.

That is why the Hill cannot be investigated responsibly by jumping directly from an impressive spelling event to a theory of disembodied minds.

We first need to understand the room.

We need to know who knew the target. Where everyone was looking. What moved. What could be heard. How the board was held. Whether the receiver’s gaze led the pointing movement. Whether an assistant’s movement predicted the next letter. Whether the response survives when the assistant is blind. Whether it survives on a stationary board. Whether it survives with a different communication partner. Whether it survives when nobody present knows the answer.

These are not accusations. They are measurements of the system that exists before any unknown system is proposed.

A surprising result that survives them becomes more surprising.

This is the part of skepticism that is sometimes misunderstood. Proper skepticism does not make an anomaly smaller. It strips away the explanations that would make the anomaly ordinary. Every channel you successfully close increases the informational burden on whatever remains.

Imagine a receiver identifying one of four hidden images at 70 percent accuracy across hundreds of preregistered trials. If the facilitator knows the image, the result may be interesting evidence about dyadic cueing. If nobody in the room knows it, the experimental question changes. If the sender is in another room with no electronic connection, it changes again. If sender-absent trials fall to chance while sender-present trials remain high, timing matters. If a Faraday enclosure has no effect but temporal delay destroys performance, one class of mechanisms becomes less likely and another becomes more interesting. If the effect vanishes when visual contact with the facilitator is removed even though the facilitator does not know the target, the facilitator may be part of a state-regulation loop rather than a semantic source.

Each manipulation turns mystery into shape.

That is how a pathway is discovered.

The Hill offers one more advantage: it asks for mapping rather than mind reading.

Mind reading is an intoxicating phrase because it suggests unlimited access. Real communication systems are rarely unlimited. They have bandwidth. A channel might carry one binary decision per minute and still be revolutionary. It might carry arousal but not nouns. It might transmit timing but not identity. It might work only between trained pairs. It might depend on proximity. It might be asymmetric. It might be noisy enough that meaning emerges only across many repetitions.

A spatial task can reveal these constraints.

Instead of asking, “Can you read what I am thinking?” ask whether one participant can transmit one randomly selected feature into a shared map. Left or right. Near or far. One landmark out of four. A route with two turns. The location of an object in a grid. These tasks create information quantities that can be measured without pretending the mind is a file server.

If performance is above chance, increase the target set. Increase distance. Add delay. Change partners. Reduce shared context. Vary sensory barriers. See where the signal breaks.

Failure is useful because every channel has a cliff somewhere.

Ordinary vision fails when photons no longer reach the eye. Speech fails when the signal-to-noise ratio collapses. Touch fails across distance. Wi-Fi loses throughput with attenuation and interference. A biological telepathy mechanism, if one exists, should not be uniquely free of constraints. Even quantum correlations, so often invoked casually around consciousness, do not provide a magical classical messaging service that ignores the structure of physics.

A real Hill would have weather.

Not literal rain, necessarily. Conditions. Good nights and bad ones. Distances that matter or do not. Pairings that work better or worse. States that increase or decrease accuracy. Some kind of relation between the strength of the phenomenon and the variables that produce it.

Without such structure, the claim remains difficult to distinguish from story.

With structure, it becomes research.

There is an ethical reason to want that distinction.

Families of nonspeaking people often live with enormous uncertainty about what the person understands, what they want, how much control they have over movement, and how to provide reliable access to communication. A method that appears to unlock language can change family life. It can change educational expectations, legal decisions, consent, medical choices, relationships, and the person’s sense of self. Claims of telepathy add another layer of consequence.

If the output is authentic, dismissing it can silence someone.

If the output is being influenced by another person, treating it as unquestionably authentic can also silence someone.

The only humane response is to become better at finding out.

That means designing authorship tests that do not feel like traps. It means giving participants practice, regulating sensory demands, preserving preferred communication supports where they do not compromise target concealment, and separating the question of method validity from the question of human worth. It means acknowledging that a person can fail a laboratory task for many reasons while also refusing to declare every failure irrelevant.

Good experiments are not courtroom cross-examinations. They are instruments for separating possibilities.

The Hill deserves that instrument.

Perhaps it will turn out to be a remarkable shared imaginative culture among people whose communication communities have been poorly understood. Perhaps careful mapping will reveal how stories, dreams, expectations, and relationships create common mental geography. That would be worth knowing.

Perhaps physiological measurements will show that certain pairs enter unusually coordinated states, with breathing, movement, arousal, and neural timing aligning in ways that help explain intense feelings of connection without transferring hidden semantic content. That would be worth knowing too.

Perhaps the central claims will collapse when facilitators are blinded or ordinary channels are controlled. That result would be uncomfortable for some people and important for protecting the autonomy of nonspeaking communicators.

Or perhaps, after all of that, a residue will remain: repeatable transfer of novel information under conditions in which nobody with access to the receiver knows the target and ordinary sensory transmission has been blocked.

If that happens, the scientific response should not be applause.

It should be replication.

Then isolation.

Then perturbation.

Then mechanism.

The Hill would stop being a story science was asked to believe. It would become a set of observations science was required to explain.

We are not there.

But there is a path from here to there.

It begins by refusing to teleport over the difficult parts.
