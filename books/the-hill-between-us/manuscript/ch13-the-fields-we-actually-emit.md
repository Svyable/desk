# Chapter 13 — The Fields We Actually Emit

Your brain is electrical.

That sentence is true and almost useless by itself.

Every action potential depends on ions moving across cell membranes. Populations of neurons create electrical currents. Those currents produce electric and magnetic fields. Electroencephalography measures voltage differences at the scalp. Magnetoencephalography measures extraordinarily weak magnetic fields produced by coordinated neural activity.

Nothing supernatural is required for a brain to produce fields outside the skull.

The difficult question is whether those fields can do anything to another brain at ordinary interpersonal distances.

The distinction between *detectable* and *biologically communicative* is where many telepathy arguments become slippery.

A laboratory instrument can detect a signal that another nervous system cannot. A field can exist without carrying enough target-specific information to matter. A signal can carry information in principle but attenuate too rapidly for a receiver to recover it through environmental noise. A receiver can be physically exposed to a field without possessing any receptor capable of transducing it into useful neural activity.

Existence is the beginning of the mechanism, not the end.

Consider magnetoencephalography.

MEG systems use ultrasensitive magnetic sensors positioned around the head. The National Institute of Mental Health describes SQUID sensors in its MEG facility as detecting brain-generated fields outside the head on the order of 10^-14 tesla. These measurements are difficult enough that MEG systems are commonly operated in magnetically controlled environments to reduce interference from much larger ambient magnetic sources.

A successful telepathic magnetic channel would have to solve the inverse problem of the instrument.

Instead of a large array of cryogenic or otherwise specialized sensors sitting millimeters or centimeters from the scalp, another human body would have to detect a weak, changing field at a distance and convert relevant differences into neural information.

What tissue does that?

Where is it?

What frequencies does it respond to?

What field strengths?

How does it reject the Earth’s magnetic field, electrical infrastructure, vehicles, electronics, and the receiver’s own physiological magnetic activity?

How does it distinguish one sender from another?

How is a word encoded?

These questions are not intended to ridicule the hypothesis.

They are what makes it a hypothesis.

The first step would be to measure the sender field under telepathy-task conditions.

Suppose a sender alternates between two well-controlled mental states corresponding to target A and target B. MEG or another appropriate magnetometer can test whether external field patterns differ reliably between the states. They almost certainly will in some statistical sense if the mental tasks differ enough, because brain activity differs across tasks.

But that does not establish a broadcast code.

The relevant question is whether the discriminating features remain physically available at the location of the receiver with sufficient signal-to-noise ratio.

Move the sensor away from the head.

Ten centimeters.

Half a meter.

One meter.

Several meters.

Measure how target-decoding performance changes.

If a laboratory instrument cannot reliably distinguish target states at the receiver’s position under the same environmental conditions, a biological detector would need extraordinary sensitivity or a different feature of the field than the instrument was measuring.

That possibility can be investigated, but it cannot be assumed.

Electric fields face similar questions.

Neural activity generates potentials measurable at the scalp because conductive tissues and electrodes form a circuit. The electric environment outside a body is affected by skin, clothing, grounding, static charge, mains fields, electronics, humidity, and geometry. A receiver separated in space is not connected to the sender as an EEG amplifier is.

If external electric coupling is proposed, the experiment should measure it directly.

Place high-impedance sensors near the receiver. Characterize environmental fields. Compare sender-present and sender-absent trials. Change grounding. Change distance. Insert conductive shielding. Rotate bodies. Ask whether any target-specific external variable survives.

Then ask whether manipulating that variable changes behavior.

This last step is crucial.

Correlation with a field is not evidence the field carries the effect unless perturbing the field changes the effect.

A sender’s neural state can correlate with thousands of bodily variables. If hidden-target accuracy happens to occur at the same time, several correlates will appear. Mechanism requires intervention.

Shield the candidate field while preserving other conditions.

Artificially reproduce it without the sender if possible.

Add controlled noise.

Invert or delay the signal.

Change orientation.

A real carrier should respond to at least some manipulations predicted by its physics.

This is where electromagnetic hypotheses have an advantage over vague nonphysical proposals.

Fields can be measured.

Materials interact with them in known ways.

Frequency spectra can be characterized.

Attenuation can be quantified.

Noise can be injected.

A magnetic telepathy hypothesis is therefore risky enough to become science.

If a Faraday enclosure has no effect, that does not rule out every form of electromagnetic coupling because different shields affect electric and magnetic fields differently across frequency ranges. A casual experiment with aluminum foil tells us little. The enclosure has to be characterized for the frequency and field type under consideration.

But a serious shield can still generate information.

Suppose behavioral transfer drops sharply only when low-frequency magnetic shielding is introduced, while acoustic, visual, and procedural conditions remain unchanged. That would be striking.

Suppose transfer is completely unaffected across shielding conditions that reduce the candidate field far below baseline. The field hypothesis weakens.

Suppose accuracy falls with distance according to the same profile as measured field strength. Stronger.

Suppose accuracy does not change from one meter to a thousand kilometers. A local weak-field mechanism becomes difficult to defend unless some other propagation process is proposed.

Distance is physics asking for a receipt.

The same is true of orientation.

Many field interactions depend on geometry. If a hypothetical receptor is directional, facing the sender may matter. If orientation never matters across rigorous tests, directional field models lose support.

The brain’s own field spectrum also places constraints on bandwidth.

Neural oscillations are often discussed in frequency bands—delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma and so on—but these labels summarize aspects of population activity. They are not carrier stations labeled with thoughts. An alpha rhythm does not become *orchid* when someone thinks of an orchid.

To transmit content, the sender would need target-dependent modulations that survive outside the head and a receiver capable of extracting them.

One could search for such modulations without presuming what they look like.

Train a decoder on sender MEG to distinguish targets. Identify which spatial and spectral features support decoding. Then estimate the external field those features create at increasing distances. Use physical sensors at receiver positions to determine whether they remain measurable.

Now compare successful telepathy trials, if any, with the strength of those features.

Does behavior depend on trials where the target-specific external field is stronger?

If yes, the candidate gains credibility.

If no, look elsewhere.

There is a second electromagnetic possibility that avoids ordinary radiation across the room: indirect environmental coupling.

A body may alter local electric fields, capacitance, static potential, or conductive pathways through shared furniture or grounding. Devices can couple through power lines. Sensors can pick up mains interference modulated by movement. Two people touching the same conductive object can share signals.

These are exactly the kinds of effects that can produce strange correlations in instrumentation.

They are known physics, not telepathy, but an experiment could accidentally interpret them as a direct brain-brain relationship.

A high-quality study therefore measures the environment as aggressively as the brains.

Put electrodes and magnetometers on empty chairs.

Run sham sessions without participants.

Swap sensor cables.

Place participants in separate electrical environments.

Record reference channels that cannot contain neural signals but can capture ambient noise.

If “inter-brain synchrony” appears between two saline phantoms or empty sensor rigs, the building is talking.

The phrase *the building is talking* is less glamorous than telepathy.

It is often more useful.

There is also the possibility of biological sensitivity to magnetic fields through mechanisms unrelated to direct neural-field detection. Some animals show magnetoreceptive behavior, and serious scientific work explores candidate mechanisms. That fact is sometimes used as a shortcut: if one organism can detect magnetic fields, perhaps humans can detect another brain’s fields.

The inference is too large.

A receptor adapted to environmental magnetic information does not automatically have the sensitivity, bandwidth, spatial resolution, or neural wiring required to decode another brain. The field strengths and signal structures differ. The existence of magnetoreception somewhere in biology demonstrates biological possibility of magnetic sensing in general, not human telepathy in particular.

Still, the comparison suggests experiments.

If humans possess an underappreciated magnetic sensitivity relevant to telepathy, performance should depend on controlled magnetic manipulations even in the absence of a sender. Expose participants, safely and within established research norms, to well-characterized fields that mimic candidate sender signatures. Can they detect presence versus absence? Can they discriminate two patterns? Does performance exceed chance under blinding?

This test separates receptor plausibility from the complexity of two-person transfer.

If humans cannot detect a much stronger artificial version of the proposed signal, detecting the weaker natural version becomes harder to explain.

If some participants can detect it reliably, the receptor question becomes interesting in its own right.

The artificial-signal principle applies beyond electromagnetism.

Whenever a natural telepathy carrier is proposed, amplify it.

If the signal is acoustic, reproduce it through a speaker below ordinary conscious thresholds and test discrimination.

If mechanical, reproduce vibration.

If chemical, deliver controlled concentrations.

If thermal, reproduce temperature patterns.

A receptor that supposedly detects a weak natural signal should usually respond to a stronger controlled version unless the hypothesis includes a specific nonlinearity or coding requirement.

This is how physiology can be challenged directly.

The field hypothesis also clarifies why “brainwaves” is a dangerous word in popular explanations.

EEG traces look like waves, but the brain is not a radio tower sending finished thoughts into the room. EEG is a measurement of summed electrical activity shaped by volume conduction, electrode placement, reference choices, and neural population dynamics. The waveform becomes meaningful only through a measurement apparatus and an analysis model.

MEG is similar. The magnetic field is real. The leap from field to readable thought is enormous.

Engineered brain-computer interfaces illustrate the scale of that leap.

Decoding a simple intended choice from EEG usually requires electrodes, signal processing, calibration, repeated trials, and constrained tasks. Even with hardware directly on the sender, extracting reliable information from neural activity is hard.

A natural receiver would need to perform comparable discrimination on a vastly weaker signal after it crossed space—and then inject the decoded information into its own neural processing.

Nature sometimes solves problems engineers find difficult.

But nature leaves anatomy and behavior behind when it does.

Bats have ears and neural systems adapted to echolocation. Electric fish have specialized receptors. Eyes contain photoreceptors. If humans possess a telepathic receptor, we should eventually be able to find its biological footprint.

Which tissue is necessary?

Do lesions disrupt the ability?

Does age change it?

Does the receptor adapt with sustained stimulation?

Can pharmacology alter it safely?

Does genetics matter?

Does the response appear in peripheral nerves before cortex?

These questions move the story from “the brain emits energy” to a research program in sensory physiology.

The Hill makes the field hypothesis emotionally attractive because distance seems to disappear there.

But if Hill meetings occur over very long distances with no decline, weak local electromagnetic fields become a poor fit unless the model includes an additional propagation process. That is not fatal to the larger telepathy claim. It is evidence against one proposed carrier.

We should want hypotheses to die at that level of specificity.

Killing the wrong mechanism protects the anomaly from being tied to bad physics.

There is a discipline in saying: the brain produces this field, we measured it, we know roughly how it behaves, and it does not appear capable of doing the proposed job under these conditions.

There is equal discipline in saying: the field changes with the target, remains detectable where the receiver sits, the receiver can discriminate an artificial version, and shielding removes behavioral transfer.

If that second sequence ever occurred reproducibly, skepticism would have to move.

Not because “energy is mysterious.”

Because a carrier had begun to identify itself.

The fields we emit are real.

The question is whether any of them are speaking.
