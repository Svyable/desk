# Chapter 23 — The Clever Hans Problem

A horse once taught experimental psychology a lesson it has not finished learning.

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Clever Hans became famous for apparently answering arithmetic and other questions by tapping a hoof. The striking part of the story is not that the horse was stupid. He was not. The striking part is that he had learned a problem different from the one the audience thought he was solving.

Hans did not need to calculate the sum in the experimenter’s head. He needed to detect when the experimenter’s body expected the tapping to stop.

Tiny changes in posture and expression were enough.

That distinction still matters because people often imagine cueing as an obvious act. A raised eyebrow. A finger pointing toward the answer. A whispered hint. If nobody remembers giving such a cue, they conclude no cue existed.

The Clever Hans problem is harder than that.

A cue can be small enough that neither person can describe it, reliable enough that another organism can learn it, and information-bearing enough to produce an answer that looks as if it came from somewhere else.

The cue does not need to be deceitful.

It does not even need to be conscious.

That is why the problem belongs near the center of any serious study of telepathy involving assisted communication.

Suppose a communication partner holds a letterboard. The partner knows the answer to a question. The person spelling moves a finger toward one of several letters. During that movement the board can rotate by a fraction, rise or fall, slow, retreat, or become more stable. The partner’s breathing can change. Their grip can change. Their voice can become more or less encouraging. Their next prompt can arrive a little sooner. Their body may tense when the finger approaches an unexpected letter and relax when it approaches an expected one.

None of these changes has to be large.

None has to be planned.

The person pointing may be exquisitely practiced at using exactly those dynamics to control a difficult motor task.

Now reverse the causal story.

The same small movements can also help the speller compensate for motor difficulty without selecting the semantic content. A board holder may stabilize the target plane, maintain distance, re-present letters after a missed reach, or keep the interaction within a learned rhythm while the speller chooses the letters. Observing two bodies influencing one another therefore does not settle authorship either way.

The question is not whether influence exists.

In a dyad, influence is almost guaranteed.

The question is which variables carry the information that determines the message.

This is why a video can be persuasive and still be insufficient.

We are poor at recovering causality from fluent interaction. Once a sequence looks smooth, the mind tends to assign agency according to the visible goal: the finger lands on letters, therefore the finger chose them; the partner never touches the hand, therefore the partner could not have contributed to selection; the partner looks away, therefore visual cueing is impossible.

But a coupled motor system can distribute control in ways that are not obvious from appearance.

A sailboat is steered by a rudder, but wind, keel, current, trim, and hull shape all influence the path. Watching the rudder alone does not tell you which force supplied the information about where to go.

The appropriate experiment changes who knows the destination.

This is the logic behind message-passing tests in facilitated communication research. If only the communicator sees one target and only the facilitator sees another, the output reveals whose knowledge it tracks. When controlled studies repeatedly found messages corresponding to facilitator knowledge rather than the disabled person’s private target, the result was not that unconscious influence must be obvious. It was the opposite. It showed that sincere participants could produce compelling interactions while causal control resided somewhere other than they believed.

The history is uncomfortable because the stakes were not merely academic. Facilitated messages were at times treated as testimony, accusation, preference, educational achievement, and evidence of hidden competence. When authorship is uncertain, the output can carry consequences far beyond the laboratory.

That history creates two temptations today.

One is to pretend it never happened because newer spelling practices differ in mechanics.

The other is to assume the old result has already answered every question about every newer practice and every individual user.

Both are shortcuts.

A method with a handheld board and no physical support at the wrist is not mechanically identical to classical facilitated communication. A person who later types independently presents a different authorship problem from a person who can spell only with a familiar partner holding a moving board. Eye movements that systematically precede pointing can contain evidence about motor planning that a crude message-passing test misses. Technique, access needs, training history, and individual ability matter.

But none of those differences removes the Clever Hans problem by declaration.

If another person knows the target and participates physically or procedurally in producing the response, the target can leak through interaction unless the experiment prevents it.

The prevention is conceptually simple.

The person interacting with the receiver cannot know the answer.

Not “tries not to reveal it.”

Cannot know it.

That change is so powerful because it converts a psychological promise into an information barrier.

A facilitator who does not know whether the hidden image is a lighthouse or a violin cannot unconsciously steer toward the correct word. Their micro-movements may still influence the motor path, but those movements cannot contain target identity unless the target has reached them through some other route.

The same principle applies to researchers.

If the experimenter in the receiver’s room knows the target, their body becomes part of the communication apparatus whether they intend it or not. If the analyst knows which trials are supposed to be successful while deciding how to clean the data, the analysis itself can become a route. If a parent receives live feedback from another room, their reaction can create a cue. If a camera operator knows when the sender begins concentrating, the rhythm of the session can leak timing.

Blinding is not a ritual performed to satisfy skeptical reviewers.

It is channel engineering.

The Clever Hans problem becomes more interesting when we stop treating it merely as an error.

Hans was detecting real information.

His ability says something impressive about animal learning, sensitivity to human movement, and the density of social signals. The mistake belonged to the interpretation of the channel.

That possibility should be taken seriously with nonspeaking people too.

A person who has spent years communicating through close bodily coordination may learn predictive features of another person that ordinary speakers barely notice. They may use timing, board angle, muscle tension, gaze, breath, prosody, and routine with remarkable precision. They may predict the next prompt before it is spoken. They may exploit a partner’s stabilization in ways neither could explain explicitly.

Calling all of that “just cueing” would miss the biology.

It may represent a sophisticated form of interpersonal control.

The important scientific question is how much information it can carry and where that information originates.

Imagine instrumenting the spelling interaction as if it were a robot-control problem.

Place motion markers on the board, hand, wrist, elbow, shoulder, head, and torso. Record high-speed video. Track both people’s eyes. Measure grip force on the board. Put force sensors under the board holder’s fingers. Record respiration, speech audio, and the precise timing of every prompt. If appropriate and comfortable, record muscle activity from relevant limbs. Synchronize all streams to the millisecond.

Now manipulate knowledge.

On some trials only the speller knows the answer.

On some only the partner knows.

On some both know.

On some neither knows and the response must be guessed.

A machine-learning classifier can then ask an unusually revealing question: before the speller commits to a letter, can the partner’s behavior predict which letter will be selected?

Prediction alone would not prove partner authorship. The partner could be responding to early movement generated by the speller. Timing matters.

Does the speller’s gaze move first?

Does the hand trajectory begin first?

Does the board rotate first?

Does partner muscle activity precede the speller’s directional commitment?

Does the causal order change depending on who knows the answer?

This turns an argument about appearances into a temporal map.

If speller gaze reliably lands near the eventual target before any target-discriminating partner movement, that strengthens the case that the speller is planning the selection. If partner-only knowledge predicts board motion before the speller’s eye or hand commits, the opposite concern grows. If both streams change together within measurement error, the experiment needs better temporal resolution or a perturbation.

Perturbation is the next step.

A mechanism becomes clearer when you disturb it.

Keep the same partner but mount the letterboard on a rigid stand.

Keep the handheld board but mask the partner from the target.

Keep the geometry but randomize the board’s letter positions between trials after the partner can no longer see them.

Reduce verbal prompts.

Insert controlled delays.

Change partner.

Use an eye-gaze interface that removes the board holder’s motion while preserving the alphabetic task.

Move gradually toward independent typing if that is physically accessible.

Performance profiles across these conditions tell us more than a single pass/fail label.

Suppose a person spells accurately with a familiar handheld board, remains accurate when the partner is target-blind, but performance falls when the board is fixed to a stand. That pattern suggests the moving board is providing useful access support without necessarily carrying semantic answers.

Suppose accuracy remains high only when the partner knows the target, regardless of whether physical support is present. That pattern points toward informational influence.

Suppose performance transfers to independent eye gaze or typing. The authorship question changes dramatically because the communication channel no longer depends on another person’s moment-to-moment movement.

Suppose results vary with fatigue and motor state but not partner knowledge. That is a motor-access story, not an authorship failure.

The point is not to design one test everybody must pass.

It is to identify the causal dependency for each person.

There is a parallel problem in telepathy experiments that do not use spelling at all.

Human beings can cue one another through experiment structure. A sender may breathe differently on target trials. A receiver may hear a faint relay click when a stimulus appears. A researcher may linger outside the room longer for one condition. A randomization system may produce visible metadata. A wireless device may emit a different pattern. Participants may infer trial type from the sequence because the randomization is constrained.

Every hidden-target study therefore has a Clever Hans layer even when no facilitator exists.

The laboratory can become Hans’s questioner.

The target can leak through the building.

This is why sender-absent controls are so useful. Run the entire apparatus exactly as if a sender were present, including target generation and stimulus display, but leave the sender room empty. If the receiver still performs above chance, something other than a human mind is carrying information.

That result would be embarrassing only if the experimenter cared more about the telepathy claim than about finding the channel.

A good laboratory would celebrate it.

The mystery got smaller.

The history of ideomotor effects makes sincerity especially important here. People can make small movements that reflect expectations without experiencing those movements as intentional. In controlled work on facilitated communication-like tasks, participants have produced answers based on information available to themselves while attributing the output to another person. The lesson is not that humans are gullible in some contemptible way. It is that conscious access to the causes of our own action is incomplete.

You can believe you are merely holding something steady while your muscles participate in steering it.

You can believe you are following a movement while your body helps initiate it.

You can believe you detected another person’s intention when you detected your own prediction becoming action.

None of these possibilities is a moral failure.

They are reasons to measure.

The same courtesy should be extended in both directions. A parent who reports hundreds of uncanny episodes should not be accused of fraud merely because ordinary channels remain possible. A critic who asks for blinding is not thereby denying the nonspeaking person’s mind. A communication practitioner can be completely sincere and still be wrong about causal control. A scientist can be correct about a confound and still be wrong about a person’s cognitive capacity.

Several propositions can coexist.

That is why dignity cannot substitute for validation, and validation cannot be treated as an attack on dignity.

There is a deeper reason the Clever Hans problem belongs in a book about physiobiological pathways.

If the ordinary channel turns out to be extraordinarily subtle, it may reveal something genuinely new about human coordination.

Consider a dyad that has practiced together for years. The receiver may learn a personalized sensorium of the partner: minute board accelerations, respiratory phase, vocal onset, gaze timing, movement stiffness, and error-correction rhythm. The information may be integrated below verbal awareness. To outsiders, the resulting speed could look impossible. To the nervous system, it may be a learned control policy.

This is not telepathy under the operational definition used in this book because the information travels through measurable sensory and motor channels.

It is still a remarkable biological achievement.

And it offers predictions.

Disrupt the channel and performance should change.

Add mechanical jitter to the board.

Delay visual feedback by a few hundred milliseconds.

Mask respiratory sound.

Change partner posture.

Swap to a board whose inertia differs.

If a particular perturbation causes a selective collapse, it identifies part of the coordination system.

A telepathy claim should survive these ordinary explanations only after they have been allowed to become as sophisticated as the behavior demands.

There is no scientific virtue in setting up a weak skeptic made of obvious cues and then defeating it.

The alternative explanation deserves its best version.

So does the anomaly.

Suppose all of this is done.

The receiver’s output is independently authored under target-blind support. Partner kinematics do not contain the target. Auditory and visual leakage are controlled. The apparatus passes sender-absent tests. Random targets are generated after isolation. The receiver continues to identify target-specific information above chance across many trials and a second laboratory reproduces the effect.

At that point, saying “Clever Hans” would no longer be an explanation.

It would be a historical reflex.

The ordinary channel had been tested and failed to pay the bill.

That is exactly what believers should want.

A phenomenon becomes harder to dismiss each time a strong alternative is given a fair chance and loses.

It is also what skeptics should want.

If the effect disappears when knowledge is separated correctly, nobody needs a theory of brain-to-brain fields to explain the result.

The test has done real work either way.

The horse did not embarrass science by reading body language.

Science embarrassed itself only when it asked the wrong question of what the horse was doing.

The lesson is not to distrust extraordinary performers.

It is to identify the information available to them.

Before we ask how a thought crossed a room, we should know what else crossed with it.
