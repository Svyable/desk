# Chapter 29 — The Hill Challenge

A strange claim deserves a beautiful test.

Not a complicated test for the sake of theater. A beautiful test is one in which the result becomes difficult to misunderstand.

The Hill offers that possibility because it turns an expansive metaphysical report into a spatial information problem.

People who describe meeting there often speak as if the Hill has some continuity: a place can be revisited; people can be encountered; features can recur. If that experience also permits information to move between minds, then a new feature placed into the experience by one participant should sometimes become available to another participant who had no ordinary access to it.

The challenge is to make “new” genuinely new.

That begins long before the first target trial.

Recruit participants who already report Hill experiences rather than teaching the idea during the study. Document their descriptions independently and privately. Ask them to map recurring features, names, routes, landmarks, people, colors, weather, buildings, vegetation, and any conventions they associate with the place.

The point is not to decide which descriptions are true.

It is to build a contamination map.

Anything already circulating in the participants’ shared culture cannot later count as a novel transfer.

If everyone has heard about a tree at the top, two reports of the tree are not evidence of mind-to-mind communication.

If several families have discussed a blue house, the blue house belongs to the prior.

If an image appeared on a podcast, Discord server, private group, conference slide, therapist handout, or family text thread, it belongs to the prior too.

The target bank must live outside that vocabulary.

Build it with components that can be combined randomly.

Shape.

Material.

Color.

Number.

Orientation.

Motion.

Relation.

A trial might produce a transparent blue arch containing three orange spheres.

Another might produce a black spiral staircase descending into a shallow silver pool.

Another might produce five white cubes suspended above a red canoe.

The combinations should be odd enough that shared cultural expectation cannot predict them and structured enough that independent scorers can tell them apart.

Generate many candidates before the study. Have people unfamiliar with the Hill rate their visual similarity, semantic similarity, memorability, and ambiguity. Construct matched sets in which decoys differ by specific features rather than by overall category.

The target is not competing against nonsense.

It is competing against plausible alternatives.

Now establish the communication channel of the receiver.

This is not optional.

If the receiver spells with another person’s support, authorship needs to have been demonstrated independently under participant-only knowledge conditions before the Hill challenge begins. If the participant uses independent typing, eye gaze, speech, symbols, or another validated output, document it.

Do not use a telepathy result to prove the authorship of the response used to claim telepathy.

That circle is too easy to draw and too hard to escape.

The sender needs a validated output channel too if sender reports will be compared with receiver reports. The sender’s role in the core forced-choice task can be simpler—they only need to see and attend to the target—but any narrative account attributed to them should have the same authorship discipline.

Then separate the sites.

The strongest first Hill Challenge should not put sender and receiver in adjacent rooms.

Use independent locations with no shared staff and no shared local network. The receiver’s local team does not know the target. The sender’s local team can know only what is necessary to administer sender procedures, and ideally the target is displayed by software after isolation so even the sender-side staff remain blind.

A central randomization service selects the target only after both sites certify readiness.

The selection is cryptographically committed or otherwise immutably logged before either response is recorded.

Only the sender display receives the target image and description.

The receiver site receives a trial identifier and timing cue containing no target information.

The two sites then proceed independently.

The sender is invited to engage in the Hill experience in whatever standardized manner the participant considers natural and the ethics protocol allows. The research team does not need to decide whether the person is imagining, meditating, dreaming, entering an altered attentional state, or visiting a literal place.

The intervention is simply: encounter this target and, if possible, place or attend to it within the Hill experience.

The receiver is asked to enter their own Hill-associated state without being told which target set is in use.

No live communication occurs.

No facilitator knows the target.

No parent in the receiver location knows the target.

No experimenter in the receiver location knows whether a real sender is active.

The receiver then completes two outputs.

First, a free report.

Describe or draw anything newly encountered. Use the participant’s validated communication mode. Do not present candidate targets yet.

Second, a forced choice.

After the free report is locked and timestamped, present several matched target images in randomized positions and ask which, if any, best corresponds to the experience.

The order matters because forced-choice images can contaminate the free report if shown first.

The free report tests spontaneous content.

The forced choice gives a clean statistical endpoint.

Now add the control trials.

Some trials have a real sender and real target.

Some have no sender: the sender room remains empty or the participant is not shown a target, while the receiver procedure looks identical.

Some use a sender who sees a different non-Hill task to control for general timing and arousal.

Some introduce a delay between sender exposure and receiver session.

Some reverse the order as an exploratory control, with the receiver completing the session before the target is generated. These reverse-order trials must be analyzed separately because success would imply a different claim from forward telepathy.

Some use an unfamiliar sender.

Some use a familiar sender.

The receiver should not know which condition is active.

The trial schedule should be generated in advance and concealed from local teams.

The primary endpoint can be brutally simple.

Suppose each forced-choice trial presents four matched candidates. The receiver chooses one. The preregistered analysis asks whether the correct target is selected more often than expected under the specified randomization model across the confirmatory trial set.

No semantic interpretation is needed.

No judge needs to decide whether “warm doorway” is close enough to “orange arch.”

The receiver either selects the target or does not.

That is the one-bit protocol grown into a two-bit spatial test.

The free reports provide a second endpoint with independent value.

Before unblinding, give each receiver report to multiple judges who see the true target and matched decoys in random order. The judges rank which candidate best matches the report. They do not know which is correct. Use aggregation rules fixed in advance.

Better still, code target features before the study and score reports against those features under a blinded procedure.

If the target contains three orange spheres inside a transparent blue arch, the information-bearing features are not “beautiful,” “portal,” or “felt like connection.” They are count, color, object, containment, material, and shape.

A report that says “a blue glass doorway with three orange balls” is impressive.

A report that says “I felt a warm entrance” may be meaningful to the participant but contains much less target-specific information.

The distinction should be preserved rather than argued away.

The Hill Challenge needs a contamination archive.

Record every target used.

Record when participants, families, staff, and scorers gain access to it.

Record public releases, presentations, and social-media posts during the study period. Freeze target sets after use so a target never re-enters a future confirmatory bank once participants could have learned it.

If participants talk to one another between sessions, document it. Their community life should not be policed, but the scientific analysis must know which future targets may have become contaminated.

The easiest solution is to generate targets procedurally after isolation so nobody could have discussed the exact combination beforehand.

A blue arch may be familiar.

Three orange spheres may be familiar.

The specific randomized conjunction can still be novel.

Novelty should be quantified, not assumed.

The Challenge should also collect experience ratings that are independent of accuracy.

Did the receiver feel present on the Hill?

Did they feel the sender present?

How vivid was the experience?

How confident were they in the target choice?

Did they experience imagery, words, bodily sensations, or a simple knowing?

These measures can reveal whether subjective intensity predicts target information.

If vivid Hill experiences occur equally often on sender-absent trials and real trials, the experience itself may not require external transfer.

If confidence predicts accuracy only when a sender is active, that is more interesting.

If the receiver chooses the correct target above chance while denying any unusual experience, the channel may be less phenomenologically dramatic than the story suggests.

Do not force the mechanism to resemble the testimony.

Physiology comes after the behavior but can be collected from the beginning if it does not compromise the task.

Record respiration, heart rate, movement, and perhaps EEG or other noninvasive measures according to participant tolerance. The primary behavioral result remains primary. Physiological signals are used to ask when successful trials differ.

Does receiver physiology change after sender exposure but before the forced choice?

Does any cross-person synchrony exceed pseudo-pair controls?

Does it contain target identity or only trial timing?

Are successful trials associated with a particular arousal state?

These analyses should be preregistered narrowly or treated as discovery to be confirmed in a later sample.

A giant hyperscanning fishing expedition should not be allowed to rescue chance target performance.

Mechanism earns attention after information transfer earns existence.

Range can be built into later stages.

The first replicated effect should be reproduced at the same approximate separation before the team varies distance aggressively. Then run the Challenge across buildings, cities, and eventually larger separations using the same endpoint.

If performance declines, estimate the curve.

If it does not, local acoustic, mechanical, chemical, and weak near-field explanations lose ground.

If it survives only with familiar pairs, investigate learning and shared priors.

If it survives with unfamiliar pairs, personalized cue models become less plausible.

Every branch of the result creates a next experiment.

The Challenge should have a stopping rule for success that is strong enough to matter and modest enough to be attainable by a real effect.

Do not demand one perfect demonstration.

Demand replication.

For example, a first site can meet a preregistered evidentiary threshold on the primary endpoint. That result triggers an independently run confirmation at a second site using new targets. The second site’s analysis is locked before its outcome data are unblinded.

Only after both succeed does the consortium publicly describe the effect as replicated anomalous target transfer.

Not proof of a nonphysical realm.

Not proof of quantum consciousness.

Not validation of every story attached to the Hill.

Replicated anomalous target transfer.

The narrower phrase would be one of the most consequential sentences neuroscience had written in decades if it were true.

A null result needs equal dignity.

If performance stays at chance across well-powered trials while participants continue to report vivid Hill experiences, publish both facts.

The experience survived.

The target information did not.

That pattern would favor explanations in which the Hill is internally or socially generated rather than a reliable channel for concealed information under those conditions. It would not tell participants what the Hill means to them.

If a subset performs above chance only when a familiar partner holds the board and knows the target, the authorship/cueing problem becomes central.

If performance follows the receiver-room experimenter’s knowledge, the lab has found a conventional channel.

If sender-absent trials also succeed, investigate apparatus leakage or analysis first.

A good challenge has interpretable failure modes.

There should be a public protocol before the public spectacle.

Publish the target-generation code.

Publish the preregistration.

Publish the scoring rules.

Describe the isolation architecture and digital threat model. Invite independent experts in AAC, autism, experimental psychology, statistics, information security, sensory neuroscience, and anomalous-cognition research to inspect it.

Let critics identify leaks before data collection.

Let advocates identify access failures before data collection.

Fix both.

Then freeze the design.

The consortium should also precommit to releasing the primary outcome regardless of direction. No disappearing null study. No teaser clips before the confirmatory analysis. No changing the denominator after a disappointing session. No social-media release of striking trials while misses remain private.

The participant should never become a content machine for the experiment.

Sessions need limits. Breaks need to be available. Withdrawal needs to be ordinary, not treated as evidence for or against the phenomenon. Participation should not interfere with communication access, schooling, therapy, family life, or privacy more than the protocol requires.

If the study succeeds, no one should rush the participant onto a stage to perform harder claims.

Replication is the next task.

If the study fails, no one should make the participant apologize for the hypothesis.

The claim belonged to the research design too.

Why call it a Challenge at all?

Because the subject has spent too long in a form where each side can explain away the other’s evidence after the fact.

A challenge creates a shared object.

Here is the target bank.

Here is the authorship gate.

Here is the information barrier.

Here are the controls.

Here is the threshold.

Here is what success will mean.

Here is what failure will mean.

Agree now.

Then collect the data.

The Hill does not need to become less mysterious for this to work.

It needs one feature that can be hidden.

A transparent blue arch.

Three orange spheres.

A target generated after two people are apart.

If the second person describes it anyway, repeatedly, under a protocol built to make ordinary information unavailable, the scientific world will have something it cannot reasonably solve by tone of voice.

If they do not, we will have learned where one extraordinary interpretation reaches its boundary.

Either result is larger than another argument.

A beautiful test does not guarantee a beautiful answer.

It guarantees that the answer has somewhere to land.
