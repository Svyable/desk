# Chapter 10 — Bodies That Keep Time Together

Watch two people carry a couch through a doorway.

They do not need to discuss every correction. One slows and the other feels the load change. A corner approaches and both rotate. The object tilts, weight shifts through arms and shoulders, and the pair becomes a temporary control system organized around a shared problem.

Timing is the hidden currency.

Conversation has it too. People alternate turns with gaps too short for leisurely planning. Laughter spreads through groups. Walking partners drift toward similar pace. Musicians coordinate attacks and releases. Dancers anticipate weight transfers. Parents rock infants. Crowds clap together and then sometimes fall out of phase.

The nervous system is built to live among other nervous systems.

This creates a natural temptation when people describe unusually close nonspeaking pairs: perhaps their bodies synchronize more strongly than ordinary pairs, and perhaps that synchrony supplies a pathway toward experiences labeled telepathy.

The first half of that possibility is plausible enough to test.

The second half requires more work.

Interpersonal synchrony is not one thing. Movement can synchronize. Breathing can synchronize. Heart rate can covary. Skin conductance can rise and fall together. Neural activity can show temporal relationships. Speech rhythms can align. Attention can become coordinated around the same event.

Each measure lives on a different timescale and carries different information.

Breathing may cycle every few seconds.

A heartbeat occurs roughly once per second in a resting adult, with continuous variability.

Eye movements can change several times per second.

Neural signals fluctuate across milliseconds to seconds and beyond.

A conversational turn may last seconds.

A shared routine may coordinate behavior across hours or years.

Calling all of these *synchrony* can hide more than it reveals.

The first question should always be: synchronized in what variable, over what time window, and because of what coupling?

Consider breathing.

Two people singing together coordinate breath because the musical phrase requires it. Two people meditating to the same instruction may settle into similar respiratory patterns. A parent holding a child may influence breathing through touch and movement. People speaking to one another continually perturb their respiratory cycles because speech itself is organized exhalation.

If researchers place respiratory belts around two participants and observe related rhythms, the finding may simply be the physiology of the shared task.

That can still matter.

Coordinated breathing changes arousal. It can change movement timing. It can alter when speech or pointing is easiest. If spelling performance depends on a familiar partner’s pacing, breath may be one component of the timing scaffold.

Now isolate the participants.

If respiratory alignment persists during randomly timed periods when one participant begins a private breathing manipulation unknown to the other, the phenomenon becomes more interesting. But even then, we need to check common clocks, building vibrations, audio leakage, protocol timing, and analytic artifacts.

Only after those are excluded can the synchrony itself become a candidate anomaly.

Heart rate raises similar problems.

The heart is not a metronome. Beat-to-beat intervals vary with breathing, autonomic regulation, posture, activity, emotion, temperature, and many other factors. Two people watching the same event may show related changes because the event affects both. Two people interacting may influence each other through perception and emotion.

A finding that their heart rates “sync” tells us almost nothing without the causal structure around it.

Skin conductance is more obviously tied to sympathetic arousal. A startling sound can produce a response after a delay. If both participants hear the sound, correlated responses are expected. If only the sender hears it and an isolated receiver repeatedly shows time-locked responses, we have a cleaner question.

But still not semantic telepathy.

The distinction keeps returning because it is easy to forget once graphs look impressive.

A nervous system can receive information about another person’s *state* without receiving information about the *cause* of the state.

Suppose I feel your hand tense while we sit together. I know something changed. I do not know whether you saw a spider, remembered a debt, received good news, or developed a cramp. The channel carries arousal more readily than explanation.

Physiological synchrony is often like that.

Low-dimensional variables can coordinate strongly.

Meaning remains underdetermined.

This suggests a hierarchy of tests.

At the first level, ask whether two bodies show more real-time coordination than matched pseudo-pairs who never interacted.

At the second, remove shared stimuli and ask whether coordination remains.

At the third, manipulate one participant’s state unpredictably and ask whether the other tracks it.

At the fourth, manipulate *which* state or target the sender enters and ask whether the receiver can distinguish categories.

At the fifth, use arbitrary targets with no inherent physiological meaning and ask whether target identity transfers.

Each level increases the information burden.

This hierarchy could be applied directly to people who report meeting on the Hill.

First, measure pairs during ordinary spelling and during ordinary quiet interaction. Establish their baseline patterns.

Then ask both to enter whatever state they associate with the Hill while sitting together. Compare physiology with matched rest periods.

Then separate them but give them synchronized start cues. Any remaining similarity could come from shared timing or learned routines.

Next, remove the shared start cue. A computer privately tells the sender when to begin. The receiver simply waits. If the receiver’s physiology changes at sender onset, that is more interesting.

Next, randomize whether a sender is present at all.

Finally, assign target content and test whether the receiver can identify it.

This sequence transforms a community description into a staircase of increasingly demanding claims.

It also respects the possibility that the subjective “meeting” is real as an altered interpersonal state even if the final staircase never reaches hidden information transfer.

There is a broader evolutionary point here.

Social animals benefit from coordination.

A group moving together can forage, flee, defend, mate, care for young, or hunt more effectively. Signals do not have to be consciously interpreted to be useful. Vocalizations, gaze, body orientation, odor, touch, and movement rhythms can coordinate group behavior. Humans add language, shared culture, and deliberate synchronization to this older biological foundation.

We therefore should expect interpersonal entrainment.

We should not expect it to violate the physics of the channels that produce it.

This expectation helps keep extraordinary claims calibrated.

If two nervous systems synchronize when they share rich sensory contact, that fits existing social biology.

If they synchronize after sensory contact is removed but both follow the same protocol clock, that fits common entrainment.

If they synchronize during unpredictable sender-only events despite physical isolation, that demands closer investigation.

If the synchrony contains decodable arbitrary semantic information, the scientific problem changes dramatically.

A major challenge is deciding what “contains information” means.

Suppose sender A is shown either a red square or a blue circle. The receiver’s heart rate differs slightly between the two conditions. If a preregistered classifier predicts target identity at 55 percent accuracy instead of 50 percent, is that semantic transfer?

Possibly—but only after replication and careful exclusion of leakage.

The effect size may be small but the claim is precise. The receiver’s physiology carries target-related information above chance. Researchers can then ask whether the receiver consciously knows the target, whether the information generalizes to new stimuli, and what physiological channel mediates the distinction.

This is much stronger than showing that the two heart-rate curves look similar.

Classification forces specificity.

It can also produce false confidence if handled badly.

Machine-learning models are talented at exploiting leakage. If data from the same trial appear in both training and test sets, temporal autocorrelation can inflate performance. If target order correlates with session time, the classifier may learn drift rather than information. If one experimenter runs mostly red trials and another mostly blue, the model may classify experimenter artifacts.

Cross-validation does not rescue a flawed causal design.

The split must reflect the claim.

Train on some participants and test on new ones if the proposed signal is general.

Train on some days and test on later days if stability is claimed.

Hold out entire sessions.

Use permutation baselines that preserve temporal structure.

Compare real dyads with shuffled dyads.

If the effect survives, the data become harder to explain casually.

There is another possibility: synchrony may help communication without carrying the content itself.

Think of a radio receiver. Tuning to the correct frequency does not create the message. It creates conditions in which the message can be extracted.

A telepathy proponent might therefore propose that interpersonal physiological synchrony is a gating state. When two people become aligned, some other weak information channel becomes detectable.

This idea is speculative but testable.

Measure synchrony independently. Divide trials into high- and low-synchrony periods using criteria defined without looking at target accuracy. Then compare hidden-target performance.

If accuracy is consistently higher during high-synchrony periods across preregistered replications, synchrony becomes a candidate moderator.

Next manipulate synchrony.

Have pairs engage in paced breathing together before separation. Compare with mismatched rhythms. Use familiar versus unfamiliar preparatory routines. Change temporal coordination while keeping target presentation identical.

If altering synchrony causally changes target transfer, the gating hypothesis gains support.

If accuracy is unrelated to synchrony, the hypothesis weakens.

This is how we can investigate ideas that sound vague without allowing them to remain vague.

The phrase *on the same wavelength* is metaphorical in ordinary speech.

A scientific version would identify the wavelength—or decide the metaphor is not physical.

The Hill reports are full of relational language. Meeting. Finding. Joining. Being together. These words may point toward a state more than a signal. A meeting is not simply a packet of information; it is a coordinated event.

This distinction suggests that the first physiological signature of the Hill, if one exists, may be state synchronization rather than message accuracy.

That would be worth discovering.

Imagine two participants in separate rooms who independently press a switch when they believe they have “arrived.” If arrival times cluster more closely for claimed partners than for random pairings, despite no shared cue, that is a behavioral synchrony effect. Add respiration and EEG. Does a common state emerge around those self-reported arrivals?

Now insert sender-absent trials without telling the receiver.

Does the receiver still report arrival at the same rate?

If yes, the state may be self-generated.

If no, partner participation may matter.

Swap partners.

Does the effect follow familiarity?

Shift clocks so the sender begins later than expected.

Does the receiver track the actual or expected schedule?

These experiments can reveal whether “meeting” is driven by expectation, shared routine, interpersonal coupling, or something not yet captured.

They also make room for individual differences.

Some pairs may show strong physiological coordination because they have practiced together for years. Others may not. Some participants may have idiosyncratic Hill states. A group average can erase the very pattern researchers need to understand.

The solution is not to abandon statistics for anecdotes.

Use hierarchical models that estimate both individual and group effects. Predefine within-person markers. Replicate the same dyad across many sessions. Then test generalization across dyads.

A phenomenon can be rare and still real.

Rare phenomena demand more repeated measurement, not less.

This becomes crucial if telepathy claims concentrate among a small subset of participants. A twenty-person group average may dilute a genuine effect present in two people. But identifying those two after examining the data creates selection bias.

The correct design includes a discovery phase and a confirmatory phase.

Find candidate responders using one dataset.

Freeze the criteria.

Test the same people prospectively on new targets.

Then recruit new candidates independently.

This prevents “special responder” from becoming another movable explanation.

Bodies keep time together for many reasons.

Some are obvious. Music. Speech. Touch. Shared motion.

Some are subtle. Anticipation. Familiarity. Co-regulation. Learned rhythm.

Some may not yet be well understood.

The existence of synchrony is therefore not surprising enough to prove telepathy.

But it may be the terrain on which telepathy claims arise.

A person who repeatedly experiences another body becoming predictable, another rhythm becoming familiar, another nervous system helping organize their own action may reasonably describe the relationship as unusually connected.

Science does not need to strip that experience of meaning.

It needs to ask which part of the connection carries time, which part carries state, and whether any part carries hidden content.

Timing is cheap compared with meaning.

The next chapter moves closer to meaning by asking how one brain predicts another before either of them needs a new force of nature.
