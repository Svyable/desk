# Chapter 27 — Distance Is a Mechanism

Move two people apart and theories begin to separate from one another.

At twenty centimeters, almost everything is possible as an ordinary channel. Breath can be heard. Eyes can be tracked. Heat crosses the gap. Air moves. The floor transmits vibration. A letterboard can become a shared mechanical object. Small electric and magnetic interactions are physically larger than they will be farther away. Familiar people can read posture and timing with extraordinary efficiency.

At twenty meters, many of those channels weaken.

Across two sealed rooms, more disappear.

Across a city, the candidate list changes again.

Across an ocean, any explanation based on the weak local field around a human body has a very large problem.

Distance is therefore not merely a dramatic feature of a telepathy claim.

It is a mechanism test.

Every physical carrier has some relationship with range.

Sound loses intensity and is blocked or reflected by materials. Light follows geometry and requires a path. Chemical signals diffuse or ride moving air. Mechanical vibrations attenuate through structures. Electric and magnetic near fields behave according to source geometry, frequency, materials, and separation. Radiofrequency radiation propagates differently from quasi-static fields. Biological signals transmitted through shared objects depend on the coupling those objects provide.

If a proposed effect does not change when the physical variable carrying it should change enormously, the proposed carrier is probably wrong.

This sounds obvious until anecdotes enter the room.

People often describe a close-range experience and a remote experience using the same word: telepathy. The label encourages us to search for one explanation. But the two events may not belong to one mechanism at all.

A parent sitting beside a child might be detecting subtle behavior.

A remote coincidence might arise from shared routine and memory.

A laboratory anomaly, if replicated, might involve a third process.

The job of science is not to preserve the unity of the word.

It is to discover which effects share a dependency pattern.

Range is one of the best ways to do that.

The first experiment should not jump from one meter to one thousand kilometers.

Build a distance curve.

If sender and receiver can perform a clean hidden-target task under controlled conditions, begin close enough that the apparatus can measure every plausible ordinary channel.

Half a meter.

One meter.

Two.

Five.

Ten.

Separate rooms.

Separate buildings.

Then longer distances if the effect survives.

Randomize distance across sessions when possible so practice, fatigue, and expectation do not track the range condition.

Keep target difficulty constant.

Keep blinding constant.

Measure the candidate environment at every distance.

The result is not just a graph of accuracy.

It is a fingerprint.

Suppose accuracy drops sharply between one and three meters and then reaches chance. That pattern invites local sensory or field explanations.

Suppose accuracy survives across rooms but falls when sender and receiver are placed in separate buildings. Shared infrastructure becomes interesting: wiring, network devices, timing systems, structural vibration, experimenter movement.

Suppose accuracy is identical at one meter and one thousand kilometers. Any direct weak-field mechanism whose measured strength collapses over that range becomes a poor fit.

Suppose accuracy improves with distance.

That would be strange, but it would not license mysticism. Perhaps nearby conditions create distraction. Perhaps close-range sessions use a different apparatus. Perhaps the statistical fluctuation will disappear. Replicate the shape before theorizing it.

Scaling laws are powerful because they constrain stories.

A mechanism that predicts attenuation cannot shrug when attenuation is absent.

This is particularly important for electromagnetic explanations.

The fields measured by EEG and MEG arise in a regime where source geometry and distance matter. The detailed mathematics depends on whether one is considering electric potentials in conductive tissue, quasi-static magnetic fields, radiative components, or some other configuration. We do not need a single cartoon formula to make the experimental point.

Move away from the source and measure.

The instrument tells us how the candidate field changes in the actual geometry.

Then compare behavior to the measurement.

If behavioral performance tracks a particular measurable field component across distance, the mechanism gains support.

If the field vanishes into noise while behavior remains flat, either the receiver is far more sensitive than the instrument to some unmeasured feature or the carrier is something else.

Both possibilities demand new evidence.

Distance also helps separate shared environment from direct coupling.

Two people in neighboring rooms may experience the same building rhythms. Heating systems cycle. Traffic passes. Lights flicker. Power-line fields vary. Network traffic produces device activity. Researchers move according to the protocol. Clocks synchronize events.

These shared causes can create correlations between physiological recordings without any person-to-person transfer.

Put one participant in another building and many common inputs change.

Better yet, reproduce the same stimulus schedule independently in two sites while ensuring that only one site contains the sender. If receiver performance follows the sender rather than the common schedule, that distinction matters.

The strongest version uses several sites.

A central server generates targets after all participants are isolated. It sends the target only to one sender site. Receiver sites are blind to whether they are paired with a real sender, an absent sender, or a replayed timing schedule. Local experimenters never see the target. All equipment logs are synchronized and later audited.

Now range can vary without the receiver knowing it.

A receiver might be paired with someone in the next room on one trial and someone hundreds of kilometers away on another. If the subjective task feels identical, expectation cannot easily explain a distance curve.

Timing belongs beside distance because a mechanism travels through both.

A target cannot influence a receiver through an ordinary forward causal channel before the target exists.

That sentence should be written into every protocol.

Generate the target using a secure random procedure after isolation.

Timestamp generation.

Timestamp when the sender first receives it.

Timestamp the receiver’s response.

Preserve the logs.

If analysis later appears to show that receiver physiology changes before target generation, the first explanations should be clock error, filtering artifact, trial alignment, data leakage, or flexible analysis.

Only after those are excluded does the claim become something more exotic than telepathy as defined in this book.

Pre-target effects are not stronger telepathy.

They are a different causal claim.

The distinction is important because signal processing can manufacture apparent anticipation. Filters can smear information backward in time. Averaging around events can create shapes that look predictive. Selecting trials based on future outcomes can contaminate earlier windows. Multiple comparisons can identify a pre-event interval by chance.

Good clocks are not enough.

Analysis must preserve causal order too.

A distance experiment should therefore preregister the time window in which a receiver response is allowed to count.

If the sender sees the target at time zero and the receiver must answer within thirty seconds, define that before the data arrive. If exploratory physiological analyses search wider windows, label them exploratory and replicate any discovered timing pattern later.

Latency can reveal processing stages.

Suppose a receiver’s accuracy peaks when answering three to five seconds after sender exposure and declines with longer delays. That suggests a transient process.

Suppose performance requires a minute of sender attention. Encoding or integration may be slow.

Suppose delay has no effect over hours. The mechanism would look less like immediate signal transmission and more like persistent information, memory, shared prediction, or a different anomaly.

Again, no single pattern proves a pathway.

The pattern narrows the space.

Distance can be manipulated physically and informationally.

Physical distance is obvious.

Informational distance is less so.

Two people can be geographically separated and still connected by phones, cloud accounts, smart-home devices, shared calendars, social media, messaging notifications, cameras, location services, or mutual contacts. A remote experiment can be physically distant while informationally porous.

The modern world is hostile to clean isolation.

A participant does not have to cheat for information to leak.

A smartwatch may show a notification.

A phone may vibrate differently because a remote device wakes.

A video-conferencing system may adapt bandwidth when someone joins.

A parent may post a picture unknowingly visible to a researcher.

The sender’s room may share a cloud document with the receiver’s household.

Any serious remote test needs a digital threat model.

Inventory devices.

Remove or isolate networked equipment not required for the protocol.

Use separate accounts and networks.

Log network traffic where appropriate.

Generate targets on systems whose output cannot reach the receiver except through the intended experimental path.

Audit metadata after the session.

This may sound like cybersecurity because it is.

A claim about information transfer should borrow methods from every field that studies information leakage.

Geographic distance without digital separation proves little in 2026.

There is a more subtle kind of proximity too: shared history.

Two people on opposite continents can remain cognitively close. They may wake at predictable times, know each other’s routines, choose similar images, and interpret ambiguous experiences through the same vocabulary. Random targets solve much of this because the sender’s own preferences no longer determine what has to be guessed.

If the computer chooses the target after separation, intimate knowledge cannot predict which random outcome occurred.

That is why randomization is stronger than using whatever thought naturally comes to the sender.

Natural thoughts are confounded with personality.

Random targets isolate transfer.

The Hill presents an especially sharp range question.

If people say they meet there regardless of physical distance, the report itself predicts distance invariance at the phenomenological level. That does not tell us whether target-specific information is invariant.

Separate the two.

Ask participants after every session whether they experienced presence, connection, or the Hill.

Then score concealed-target accuracy independently.

It is possible that subjective connection remains equally strong across distance while target transfer stays at chance.

That would tell us the Hill experience is not a simple readout of hidden information.

It is also possible, though unestablished, that target accuracy remains above chance across distance.

Then local sensory and near-field models lose plausibility.

A third pattern could show subjective presence at all ranges but target accuracy only nearby. That would suggest more than one process under the same experiential label.

This is why we should not force every aspect of a report into one mechanism.

Physiology often has layers.

The feeling of connection may arise from memory and state.

A local cue may provide timing.

A separate communication method may supply language.

The conscious experience can unify them even when causality does not.

Distance helps pull the layers apart.

A rigorous program should also test barriers rather than range alone.

Two people at the same physical separation can be placed in different coupling conditions.

Open air.

Acoustic isolation.

Optical barrier.

Vibration isolation.

Electromagnetic shielding characterized for the proposed frequencies.

Separate electrical grounds.

Independent ventilation.

No shared network.

Each barrier changes particular channels while leaving distance constant.

The resulting pattern can distinguish attenuation from categorical blocking.

Suppose moving from two to ten meters has little effect, but acoustic isolation abolishes performance. The channel was not distance-limited over that range; it was sound-dependent.

Suppose magnetic shielding matters but acoustic and optical isolation do not. That would be far more interesting for a field hypothesis.

Suppose only removing live human staff from the receiver room matters. Experimenter cueing becomes likely.

Isolation is not one condition.

It is a matrix.

There is an ethical dimension to range testing too.

Remote protocols may be easier for participants who find laboratories stressful. Familiar environments can improve access and reduce sensory burden. But home testing increases uncontrolled channels and devices.

The solution is not to exclude participants who cannot tolerate a conventional lab.

It is to design remote methods whose information security is explicit.

A sealed target tablet can reveal a stimulus locally to the sender after cryptographic commitment. A separate receiver device can record choices without receiving target data. Video can document room conditions without transmitting target content. Independent technicians can audit logs without interacting with participants.

Good experimental design should adapt to the person without surrendering the causal question.

The larger scientific lesson is simple.

When someone says distance does not matter, ask them to draw the curve.

When someone says a field carries the signal, measure the field across that curve.

When someone says timing is instantaneous, synchronize the clocks.

When someone says people connect remotely, isolate the networks too.

Range claims are not decoration.

They are equations waiting for data.

A real channel will have a geometry, even if that geometry surprises us.

The shape may not match any known carrier.

That would be extraordinary.

But first we have to measure the shape.

Distance is not what makes telepathy mysterious.

Distance is what can make a mechanism confess.
