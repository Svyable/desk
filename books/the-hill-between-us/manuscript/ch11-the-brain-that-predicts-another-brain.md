# Chapter 11 — The Brain That Predicts Another Brain

A familiar person begins a sentence and you already know where it is going.

Sometimes you are wrong. Sometimes you interrupt with the exact word. Sometimes both of you say it together and laugh.

Nothing moved directly from one skull to the other.

A model did the work.

Brains do not wait passively for the world to finish happening. Perception and action depend on expectation. The nervous system uses prior experience and current sensory evidence to anticipate what is likely next, then updates when reality disagrees. Different theories formalize this process in different ways, and the role of predictive processing in autism remains debated, but prediction itself is not controversial. A nervous system that could only react after complete information arrived would be too slow for much of ordinary life.

Social interaction makes the prediction problem recursive.

I predict you.

You predict me.

I react to what I think you are about to do.

You react to my reaction.

A stable relationship becomes a library of compressed expectations.

This can produce something that looks eerily like access to another mind.

A spouse knows the complaint before hearing it.

A parent knows which object a child wants from one shift of posture.

A close collaborator knows which objection is coming.

A chess player predicts an opponent’s plan.

A good interviewer hears the answer in the pause before it arrives.

The prediction may be conscious or not. Its success can feel immediate.

Telepathy claims involving familiar pairs therefore have to defeat a powerful competitor: a richly trained model of the other person.

The closer the relationship, the larger the model.

A parent may know what foods the child prefers, which people matter, which routines are upsetting, what happened yesterday, what happened years ago, and how the child behaves before certain choices. The child may know the parent’s habits equally well. Ask either person to “think of something” and the answer space is not remotely random.

This is why hidden-target experiments should use information generated after separation.

New randomness starves prediction.

If the sender has not chosen the target until a computer chooses it, the receiver cannot use years of shared history to know which target will appear. They can still predict how the sender responds to it, but only if some cue from that response reaches them.

That is the line between inference and transfer.

Consider an emotionally meaningful test.

A sender chooses one of four family photographs. The receiver identifies the correct one. Impressive.

Now let the computer select one of four unfamiliar geometric patterns after the sender is isolated. The receiver falls to chance.

The first result may reflect shared priors rather than telepathy.

Now reverse the pattern. The receiver succeeds equally well on arbitrary computer-selected targets. Shared history is no longer enough.

The target distribution becomes a mechanistic tool.

We can vary how predictable the target is.

At one end are highly familiar choices: favorite foods, family members, repeated routines.

Then common semantic categories: animals, household objects, colors.

Then unfamiliar photographs.

Then generated abstract shapes.

Then random strings or numbers with no semantic association.

If performance decreases as prior knowledge becomes less useful, inference is likely contributing.

If performance remains stable, a target-specific channel deserves more attention.

This gradient is better than arguing about whether an individual example “could have been guessed.”

Human guessing is too flexible for anecdotes.

Experimental priors can be quantified.

Prediction also operates at the level of movement.

A communication partner watching a hand begin to move can forecast where it will land. The hand, in turn, can respond to board motion generated by the partner. Each system predicts the other, making the final movement smoother than either could produce alone.

In motor control, prediction is often useful because sensory feedback arrives with delays. The brain estimates the consequences of action before all feedback returns. In a practiced dyad, prediction can extend across people.

This may explain why removing a familiar facilitator can make a speller’s performance deteriorate even if the facilitator never supplies semantic content.

The person has lost part of a learned control environment.

That does not establish independent authorship by itself, but it suggests why the path to independence may require replacing predictability rather than simply removing the person.

A mechanical board with stable timing could provide some of it.

A standardized auditory rhythm could provide some.

A familiar positioning protocol could provide some.

The scientific question becomes which predictive scaffold matters.

This view also changes how we interpret “mind merging,” a phrase sometimes used in reports around intense interpersonal connection.

Two predictive systems can become mutually adapted without literally sharing representations.

Think of elite doubles tennis. Partners know where the other will move because both have learned the same tactical situation and each other’s habits. Their actions can look like one distributed organism. The coordination is real. The neural states remain physically separate, connected through perception and action.

A pair that communicates for years through an unusual motor interface may develop an equally specialized coordination skill.

Outsiders may underestimate how much information sits in tiny timing differences because outsiders have not learned the code.

This suggests a powerful research strategy: try to decode the code.

Record the interaction at high resolution. Use models to predict the next letter from variables available before selection.

Start with the receiver’s gaze and hand movement.

Then add board motion.

Add facilitator gaze.

Add facial movement.

Add prompt timing.

Add breathing and posture.

Train on some sessions and test on unseen sessions.

If a model can predict upcoming letters from the facilitator’s behavior even when the facilitator believes they are neutral, the hidden channel has become visible.

If the facilitator is blind to the target and their behavior still predicts the participant’s letters, that may simply reflect reading the participant’s early movement. Directionality analyses and perturbations can separate leader from follower.

If no measurable variable carries target information and performance still succeeds under concealed-target conditions, the predictive-cue account weakens.

This approach turns skepticism into signal hunting.

Rather than saying “there must be cues,” identify them.

That is a higher standard for skeptics too.

An unexplained result should not be dismissed indefinitely by hypothetical leakage that becomes less plausible with every control. At some point, the burden shifts from the claimant to the critic to propose a channel compatible with the actual protocol.

Prediction is also central to subjective certainty.

When a forecast is correct, the brain often does not experience the probabilistic machinery that generated it. The answer simply feels obvious.

A parent may say, “I just knew.”

The phrase is psychologically accurate.

It does not distinguish the route.

Expertise often produces this source-less knowing. Radiologists can detect abnormalities rapidly. Firefighters can sense that a situation is wrong before articulating the cue. Athletes anticipate movement from partial patterns. The conscious mind receives a conclusion generated by processes it cannot fully inspect.

Close-person prediction can feel even more intimate because the target is another mind.

We should therefore expect people to misclassify some successful inference as direct access.

This is not a flaw unique to believers in telepathy.

Scientists misattribute intuitions too. Traders think they sensed the market. Doctors overlearn anecdotes. Interviewers feel they can detect lies. Humans are poor at estimating how much information a skilled model extracted from context.

The remedy is the same: break the context.

If the effect depends on prediction, remove predictive structure.

Use random targets.

Use unfamiliar senders.

Hide trial timing.

Balance target frequencies.

Introduce decoys.

Change the mapping between target and response.

Ask whether performance adapts when the statistical environment changes.

A predictive system should reveal itself through learning.

Suppose the target probabilities are secretly altered halfway through a long experiment. One symbol becomes more frequent. If the receiver’s guesses gradually track the new distribution even when no sender is present, they are learning task statistics. That learning can masquerade as improved telepathy if researchers compare late sessions with early ones.

Sender-absent controls expose it.

This is why no-sender trials are so valuable.

On those trials, the receiver experiences the full ritual. Same room. Same timing uncertainty. Same communication partner. Same belief that a target may exist. But no sender sees a target.

If the receiver still produces above-chance-looking patterns because the analysis is flexible or targets are predictable, the design has a problem.

If performance cleanly separates sender-present from sender-absent trials, causality becomes more plausible.

The brain’s predictive nature also complicates the Hill as a shared place.

Once a community has a concept of the Hill, expectations shape experience.

A person entering a meditative or dreamlike state may predict elevation, landscape, other people, or familiar landmarks because those features belong to the learned schema. Prediction can organize ambiguous internal imagery just as it organizes ambiguous sensory input.

This does not make the experience fake.

Perception itself is partly constructive. Dreams are profoundly constructive. Memory is reconstructive. A shared schema can make separate private experiences converge.

The test is novelty.

What appears on the Hill that the receiver had no reason to predict?

A novel object assigned after separation is more informative than a familiar tree.

A randomized route is more informative than a known meeting point.

A precise feature is more informative than a mood.

The sender’s role can also be manipulated.

If the sender privately receives a target but is instructed not to think about it until a random cue, when does receiver performance change?

If target information is somehow broadcast automatically by the sender’s brain, exposure may be enough.

If intentional attention is required, accuracy should rise only during instructed focus.

If the receiver is mainly predicting the sender’s likely mental state from the protocol, actual attention may not matter.

This distinction begins to separate a “mind as transmitter” model from a “relationship as prediction” model.

Prediction has physiological signatures too.

Expectation can change pupil size, eye movements, reaction time, neural responses, and motor preparation. A receiver guessing a likely target may show anticipatory behavior before the sender even sees the target. If researchers observe target-looking behavior that begins before target generation, they have found evidence of bias or leakage in the analysis, not precognition.

Time ordering is unforgiving that way.

The target must exist before it can cause a signal.

This seems obvious until complex analyses create apparent effects that extend backward in time because filters, averaging windows, or alignment procedures use future data. Signal processing can manufacture temporal mystery if researchers are careless.

A rigorous telepathy program therefore needs causal analysis pipelines.

Filters should be chosen to avoid smearing post-target activity into pre-target periods. Trial windows should be defined prospectively. Baseline periods should be independent. Researchers should test the pipeline on simulated data where no effect exists to ensure it does not invent one.

The closer we get to neural signals, the more important these details become.

Predictive processing is sometimes invoked in autism with sweeping claims: autistic people are said to have fundamentally weak priors, overly precise sensory evidence, unusual prediction errors, or other broad differences. The literature is more mixed than any single slogan suggests. Reviews find evidence for differences in some predictive-learning contexts and not others. Interoception findings are also heterogeneous across age, task, and measurement method.

That uncertainty matters here.

We should not build a telepathy theory on the unsupported premise that autistic people universally perceive reality with fewer predictive filters or possess heightened access to raw signals.

The hypothesis can be tested at the individual level.

Does a participant rely less on target priors than controls?

Do they update differently when probabilities change?

Does predictive style correlate with hidden-target performance?

If not, leave the story behind.

There is a larger philosophical lure in the prediction idea. If minds are constantly modeling other minds, perhaps sufficiently accurate modeling becomes functionally indistinguishable from access.

At ordinary scales, maybe.

If I know my closest friend will order tea, the distinction between prediction and mind reading may not matter at lunch.

Science cares when the target is truly novel.

No model of my history can predict a cryptographically generated 128-bit string I have not seen.

If you reproduce that string after I see it, we need a channel.

This is why arbitrary information is the solvent that separates intuition from transmission.

Meaningful targets are humanly rich.

Random targets are mechanistically clean.

A mature research program needs both.

Start with the clean target to establish that information can move.

Then return to meaning to understand how minds use the channel, if one exists.

Without that order, the brain’s gift for prediction can impersonate a bridge that was never there.

We are built to anticipate one another.

Before announcing a new way for minds to connect, we should first appreciate how astonishingly connected prediction already makes us.
