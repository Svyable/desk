# Chapter 4 — The Authorship Problem

A sentence can be true and still belong to the wrong person.

That is the danger at the center of assisted communication.

It is also why authorship has to be solved before telepathy can be studied with spelling output. If the person generating the letters cannot be identified, then a correct hidden answer does not tell us whose information arrived on the page, much less how it traveled.

The problem sounds legal because the word *authorship* carries associations with books, signatures, and ownership. In the laboratory it is simpler. Who controls the information-bearing choices?

Not who touches the equipment.

Not who feels responsible.

Not who is trying hardest.

Who controls the sequence that distinguishes one message from another?

A person can receive extensive support while remaining the author. A court reporter may type words spoken by somebody else without becoming the author of the testimony. Predictive text can complete a word while the user retains control over the intended message. A communication device can stabilize a shaky movement without supplying the semantic target. Conversely, a person can appear physically independent while their choices are strongly shaped by another person’s cues.

Authorship is therefore a causal question.

Causal questions benefit from interventions.

The classic logic is information separation. Give two people different knowledge and observe which knowledge appears in the output. This principle is powerful because it does not require anyone to infer intentions. The output itself identifies the information source.

Imagine a communication partner and a nonspeaking speller sitting together. A screen visible only to the speller displays a photograph of a bicycle. A second screen visible only to the partner displays a photograph of a lighthouse. Neither person knows that the images differ. The prompt asks, “What did you see?”

If the response is *bicycle* across repeated trials of this type, the speller’s access controls the output.

If it is *lighthouse*, the partner’s access controls it.

If responses become confused, mixed, or inaccurate when knowledge diverges, the dyad may rely on shared context.

Now add a condition in which both see bicycle. Performance there confirms that the communication arrangement can work when knowledge is shared. This matters because a test that is simply too difficult or stressful can create false negatives. The goal is not to make the participant fail. It is to make the causal alternatives separate.

A strong authorship study therefore looks less like a challenge and more like a diagnostic map.

There should be easy trials and hard trials. Familiarization. Baseline performance. Conditions in which both people know the answer. Conditions in which only one does. Conditions without a single correct answer. Enough repetitions to distinguish a stable pattern from a lucky streak. Video. Time stamps. Prespecified scoring.

The communication partner should be treated as part of the system, not as a suspect.

That changes how the experiment feels.

Instead of telling a facilitator, “We are testing whether you are controlling the participant,” the research team can say, accurately, “We are mapping how information moves through this interaction.” The partner’s expertise becomes useful. They can explain the participant’s normal pacing, sensory needs, signs of fatigue, board position, and prompting routine. The protocol can preserve those features until the specific variable under study requires changing one.

Respect does not require methodological softness.

In fact, a partner who believes strongly in the participant’s independent authorship has every reason to want a test that makes alternative explanations difficult.

This is where research culture often goes wrong. A skeptical team may design an alien task in an alien room, remove every support at once, and interpret collapse as proof the original communication was never authentic. A committed team may preserve the familiar interaction so completely that the person who knows the answer remains entangled with the output, making success uninterpretable.

Both designs can fail.

A better design changes one source of information at a time while leaving the rest of the support ecology as intact as possible.

Suppose the letterboard normally moves. Keep it moving during the first blinded condition, but ensure the board holder does not know the target. If the participant succeeds, board movement alone cannot explain semantic cueing from target knowledge. Next compare the moving board with a fixed board. If performance changes, the motion may be an access aid. That does not erase authorship; it identifies a motor dependency worth understanding.

Suppose verbal prompts are normally used. Standardize them. Use a recorded prompt sequence or a script so the content and timing do not vary with target knowledge. Later remove prompts to see whether they are necessary.

Suppose the participant relies on a familiar partner for regulation. Keep the partner present but blind. Later place the partner behind an opaque divider while preserving voice. Then preserve presence without voice. Each step asks what part of the relationship matters.

This is experimental decomposition.

It is kinder and more informative than ripping away the entire scaffold.

The distinction becomes especially important because a communication partner can contribute in at least three different ways.

The first is **access support**. They position a board, help the person regulate, keep the task organized, or provide sensory and attentional conditions in which movement becomes possible.

The second is **interactional influence**. Their timing, gaze, movement, prompts, and expectations alter the probability of particular actions even when they do not know the target or intend to direct an answer.

The third is **informational influence**. Knowledge possessed by the partner enters the output.

Only the third directly invalidates independent semantic authorship of a hidden target. The second may still matter because it can make the output less independent in a motor sense, but it is possible for another person to stabilize behavior without supplying message content. The first is compatible with genuine authorship in the ordinary assistive-technology sense.

Good research tries to tell them apart.

Technology can help.

High-speed cameras can track the finger and board. Inertial sensors can measure board rotation and acceleration. Eye trackers can identify fixation sequences. Microphones can capture prompts and breathing. Force sensors can measure contact. Computer vision can estimate the partner’s head pose and gaze. A synchronized clock can put all these streams on the same timeline.

Then the question becomes computational.

How much information about the next selected letter is available in the partner’s behavior before the participant commits to the movement?

How much is available in the participant’s gaze?

Does the causal ordering change depending on who knows the answer?

If the participant’s eyes repeatedly arrive at the target before the partner’s board adjustment, the flow looks different than if the board begins moving toward the target before the participant’s gaze selects it.

This still requires careful interpretation. Correlation is not command. A partner might predict the participant’s intended letter from early hand movement and then reposition the board helpfully. The board would contain information about the target without being its source. Temporal precedence helps, but intervention is stronger.

Randomly perturb the board.

On some trials, a motorized stand could introduce a small standardized displacement unrelated to the target. Does the participant compensate? Does the trajectory preserve the intended letter? If a partner usually provides stabilizing corrections, compare human-held and mechanically stabilized conditions. If performance survives with a fixed display and independent selection, the causal story becomes cleaner still.

The ideal endpoint is not a particular technology. It is control that generalizes.

A person who can independently answer concealed questions using eye gaze may prefer a handheld letterboard in daily life because it is faster or more comfortable. Validation does not require abandoning the preferred method. It can provide an external anchor demonstrating that the person’s language does not depend on another individual’s target knowledge.

That anchor matters most when messages become consequential.

Consider the difference between spelling *I want tea* and spelling *my caregiver hurt me*.

Both deserve attention. The second can trigger investigations, family separation, criminal proceedings, or protective interventions. If the communication method has not established independent authorship, the legal and ethical risks become severe in both directions. A genuine allegation might be dismissed because the method is controversial. An unintentionally facilitator-generated allegation might be treated as the person’s testimony.

The person at the center loses either way when validation is weak.

This is why professional concerns about facilitated communication have remained intense long after the first controlled studies. The issue is not merely theoretical purity. Attribution has consequences.

Telepathy claims raise those consequences in a different way.

If a nonspeaking person spells private information about someone else, families may conclude that privacy itself works differently around that person. A parent may become anxious that thoughts are exposed. Boundaries can shift. Ordinary coincidence can acquire moral meaning. The person may be treated as spiritually exceptional or burdened with expectations to know things nobody could reasonably know.

Before any of that happens, authorship should be boringly solid.

The word *boringly* is useful here.

Science becomes most persuasive when the validation step stops being dramatic. A participant answers concealed questions correctly across sessions. Different blinded partners obtain the same result. The participant moves to a fixed keyboard and remains accurate. Independent labs reproduce the performance. Nobody needs to stage a reveal because the data have become routine.

Then the extraordinary claim can begin.

Suppose the participant is independently authoring responses. The next question is not yet “Can they read minds?” It is “What information did they have access to?”

This is where target construction matters.

A hidden target should not be guessable from context. If the participant is asked what color a researcher selected and the choices are red, blue, green, and yellow, chance is one in four. That is measurable, but repeated exposure can create sequence expectations. Good randomization is required.

Open-ended targets create richer tests but harder scoring. If the sender sees an image of a lighthouse in a storm and the receiver writes *water, light, tall*, that resemblance feels meaningful. But how often would those words fit decoy images? Would blinded judges rank the real target first among five options? Would a preregistered semantic model score it higher than controls? The scoring method has to exist before researchers know which description belongs to which target.

The target should also be generated after isolation whenever possible.

Preselected targets can leak. A researcher may know them. Files may remain on a device. Trial orders may be inferred. Somebody may overhear setup. The simplest defense is to reduce the number of humans who ever know the answer.

A computer can choose the target after sender and receiver are separated. The sender alone sees it. The receiver’s room contains no display of the target. The experimenter interacting with the receiver sees only a trial number. The correct answer is revealed only after the response is locked.

This design changes the emotional atmosphere.

There is no person in the receiver’s room whose face can betray success.

There is no facilitator who can accidentally know the answer.

There is no host who can lean forward when the right number begins to appear.

The information source has been physically moved away from the output.

Now the authorship problem has done its job. It has become infrastructure.

If the receiver succeeds, we can ask how the information crossed the separation.

If the receiver fails, we know the failure occurred after authorship was established, not because a disputed communication method collapsed under changed conditions.

That distinction protects the person from being reduced to the hypothesis.

It also creates the kind of evidence skeptics should want to see.

A common complaint in extraordinary-claims research is that believers always move the goalposts. When a test fails, stress is blamed. When it succeeds weakly, the weak success is celebrated. When controls tighten, the phenomenon becomes more fragile. Eventually no conceivable result can count against the claim.

That is not a telepathy-specific problem. Humans defend favored explanations everywhere.

The antidote is to define failure in advance.

For authorship, perhaps the participant must identify concealed targets at a specified accuracy across a fixed number of trials, with confidence intervals excluding chance and replication on another day. Perhaps the criterion is individualized based on demonstrated baseline performance. Perhaps the protocol includes a stopping rule for fatigue. Whatever the choices, they are written before target data are inspected.

A failed test then means something bounded.

It means the method, under those conditions, did not establish independent authorship at the prespecified level.

It does not mean the participant lacks language.

It does not mean the partner intended to influence the output.

It does not mean no other access method could work.

It does mean researchers should not use that output as a measurement instrument for hidden-information transfer until the authorship problem is solved.

This is an important ethic of inference: conclusions should be no larger than the experiment.

Successful tests need the same discipline.

If a participant identifies concealed targets while the facilitator is blind, that establishes that the facilitator’s target knowledge is not necessary. It does not yet prove the absence of all subtle cues. If the participant succeeds on a fixed keyboard with nobody nearby knowing the answer, the independence claim becomes stronger. If target information exists only in another isolated person’s mind and the receiver still succeeds, then we have crossed into a different scientific territory.

Each success buys permission to ask the next question.

No step buys all the others at once.

This may feel painfully incremental compared with a story in which a person names a secret word and everybody in the room gasps.

But the incremental path leads somewhere the gasp cannot.

It leads to transportable knowledge.

Once independent authorship is established, the participant does not need a sympathetic audience to be believed. The result can be shown to a clinician, an educator, a court, another scientist, or a stranger. The person’s communication gains authority precisely because the method survived attempts to confuse it with someone else’s influence.

For people whose voices have historically been doubted, that kind of authority is not a technicality.

It is freedom from needing the right witness.

The telepathy question can wait long enough to earn that foundation.
