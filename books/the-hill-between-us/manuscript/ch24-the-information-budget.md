# Chapter 24 — The Information Budget

A feeling can be enormous while carrying almost no information.

That is not an insult to feeling. It is a distinction between intensity and specificity.

If a receiver suddenly becomes certain that another person is thinking about them, the experience may be vivid enough to reorganize an evening. Yet the experience does not tell us whether the sender is thinking of a red bicycle, a childhood kitchen, the number 47, or nothing in particular. A change in arousal can announce that *something* happened while conveying almost nothing about *what* happened.

Telepathy, if it is an information-transfer phenomenon, eventually has to answer an accounting question.

How much information crossed?

This is where the language of bandwidth becomes useful because it refuses to be impressed by richness alone.

Imagine the simplest hidden target possible: a computer randomly chooses left or right with equal probability. Before the target appears, the receiver has two equally plausible possibilities. A perfect response identifies one of two alternatives.

One bit has moved.

Now increase the target set to four equally likely choices. A perfect response distinguishes among four alternatives. That requires two bits.

Eight alternatives require three.

The numbers are elementary, but their consequences are not.

A claim that sounds modest—“he knows which picture I am looking at”—can demand much more information than “he knows whether I am looking at anything.” A claim of receiving a sentence demands more still unless the sentence is highly predictable from shared context.

Shared context is the loophole through which a tiny signal can feel huge.

Suppose two people have lived together for twenty years. They know the same routines, jokes, worries, relatives, rooms, television shows, appointments, and recurring conflicts. One person receives only a binary cue: yes or no. That single bit can steer a very rich internal model.

Are you thinking about tomorrow’s appointment?

No.

The dog?

No.

Your sister?

Yes.

Three bits of feedback can narrow an enormous subjective space because the receiver already supplies almost everything from memory.

Ordinary conversation works this way constantly. We do not transmit complete mental states. We send compressed signals into a receiver loaded with language, culture, context, and predictions. A raised eyebrow can stand in for a paragraph because both people know the paragraph already.

A hypothetical weak telepathic channel could exploit the same architecture.

This possibility matters because anecdotal descriptions often jump from a rich experience to a rich channel.

A receiver reports an image of the Hill, the presence of a familiar person, a sense of reassurance, and one specific feature that later matches the sender’s account. The resulting story may feel like a high-bandwidth exchange. But the actual transferred information, if any, could be much smaller. Shared culture, memory, and expectation may reconstruct most of the scene.

The right experiment separates transferred bits from reconstructed meaning.

Start with targets that resist prediction.

Random binary strings are ugly and therefore valuable.

A sequence such as 10110100 has no family history. It has no emotional resonance. It cannot be guessed from the Hill’s established landmarks. It does not reward a receiver for producing a beautiful metaphor.

If a claimed channel transmits arbitrary target-specific information, performance should remain measurable when meaning is stripped away.

Maybe accuracy falls sharply. That would be informative rather than disappointing.

It could reveal that the phenomenon depends on semantic structure or shared priors.

Maybe binary targets work but arbitrary strings do not. Then the channel may carry very little information per unit time.

Maybe familiar images work better than random geometric patterns. Then we can ask whether the advantage comes from receiver reconstruction, sender encoding, or an ordinary cue correlated with emotional salience.

Maybe only personally meaningful targets work. That would create a new hypothesis about state-dependent gating rather than a license to abandon controls.

The information budget forces every version to become specific.

It also protects against an easy statistical illusion.

Suppose a receiver is shown four possible pictures after a session and asked which one feels right. Twenty-five percent accuracy is expected by chance if the choices are equally likely and trials are independent. If performance is 35 percent over a small number of trials, the result may look intriguing but the uncertainty can be large. If the same accuracy persists over hundreds of preregistered trials and independent labs, the evidentiary situation changes.

Accuracy alone is not the whole story.

Errors contain information too.

A system that selects the correct target 70 percent of the time from two options carries less than one perfect bit per trial because errors make the receiver uncertain. Information theory gives us ways to quantify that reduction. We do not need to turn the book into a communications textbook to appreciate the principle: a noisy channel has a capacity, and repeated errors reduce the amount of dependable information it conveys.

That suggests a powerful experimental sequence.

Do not ask first whether someone can receive an essay.

Measure a psychometric curve.

Two alternatives.

Four.

Eight.

Sixteen.

Then short arbitrary strings.

Vary the time allowed for sending.

Plot information recovered against target entropy and time.

If there is a channel, its limits should begin to appear.

Perhaps it tops out at one reliable binary distinction every thirty seconds.

Perhaps it improves with practice.

Perhaps there is no measurable capacity at all.

Perhaps performance is high for images but vanishes for random symbols.

Each pattern narrows mechanism.

A channel with a low capacity could still matter biologically.

Many control systems do a great deal with little information.

A thermostat needs only enough information to decide whether the environment is above or below a set point. A neuron’s spike train can be analyzed in relation to stimulus variables without imagining that each spike contains a sentence. A small number of bits can guide behavior when the receiver already has a structured model of the world.

The question is not whether one bit is impressive.

Under the right controls, one unexplained bit would be revolutionary.

The question is what one bit licenses us to infer.

It does not license unlimited mind reading.

It does not establish transfer of imagery.

It does not establish a shared realm.

It establishes that one binary target variable influenced one receiver’s response beyond the tested ordinary channels.

That is enough.

Scientific revolutions do not need to arrive as complete mythologies.

The information budget becomes especially useful with the Hill because a shared landscape is full of redundancy.

Once a community has discussed a hill, paths, people, meeting places, weather, colors, buildings, or symbolic objects, later reports are no longer samples from an unlimited space. They are drawn from a vocabulary.

Two independent reports both mentioning a tree can feel striking until we ask how common trees are in the shared imagery.

Two reports both mentioning a transparent blue arch containing three orange spheres are different, especially if that target was generated randomly after isolation and had never appeared in the community’s prior records.

The amount of surprise depends on the alternative possibilities.

This is why target-bank design is an information problem.

Targets should be distinguishable from one another along prespecified features. They should not share so much semantic content that vague responses fit many choices. The decoys should be matched for complexity and category. A “red bird” target should not be compared against “industrial pump,” “tax form,” and “triangle” if the scoring system rewards any mention of nature.

Better decoys would preserve structure while changing information-bearing details.

Blue arch with three orange spheres.

Red arch with three orange spheres.

Blue arch with five orange spheres.

Blue bridge with three orange spheres.

Now the receiver has to identify features that discriminate the target, not merely produce language from the same semantic neighborhood.

This is where blinded ranking can outperform impressionistic matching.

Give independent judges the receiver’s report and several candidate targets. Ask them to rank which target best fits. Judges do not know which is correct. If the true target is repeatedly ranked first beyond chance, the result is easy to interpret.

Even better, convert target descriptions into machine-readable features before data collection. Color. Shape. Number. Material. Orientation. Position. Motion. Then score exact or graded matches according to a preregistered rule.

The scoring system itself has an information budget.

If researchers can reinterpret a response after seeing the target, they can manufacture information through flexibility.

“The orange spheres” becomes “warm lights.”

“Arch” becomes “gateway.”

“Transparent” becomes “spiritual.”

A poetic account can be made to resemble almost anything if the match is allowed to expand after the fact.

This is not unique to paranormal research. Flexible outcome definitions create false confidence across science. The cure is the same: define the coding rules before inspecting which target was correct.

The information budget also exposes the difference between recognition and production.

Recognition is cheaper.

If the receiver chooses among four displayed targets, the task requires discrimination among four alternatives. If the receiver must freely produce the target from an unconstrained space, success is much more surprising—but scoring also becomes harder.

A robust research program should use both.

Forced choice provides clean statistics.

Free report tests whether the channel supports richer content without researcher-supplied options.

The two results can be compared.

If forced choice succeeds but free report does not, perhaps the receiver gets only weak evidence that can bias a decision among known options. If free reports contain detailed target features beyond what forced-choice performance predicts, the claimed capacity is larger and needs stronger replication.

Timing adds another dimension.

How long does it take to move information?

A sender may stare at a target for ten seconds, a minute, or an hour. If performance improves with exposure time, estimate the slope. A genuine channel may show diminishing returns or saturation. If ten minutes carries no more information than one second, that constrains encoding models.

The receiver’s integration time matters too.

Does accuracy improve when the receiver can wait?

Does the response arrive as a sudden percept or accumulate gradually?

Can confidence predict correctness?

Calibration is revealing. If receivers report high confidence on both hits and misses, subjective certainty is a poor readout of signal strength. If confidence tracks accuracy, it may provide a useful internal measure.

One can even estimate an error-correction profile.

Ordinary communication channels become reliable by redundancy. We repeat ourselves. We use grammar. We ask for clarification. Digital systems add check bits and retransmission. A low-bandwidth biological channel might also become more reliable when the sender repeats or encodes a target redundantly.

That gives an experiment.

Send the same bit once.

Then repeat it in a fixed schedule.

Then encode it across multiple target presentations.

If accuracy improves according to repetition, we have learned something about noise and integration.

If repetition has no effect, either the channel behaves differently or there is no stable signal to accumulate.

Shared priors complicate everything in familiar dyads.

A parent may predict a child’s likely thought with extraordinary accuracy because the parent knows the child. A child may predict a parent for the same reason. This is not a nuisance variable to be waved away. It is part of the phenomenon people often describe as uncanny connection.

The cleanest way to measure it is to separate semantic predictability from hidden information.

Before the telepathy task, ask the receiver to predict the sender’s choices without the sender seeing any target. This creates a prior model.

Then randomize targets by computer so the sender cannot choose according to preference.

Now the receiver’s baseline prediction cannot help much because the sender’s target is imposed randomly.

Any improvement above that baseline is easier to attribute to trial-specific information.

Randomization is therefore not merely a statistical convenience.

It breaks the shared-prior shortcut.

The Hill offers a related challenge. If two participants have spent months discussing what the Hill contains, convergence later is expected. To test transfer, preserve their shared model and inject a random element that neither could predict beforehand.

Do not erase the meaningful world.

Add one controlled surprise to it.

That design respects the phenomenology while protecting the inference.

The information budget also creates a discipline for mechanism claims.

Suppose an electromagnetic model is proposed. The measured field at the receiver’s location fluctuates with sender state, but a classifier can distinguish target A from target B only 52 percent of the time. Yet the human receiver supposedly identifies one of sixteen targets with 90 percent accuracy.

The numbers do not fit.

The proposed carrier does not contain enough recoverable target information, at least as measured.

Perhaps the instrument misses the relevant feature. Fine. Identify another feature.

Perhaps the biological receiver performs nonlinear amplification. Fine. Demonstrate sensitivity to an artificial signal.

What cannot happen is for the carrier’s information deficit to disappear because the narrative is compelling.

Mechanisms must have enough capacity to support the behavior they explain.

The same logic applies to physiological synchrony.

Two heart rates can become correlated. Two breathing patterns can align. Two neural signals can show temporal coupling. But a correlation coefficient is not a message alphabet.

If the synchrony metric differs between target A and target B in a reproducible way, then it may contain target information. Measure it.

If it only distinguishes social interaction from isolation, it may carry state but not content.

Shared state is not shared content becomes quantitative here.

How many alternatives can the shared state distinguish?

How reliably?

How fast?

Those questions convert a slogan into an experiment.

There is also a humane benefit to doing the arithmetic.

It prevents a person from being burdened with powers they have not claimed or cannot reliably demonstrate.

If a nonspeaking participant succeeds at a two-choice hidden-target task, celebrate exactly that result. Do not immediately ask them to locate missing people, predict disasters, read strangers, or adjudicate family secrets.

A measured capacity deserves boundaries.

Boundaries protect both science and participants.

They also make replication easier. Another laboratory can reproduce a one-bit protocol far more cleanly than a dramatic open-ended demonstration.

The first laboratory that discovers a real anomalous channel will be tempted to make the result spectacular.

The stronger choice will be to make it boring.

A thousand binary trials.

Clean timestamps.

No target-aware person near the receiver.

No missing data hidden in a drawer.

A preregistered analysis.

Then another lab does the same thing.

If the bit keeps moving, the world will provide the spectacle on its own.

Information theory has a reputation for abstraction because it treats messages without caring what they mean.

That indifference is exactly why it helps here.

A bit does not care whether the sender calls the experience prayer, intuition, the Hill, resonance, or telepathy.

It asks a narrower question.

Did uncertainty decrease at the receiver because of information available only after the sender saw the target?

If yes, how much?

If no, what looked like transfer may have been prediction, synchrony, cueing, coincidence, or interpretation.

The accounting can be cold.

The phenomenon does not have to be.

A whisper is emotionally quiet compared with a declaration of love, yet both travel through the same air.

If there is a hidden human channel, the first task is not to decide what it means for consciousness.

It is to measure how many bits fit through the door.
