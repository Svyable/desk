# Chapter 14 — The Electromagnetic Problem

A mechanism can be possible in one sentence and implausible in the next.

Brains generate electromagnetic fields.

Brains therefore communicate thoughts through those fields.

The first statement is measurement. The second is an engineering specification nobody has yet satisfied.

Between them sits the electromagnetic problem.

The problem has three parts: strength, code, and receiver.

Strength asks whether a target-dependent signal from the sender remains physically available where the receiver is located.

Code asks whether different mental contents create distinguishable external patterns that could carry useful information.

Receiver asks what biological structure detects those patterns and converts them into neural activity.

A proposal that answers only one part is incomplete.

Start with strength.

The magnetic signals generated by coordinated neural currents are so weak outside the head that magnetoencephalography relies on specialized sensors and careful control of environmental interference. The NIMH MEG facility describes sensors capable of detecting fields around 10^-14 tesla outside the head. That is an extraordinary measurement achievement.

The number matters less than the contrast.

A brain is not broadcasting into a quiet universe.

The receiver sits inside the Earth’s magnetic field, near electrical wiring, motors, vehicles, phones, transformers, metal structures, and their own heart and brain. Ambient fields can exceed brain-generated signals by enormous margins. Instruments solve the problem through shielding, gradiometry, sensor design, spatial arrays, calibration, and signal processing.

A biological telepathy receiver would need its own solution.

Perhaps it is exquisitely local and subtracts common background.

Perhaps it responds only to a narrow temporal pattern.

Perhaps two people must be extremely close.

Perhaps a tissue geometry acts as a differential detector.

Perhaps the field is not the carrier at all.

Each possibility has consequences.

If extreme proximity is required, accuracy should fall with separation.

If narrow-band detection matters, controlled noise in that band should interfere more than broadband changes elsewhere.

If orientation matters, rotating sender or receiver should change performance.

If a specialized tissue is involved, stimulation near that tissue should produce measurable responses.

The hypothesis becomes stronger as it becomes easier to kill.

Now consider code.

A measurable brain signal can distinguish broad tasks because different neural populations engage differently. Researchers can sometimes decode whether a person is moving, attending to a flashing stimulus, seeing a category of image, or making a constrained decision from EEG or MEG.

But those decoders have advantages a distant biological receiver does not.

They use sensors close to the head.

They often use many channels.

They can be trained on labeled examples.

They know when the trial starts.

They can average noise.

They operate in constrained tasks.

They use computers that can fit complex statistical models.

A receiver across the room would have to perform useful decoding without electrodes on the sender.

This does not prove it cannot happen.

It tells us what evidence would be impressive.

Measure the sender’s external field at the receiver’s location while the sender alternates among randomly assigned targets. Can instruments decode target identity above chance there?

If yes, the physical channel has at least enough structure to carry the information in principle.

If no, the biological receiver must outperform the instrument or respond to a feature the instrument misses.

That is possible only if we specify what the biology is doing differently.

Then test it with an artificial source.

If the candidate target patterns can be synthesized, present them without the sender. If the receiver still discriminates target states, the field has moved from correlation toward causation.

This is one of the cleanest experiments imaginable because it removes the entire social story.

No parent.

No facilitator.

No Hill.

No sender intention.

Just a controlled physical signal and a blinded receiver.

If the proposed receptor cannot detect an amplified artificial version, we have learned something important.

The receiver problem may be the hardest.

Known human senses begin with specialized transduction.

Photoreceptors convert light into changes in cellular signaling.

Hair cells in the inner ear convert mechanical displacement into electrical activity.

Somatosensory receptors respond to pressure, stretch, temperature, and tissue state.

Olfactory receptors respond to molecules.

A telepathic electromagnetic sense needs some biological interaction that changes reliably when the sender’s field changes.

Where should we look?

One approach begins with anatomy.

Search tissues for structures capable of responding to weak fields at relevant frequencies. Characterize their physical properties. Test isolated cells or tissues under controlled fields. Look for neural responses that scale with field parameters. Then determine whether the effect exists at natural sender field strengths.

Another approach begins with behavior.

Present participants with controlled fields under double-blind conditions. Ask them to discriminate field-on from field-off or one pattern from another. If a subgroup performs above chance, measure physiological responses and localize the detector.

Behavioral sensitivity should come before speculative anatomy if no receptor has been identified.

A third approach begins with lesion or natural variation, though this is much harder ethically and practically. If a particular sensory structure is necessary, people lacking or damaging that structure should lose the ability. If no anatomical dependency emerges, the receptor hypothesis becomes harder to localize.

This is ordinary sensory science applied to an extraordinary claim.

It also reveals how unhelpful the phrase *brainwave sensitivity* is.

Which field component?

Which frequency range?

Which amplitude?

Which modulation?

Which receptor?

Which neural pathway?

Without those answers, “sensitive to brainwaves” is a placeholder.

There is another electromagnetic route worth separating: not direct detection of the sender’s brain field, but active emission through some bodily structure modulated by neural state.

Electric fish generate dedicated signals much stronger and more organized than incidental neural leakage. A hypothetical human system could in principle involve a biological transmitter rather than passive brain radiation.

There is no established evidence that humans possess such an organ for telepathy.

But the distinction matters because it changes what to measure.

If a transmitter exists, target-dependent signals might appear at the skin, in muscle activity, in emitted electromagnetic radiation, or through some other bodily output. Whole-body environmental sensing would be more appropriate than MEG alone.

Map the spectrum around the person during claimed successful trials.

Compare it with rest and sham trials.

Move antennas and field sensors around the body.

Check whether any anomaly localizes to the head, chest, limbs, or devices.

Then repeat in an electromagnetically quiet environment.

This is anomaly hunting with guardrails.

The guardrails matter because humans and laboratory equipment generate countless signals. A wearable device could produce target-correlated radio activity if its software receives trial timing. Muscle activity could change with attention. Power consumption of a stimulus computer could vary by image. Monitors can emit characteristic electromagnetic patterns. Even an LED display can leak information through electromagnetic side channels.

Security researchers exploit such emissions in other contexts.

A telepathy experiment has to assume the laboratory itself may be broadcasting the target.

The stronger the claim, the more aggressively the physical environment should be audited.

Use battery-powered equipment where possible.

Separate target generation electrically from the receiver room.

Shield cables.

Record ambient spectra.

Run target presentations with no sender present and see whether sensors near the receiver can still classify the target. If they can, the apparatus is the transmitter.

This control is beautiful because it turns an embarrassing confound into a discovery about the measurement system.

The electromagnetic problem also sharpens the Hill claim across distance.

Reports of a shared mental place are not usually framed as requiring two people to sit centimeters apart. If participants claim to meet while separated by buildings, cities, or greater distances, direct coupling from the weak near fields measured around the head becomes progressively less plausible as the mechanism.

That does not disprove the reported experience.

It means the mechanism has to be something else.

Perhaps the reports are produced by shared cognition and culture.

Perhaps some technological or ordinary information route exists.

Perhaps an unknown physical process is involved.

But “the brain has electromagnetic fields” cannot be stretched indefinitely across scales without a propagation model.

Range is a merciless filter.

If claimed performance is equally strong at ten centimeters and ten thousand kilometers, any local field model must explain why attenuation does not matter.

If performance declines with distance, fit the decline.

Does it resemble any measured environmental signal?

Can the distance curve predict unseen data?

Mechanisms reveal themselves through scaling laws.

Timing provides another filter.

Electromagnetic propagation over human distances is effectively fast relative to neural processing, but a biological receiver and cognitive decoder would introduce delays. A claimed effect with consistent multi-second latency may reflect neural integration rather than carrier travel. An effect that appears before the sender sees the target cannot be explained by ordinary forward electromagnetic transfer at all; it would more likely indicate analysis error, protocol leakage, or a different claim such as precognition.

Good clocks can eliminate metaphysical confusion.

Synchronize sender and receiver systems precisely.

Timestamp target generation.

Timestamp sender exposure.

Timestamp physiological changes.

Timestamp receiver choice.

Make the causal order public.

The electromagnetic problem is therefore not one experiment.

It is a chain.

First establish behavioral transfer.

Then measure fields during transfer.

Then show target information in the candidate field.

Then show the field is physically available at the receiver.

Then demonstrate receiver sensitivity to an artificial version.

Then perturb or shield the field and reduce behavioral performance.

Then reproduce the chain elsewhere.

Each link can fail independently.

If behavioral transfer never establishes, field studies are fishing expeditions.

If behavior establishes but no field carries target information, look for another mechanism.

If the field carries information but artificial exposure does nothing, the receiver may not use that field.

If shielding leaves behavior untouched, the field may be a correlate rather than the carrier.

This layered logic is what makes a mechanism credible.

It is also how a revolutionary finding would become convincing without requiring anyone to undergo a conversion experience.

Imagine the full chain succeeding.

Receivers identify concealed targets above chance across independent labs.

A target-dependent weak electromagnetic pattern is measured outside the sender.

The same pattern remains measurable at the receiver’s position.

Artificial reproduction biases receiver choices in the predicted direction.

Shielding or adding matched noise reduces both neural response and behavioral accuracy.

The effect returns when the shield is removed.

At that point, the question “Do you believe in telepathy?” would sound almost quaint.

There would be a sensory system to study.

Textbooks would not need faith.

They would need diagrams.

We are nowhere near that evidentiary chain today.

That is not a statement about what nature is allowed to contain.

It is a statement about what has been demonstrated.

The difference is the entire discipline.

Electromagnetism is attractive because it is real, invisible, and already connected to neural activity. Those qualities make it a plausible place to ask questions and an easy place to smuggle conclusions.

The right attitude is neither “brain fields prove mind reading” nor “weak fields make all anomalous reports impossible.”

It is to let the fields fail or succeed on measurements.

Strength.

Code.

Receiver.

If all three appear, we have a mechanism.

Until then, we have a candidate with a large unpaid bill.
