# Chapter 7 — Eyes Before Fingers

The eye often arrives before the hand.

Reach for a coffee cup and your gaze will usually settle where the movement needs to go. Read a word and the eyes jump through it before the mouth can finish saying it. Type a familiar phrase and visual attention, motor planning, memory, and finger movement overlap in time.

That sequence gives researchers a way to look inside an action without asking the actor to explain it.

In the controversy around assisted spelling, anticipatory gaze has become unusually important because it can reveal target selection before contact with the board.

If a nonspeaking person looks toward the letter *K*, then points to *K*, then looks toward *N* before pointing to *N*, the eye movement contains information about the upcoming motor target. Repeat that pattern across a long sequence and the most simplistic form of external steering becomes harder to sustain. The user is not merely discovering where the finger landed after somebody else moved the board. Their visual system is participating in selection ahead of the movement.

The 2020 eye-tracking study of nine experienced letterboard users drew attention for exactly this reason. Participants wore head-mounted eye trackers while answering questions in a familiar spelling context. They pointed independently while an assistant held the letterboard. The researchers reported that gaze typically reached letters before the finger did, spelling errors were uncommon, and timing patterns resembled aspects of fluent spelling production. They interpreted the data as evidence that these participants were actively directing their own pointing.

The study is worth lingering over because it demonstrates what good intermediate evidence can do.

It did not answer every question.

It changed which explanations remained plausible.

Before eye tracking, an observer can tell many causal stories about a moving board and pointing finger. After repeated evidence that the speller fixates target letters in advance, any explanation has to account for those fixations too.

This is scientific progress even when the larger controversy remains unresolved.

The mistake would be turning that progress into a universal certificate.

Gaze does not carry a notarized statement of authorship.

People look at things for many reasons. An assistant’s movement could draw gaze toward a region. A familiar question can make likely letters predictable. The eye tracker itself has calibration error. Some letters sit close together. Head movement and board movement complicate coordinate systems. The participant may visually anticipate a letter without necessarily controlling every semantic feature of the message.

Most importantly, anticipatory gaze in a shared-knowledge conversational task is not the same thing as proving that the participant can communicate information the assistant does not know.

The next experiment is obvious.

Keep the eye tracker.

Separate knowledge.

Now the gaze stream becomes a causal witness.

The participant sees a hidden target. The assistant holding the board does not. If the participant looks ahead to letters spelling the correct target, the evidence for independent content selection becomes much stronger. If the assistant sees a different target, researchers can ask whether participant gaze follows participant knowledge or assistant knowledge. If the board begins moving toward the assistant’s target before the participant fixates it, that pattern tells a different story.

The temporal order matters.

But temporal order alone can mislead when a dyad predicts one another.

Suppose the participant begins a small hand movement toward the upper-left region of the board. The assistant, skilled through years of practice, predicts the likely target and rotates the board slightly. The participant’s gaze then snaps to the letter now approaching. An eye tracker might show gaze before final finger contact while the earlier hand movement initiated the chain.

A complete analysis therefore needs hand, board, head, and eye on the same clock.

This is not technologically exotic. Motion capture systems can sample movement hundreds of times per second. Inertial sensors can measure board acceleration. Computer vision can track pose. Eye trackers can estimate fixation. Audio can capture prompts. A synchronized dataset can reveal who or what leads the trajectory.

That dataset would be fascinating even if telepathy never appears.

It could show how an unconventional communication skill is actually performed.

Some skilled actions depend on anticipation more than outsiders realize. A musician sight-reads ahead of the notes being played. A driver looks through a curve rather than staring at the front bumper. A typist prepares upcoming letters while the previous keystroke is still completing. Fluent action is pipelined.

If experienced letterboard users show similar planning, that is evidence of skill acquisition.

Skill has signatures.

Practice changes variability. Movements become more efficient. Eye-hand timing stabilizes. Errors acquire patterns. Preparatory signals emerge earlier. The performer becomes able to compensate for perturbations.

Perturbation is especially revealing.

Imagine researchers secretly shift the letterboard a few centimeters between trials while maintaining comfortable viewing. A participant who has selected a target based on the visual layout should adjust the pointing trajectory. A participant relying heavily on a partner’s usual board movement may show a different adaptation pattern. Rotate the board slightly. Change letter spacing. Swap to a fixed transparent screen. Preserve the alphabet but alter geometry.

The point is not to make spelling artificially difficult.

The point is to identify which coordinate system controls the action.

Does the user aim visually at letter identities?

At memorized spatial locations?

At board motion trajectories?

At cues provided by the partner?

Different answers imply different support needs.

There is an analogy in eye-gaze communication systems. A person may select letters by dwelling on a visual target while a camera estimates eye position. Calibration matters. Fatigue matters. Head position matters. The user may need large targets or predictive software. Nobody would conclude that because the device contributes heavily to access, the device authored the sentence. The informational control remains with the user if their gaze distinctions drive the semantic choices.

A handheld letterboard can be analyzed the same way.

The board holder may be part of the access technology.

The task is to determine whether the holder is also part of the semantic encoder.

Eye movements provide one window onto that difference.

They also provide a bridge toward the telepathy question because gaze reveals what a receiver is doing before an answer becomes explicit.

Suppose a participant is asked to receive one of four hidden visual targets from a sender in another room. The receiver sees four icons on a stationary display but is not told which is correct. Their eyes are tracked continuously.

If telepathic information reaches awareness only at the moment of final choice, gaze might remain random until selection.

If information accumulates gradually, gaze may begin favoring the target before the participant reports it.

If the receiver consciously imagines the target before recognizing it among options, pupil and fixation patterns may show a different sequence.

If no transfer exists, target-directed gaze should not systematically exceed chance before ordinary feedback.

This turns eye tracking from an authorship tool into a timing tool.

Timing can help constrain mechanism.

How long after the sender sees the target does the receiver’s gaze begin to change?

Milliseconds? Seconds? Only after a cue that the trial has begun? Only when sender and receiver perform a synchronized ritual? Does the effect depend on the sender actively attending, or does mere exposure suffice?

A claimed information channel with no temporal profile is hard to distinguish from post hoc interpretation.

A temporal profile becomes physiology.

The eye is also useful because it makes some forms of unconscious processing visible.

People can orient toward stimuli before they can articulate why. Attention can be biased by expectation. Familiarity can alter dwell time. A receiver may show weak target preference without being able to produce a correct explicit answer on every trial. If an anomalous effect existed at low signal strength, eye movement might provide a continuous measure more sensitive than all-or-nothing verbal reports.

That possibility should be approached carefully.

Continuous measures create many analytical choices. Which time window counts? Which fixation metric? First look, total dwell, revisits, pupil size, saccade direction? If researchers inspect enough possibilities after seeing the target labels, some pattern will eventually look interesting.

Pre-registration matters more, not less, when the data are rich.

Define the primary eye-tracking endpoint before the experiment. Perhaps it is the proportion of dwell time on the hidden target in a fixed two-second window after sender exposure. Perhaps it is the probability that the first fixation after a neutral cue lands on the target. Exploratory analyses can follow, clearly labeled as exploratory.

Rich data should create hypotheses, not retroactive certainty.

There is another reason eyes matter in this particular population.

Conventional social assumptions about gaze can be misleading in autism. Eye contact can be uncomfortable or distracting for some autistic people. Looking away does not necessarily mean not attending. A person may use peripheral vision differently from what a clinician expects. The social meaning of gaze is not universal.

An eye tracker therefore should not be used as a moral instrument.

It should measure where the eyes are, not whether the participant is “engaged correctly.”

This is especially important when researchers begin looking for subtle channels. It would be easy to romanticize unusual gaze as evidence of heightened perception. There is no need. The question is narrower: does measurable visual behavior carry target-relevant information, and if so, when and from where?

The answer may vary by person.

One participant may clearly select letters visually in advance.

Another may use peripheral vision and show noisy central fixations.

Another may find the eye tracker intolerable.

Another may rely more on proprioception than visual guidance.

The experimental program should not require one sensory profile to validate all communicators.

This individual variation is a recurring theme because broad labels conceal control strategies.

The phrase *motor planning difficulty* can describe many phenomena. The phrase *sensory difference* can hide several distinct processes. Good experiments replace umbrella terms with measurable behavior.

Where does the gaze go?

When?

How stable is it?

What happens when the target moves?

What happens when the partner is blind?

What happens when the board is fixed?

What happens when the participant is tired?

Answers build a model.

The same discipline can be applied to reports of mind reading.

Suppose a parent thinks of a letter and the child points to it. Before invoking transfer, track the parent’s eyes. Parents may glance toward the target unconsciously. Put the target behind the child while keeping the parent visible. Does performance remain? Hide the parent’s eyes. Use mirrored goggles. Replace the parent with a video feed delayed by several seconds. Put the knowledgeable person in another room.

These manipulations are simple because vision is a physical channel with known constraints.

If the effect survives complete visual isolation, vision becomes less plausible.

If it disappears, we have learned something valuable about the receiver’s sensitivity.

An ability to read microgaze at levels neither person recognizes consciously would itself be remarkable.

It would also explain why close relationships matter.

Familiarity trains perception. We know a partner’s neutral face well enough to detect a half-smile. Parents notice changes in children that strangers miss. Teammates read body orientation. Musicians anticipate breath. People who communicate without reliable speech may have unusual incentives to attend to nonverbal regularities because so much of interaction depends on them.

That is a hypothesis, not a generalization about autistic perception.

It predicts training effects and partner specificity.

If receivers are exploiting familiar gaze patterns, accuracy should drop with unfamiliar senders even when the task remains the same. It should improve as pairs practice. Masking the eyes should matter. Motion models trained on the sender should decode some target information.

Telepathy, depending on the proposed mechanism, may predict something quite different.

A direct brain-to-brain channel might not care whether the sender’s face is visible. It might even work with strangers if the biological apparatus is general. Or it might depend heavily on learned pair-specific coding. The experiment has to remain open to either pattern.

This is what mechanistic thinking does to an argument.

It replaces labels with dependencies.

Instead of asking whether the event was “really telepathy,” ask which interventions change accuracy and which signals contain information.

Eyes before fingers.

Hand before board.

Board before hand.

Sender attention before receiver fixation.

Receiver fixation before explicit choice.

The order of events does not solve the mystery by itself.

But it tells us where to place the next instrument.

Science often advances by finding the moment before the obvious thing happens.

The neuron fires before the muscle contracts. The pressure drops before the storm arrives. The immune marker changes before symptoms become visible. The eyes move before the finger lands.

A letter on a board is the end of a sequence.

If we want to know who selected it—or whether information arrived from somewhere unexpected—we have to look earlier.
