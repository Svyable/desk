# Chapter 18 — The Receiver Hypothesis

Transmission gets most of the attention because it sounds active.

Somebody sends.

Something travels.

Somebody receives.

But the receiver may be the harder half of the problem.

A signal can fill a room and remain functionally nonexistent to an organism without a detector. Radio stations pass through human bodies without becoming music in consciousness. Ultraviolet light reaches skin without producing ordinary visual experience because our eyes do not contain receptors tuned to the same range as many other animals. Ultrasound can exist around us without sounding like anything.

Perception begins with selectivity.

If a telepathic channel exists, what makes one human nervous system sensitive to another?

This question becomes especially important because public telepathy claims often cluster around particular people. Some nonspeaking “spellers” are described as unusually receptive. Familiar pairs may seem more successful than strangers. A parent may be “easy to read.” A participant may report that some minds are louder than others.

Those patterns, if documented prospectively rather than remembered selectively, could tell us whether the phenomenon is transmitter-limited, receiver-limited, or pair-specific.

The simplest experiment is a matrix.

Recruit several independently communicating receivers and several senders. Test every feasible pairing under the same concealed-target protocol. Randomize order. Keep target generation identical. Include sender-absent trials.

The result is not merely an average accuracy.

It is a table.

Does receiver R1 perform above chance with everyone while R2 does not?

Does sender S3 produce high accuracy across receivers?

Does one specific pair outperform every other combination?

Do familiar pairs show an advantage?

Does performance improve with practice?

Different patterns imply different biology.

A receiver-general effect suggests a trait of the receiver.

A sender-general effect suggests some people produce stronger signals.

A pair-specific effect suggests learned coupling, compatibility, shared priors, or a relationship-dependent state.

No stable pattern suggests the apparent successes may be noise or uncontrolled context.

This matrix should come before elaborate claims about gifted populations.

If a receiver trait exists, we can phenotype it.

Not by assuming which trait matters.

Measure candidate variables.

Sensory thresholds. Attention. motor control. interoceptive tasks. autonomic regulation. sleep patterns. meditation experience. anxiety. social familiarity. neural responses to weak sensory stimuli. perhaps controlled electromagnetic sensitivity if the carrier hypothesis warrants it.

Then test whether any variable predicts performance on future hidden-target trials.

The future part matters.

A trait discovered after fishing through hundreds of measures can look meaningful by chance. Use one sample to generate hypotheses and another to confirm them.

This protects a vulnerable field from finding a “telepathy phenotype” everywhere it looks.

Autism creates a special temptation here.

Because autistic sensory experience can differ from non-autistic experience in many ways, it is easy to leap from *different* to *more sensitive to hidden signals*. Research on sensory processing and interoception is heterogeneous. Some individuals report intense sensitivity in particular domains; others have reduced sensitivity or difficulty interpreting internal states; measures vary with age and task. There is no scientific basis for treating autistic people as a uniform population with enhanced reception of subtle energies.

A receiver hypothesis has to be individual and measured.

Suppose a participant reports hearing sounds others miss. Test auditory thresholds.

Suppose they report sensing physiological changes in others. Test discrimination of controlled breathing, heartbeat-related cues, or facial microchanges through known channels.

Suppose they show exceptional performance in telepathy trials. Ask whether that performance correlates with any measured sensory or autonomic trait.

If no relation appears, do not force one.

There is another receiver possibility: the relevant signal may never enter conscious perception directly.

The receiver might not experience a voice, image, or sensation. A weak input could bias attention or choice. The person might simply feel drawn toward one target.

This is common in ordinary perception. Subthreshold or ambiguous inputs can influence behavior without producing a clear conscious object. Context can bias interpretation. Motor preparation can shift before a person verbalizes a decision.

A telepathy experiment should therefore measure more than final answers if a behavioral effect is established.

Reaction time can reveal partial information.

Eye tracking can reveal target preference.

Pupil changes can reveal orienting.

EEG can reveal differential processing.

Confidence can reveal subjective access.

A receiver might perform only slightly above chance but show faster responses on correct trials. Or gaze may spend more time on the correct target even when the final answer is wrong.

These patterns could help identify where the signal enters processing.

They could also arise from ordinary biases and require the same controls as everything else.

A particularly powerful method is signal detection theory.

Instead of asking only how many answers were correct, separate sensitivity from response bias. A receiver may favor one option. They may say “yes” frequently. They may become conservative after errors. Proper task design can estimate whether they actually discriminate signal states or simply change criterion.

This is basic psychophysics.

If telepathy is a sense, psychophysics is where it should eventually live.

Thresholds.

Dose-response curves.

Adaptation.

Masking.

Temporal integration.

Discrimination functions.

Individual variation.

These concepts are more useful than asking whether somebody is “psychic.”

Imagine that a behavioral effect survives replication. The next question is sensitivity as a function of signal strength.

But what counts as signal strength?

For an electromagnetic hypothesis, measured field amplitude might.

For a sender-attention hypothesis, the signal may scale with neural engagement or repetition.

For a pair-coupling hypothesis, synchrony may index readiness.

For an unknown channel, researchers can begin operationally: vary sender effort, duration, distance, or number of senders and see whether accuracy changes monotonically.

A real sensory system often has a psychometric curve.

At low intensity, performance approaches chance.

As intensity rises, discrimination improves and may eventually saturate.

If telepathy accuracy is completely unrelated to every plausible “dose” and varies unpredictably, mechanism becomes harder to infer.

That does not disprove the phenomenon if the behavioral effect is real.

It tells us the manipulated variable was not the signal strength.

Receiver adaptation offers another test.

Known sensory systems change response with sustained input. Smells fade from awareness. Repeated touch becomes less salient. Visual systems adapt to light levels. Neurons habituate to repeated stimuli in many contexts.

Would a telepathic receptor adapt?

If the same sender transmits the same target category repeatedly, does accuracy decline?

Does a rest period restore it?

Does alternating targets reduce adaptation?

The answers could distinguish a sensory-like mechanism from a one-off inferential strategy.

Masking is equally important.

Every sensory channel can be overwhelmed by competing signal.

If one sender transmits target A while another simultaneously transmits target B, what happens?

Does the receiver report a mixture?

Choose one sender preferentially?

Fall to chance?

If two senders strengthen the same target, does accuracy improve?

These multi-sender experiments are ambitious, but they reveal whether the channel behaves like superposed physical signals, attention-limited communication, or something else.

Engineered brain-to-brain interfaces again offer a useful contrast. In BrainNet, two senders transmitted simple choices through EEG decoding, computer communication, and TMS to a receiver. The receiver could learn which sender was more reliable. The system’s architecture made competing inputs explicit.

A biological receiver, if it exists, may also need source separation.

How does it know whose signal is whose?

The social environment is full of brains.

If minds broadcast continuously and receivers are sensitive, why are we not overwhelmed?

Any strong telepathy theory needs a gating mechanism.

Perhaps attention gates reception.

Perhaps familiarity creates a learned filter.

Perhaps only intentional transmission produces a signal.

Perhaps physical proximity determines salience.

Perhaps the hypothetical receptor is usually below threshold.

Perhaps there is no such channel.

Again, each answer produces experiments.

If attention gates reception, tell the receiver which of two possible senders to attend to while keeping both active. Can they select one?

If familiarity gates it, compare familiar and unfamiliar senders under identical conditions.

If intention matters, expose senders to targets while instructing active transmission on some trials and distraction on others.

If proximity matters, vary distance.

Gating is not a philosophical afterthought. It is required to explain why a proposed sense is not constantly saturated.

The Hill reports may contain a clue here because they often frame connection as something entered rather than something involuntarily endured.

If participants describe deliberately going to a place to meet, perhaps the relevant state is attentional gating. The metaphor of a place may organize a receiver mode.

This is speculative.

It can be tested.

Teach a standardized Hill-entry routine to participants who do not claim telepathy. Does hidden-target performance change?

Compare claimed Hill users during self-reported Hill states and ordinary wakefulness.

Randomize whether a sender is actually present.

Measure whether receiver physiology differs before successful trials.

If a reproducible state predicts performance, isolate its components.

Breathing?

Eyes closed?

Reduced movement?

Expectation?

Familiar imagery?

Sleepiness?

Social belief?

The word *state* becomes a list of variables.

There is a delicate ethical issue in looking for a receiver phenotype among nonspeaking autistic people.

Extraordinary ability can become another stereotype.

A group long underestimated can suddenly be overburdened with claims of hidden superiority. The person may be treated as a portal, oracle, or proof of a theory rather than someone with ordinary preferences, privacy, limitations, and rights.

Research must actively resist that transformation.

Nobody owes the laboratory a miracle.

A participant who previously performed above chance can have an ordinary bad day without betraying a community. A person who performs at chance is not less intelligent. A child should not be repeatedly tested for telepathy because adults find the possibility exciting.

A receiver hypothesis should improve understanding, not create a new hierarchy of human value.

This also applies to speaking participants and non-autistic controls.

If the claimed channel is biologically human rather than autism-specific, comparison groups belong in the design. Perhaps subtle above-chance effects appear everywhere. Perhaps only practiced pairs show them. Perhaps only people selected from a telepathy-believing community do, suggesting motivational or procedural factors. Perhaps nobody does under blinded conditions.

Without comparison, population myths grow easily.

There is a further receiver problem that any semantic theory must face: translation.

Suppose the sender thinks in Spanish and the receiver knows only English.

What arrives?

A sound pattern?

A concept?

An image?

An emotional association?

Nothing?

Suppose the sender imagines a geometric shape the receiver has never seen. Can the receiver reproduce it?

Suppose the sender sees a random string such as Q7M2K. Can the receiver report exact symbols?

These tasks distinguish language-level from concept-level transfer.

If only meaning transfers, arbitrary strings should be difficult.

If exact perceptual representations transfer, strings may work.

If only affective state transfers, neither should.

A mechanism has representational granularity.

We can measure it.

The Hill makes this question richer because shared places combine spatial, emotional, and social representations. Perhaps, under an anomalous channel, spatial relations transfer more readily than words. Perhaps faces or people do. Perhaps emotional salience improves accuracy by amplifying sender state. Perhaps none of those variables matter once ordinary leakage is controlled.

Test categories systematically.

Do not let the most dramatic anecdote define the ontology.

The receiver hypothesis ultimately asks a humble question.

What can this organism discriminate that another organism cannot?

If the answer is a weak physical signal, sensory physiology should find it.

If the answer is another person’s target state, psychophysics should characterize it.

If the answer is nothing beyond known cues, the same methods should reveal that too.

A hidden sense, if it exists, will eventually stop being hidden because receivers will have thresholds.

They will have errors.

They will have conditions under which the sense works better and worse.

They may have anatomy.

They may adapt.

They may be masked.

They will be biological.

That is a more interesting destination than calling someone gifted and leaving the mechanism untouched.
