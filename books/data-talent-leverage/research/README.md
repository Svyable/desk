# Research Trail — Data Talent & Leverage

This folder is the evidence trail for *Data Talent & Leverage: How the New Economy Compounds Power*.

The manuscript should make a strong claim only when the mechanism is visible. "Data," "talent," "AI," "network effects," and "leverage" are unusually easy words to use as explanations after the fact. The research standard is to identify what information existed, who could use it, what decision changed, how the change was multiplied, and which measurable outcome followed.

## Evidence rules

1. Treat company statements as evidence of what the company says, not independent proof of effectiveness.
2. Prefer SEC filings, regulatory records, peer-reviewed research, official datasets, court records, and primary institutional publications for consequential factual claims.
3. Separate productivity from value capture. A worker can produce more without receiving more compensation; a customer can capture gains through lower prices; a platform or supplier can capture the surplus instead.
4. Separate data possession from data rights. Record who generated the information, what consent or contract governs it, whether reuse is permitted, and whether the claimed advantage survives if access changes.
5. Separate star talent from organizational capability. A famous person is not evidence that the surrounding team became more productive.
6. Treat AI productivity estimates as task- and population-specific. Record the task, baseline, sample, quality measure, worker skill distribution, and whether the study measured speed, output, revenue, learning, or something else.
7. Do not call every positive feedback process a network effect. A recommender learning from use, a marketplace gaining liquidity, and a social network gaining users are different mechanisms.
8. Preserve the strongest countermodel. Capital scale, market power, brand, regulation, luck, or ordinary management may explain a result more parsimoniously than the book's preferred loop.
9. Follow ownership. In each case, identify who owns the data, employment relationship, model, compute, customer interface, distribution, and capital.
10. Do not infer secret data access, hiring motives, or deal terms beyond the public record.

## Opening-case sources: Meta and Scale AI

- Scale AI, "Scale AI Announces Next Phase of Company's Evolution" (June 12, 2025): https://scale.com/blog/scale-ai-announces-next-phase-of-company-evolution  
  Primary source for Meta's significant minority investment, valuation above $29 billion, expanded commercial relationship, Alexandr Wang's move to Meta's AI effort, and Scale's leadership transition.

- Scale AI, "What Meta's Investment Means for Our Customers, Partners, and Contributors" (June 18, 2025): https://scale.com/blog/customer-trust-scale-meta-deal  
  Primary source for Scale's statement that it remained independent and that Meta would not gain access to Scale internal systems or customer confidential information.

- Meta Platforms 2025 Form 10-K: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026025534/meta-12312025x10kars.htm  
  Primary source for Meta's 2025 property-and-equipment investment and its 2026 capital-expenditure guidance. Use to establish that AI leverage is physically capital intensive rather than weightless.

- Reuters, reporting on the transaction (June 13, 2025): https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/meta-finalizes-investment-scale-ai-valuing-startup-29-billion-2025-06-13/  
  Secondary reporting for the widely reported approximately $14.3 billion investment and 49% stake. Keep attributed to Reuters unless a primary filing supports the exact amount and percentage.

## Data, rights, and feedback

  Primary description of public data, proprietary partnerships, and human feedback used for model development.

- OpenAI, business data privacy: https://openai.com/business-data/  
  Primary source for the default policy that business customer data is not used to train models. Useful for the distinction between needing data and having rights to use customer data.

  Primary example of interaction data becoming a recommendation signal. Treat privacy and regulatory implications separately.

## Organizational data systems

- Palantir Technologies 2025 Form 10-K: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321655/000132165526000011/pltr-20251231.htm  
  Primary source for customer count, revenue split, Apollo description, and Palantir's account of helping organizations use data in operations.

- Palantir Technologies Q2 2026 filing: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321655/000132165526000041/pltr-20260630.htm  
  Current public-company snapshot for customer growth and concentration. Use dated numbers only with the period attached.

Research question: how much of Palantir's defensibility comes from software capability versus accumulated customer-specific implementation, procurement, trust, and switching costs? Avoid treating the company's own "ontology" language as independent proof of a moat.

## Talent, management, and spillovers

- Bender, Bloom, Card, Van Reenen, and Wolter, "Management Practices, Workforce Selection and Productivity": https://www.nber.org/papers/w22101  
  Evidence that management quality, worker ability, pay, and productivity are complementary rather than separable.

- Bloom, Sadun, and Van Reenen, "Management as a Technology?": https://www.nber.org/papers/w22327  
  Large cross-country/firm evidence on management practices and productivity. Useful counterweight to founder/star narratives.

- Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Wang, "Superstar Extinction": https://www.nber.org/papers/w14577  
  Study of unexpected superstar scientist deaths and collaborator publication declines. Useful for spillover and key-person mechanisms.

- Azoulay, Fons-Rosen, and Graff Zivin, "Does Science Advance One Funeral at a Time?": https://www.nber.org/papers/w21788  
  Important counterevidence: outsiders' contributions to a field can increase after an eminent scientist dies. Talent can create spillovers and barriers simultaneously.

Research burden: distinguish salary from marginal product, and individual brilliance from the resources and colleagues that make the brilliance economically consequential.

## AI as leverage on expertise

- Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, "Generative AI at Work": https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161  
  Study of 5,179 customer-support agents. The 2023 working-paper revision reports a 14% average productivity increase, much larger gains for novice/lower-skilled workers, and minimal effects for the most experienced workers. Published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2025.

- Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon, and Demirer, "The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot": https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590  
  Controlled experiment reporting faster completion of a bounded coding task with Copilot. Treat the task and sample as narrower than software engineering as a whole.

Research question: does AI raise the productivity of high performers, diffuse their methods to others, compress skill gaps, or change which skills become scarce? The answer may differ by task.

## Distribution leverage

- Meta, Facebook announcement of the WhatsApp acquisition (2014): https://about.fb.com/news/2014/02/facebook-to-acquire-whatsapp/  
  Primary transaction source. Useful only if developed beyond the familiar valuation anecdote: messaging scale, network distribution, strategic access, and the distinction between user network and employee count.

Research lane: find cases where superior distribution defeated a technically superior product, and countercases where distribution could not rescue weak product-market fit. Avoid generic "distribution is everything" claims.

## Candidate narrative cases

These are research tasks, not pre-approved conclusions.

- Meta / Scale AI: separable ownership of company, founder, data rights, compute, and distribution.
- Palantir: customer-specific operational context as a potential switching-cost and workflow moat.
- AlphaFold: concentrated research talent turned into widely distributed scientific infrastructure.
- AI customer support: expert practice partially transmitted through a model.
- Frontier-lab talent markets: how compensation changes when researchers can redirect very large compute budgets.
- Quantitative finance: data, scarce researchers, capital, and execution systems as an older version of the loop.
- Defense software: classified data and procurement authority as leverage with unusually high institutional barriers.
- Elite law/accounting/consulting: whether AI encodes expert methods or commoditizes the junior apprenticeship layer.
- One-person firms: where modern software and model leverage genuinely reduce required headcount, and where sales, trust, regulation, service, or liability keep organizations large.
- Open source: cases where capability escapes the firm that paid to develop it and advantage migrates to deployment, data, hardware, or distribution.

## Falsification questions

- If competitors can buy the same data, model, and cloud capacity, what remains proprietary?
- Does additional data improve decisions after controlling for scale, capital, and management quality?
- Is the "star" actually producing spillovers, or is the organization paying for reputation?
- Does AI create new value or merely shift work to customers and reviewers?
- Are reported productivity gains durable after novelty, task selection, quality control, and integration costs?
- Who captures the measured productivity gain?
- Does a feedback loop improve the product, or does it merely increase surveillance?
- Could a regulatory or contractual change legally sever the claimed data advantage?
- Does concentration of talent increase innovation or create a single point of failure?
- Does leverage reduce marginal cost while increasing fixed-cost fragility?
- Is the company compounding an owned advantage or renting its model, distribution, and compute from firms that can change the terms?
- Would the thesis survive if the current generation of frontier models became a cheap commodity?

## Publication burden

Before a case graduates from research into finished manuscript prose, record enough evidence to answer:

**Input:** What data, people, capital, infrastructure, or rights existed before the claimed advantage?

**Mechanism:** What changed in a decision or workflow?

**Multiplier:** What made the decision travel farther than the original person's effort?

**Feedback:** What outcome generated new information?

**Capture:** Who received the economic gain?

**Counterfactual:** What alternative explanation could produce the same observed result?

**Failure mode:** What would break the loop?
