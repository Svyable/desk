# Matsushiro's Electric Weather

In August 1965 the ground around Matsushiro in central Japan began refusing to stop.

The swarm grew until thousands of felt and unfelt earthquakes were being recorded per day. A Japan Meteorological Agency account later put the cumulative count in the hundreds of thousands. This was not one clean mainshock with a before and after. It was a long mechanical argument inside the crust.

That makes Matsushiro unusually valuable for seismo-electromagnetic history.

Single earthquakes are terrible laboratories for rare precursors because there is one realization. A swarm produces repetition. Instruments remain in place while stress, fluids and rupture migrate. People keep watching. If electrical or atmospheric effects genuinely couple to fracture, a prolonged swarm gives them multiple chances to appear.

Reports from Matsushiro included unusual lights, geomagnetic variations and atmospheric electric-field changes. Decades later, Yuji Enomoto and colleagues revisited the sequence and proposed a mechanism involving hydro-mechanical rupture, carbon-dioxide and water movement, electrification of gas passing through fresh fracture surfaces, and discharge processes in the atmosphere. Their 2017 Scientific Reports paper is useful precisely because it is not content to say lights happened. It tries to close the causal loop with laboratory experiments.

The proposed picture is messy in the right way.

The crust fractures. Fluids and gas respond. Fresh surfaces matter. Charge separation occurs. Gas flow can transport or create electrical effects. Atmospheric electric fields shift. Under suitable conditions, discharge becomes luminous.

This is not a single magical precursor particle escaping the hypocenter. It is a coupled geophysical system.

The coupling also creates ambiguity. Carbon dioxide and groundwater do not wait politely for earthquakes to become interesting. Meteorology changes atmospheric fields. Thunderstorms exist. Local geology shapes fluid pathways. If an electrical anomaly appears during a swarm, assigning causal credit requires more than temporal proximity.

Matsushiro therefore sits in a different evidentiary category from a prospective alarm. It is a natural laboratory for mechanism reconstruction.

That distinction should save the case from both camps.

A skeptic does not need to deny every reported light to reject earthquake prediction. An advocate does not need to claim a practical warning system to argue that rupture can produce unusual electromagnetic phenomena. The swarm can matter scientifically even if it never tells us when Tokyo should evacuate.

There is another reason to linger here. Earthquake research inherited a cultural preference for mechanical variables. Stress, strain, displacement, friction, pore pressure, seismic velocity. These are obviously central. Electrical and atmospheric measurements can feel secondary, even decorative, because the earthquake is a mechanical failure.

But mechanical systems routinely create electrical consequences. Crushing ice electrifies. Sandstorms charge particles. Fracture produces fresh surfaces. Fluid flow in porous media creates streaming potentials. The oddity is not that rock failure might have electrical side effects. The oddity would be insisting that a planet-sized fracture system never does.

What remains hard is timing.

The most visually dramatic phenomena may occur during the rupture process itself, when crack growth, gas release and strong deformation are already underway. That can illuminate physics while offering no useful lead time. A lightning-like flash seconds before shaking arrives at one location could still be caused by rupture that began elsewhere. To a witness it feels precursory. To a seismologist it may be co-seismic propagation.

This matters because humans experience an earthquake locally, while origin time is global to the event. P waves and electromagnetic disturbances travel at very different speeds. A phenomenon generated after fault rupture begins can reach an observer before destructive surface waves do. It is a warning of incoming shaking, but not a prediction of the earthquake.

That is not trivial. Modern earthquake early-warning systems exploit exactly this separation using seismic waves: detect the fast, less damaging waves and send an electronic alert ahead of stronger shaking. If an electromagnetic channel could robustly outrun seismic waves, it might contribute to early warning even without days of precursor lead time.

But that would be a different technology from prediction.

Matsushiro teaches the habit the whole field needs: keep the clock attached to the mechanism.

Before nucleation, after nucleation but before local shaking, during strong motion, and after the earthquake are four different worlds.

The light does not tell you which one you are in merely because it appears first to your eyes.
