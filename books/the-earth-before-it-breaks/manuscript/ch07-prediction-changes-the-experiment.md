# Prediction Changes the Experiment

The moment a scientist says before, the experiment changes species.

A retrospective study asks whether a variable behaved unusually around known earthquakes. A prospective prediction asks whether the variable can identify an earthquake before its time and location are known. Those are not two versions of the same analysis. They have different opportunities for bias.

Retrospective work is often necessary. Rare events do not wait for preregistration. Historical catalogs, archived magnetometer streams and old satellite data can reveal patterns worth testing. The danger begins when discovery statistics are mistaken for operational performance.

Suppose an analyst takes ten years of ionospheric data and a catalog of magnitude-seven earthquakes. She examines several frequency bands, multiple spatial radii, day and night passes, different geomagnetic cutoffs, several anomaly thresholds and lead windows from one to thirty days. Eventually one combination produces a striking excess five days before shallow events at night.

That may be a discovery.

It is not yet a five-day predictor.

The method has effectively asked many questions of the same data. Even if each individual test has a low probability of a chance result, the search as a whole can manufacture significance. The obvious cure is correction for multiple comparisons, but that only addresses part of the problem. Analytical choices are often correlated, thresholds are informed by prior plots, and unsuccessful variants disappear from the final paper.

Then there is event selection. Large shallow earthquakes are easier to associate with atmospheric or ionospheric disturbances than deep small ones because their surface effects are greater. Selecting only events likely to produce a signal may be physically justified. It also changes what the claimed technology can do.

A precursor for magnitude seven shallow continental earthquakes is not an earthquake precursor in general.

The reverse problem is background selection. The ionosphere has seasons, local-time structure, latitude dependence, solar-cycle variation and geomagnetic disturbances. A background window too close to an event may contain the same process being studied. Too far away and it may represent a different solar or seasonal regime. A control region may differ magnetically from the epicentral region. There is no neutral background waiting to be sampled.

These are ordinary statistical problems. Their presence does not make the field pseudoscience.

Their neglect does.

Prediction also creates feedback. Once scientists begin issuing alerts, instrumentation and human behavior can change. Stations receive maintenance. Reporters call. Citizens send observations. Analysts scrutinize data differently. The system becomes partly social.

This was visible at Parkfield, where alert procedures were tied to combinations of possible precursors. The project was admirably explicit about operational response. That transparency also made failure visible.

A mature precursor science would embrace such exposure.

The best experiment is cruel to the hypothesis. Freeze the algorithm. Feed it real-time data. Hide the future catalog. Require it to issue probabilities continuously, including during boring months. Score every day, not just the days before earthquakes. Publish the false alarms. Preserve outages and sensor faults. Let an independent group run the scoring.

The resulting performance may look worse than the retrospective paper.

Good.

A warning system that survives that transition has become something different: infrastructure.

There is a persistent romance in anomalous science around hostile institutions suppressing the one signal that could overturn a field. Sometimes institutions are conservative. Careers, funding and embarrassment matter. But prospective testing is the antidote to both suppression stories and establishment complacency because the future belongs to neither camp.

An earthquake that has not happened yet cannot be persuaded by peer review.

That is why prediction changes the experiment. It removes the most powerful hidden variable in the entire literature: knowledge of the answer.
