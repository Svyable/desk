# Positive Holes

The name is unfortunate.

A positive hole sounds like a joke a physicist makes after midnight. In semiconductor language it is ordinary. Remove an electron from a filled electronic state and the missing negative charge can behave mathematically like a mobile positive carrier. Solid-state devices rely on this bookkeeping every day.

Friedemann Freund's geological claim is that analogous positive-hole charge carriers can be activated in common rocks under stress and may travel far enough to matter before fracture.

That sentence deserves neither worship nor ridicule. It deserves disassembly.

The proposed source begins with peroxy defects, oxygen-oxygen bonds incorporated into mineral structures. Under mechanical stress, bonds can break in a way that creates defect electrons and positive-hole states associated with oxygen anions. In Freund's experiments, stressing part of a rock sample could generate electrical currents measured through contacts outside the loaded volume. The carriers were argued to propagate through an oxygen anion sublattice.

If the effect is robust, it solves one conceptual problem that has always plagued earthquake-electricity stories: how does information about a stressed volume get away from the exact crack surface before macroscopic rupture?

A mobile carrier is an answer.

It is not yet an earthquake precursor.

The laboratory evidence has to survive several translations. The mineral defect must exist at relevant concentrations in natural crust. Stress must activate it under geological rates and temperatures. Carriers must migrate through real heterogeneous rock. The current must be large enough to emerge above electrochemical and anthropogenic background. It must reach a place where an instrument can distinguish it. And the activation must occur early enough, consistently enough and specifically enough to be useful before failure.

Each translation can fail independently.

This layered structure is one reason frontier fields become rhetorically unstable. Advocates may point to a successful bench experiment as evidence that a crustal precursor mechanism is established. Critics may attack the prediction claim and speak as though the laboratory effect therefore never existed. Both compress too much.

A useful book has to let a mechanism be real at centimeter scale and unproven at kilometer scale without experiencing cognitive discomfort.

There is precedent everywhere in physics. Friction laws measured in the laboratory require careful scaling to faults. Turbulence behaves differently across Reynolds numbers. Crack propagation in a coupon does not map trivially onto a tectonic rupture. Plasma instabilities depend on geometry and boundary conditions. We do not call the underlying effect fake because scale complicates it.

What makes positive holes unusually controversial is that the proposed scaling would be spectacular. Mobile charge emerging from deep stress could, in principle, alter ground potentials, ionize near-surface air, generate infrared signatures through recombination, influence atmospheric conductivity and contribute to luminous phenomena. One microscopic defect would become a bridge from mineral physics to the sky.

That breadth is scientifically productive and epistemically dangerous.

Once a mechanism can explain electric fields, magnetic effects, ionization, infrared anomalies and lights, almost any pre-earthquake observation can be attached to it. The theory risks becoming a narrative solvent.

The cure is prediction by constraint. If positive holes dominate a particular precursor, the model should tell us which rocks are favorable, how carrier lifetime changes with temperature, what current density should appear, how surface potential scales with stressed volume, whether signals should propagate through saturated fractures, and what spectral features separate them from telluric and anthropogenic noise.

It should also explain nulls.

Why do most earthquakes not produce famous lights? Why do many dense magnetic arrays see nothing convincing before large events? Why did the Parkfield experiment, with its extraordinary instrument concentration, fail to deliver a clean universal precursor when the expected magnitude-six earthquake finally came in 2004?

A strong mechanism becomes stronger when it tells us why it is usually invisible.

That may sound like giving the theory an escape route. It is the opposite if the conditions are specified beforehand. Biology is full of conditional effects. So is geology. A precursor restricted to shallow crustal earthquakes in particular lithologies would be less universal and more testable than a precursor invoked whenever a signal happens to appear.

The practical burden remains ugly. Earthquake prediction requires not just sensitivity but specificity. If stressed rocks produce electrical currents during countless non-catastrophic adjustments, then the detector may be hearing tectonics honestly while issuing useless alarms.

The most interesting possibility is therefore narrower than the popular one.

Perhaps the crust broadcasts aspects of damage evolution electrically.

Perhaps that broadcast is not an appointment calendar.

There is still a science there.
