# Rock Is Not an Insulator

A granite countertop encourages the wrong intuition about a mountain.

It sits there. It is dry. It does not spark when breakfast is placed on it. We classify rock mentally as the opposite of electronics: passive, heavy, electrically boring.

Geophysics has never had the luxury of that simplification. The crust conducts. Fluids carry ions. Mineral grains have defects. Pressure changes contact networks. Fracture creates new surfaces. Temperature matters. Salinity matters. Graphitic films matter. Metallic sulfides matter. Quartz is piezoelectric under the right symmetry and loading. Electrokinetic potentials can arise when fluids move through pores. Chemistry and mechanics keep trespassing into the electrical measurement.

This is why an electrode placed in the ground is not listening to one thing.

It hears the geological equivalent of a crowded restaurant.

The possibility of electrical effects during rock deformation predates modern earthquake-prediction programs. Laboratory workers have measured electrification associated with fracture, friction and stress in various materials. Some mechanisms are straightforward surface physics. Crack two materials and charges may separate. Force electrolyte through porous material and streaming potentials can appear. Change stress and the connectivity of conductive pathways changes. None requires exotic physics.

The controversy begins when local effects are promoted into a long-range pre-earthquake signal.

One family of hypotheses emphasizes electrokinetics. Increasing stress and pore pressure reorganize fluid flow; charged mineral surfaces and mobile ions create electrical potentials. Another emphasizes piezomagnetism or stress-induced magnetic changes. Another uses radon release and atmospheric ionization. Freund's positive-hole model emphasizes electronic charge carriers activated by stress in oxygen-bearing minerals.

These mechanisms are not interchangeable. A field observation described lazily as an electromagnetic anomaly can be made compatible with almost any of them after the fact. That is exactly why the mechanism has to make predictions before the anomaly arrives.

Where should the signal appear?

What polarity?

What frequency band?

How should amplitude scale with distance, stress rate, rock type, depth, groundwater and fault geometry?

What happens in sedimentary basins compared with crystalline rock? What happens beneath the ocean? What happens during large aseismic deformation? What happens during mining, quarry blasting, hydraulic stimulation or volcanic unrest?

A mechanism that answers none of these can explain everything and therefore almost nothing.

The positive-hole work is attractive partly because it offers a carrier rather than a metaphor. In the proposed model, peroxy defects in minerals break under stress and release mobile electronic charge states that can propagate through an otherwise unstressed rock volume. Laboratory experiments reported currents leaving stressed rock regions and surface potentials developing away from the direct load.

The ambition is unusual. Most mechanical precursor models keep the effect near cracks and fluids. A mobile electronic defect raises the possibility that stress in one region could produce an electrical signature some distance away.

That is precisely where scale becomes the trial.

The crust is not a homogeneous laboratory slab. Grain boundaries, alteration zones, faults, brines and temperature gradients are not inconveniences around the experiment; they are the Earth. If the carrier survives only in dry crystalline specimens under particular contacts, the field relevance shrinks dramatically. If it survives heterogeneous natural paths, the model becomes much more consequential.

There is another difficulty: stress is everywhere in active crust. If charge activation simply tracks stress, why are strong signals not continuous? The relevant variable may have to be stress rate, concentration near asperities, damage evolution, fluid state, or some threshold behavior. Every additional conditional makes the mechanism more realistic and the prediction problem harder.

The earthquake itself also produces electrical effects. Seismic waves deform rock over huge volumes, move fluids, shake cables, alter electrode contacts and disturb the ionosphere through acoustic-gravity waves. A beautiful post-earthquake signal can teach us physics while telling us nothing about the future.

This distinction is often lost because papers use the phrase earthquake-related to cover both sides of origin time.

Related is not precursory.

The book will be severe about that word because the consequences of confusion are severe. A country can fund basic research on seismo-electromagnetic coupling without believing earthquakes can be predicted next Tuesday. A satellite can be designed to measure correlations without becoming a warning system. A laboratory mechanism can be real and still fail every operational requirement.

The correct starting point is therefore modest and strangely liberating.

Rock is electrically alive enough to deserve instruments.

The instruments do not owe us prophecy.
