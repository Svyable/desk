# Space Weather Is the Adversary

The Sun is the worst control subject in earthquake science.

It changes everything at once.

A coronal mass ejection strikes Earth's magnetosphere and currents intensify. Auroral electrojets move. Radiation-belt particles change. Ionospheric density reorganizes over enormous regions. Ground magnetometers register disturbances thousands of kilometers apart. A precursor analyst staring at one station can mistake a planetary magnetic event for a local tectonic secret.

USGS geomagnetism researchers have spent years making this point because many alleged pre-earthquake magnetic anomalies weaken when global magnetic context is restored.

The Loma Prieta case failed partly through instrumentation. Other cases fail because the supposed anomaly is not local at all.

This is why space weather has to be treated as an adversary rather than a nuisance to be filtered at the end.

The prediction algorithm should be asked to survive solar maximum, geomagnetic storms, equatorial plasma irregularities, lightning seasons and human radio interference. If it works only after experts manually remove inconvenient periods, the operational system includes those experts and their judgment.

That can still be useful. Weather forecasting includes quality control. Medical diagnostics include exclusions. The issue is whether the exclusion rule is prospective and reproducible.

Satellite work faces a particular problem in the South Atlantic Anomaly, where Earth's inner radiation belt approaches unusually close to the surface and energetic-particle backgrounds become peculiar. Studies comparing DEMETER and CSES have noted that apparent alarm rates can be high in this region while the relationship to seismic activity is weak.

This is the kind of ugly result that improves a field.

A detector can be sensitive to exactly the wrong thing.

Geomagnetic latitude also means that an anomaly may appear in a magnetically conjugate region rather than directly over the epicenter. That is physically plausible because field lines connect distant ionospheric locations. It is statistically dangerous because every permitted displacement expands the search area.

A model must therefore specify its geometry before looking.

The deeper lesson is that earthquake precursor research is not a fight between signal and random noise. The noise has physics.

Solar storms have physics. Thunderstorms have physics. Ocean tides have physics. Cultural electromagnetic noise has schedules, infrastructure and economics. Sensor temperature drift has physics. The null model is not a flat line.

This is why extraordinary claims sometimes emerge from ordinary fields when analysts underestimate the structure of background variation.

The best skeptical work does not say nothing happens.

It explains what happened instead.
