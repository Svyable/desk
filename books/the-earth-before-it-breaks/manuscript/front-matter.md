# The Earth Before It Breaks

There are two bad ways to begin a book about earthquake prediction.

The first is with a prophet. Somebody saw a light, watched a dog behave strangely, measured a pulse in a wire, and knew the ground was about to move. The second is with a sneer. Earthquakes cannot be predicted; people who think otherwise are cranks; end of story.

Both versions throw away the interesting material.

The Earth is not mechanically silent before rupture. Rock deforms. Fluids move. Cracks open and close. Pore pressure changes. Minerals fracture. Charges can separate. Radon can move. Electrical resistivity can change. Stress can alter magnetic properties. The atmosphere above the crust is electrically connected to an ionosphere whose own behavior is being kicked continuously by the Sun, weather, lightning, human transmitters, aurorae and the geometry of Earth's magnetic field. There is no physical law requiring the hours or days before an earthquake to be electromagnetically featureless.

There is also no physical law requiring any pre-rupture signal to be large, unique, stable, localizable, timely, or useful.

That distinction is the book.

For more than a century, observers have reported lights associated with earthquakes, changes in ground electric potential, ultra-low-frequency magnetic anomalies, radio noise, ionospheric density changes, and other effects around seismic activity. Some reports are eyewitness material of uneven quality. Some are laboratory measurements. Some are instrumental time series. Some are retrospective selections made after the epicenter and origin time are already known. Some disappear when a failed sensor is examined properly. Others survive statistical aggregation well enough to remain annoying.

Governments have not treated the subject as merely comic. The United States Geological Survey has investigated electromagnetic precursor claims and is openly skeptical. France launched DEMETER in 2004, a microsatellite whose name expanded to Detection of Electro-Magnetic Emissions Transmitted from Earthquake Regions. China launched CSES-01 in 2018 and, with Italian participation, CSES-02 in June 2025. These missions carry electric-field, magnetic-field, plasma and particle instruments partly because lithosphere-atmosphere-ionosphere coupling remains an empirical question worth measuring.

Worth measuring is not the same phrase as demonstrated prediction.

CNES's own retrospective on DEMETER is unusually healthy on this point. Statistical work found perturbations associated with earthquake populations, but the agency cautions that nothing useful can therefore be said about the appearance of a disturbance before one particular earthquake or, more generally, about the possibility of predicting earthquakes. USGS is blunter: after decades of work, it finds no convincing evidence for electromagnetic precursors that provide reliable prediction.

The disagreement is not as clean as believers versus skeptics. It is partly a disagreement over the object being claimed.

Can stressed rock generate charge? That is one question.

Can crustal rupture produce electromagnetic effects? Another.

Can some earthquakes perturb the atmosphere or ionosphere? Another.

Can an instrument detect a population-level statistical association? Another.

Can that association identify the location, magnitude and time window of a future earthquake prospectively, with a false-alarm rate society can tolerate? That is the question the public hears when somebody says prediction.

A yes to any earlier question does not carry the later ones for free.

This book follows those steps separately. It begins with the physical insult we do to the word rock when we call it inert. It moves through stress-activated charge-carrier hypotheses, fracture electrification, gas and fluid effects, earthquake lights, the Greek VAN controversy over seismic electric signals, the famous magnetic anomaly before the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake and its later dismantling, the Parkfield prediction experiment, DEMETER, modern ionospheric statistics, and the current China Seismo-Electromagnetic Satellite program.

The evidentiary posture is deliberately asymmetrical. A null can kill a prediction claim without killing all coupling physics. A successful laboratory effect can establish mechanism without establishing field relevance. A satellite correlation can justify another instrument without justifying an evacuation order.

I am interested in the awkward middle because that is where science is usually alive. The field has attracted overclaim, selective hindsight and genuine methodological failure. It has also produced serious hardware, serious geophysics and measurements that do not become imaginary merely because the grand promise has not survived.

The question is not whether the Earth whispers before it breaks.

The question is whether we can tell its whisper from everything else making noise.
