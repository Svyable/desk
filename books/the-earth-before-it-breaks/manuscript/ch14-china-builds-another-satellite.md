# China Builds Another Satellite

If the French mission ended the story, skepticism would be easy.

It did not.

China launched the first China Seismo-Electromagnetic Satellite, CSES-01, in February 2018. The mission, also known as Zhangheng-1, was designed to measure electric and magnetic fields, plasma and energetic particles while investigating possible relationships among the lithosphere, atmosphere and ionosphere.

Then it built a second one.

CSES-02 launched on June 14, 2025. Italian institutions participate through the Limadou collaboration. The result is now a two-satellite constellation devoted in part to a question that American public-facing earthquake science treats with pronounced skepticism.

This is not proof that China knows something the United States does not.

It is evidence that national research portfolios assign uncertainty differently.

The distinction matters in every discussion of suppressed science. States fund things for many reasons: potential payoff, strategic anxiety, scientific curiosity, bureaucratic inheritance, prestige, domestic expertise, rival activity, or simply because a measurement platform can answer several questions at once.

CSES instruments also study Earth's magnetic field, ionospheric plasma, radiation environment and solar effects. An earthquake hypothesis can help justify a satellite without monopolizing its value.

Still, the persistence is revealing.

After DEMETER, after decades of criticism, after the failure of electromagnetic prediction to become routine operational seismology, a major state and international partners continue to put precision magnetometers and plasma instruments into orbit around the question.

This is the kind of fact that should make both believers and skeptics slightly uncomfortable.

Believers cannot claim suppression when the experiments are literally flying.

Skeptics cannot honestly say nobody serious studies the subject.

The scientific question remains where it belongs: in the data.

CSES papers have reported statistical anomalies in electric fields, electron density, ion density and energetic particles around strong earthquakes. Some analyses describe higher anomaly occurrence in the days preceding large shallow events. The mission literature also repeatedly acknowledges the central problem: a single satellite and a single parameter cannot reliably identify a true seismic precursor amid ionospheric variability.

That is not rhetorical caution pasted onto a triumph. It is the hard part of the measurement.

A two-satellite constellation improves revisit time and cross-calibration. Multiple instruments make mechanism tests possible. International participation can create independent hardware and analysis pathways.

But more channels also create more anomaly opportunities.

The next generation of seismo-electromagnetic research therefore needs more than better sensors. It needs better rules for refusing coincidences.
