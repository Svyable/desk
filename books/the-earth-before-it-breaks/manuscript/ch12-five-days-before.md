# Five Days Before

Five days is a dangerous number.

It is long enough to sound useful and short enough to sound causal.

A 2017 statistical analysis of DEMETER data reported nighttime ion-density perturbations near earthquakes larger than magnitude five, with a concentration around five days before the events. The study used a superposed-epoch approach: align many earthquakes at a common origin time, examine satellite measurements around them, and ask whether the statistical background changes systematically before or after rupture.

This is exactly the right tool for a weak population signal.

It is also exactly the sort of result that popular retellings can accidentally convert into a prediction algorithm.

The distinction matters. A superposed-epoch plot can reveal a small average excess that would be invisible in any one event. It does not tell you that an anomaly observed tonight means a magnitude-six earthquake will occur five days from now.

To invert the conditional, we need the base rate.

How often do comparable ion-density anomalies occur without a subsequent earthquake? How many earthquakes occur without the anomaly? How does performance change with latitude, local time, season, solar cycle and geomagnetic activity? What spatial radius is allowed between the anomaly and the eventual epicenter? How many days count as a hit?

Prediction is Bayes arriving with a clipboard.

The DEMETER literature contains both event studies and population studies. Event studies are vivid. Haiti. Samoa. Chile. Sichuan. Researchers can examine every available parameter around a major earthquake and find unusual behavior worth explaining.

But a magnitude-eight earthquake is not sampled from the world blindly after the analysis. It was selected because it happened.

The strongest evidence therefore comes from methods that define background systematically and include all qualifying events, not only famous ones.

Even then, satellite geometry imposes selection. DEMETER sampled only particular local times and latitudes. An epicenter did not receive continuous coverage. Strong geomagnetic periods may be excluded because they make interpretation impossible, which is scientifically sensible but operationally revealing: the precursor system may go blind exactly when space weather is loud.

There is also a timing asymmetry around origin time. Perturbations observed on the earthquake day may include genuine pre-rupture effects, effects generated after nucleation but before the satellite pass, or ordinary co-seismic consequences. A daily bin is too coarse to settle mechanism.

The closer the anomaly approaches zero time, the stronger the association may become and the less useful the lead time may be.

This is not failure. It is the ordinary trade between detectability and warning.

The most important thing the five-day result does is preserve a live question. There may be weak ionospheric structure associated with the preparation or occurrence of strong earthquakes. The effect is worth independent replication and mechanistic work.

The five-day result does not owe us a siren.

A mature field should be able to say both sentences without feeling that one betrays the other.
