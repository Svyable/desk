# The Greek Electrodes

The VAN method begins with a pair of electrodes in the ground and ends in a fight about what prediction means.

Named for Panayiotis Varotsos, Kessar Alexopoulos and Konstantine Nomicos, the Greek program argued that low-frequency seismic electric signals could appear before earthquakes and could be used, under some conditions, to forecast magnitude, location and time window. The claims produced an unusually public scientific controversy because they were neither vague folklore nor easy laboratory curiosities. They were operational enough to be scored.

That is exactly what made the argument useful.

The VAN group did not merely say that the Earth behaves electrically. Geophysicists already knew that. They claimed a relationship between particular electrical transients and future seismic events. Once a claim becomes prospective, the bookkeeping changes.

Which signals count?

Which earthquakes count?

How large is the geographical window?

How long is the temporal window?

When was the forecast frozen?

What happens when there is a signal and no earthquake? What happens when there is an earthquake and no signal? How are aftershocks handled? How are multiple candidate events counted? How much tuning occurred after the fact?

A prediction system lives or dies in these definitions.

VAN became famous partly because its proponents reported successes and partly because critics argued that the evaluation rules were too flexible. The scientific literature around the method includes technical arguments over selectivity maps, signal classification, statistics and the physical plausibility of transmitting electrical information through the crust over useful distances.

There is a common mistake when retelling such controversies. One side becomes courageous heretics, the other side gatekeepers. That story is emotionally efficient and scientifically cheap.

The deeper conflict concerns loss functions.

A researcher studying coupling can tolerate a weak, intermittent effect. A public-warning authority cannot. A laboratory can publish an anomaly with a modest p-value and ask for replication. An emergency manager must decide whether schools close, trains stop, hospitals alter procedures and families leave buildings. A false alarm has a cost. A missed earthquake has a cost. The acceptable balance depends on the action attached to the forecast.

This is why the VAN debate belongs beside weather forecasting more than prophecy. A probabilistic forecast can be useful without certainty, but only if its calibration is known and the warning threshold is matched to action.

Earthquake prediction has an additional cruelty: damaging events are rare at any one location. That makes prospective validation slow. A method can spend years accumulating ambiguous successes and failures while the observing network, instrumentation, land use and scientific criteria all change underneath it.

The VAN controversy also exposes how easily post hoc spatial windows can rescue a claim. If the predicted region is broad enough, some earthquake will occur. If the time window is long enough, the same is true. If magnitude tolerance is wide enough, almost any moderate event can be called close.

That does not prove VAN failed. It explains why scoring rules have to be specified before the ground moves.

The strongest version of the VAN research program would therefore look less heroic and more bureaucratic: timestamped forecasts, archived raw signals, fixed station calibrations, independent event catalogs, blinded adjudication, control periods and a scoring rule no one gets to change because the earthquake was inconvenient.

There is a lesson here for every anomalous field. The more socially contested the phenomenon, the less interpretation should happen after the outcome is known.

That is not hostility.

It is protection from ourselves.

Electrical precursor research has always lived close to pattern hunger because the signals are noisy and the events memorable. Greek electrodes did something valuable even if the strongest predictive claims remain disputed: they forced a fringe-sounding idea into a format where it could be wrong on schedule.

Science improves when claims acquire clocks.
