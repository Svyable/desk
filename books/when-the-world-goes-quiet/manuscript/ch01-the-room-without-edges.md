# The Room Without Edges

The interesting thing about an empty room is how quickly it stops being empty.

This is not mysticism. It is not even metaphor. Give the visual system a field with too little structure and people begin to see things that are not in the field. Reduce patterned sound. Flatten contrast. Remove edges, objects, motion, interruption. The first surprise is not silence. The first surprise is production.

Lines. Colors. Geometric forms. Faces sometimes. Scenes. Movement where there is no moving thing.

The laboratory name *Ganzfeld* sounds more exotic than the apparatus deserves. A uniform visual field can be made with diffusing material over the eyes and homogeneous illumination. Add unstructured sound and the participant is not deprived of energy so much as deprived of useful difference. Light is still entering the eye. Sound is still entering the ear. What has been removed is structure—the little changes by which the world keeps saying *no, not that; this*.

That distinction is the whole book.

In January 2024, researchers reporting in *Scientific Reports* compared hallucinations produced by Ganzfeld perceptual deprivation with those produced by Ganzflicker, a rapidly flickering visual field. Both generated simple and complex visual hallucinations. Simple hallucinations were more frequent. Ganzflicker generated more of the simple kind; the rate of complex hallucinations was similar across the two conditions. The authors were interested in the balance between bottom-up input and top-down processes. Their result does not tell us that the brain is a hallucination machine in any grand metaphysical sense. It tells us something cleaner: alter the structure of incoming evidence and perception changes in patterned ways.

A 2020 fMRI study of multimodal Ganzfeld went one level lower. During the altered state, the researchers reported decreased coupling between the thalamus and primary visual cortex. They proposed that reduced structured bottom-up signaling, with top-down activity still operating, could allow internally generated activity to be experienced as percept. Again: proposed. Not proved as a universal theory of hallucination. But now the empty room has circuitry.

There is an old intelligence story hiding inside that circuitry.

The story is usually told badly because the nouns are irresistible: CIA, MKULTRA, mind control, hypnosis, drugs, sensory deprivation. Put those words close enough together and they begin doing work that the documents do not do. A research program becomes a successful capability. An interest becomes a discovery. An experiment becomes a weapon. A destroyed record becomes proof of whichever missing result the narrator already wanted.

That is exactly the kind of inference this book is about.

On August 3, 1977, the United States Senate held a joint hearing on Project MKULTRA, the CIA program of research in behavioral modification. The hearing exists. The financial records exist in incomplete form. The agency's interest in drugs, hypnosis and behavioral manipulation exists. So does the destruction of many program records in 1973. None of that licenses us to fill the burned spaces with a perfected technology of human control.

The documented history is disturbing enough without supernatural assistance.

Long before intelligence agencies acquired their mythology of mind control, experimental psychologists were learning how dependent ordinary cognition is on patterned contact with the environment. A 1957 review in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* described sensory-deprivation research in unusually direct terms: people deprived of adequate perceptual contact showed an intense appetite for stimulation, increased suggestibility, impaired organized thinking and, in extreme cases, hallucinations, delusions and confusion. A 1967 study of 134 people confined for as long as seventy-two hours under varying deprivation conditions reported visual, auditory, olfactory, kinesthetic and body-image hallucinations in the total-deprivation group.

These were not occult initiates. They were subjects in rooms.

The intelligence significance almost writes itself, which is why we have to be careful not to write too much of it. A person who becomes disoriented, suggestible and cognitively impaired when ordinary perceptual anchors are stripped away may be easier to destabilize. That is a proposition about vulnerability. It is not yet a proposition about extracting truth. Breaking a person's confidence in the world is not the same technical problem as obtaining accurate information from them. Confusion can contaminate the product you wanted to collect.

This distinction is repeatedly lost in the folklore around coercive interrogation. A technique can have an effect and still fail at the task for which an institution wants to use it. Sleep deprivation works if the dependent variable is exhaustion. Isolation works if the dependent variable is isolation. Neither fact establishes a truth machine.

The same mistake appears at the opposite end of the cultural spectrum, where deprivation is treated not as coercion but as access.

Close the door. Dim the world. Reduce noise. Float. Fast. Chant. Stare. Sit in darkness. Go underground. Go into the desert. Go to sea. Enter a cave. Watch the horizon until it stops supplying events. Human cultures have discovered many ways of reducing the rate at which the environment corrects the mind. Some are punishments. Some are contemplative technologies. Some are laboratory protocols. Some are accidents of geography. They need not share purpose to share a variable.

What happens next is where the arguments begin.

A person feels another presence in the room. A face appears. A voice arrives. A scene unfolds with the peculiar authority of perception rather than imagination. The experience can be vivid enough that calling it *imaginary* is descriptively stupid. The person did not necessarily choose it. They may not experience it as thought. They may experience it as encounter.

But encounter with what?

That question is usually asked too early.

In a 2024 laboratory study, 126 participants spent thirty minutes in a sensory-deprivation context while researchers examined the feeling that another being was present. Uncertainty was positively associated with the feeling of presence. An experimental prime suggesting another person might enter did not show a clear association. That is useful precisely because it refuses the cheap explanation. The effect was not simply: tell someone there is a ghost, get a ghost. The relation among uncertainty, expectation, individual disposition and felt presence is more difficult.

Difficulty is not permission to jump categories.

If the external data stream becomes poor and a presence appears, at least four propositions are available. The first is phenomenological: the participant experienced a presence. The second is psychological: deprivation contributed causally to that experience. The third is mechanistic: a particular predictive, perceptual or neural process generated it. The fourth is ontological: the presence corresponded to an external agent.

An experiment may support one and not the next.

I call the error of sliding between them **ontological promotion**. It is the tendency to take a fact established at one level of description and quietly spend it at a more expensive level. A subject reports a figure; therefore a figure was there. A brain state correlates with the figure; therefore the brain manufactured it. A government studied the state; therefore the government validated the extraordinary interpretation. A statistical excess appears in a forced-choice or free-response experiment; therefore consciousness has escaped the skull.

Each *therefore* needs its own evidence.

This sounds like a skeptical rule. It is actually a rule against cheap skepticism too.

Suppose a Ganzfeld telepathy experiment produces a result above chance under a protocol whose randomization, blinding, target pool, judging procedure and preregistered analysis survive serious audit. It would be equally illegitimate to say, *Ganzfeld makes people hallucinate, therefore the result is hallucination.* Hallucination can explain content. It does not automatically explain correlation with a hidden target. If a target match survives controls, the correlation is the thing requiring explanation.

This matters because the Ganzfeld acquired a second life in parapsychology. For decades, researchers have used sensory homogenization in experiments testing anomalous perception. A 2024 registered-report meta-analysis covering studies from 1974 through 2020 reported a small positive overall effect, around .08. That number does not establish telepathy. Meta-analysis inherits the strengths and weaknesses of the studies it aggregates: publication selection, methodological drift, laboratory effects, judging flexibility, leakage controls and the distinction between statistical deviation and a useful information channel all matter. But dismissal by atmosphere is not analysis either. A small effect in a controversial literature is not made zero by the embarrassment of the subject.

Now the collision becomes interesting.

The same broad manipulation—reducing structured sensory evidence—sits in three literatures that rarely behave as though they belong in the same room. Experimental perception uses it to study hallucination and altered states. Intelligence and interrogation history uses isolation and environmental manipulation as tools of disorientation and dependency. Parapsychology uses sensory homogenization to reduce ordinary distraction and test whether information appears to arrive by an anomalous route.

The first literature says the brain supplies more when the world supplies less.

The second says a person can become more vulnerable when ordinary anchors are removed.

The third asks whether, under reduced ordinary input, some information nevertheless corresponds to something outside the participant's available senses.

Those claims are not mutually exclusive. That is the problem.

A subject in a Ganzfeld experiment can hallucinate vividly and still, in principle, score above chance against a concealed target. A prisoner can become disoriented without becoming truthful. A contemplative can have a transformative experience without that experience proving the cosmology used to interpret it. A pilot in an impoverished visual environment can misperceive motion without every strange aerial observation becoming autokinesis. The fact that perception is constructive does not mean the world is unreal. It means the interface between world and report has machinery.

Once you see the machinery, anomaly research changes.

The usual question—*did the witness really see it?*—is almost useless. Of course many witnesses really see what they report in the phenomenological sense. People really see migraine aura. People really experience sleep paralysis intruders. People really hear voices. People really experience sensed presences. The experience can be involuntary, structured, memorable and emotionally overwhelming. Sincerity is not the missing variable.

The missing variable is **correction pressure**.

How much independent, structured external information was available to constrain the percept as it formed? How quickly could an error be contradicted? Were there edges, reference objects, multiple sensory channels, independent observers, calibrated instruments, known distances, stable illumination? Was the observer fatigued, isolated, anxious, primed or staring into a low-information field? Did the report become more specific after social contact? Did a second sensor record the same property, or merely something at roughly the same time?

Correction pressure is not a score I can currently calculate. I do not want to fake a metric because metrics look serious. It is a proposed research variable, and it earns its keep only if it leads to better experiments.

Here is one.

Take the feeling of presence. Do not merely ask whether deprivation increases it. Vary the richness of external structure parametrically. Keep illumination energy constant while changing spatial information. Manipulate auditory structure independently. Measure when the presence begins. Require continuous confidence reports that do not themselves become suggestion. Record physiology and brain activity where feasible. Include sham sessions. Pre-register the analysis. Most importantly, introduce controlled external events at unpredictable times and ask whether the participant's reported agent tracks anything outside the participant better than chance.

If the presence is entirely an internally generated response to uncertainty, increasing reliable external structure should alter its incidence in predictable ways, and reports should not carry hidden external information. If some subset of experiences tracks external events unavailable through ordinary channels under leakage-resistant conditions, the internal-generation account becomes incomplete.

That is a defeat condition. Anomalous research needs more of them.

So does skepticism.

There is a habit in skeptical writing of finding a plausible mechanism and treating plausibility as closure. Sleep paralysis can generate sensed intruders; therefore this historical bedroom apparition was sleep paralysis. Venus can be misidentified; therefore this light was Venus. Radar can generate artifacts; therefore this return was an artifact. Memory is reconstructive; therefore this witness memory is false. These explanations may be right. But mechanism availability is not case identification.

The intelligence archive teaches the inverse lesson. Program availability is not capability identification. MKULTRA existed. That does not establish every claim later attached to MKULTRA. Government interest is cheap compared with government success. Intelligence agencies investigate possibilities because uncertainty has strategic cost. They also fund failures, chase adversary rumors, preserve weak leads, compartmentalize information, duplicate effort and misunderstand science. A file stamped by an intelligence service remains a file written by humans.

There is another reason to keep the archive in the book: it demonstrates that epistemic conditions can be engineered.

We often talk about perception as though the observer arrives at a scene with a fixed instrument called the human brain. Then we debate whether the instrument worked. But the instrument is state-dependent. Fatigue changes it. Expectation changes it. Fear changes it. Social information changes it. Structured sensory input changes it. Isolation changes it. The environment does not merely contain the event being perceived. It participates in the perceiver.

That fact has an uncomfortable consequence for the study of extraordinary experiences. The conditions traditionally considered conducive to revelation are often conditions under which external correction is reduced.

Darkness is one.

Isolation is another.

Monotony. Repetition. Sleep disruption. Fasting. Rhythmic stimulation. Long vigils. Featureless landscapes. Sensory homogenization. These conditions do not all do the same thing, and grouping them carelessly would be another category error. But many alter the ratio between structured external evidence and internally generated expectation. If an experience becomes more vivid as correction pressure falls, that is evidence worth having.

It is not a verdict.

The tempting materialist conclusion is that mystical and anomalous experiences are what the brain does when reality stops supervising it. The tempting mystical conclusion is the reverse: ordinary sensory noise masks a deeper channel, and quieting the senses permits access. Both are beautifully efficient stories. Neither gets to win by metaphor.

They make different predictions.

If sensory reduction merely unmasks an anomalous information channel, then controlled target information should become more recoverable under the relevant state, not merely more vivid. Specificity should increase without a corresponding rise in false detail. Independent laboratories should be able to reproduce the information advantage using prospectively fixed scoring. The effect should survive changes in judges, target pools and experimenters. It should be possible to distinguish *more experience* from *more information*.

That last distinction may be the hinge.

More experience is easy to produce.

The literature already tells us that. Sensory deprivation can produce perceptual disturbance. Ganzfeld can produce hallucinations. Noise characteristics can even shape content: a 2025 study of multimodal Ganzfeld found water-related auditory and integrated multisensory hallucinations were especially common, with the authors suggesting that brown noise's resemblance to water may matter. The supposed blank is not blank. The texture of the noise can leak into the world the participant constructs.

More information is harder.

Information requires a referent and a scoring rule. If I see a red lattice in a Ganzfeld, I have had an experience. If the hidden target selected before the session is a red suspension bridge, we now have a possible correspondence. But the evidentiary value depends on how many targets were possible, who judged the match, whether alternatives were shown, whether descriptions were time-stamped, whether sensory leakage was possible, whether the analysis was fixed in advance, and whether the effect repeats.

The difference between experience and information is where whole cosmologies go to die—or, conceivably, to become science.

The CIA did not discover this distinction for us. Neither did parapsychology. Neither did predictive-processing theory. It appears whenever a mind is asked to report on a world that is not giving it enough clean evidence.

There is a room without edges at the center of the problem.

Put a person inside it and wait.

Something arrives.

The first scientific question is not what it means.

It is where the information came from.
