# Hebb's Students

Donald Hebb did not set out to discover a doorway.

That matters because sensory deprivation acquired a mythology almost immediately. Put a person in a small room, reduce patterned vision, soften sound, limit touch, and within hours the mind begins producing material of its own. Once intelligence agencies became interested, the retrospective story practically wrote itself: scientists found a way to empty the mind, governments noticed, and “mind control” followed.

The actual sequence is less cinematic and more useful.

At McGill University in the early 1950s, Hebb’s group was interested in what happened when ordinary patterned stimulation was reduced. The subjects were not monks, prisoners of war, or occult volunteers. They were students paid to lie in cubicles. Their eyes were covered with translucent goggles. Their hands were enclosed. Auditory stimulation was masked. The point was not absolute sensory absence—an almost impossible laboratory condition—but a drastic reduction in meaningful variation.

This distinction keeps returning because the phrase *sensory deprivation* cheats. The nervous system was still receiving energy. The subjects could see diffuse light. They could hear monotonous sound. Their bodies still existed. What had been reduced was information-rich change.

The world had become bad at correcting them.

In the classic McGill work reported by W. Heron and colleagues, subjects found prolonged exposure surprisingly difficult. Cognitive performance deteriorated. Many experienced visual imagery and hallucination-like phenomena. They developed an appetite for stimulation so strong that material they might ordinarily reject became attractive simply because it was something.

That last fact is where psychology turns political.

If boredom can change what a person is willing to attend to, then an environment is not merely a container around persuasion. It is part of persuasion. Before anyone says *brainwashing*, though, notice the distance between the propositions. A person starved of structured input may become hungry for stimulation. That does not mean the next message can be written into the person like software. Increased receptivity is not obedience. Suggestibility is not programmability. Destabilization is not control.

The Cold War repeatedly spent that distinction.

## The cheap miracle

There is a particular kind of experimental result that intelligence institutions love: an effect that is easy to demonstrate and easy to overinterpret.

Keep someone awake long enough and cognition degrades. Isolate someone and their sense of time can distort. Remove patterned input and internally generated imagery becomes more salient. Give a psychoactive drug and behavior changes.

Every sentence above describes an effect.

None describes a weapon.

A weapon requires more. The effect has to be controllable, directional, repeatable, doseable, operationally useful and superior enough to alternatives that someone would choose it under real constraints. If I can make you confused, I have not shown that I can make you reveal a true secret. If I can increase your suggestibility, I have not shown that I can implant a durable instruction. If I can make you hallucinate, I have not shown that I can choose what you hallucinate.

This is the **capability gap**: the distance between producing a psychological effect and engineering a reliable operational outcome from it.

It is an obvious distinction. It is also where an enormous amount of historical writing fails.

The Senate’s 1977 MKULTRA hearings documented a CIA program with broad interests in behavioral modification, including drugs and other techniques. The surviving record is incomplete because many files were destroyed in 1973. The destruction is itself a documented fact and an epistemic injury. It makes reconstruction harder.

It does not make every missing capability real.

A burned filing cabinet is not a positive experimental result.

That sentence should probably be printed on the first page of every book about intelligence history.

## What Hebb actually gives us

The strongest version of the McGill story does not need a secret sponsor to become strange. It tells us that ordinary reality maintenance is labor.

Perception feels passive because the work is hidden. The room seems to arrive already assembled: wall, door, lamp, another person, distance, direction. But a nervous system is continually using changes in light, sound, pressure, proprioception and memory to keep that assembly constrained. Reduce the changes and the system does not simply power down.

It searches.

This is why the Ganzfeld belongs in the same intellectual family even though its apparatus and experimental traditions differ. A homogeneous field does not switch vision off. It deprives vision of edges and contrasts. A recent 2025 study in *Neuroscience of Consciousness* returned to this old phenomenon and carefully separated several experiences that earlier literature had muddled together: visual percepts, fade-outs, black-outs and blank-outs. The researchers emphasized how much of the phenomenology remains methodologically untidy despite nearly a century of study.

That untidiness is important.

An anomalous experience is often presented as if science has two options: explain it completely or concede the extraordinary interpretation. Real research lives in the irritating middle. We can know that homogeneous stimulation induces visual phenomena without having a final theory of every image. We can identify roles for adaptation, eye movements, attention and top-down percept formation without reducing every complex experience to one mechanism. The map can improve without becoming complete.

The 2008 electrophysiological review by Wackermann, Pütz and Allefeld is especially useful here because it resists a tempting simplification. Ganzfeld imagery was often spoken about as though the procedure merely pushed subjects toward sleep and therefore toward dreamlike material. Their EEG work did not support a simple hypnagogic account. The Ganzfeld steady state was described as activated, with alpha-band dynamics associated with attention shifts and percept formation.

The room is not simply putting the brain to sleep.

The brain is doing something.

## Hunger for structure

Imagine two instruments.

Instrument A is a camera. Cover the lens and its output becomes dark.

Instrument B is a generative model trained to infer scenes from incomplete data. Degrade its input and it may not go dark. Depending on its architecture and priors, it may become less constrained.

Human perception is not literally Instrument B, and fashionable computational metaphors have a short half-life. But the contrast catches something the deprivation literature makes hard to ignore. The nervous system is not a transparent pipe from stimulus to consciousness. When evidence becomes impoverished, perception can remain busy.

The question is what constrains the busyness.

That is why hallucination proneness matters. In a 2009 short-term sensory-deprivation experiment, participants placed in an anechoic chamber reported more perceptual disturbances and other psychosis-like experiences during deprivation; people already more prone to hallucinations showed more perceptual disturbances. The authors discussed source-monitoring difficulty as a possible mechanism.

Again, not a universal explanation. A clue.

Source monitoring is the mundane name for a profound problem: *where did this content come from?*

I remembered a sentence. Did I read it or invent it?

I heard my name. Did someone call me or did a fragment of internal speech acquire external location?

I felt watched. Was there another person, a pattern of environmental cues below conscious awareness, or a threat model running without enough contradictory data?

A healthy nervous system solves thousands of source problems without drama. Deprivation makes some of them visible.

The experience can still be completely sincere.

Sincerity and source accuracy are different variables.

## The political seduction

Cold War institutions did not need to be stupid to become interested in this work.

Suppose an adversary claims to possess techniques for breaking prisoners, altering beliefs, extracting information or producing compliant agents. You do not have to believe the claim to investigate it. The expected cost of technological surprise can justify research into possibilities that later fail.

This is one of the recurring errors in anomalous-history arguments: **funding is mistaken for validation**.

A government funded it, therefore it worked.

No.

A government funded it, therefore somebody with enough institutional leverage persuaded somebody else that uncertainty about it was worth money.

Sometimes that is because a phenomenon is real. Sometimes because an adversary is rumored to be pursuing it. Sometimes because a bureaucratic sponsor wants an option. Sometimes because the cost of a small research program is trivial compared with the imagined cost of being surprised. Sometimes because organizations are made of humans and humans become fascinated by weird things.

The archival fact is the interest.

Capability requires another file.

Preferably several.

Preferably with controls.

## Four ledgers

At this point the book needs to show its accounting.

**Documented:** sensory/perceptual deprivation experiments can produce cognitive changes, unusual imagery and hallucination-like percepts. U.S. intelligence agencies conducted behavioral-modification research. Congressional investigation documented MKULTRA and the destruction of substantial records.

**Credible but incomplete:** particular historical pathways by which academic deprivation research influenced specific intelligence techniques can often be reconstructed from publications, contracts, testimony and later scholarship, but causal chains need to be established document by document rather than inherited from cultural retellings.

**Disputed:** claims of reliable mind control, programmable agents, dependable truth extraction or other high-control capabilities are not established merely by the existence of behavioral research programs or by the fact that deprivation alters cognition.

**Speculative synthesis:** reduced correction pressure may be a useful common variable across laboratory hallucination, isolation phenomena, some coercive environments, contemplative practice and selected anomalous-experience protocols. That proposal earns scientific value only if it predicts measurable differences.

The categories are not a rhetorical disclaimer. They prevent evidence from laundering itself as it moves through the chapter.

## A machine for false certainty

There is a darker implication in the deprivation literature, but it is not the one usually advertised.

The frightening technology may not be a machine that lets one person write beliefs into another.

It may be a procedure that makes both people overconfident in bad information.

Take interrogation. The interrogator wants truth. The prisoner wants relief, survival, control, perhaps deception. Add exhaustion, isolation, uncertainty and fear. The subject’s cognition degrades. Memory becomes less reliable. Suggestibility may increase. The interrogator interprets behavioral changes as progress. The subject learns what kinds of answers alter the environment.

You have now built a coupled system.

It can generate statements.

That is not the same as generating truth.

The distinction resembles the problem in anomalous perception. A procedure can increase the volume, vividness or confidence of subjective material without increasing mutual information between that material and an external target. More output is not more signal.

This gives us a cleaner question for every extraordinary-state technology:

**What quantity improved?**

Number of images?

Confidence?

Compliance?

Hit rate?

Independent information?

Actionable accuracy?

Truth?

Those are not synonyms.

If the answer is “the subjects had stronger experiences,” the experiment has established something about experience.

It has not yet established a new channel to reality.

## The experiment Hebb could not run

Suppose we rebuilt the old deprivation paradigm now, not to reproduce Cold War psychology but to measure correction pressure directly.

Subjects enter environments with identical average luminance and sound energy but systematically different information structure. One room contains stable edges and identifiable objects. Another contains homogeneous fields. A third contains weak but genuine external signals embedded in noise. A fourth contains no signal but is otherwise matched.

Before entry, subjects complete measures of hallucination proneness, imagery vividness, absorption and source-monitoring performance.

During exposure, they continuously report perceptual events using a low-interference method. The protocol records when forms, voices, presences and scenes emerge. At unpredictable intervals, the system inserts objectively specified events too weak for easy conscious detection but above individually calibrated thresholds in some trials.

Now ask several questions at once.

Does reduced structure increase internally generated percepts?

Almost certainly, if the historical literature is pointing in the right direction.

Does it increase false alarms?

Does it change confidence?

Does it make weak real signals easier or harder to detect?

Do people who report more vivid imagery also extract more external information, or merely generate more candidates?

Can physiology distinguish an internally generated percept from successful detection before the participant reports either?

And then the dangerous question:

If an anomalous-information experiment is layered onto the same protocol under rigorous isolation, does any residual target correspondence survive after false-alarm rate, response volume, judging flexibility and individual perceptual traits are modeled?

That is the collision worth funding.

Not because “psychic perception” has been established.

Because the experiment makes the conventional and anomalous models pay rent in the same currency.

## No empty minds

The phrase *empty your mind* has survived because introspection makes mental quiet feel like subtraction.

The laboratory record suggests a stranger picture. Subtracting external structure can reveal how much active construction was there all along.

Sometimes the result is geometry.

Sometimes a face.

Sometimes a presence.

Sometimes fear.

Sometimes beauty.

Sometimes a researcher sees a data point and a government sees a technique and a mystic sees a threshold and a skeptic sees an artifact.

All four can be looking at the same event and buying different things with it.

Hebb’s students matter because they force the transaction into view.

The room becomes quiet.

The mind does not.
