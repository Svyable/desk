# Chapter 2 — What You Stop Noticing

The first time you tie a necktie, the tie is an engineering problem.

The wide end has to cross the narrow end. Something passes behind something else. A loop appears and then disappears. The hand holding tension cannot be the hand pulling the fabric. The mirror reverses the geometry. An instruction that sounds perfectly clear when somebody else says it becomes nonsense the instant both hands are occupied.

Years later, a person can tie the same knot while listening to a voicemail.

Nothing about the knot has become simpler.

The person has changed.

That small transformation is the appeal of the “four stages of competence,” one of those ideas that has escaped the training room and become cultural shorthand. In its familiar form, the sequence runs from unconscious incompetence to conscious incompetence, then conscious competence, and finally unconscious competence. It is often credited to Abraham Maslow, which is a useful warning about ideas that become popular enough to shed their paperwork. A related four-level formulation appeared in a 1969 piece by management trainer Martin Broadwell; Noel Burch and Gordon Training International later helped popularize a version of the competence hierarchy in the 1970s.

The exact genealogy matters less than the mistake people make after hearing it.

They treat the model as anatomy.

It is not.

There is no four-room structure in the brain through which a skill marches in sequence. Learning can be fast or slow, explicit or implicit, conceptual or motor, brittle or flexible. A person can move backward after a layoff, become newly conscious of a skill because the environment changes, or discover that what felt automatic in one context fails in another. Expertise is local. A chess grandmaster is not thereby an expert pilot. A surgeon can be highly practiced in one procedure and deliberately cautious with a new device. A native speaker can produce grammatical sentences effortlessly while struggling to explain the rule governing them.

The stage model survives because it describes a recognizable change in subjective experience.

That change is worth keeping.

At the beginning of a skill, attention is crowded by parts. The learner has to hold the instructions in mind and deliberately link them to action. Performance is slow because every step makes a claim on conscious control. Errors are often obvious only after they occur because the learner has not yet developed a reliable model of what “right” should feel or look like.

Then awareness sharpens.

The beginner learns enough to notice the distance between current performance and competent performance. This can be discouraging. It is also progress. Someone who hears no difference between a correct note and a wrong one cannot reliably correct the wrong one. Someone who cannot distinguish a good spreadsheet model from a bad one has no internal standard against which to improve.

A great deal of education is the construction of better disappointment.

You learn what excellent looks like, and for a while that mainly improves your ability to see that you are not there.

Eventually, with enough useful practice, performance becomes possible under conscious control. The learner knows the sequence, can execute it, and still has to pay attention. This is the person driving carefully with both hands on the wheel, checking every mirror because the instructor's voice is still present in the head. It is the language learner constructing a sentence by recalling the rule. It is the new manager rehearsing the difficult conversation line by line before entering the room.

Then the experience changes again.

The sequence compresses.

A driver looks through the turn instead of narrating steering input. A reader sees a word instead of sounding out letters. A musician anticipates a phrase rather than commanding fingers. A coder sees a familiar class of bug in a pattern of behavior. The skill becomes less verbally available at the very moment it becomes more behaviorally available.

That final change is what interests us.

The word “unconscious” creates trouble because it invites more mystery than the evidence requires. Some learned actions can proceed with very little awareness. Others remain available to consciousness but no longer require continuous monitoring. A practiced performer can usually bring attention back to parts of the skill, though doing so may help, hurt, or change the performance depending on the task. Automaticity is a better technical neighbor than unconsciousness, but even automaticity covers different processes.

There is no need to solve the vocabulary perfectly before seeing the phenomenon.

Think about reading this sentence.

Unless English print is unfamiliar to you, you are not consciously identifying the vertical line and the curve that make a lowercase b. You are not sounding out each word. You are not reconstructing the alphabet. The visual symbols arrive already organized enough that attention can move toward meaning.

That is an astonishing amount of hidden work.

A young child learning to read experiences print differently. Letters compete for attention. A word may have to be decoded piece by piece. Meaning arrives late because perception and pronunciation are consuming the available bandwidth. Fluent reading releases that bandwidth. The machinery of recognition becomes fast enough that the mind can spend itself on argument, implication, irony, surprise, and whatever else the words are doing.

Automaticity is therefore not the enemy of thought.

It is one of the things that makes higher-order thought affordable.

This pattern repeats anywhere a complex activity can be built from lower-level operations. Arithmetic facts retrieved fluently can leave more working memory for a multi-step problem. Familiar keyboard commands can free a designer to think about the design. A musician who no longer has to consciously locate every note can listen to timing and expression. A nurse who can prepare familiar equipment efficiently can pay attention to the patient instead of the packaging.

The novice's difficulty is not merely lack of knowledge. It is that too much knowledge is expensive.

Every instruction has to be fetched.

The cost of retrieval is visible when someone gives a novice directions that make sense only to an expert.

“Just feather the clutch.”

“Open a terminal.”

“Deglaze the pan.”

“Read the room.”

“Keep your weight over the skis.”

“Normalize the data.”

“Listen for the compressor.”

Each instruction hides subordinate skills. To the speaker, the phrase is one step. To the learner, it may contain twelve.

This is the first great paradox of competence: learning a skill often makes the skill look smaller.

The expert no longer experiences the same number of pieces.

Psychologists studying expertise have repeatedly found versions of this compression. Expert knowledge is not simply a bigger pile of facts. It is organized around meaningful relationships and patterns that allow many details to be treated as one configuration. A chess player does not have to remember every square independently when a familiar position expresses a known structure. An experienced clinician does not consider every possible disease from zero when a cluster of findings activates a well-developed diagnostic representation. A practiced athlete does not consciously issue instructions to each joint.

The world arrives pre-grouped.

But pre-grouping creates a problem for introspection.

Once a collection of operations has become one mental unit, the performer can forget how many operations used to be visible. This is one reason experts can be miserable at estimating how long novices will take. The expert's unit of work is not the novice's unit of work.

It is also why people sometimes mistake explanation for transfer.

An expert can give the learner a perfectly accurate statement of what to do and still fail to give the learner the ability to do it. “Relax your grip” may be correct. The learner may be gripping too hard because five other elements of the movement are unstable. “Look for asymmetry” may be correct advice to a trainee reading an image. The expert already knows which asymmetries deserve attention, what normal variation looks like, and how the rest of the image changes the meaning of the feature.

Instructions are often compressed files.

The receiver may not have the decompression software.

This explains a common experience in apprenticeship. A teacher demonstrates something once and says, “See?” The learner does not. The teacher repeats the movement more slowly and assumes the added visibility should solve the problem. It helps, but the real gap is not speed. It is representation. The teacher knows what to attend to. The learner does not yet know what the demonstration contains.

The same failure appears in organizations whenever a senior person says that a task is “obvious.”

Obviousness is frequently the residue of forgotten learning.

There is a more dangerous version. When a person can perform a task without conscious effort, the person may conclude that the skill is natural. This makes inequality in training opportunities disappear from the story. Years of exposure become “talent.” Familiarity becomes “common sense.” Cultural knowledge becomes “professionalism.” Access to mentors becomes “instinct.”

Some differences in ability are real before training. People vary. The error is to look at a mature performance and infer its developmental history from how effortless it now appears.

Effortlessness is evidence that effort is no longer being spent at the same level. It is not evidence that effort was never spent.

Watch a skilled bartender during a rush. The bottles, glassware, tickets, customers, ice, payments, conversations, and cleaning appear to coexist inside one broad field of attention. The novice experiences collisions. The expert has learned sequences, spatial arrangements, priorities, handoffs, and exceptions that prevent many of those collisions before they happen.

Put the expert in an unfamiliar bar with different equipment and a different point-of-sale system, and some of the invisible skill becomes visible again. Movements slow. Questions return. Attention drops into the lower levels.

This is useful because it shows that automaticity is not a permanent trait of the person.

It is a relationship among a person, a task, and an environment.

Change the environment enough and the relationship has to be renegotiated.

Technology does this constantly. A photographer trained on film learned to meter, expose, focus, process, and make choices under material constraints that digital cameras altered. A newspaper copy editor once worked with physical marks and production conventions that publishing software changed. Pilots move between aircraft with different automation philosophies. Surgeons adopt new tools. Accountants move between systems. Programmers move between languages and frameworks.

Old competence is neither worthless nor perfectly portable.

Some patterns transfer. Some procedures do not. Experts often move faster through the conscious phase because they possess deeper structures for understanding what is new. They also carry habits that can interfere.

This is why “beginner's mind” is appealing and frequently misunderstood. Experts do not become better by erasing expertise. They become better by noticing when a familiar pattern has stopped applying.

Competence must contain the capacity to become conscious again.

That capacity is especially important because automatic processes are fast enough to outrun explanation. If a familiar cue triggers a familiar response in the wrong environment, fluency becomes a liability. The better trained the response, the more quickly the mistake can occur.

Driving offers a mild example. A person who has driven for years in a country where traffic keeps to the right can travel to a country where traffic keeps to the left and discover how much of road position has become automatic. The rule is easy to state. The habit is harder to replace. Attention has to be deliberately recruited for a behavior that previously required almost none.

The old skill does not disappear when the new rule is explained.

This matters in medicine, aviation, manufacturing, finance, and any field where systems change beneath experienced operators. A veteran can be less dangerous than a novice because the veteran recognizes more. The veteran can also be more dangerous than a novice when old cues confidently trigger old responses in a new system.

The competent person therefore lives in two times.

There is the speed of practiced performance and the slower speed of inspection.

Most of the time, speed is useful. When something changes, inspection becomes essential.

The best performers are not permanently unconscious. They move attention up and down the stack. They can let stable components run automatically while spending consciousness on novelty, error, ambiguity, or the part of the performance they are trying to improve. Training at a high level often involves taking one small piece back out of compression, working on it deliberately, and then reintegrating it into the larger action.

A violinist may isolate a shift between two notes. A pitcher may work on one mechanical cue. A writer may spend a week noticing weak paragraph openings. A surgeon may practice a specific maneuver in simulation. The mature skill is opened, altered, and closed again.

That is more accurate than the fantasy of a final stage.

Expertise is not permanent arrival. It is a system that knows when to reopen itself.

The language of conscious and unconscious competence can still help if we treat it as a map of attention rather than a staircase of worth. A novice does not become superior by becoming less aware. The novice becomes capable of placing awareness somewhere more useful.

A driver who no longer thinks about keeping the car centered can watch the pedestrian.

A pianist who no longer thinks about the fingering can hear the phrase.

A physician who no longer has to reconstruct every common pattern can spend time on the patient who does not fit one.

This is the promise of compression: the lower levels become cheap enough that attention can be spent on the exception.

It is also the source of the book's central risk.

If lower-level skill is never acquired because a tool handles it from the beginning, the attention that appears to be “freed” may not be freed from anything. It may simply be floating above a structure that was never built.

That distinction is easy to miss because the output can look the same.

Two analysts may produce equivalent spreadsheets. One understands the formulas because she built and broke dozens of models. The other has learned to prompt a system that reliably constructs them. On an ordinary day, the second analyst may be faster. On the day a hidden assumption changes, the first analyst may possess an internal map the second never had reason to form.

Or perhaps the second analyst, assisted by an excellent system, will learn faster because the machine exposes assumptions, generates tests, and explains errors on demand.

Both futures are possible.

The tool does not decide which one occurs.

The practice design does.

That is why it matters to understand what disappears as competence grows. If we cannot see the stages being compressed, we cannot tell which ones are safe to delegate. We will automate whatever feels tedious and then discover, years later, whether the tedium was waste or tuition.

Sometimes it was waste.

Sometimes it was tuition.

The receipt arrives late.
