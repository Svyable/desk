# Chapter 18 — Keep the Reps

The future of competence will not be decided by whether people use machines.

That decision is over.

Humans have always built devices that move work out of the body and mind. Writing moved memory onto surfaces. Clocks externalized timekeeping. Maps externalized spatial knowledge. Printing multiplied recall across strangers. Calculators moved arithmetic into circuits. Search engines moved retrieval into networks. Autopilot moved control into machines. Software moved procedures into code.

Each shift changed what competent people needed to know.

Some old skills became unnecessary.

Others became more valuable because the tools existed.

The question now is faster and broader.

Generative systems can enter almost any symbolic task before the learner has built an internal representation of the task.

They can meet us at the beginning.

That changes the bargain.

The old pattern of technology was often: learn the job, then get the tool.

The emerging pattern can be: get the tool, then decide whether learning the job is still necessary.

Sometimes it is not.

This book has no interest in preserving useless struggle.

If a machine can perform a task reliably, cheaply, safely, and without leaving the human responsible for failures the human cannot understand, let the machine do it.

Civilization is not a contest to see how much difficulty we can retain.

But difficulty and waste are not synonyms.

Some repetitions are tuition.

The central discipline of the next era will be telling the difference.

That discipline begins with a better question than “Can AI do this?”

Ask what the task does to the person who performs it.

Does it build a representation?

Does it create error sensitivity?

Does it teach the boundary of a pattern?

Does it produce exposure to rare cases?

Does it calibrate confidence?

Does it build causal understanding needed for verification?

Does it train recovery?

Does it connect the person to the team?

Does it teach what matters enough to notice?

If the answer is no, automate freely.

If the answer is yes, do not conclude that the task must remain manual.

Preserve the developmental function.

That may mean manual practice.

It may mean simulation.

It may mean independent-first judgment.

It may mean delayed assistance.

It may mean adversarial cases.

It may mean a different task entirely.

The objective is not to preserve the old work.

It is to preserve the future expert.

This reframes the fear of deskilling.

Skills have always died.

Most people in industrial societies cannot perform tasks that were ordinary two centuries ago. That is not evidence of civilizational decline. Human capability moved into institutions, tools, infrastructure, and specialization.

The relevant loss is narrower.

A skill becomes dangerous to lose when the surrounding system still depends on humans possessing it.

If automation expects human rescue, train rescue.

If AI expects human verification, train verification.

If a profession retains human liability, preserve enough independent knowledge to make liability meaningful.

If the tool can fail outside its training distribution, preserve people who can recognize that the world has left the distribution.

Keep the reps where responsibility remains.

This is the first rule.

The second is less obvious.

Keep the reps where future learning depends on foundational structure.

A student can use a calculator and still learn mathematics, but some numerical fluency supports later reasoning.

A programmer can use code generation and still learn computer science, but some experience constructing and debugging systems may be necessary to understand what generated code does.

A writer can use generative tools and still become a writer, but if the tool always resolves the difficult sentence, the writer may never develop the ability to discover thought through language.

A doctor can use diagnostic assistance and still become an excellent physician, but if the system names the diagnosis before the trainee forms one, the pattern library may develop differently.

Foundations matter because expertise compresses them.

You cannot compress what was never built.

This is the deepest misunderstanding behind the phrase “AI frees us to do higher-level work.”

Sometimes it does.

But higher-level work rests on lower-level representations.

Remove the lower level before it is learned and the person may not ascend.

The person may simply occupy a higher interface.

This is not the same as expertise.

The third rule is to distinguish production from training.

Production should usually optimize for reliable output.

Training should optimize for future capability.

Trying to make one workflow serve both objectives automatically is how apprenticeship becomes accidental.

Create separate modes.

Production mode can use every appropriate tool.

Training mode can preserve selected unaided work.

Simulation mode can manufacture rare cases.

Audit mode can test the person and the system.

Learning mode can adapt support over time.

This separation is familiar in other domains.

Athletes do drills they would never perform in competition.

Pilots enter simulators to experience failures they hope never to encounter.

Musicians practice slowly to perform quickly.

Soldiers train under artificial conditions.

Emergency teams rehearse disasters.

Nobody complains that the training is inefficient relative to operational output.

Knowledge work needs the same permission.

The fourth rule is to preserve independence where disagreement matters.

If a human must verify a machine, do not always let the machine speak first.

If two reviewers are intended to provide redundancy, do not force them into a single shared reasoning path.

If a team needs a genuine second opinion, protect the conditions that make the opinion second.

Agreement is valuable only when the channels could have disagreed.

This applies to people as much as AI.

A room full of experts sharing the same incentives and assumptions can be less independent than two people from different traditions.

Competence is not only depth.

It is diversity of error.

The fifth rule is to measure what remains.

The polished artifact is no longer enough.

Ask for transfer.

Ask for retrieval.

Ask for explanation.

Ask for anomaly detection.

Ask for recovery.

Ask what the person can do when the tool is wrong.

Measure calibration.

Measure detection of seeded failures.

Measure performance after delay.

Measure improvement, not just throughput.

The age of generated output requires better evidence of human capability.

Otherwise credentials become increasingly detached from what holders can do.

That would be dangerous in high-stakes fields and merely embarrassing in others.

The sixth rule is to let competence become unconscious.

This may sound strange after a book that keeps asking people to inspect it.

The goal is not permanent self-consciousness.

A pianist should not think about every finger.

A surgeon should not narrate every movement.

A driver should not calculate every steering correction.

A writer should not diagram every sentence.

Practice earns compression.

Let the stable parts become cheap.

Then spend consciousness on surprise.

This is the real beauty of expertise.

The beginner's world is crowded.

Everything asks for attention.

The expert's world is sparse in a different way. The ordinary recedes. The meaningful deviation becomes louder.

A good tool should help produce the same effect.

It should remove noise while preserving signal.

The danger is removing the signal because it looked like noise before anyone knew how to hear it.

This is why the apprenticeship question cannot be answered from the top.

Senior people must study how they became senior.

Not romantically.

Empirically.

Which experiences actually mattered?

Which were hazing?

Which failures changed judgment?

Which routines created pattern recognition?

Which mentors supplied useful correction?

Which tasks looked menial but exposed the underlying system?

Which tasks can disappear with no loss?

The answers will surprise every profession.

Some sacred rites will turn out to be waste.

Some boring chores will turn out to have been structural.

A serious modernization effort should be willing to kill both myths.

Do not preserve pain because tradition calls it character.

Do not eliminate practice because automation calls it inefficiency.

Follow the learning mechanism.

This is also a personal strategy.

You do not need an institution to decide which repetitions belong to you.

If you are learning a field, occasionally work without the system.

Predict before asking.

Draft before generating.

Diagnose before revealing.

Estimate before calculating.

Navigate before following.

Read the source before the summary.

Build before reviewing.

Explain before checking.

Then use the tool.

Compare.

Find the gap.

This turns assistance into feedback.

The machine becomes a mirror instead of a prosthesis.

Later, once the representation is strong, lean harder on the tool.

Let it remove routine work.

Let it search farther.

Let it generate alternatives.

Let it challenge.

Let it simulate.

Let it expose you to more cases than a human career could naturally encounter.

Use leverage after leverage has something to act on.

This is not a purity test.

There is no moral virtue in unaided effort.

The objective is optionality.

Can you act when the tool is present?

Can you still understand when it is absent?

Can you detect when it is wrong?

Can you learn the new thing it cannot yet do?

That is a robust form of competence.

It does not require permanent independence.

It requires enough independence to choose dependence.

The distinction matters.

A person who can work only without tools is limited.

A person who can work only through tools is fragile.

A competent person can decide where cognition should live.

Inside memory.

On paper.

In a checklist.

Across a team.

Inside software.

Inside a model.

The allocation itself becomes a skill.

Perhaps this is the fifth stage the old competence model never named.

Not unconscious competence.

Conscious allocation.

The mature expert knows which parts should disappear from attention and which parts must remain available for inspection.

The expert does not insist on remembering what the machine remembers better.

The expert does not surrender the representation needed to know when the machine is wrong.

The expert moves between automatic and deliberate modes.

The expert knows when to reopen the skill.

The expert keeps learning.

That is the part of mastery the four-stage diagram misses.

Competence is not a final room.

It is a circulation system.

A skill becomes conscious, then practiced, then compressed. The world changes. The skill becomes conscious again. A new tool arrives. The layers are rearranged. The person practices the new relation until it becomes fluent. An anomaly appears. Consciousness returns.

Expertise lives in the switching.

This is why the title is a little dangerous.

*Unconsciously Competent* can sound like a celebration of effortless knowing.

It is really a warning about invisible infrastructure.

The smooth performance rests on things we no longer see.

Repetitions.

Errors.

Feedback.

Cases.

Teachers.

Teams.

Standards.

Sleep.

Time.

Opportunity.

The cost was paid earlier.

AI makes it possible to receive more of the performance without paying the same cost.

That is astonishing.

It is also why the cost has to be understood before we celebrate its disappearance.

There will be professions in which a generation becomes dramatically more capable because AI gives everyone a tireless tutor.

There will be professions in which a generation becomes dramatically more dependent because AI gives everyone an answer before they have a question.

Most fields will contain both.

The outcome will not be decided by the model alone.

It will be decided in classrooms, firms, training programs, interfaces, incentives, and personal habits.

The future expert may use more automation than anyone alive today.

The future expert may also practice more deliberately than anyone alive today because cheap simulation and feedback make deliberate practice abundant.

Those futures are compatible.

We can automate aggressively and train aggressively.

We can stop confusing manual labor with human value.

We can stop confusing machine output with human learning.

We can decide that some abilities no longer need to be human and become more serious about the abilities that do.

The decisive question is not whether a machine can take the task.

It is whether the human can afford to give away the repetition.

When the answer is yes, give it away.

When the answer is no, keep the reps.

Return to the Hudson for a moment.

The image that survived Flight 1549 is the airplane on the river.

Passengers on the wings.

Ferries approaching.

A machine where a machine was never supposed to be.

What is harder to photograph is the developmental history that had already happened before the birds arrived.

Training flights.

Simulator sessions.

Checklists.

Crew procedures.

Instrument habits.

Manual flying.

Thousands of ordinary decisions.

A professional culture built from previous incidents.

A certification system.

The physical design of the aircraft.

The coordination of people who had never expected to meet in that exact emergency.

The visible performance was minutes long.

The competence stack was decades deep.

This is the stack advanced automation is now entering.

It will make many layers better.

Some will disappear.

The mistake would be measuring only what remains visible.

That is how a civilization can confuse output abundance with capability abundance.

The report exists.

The code runs.

The diagnosis arrives.

The design looks complete.

The answer is persuasive.

The route works.

The artifact says nothing by itself about where the competence lives.

Maybe the person built it.

Maybe the team built it.

Maybe the machine built it.

Maybe the system is competent only while all three remain connected.

The question is not ownership.

It is resilience.

Where is enough understanding stored to handle surprise?

That question should become ordinary.

When you adopt a tool, ask what skill it replaces.

Then ask whether the replacement matters.

If not, celebrate.

If yes, decide where that skill will be maintained.

This is a more useful posture than both technological enthusiasm and technological fear.

The enthusiast sees the saved effort and assumes saved effort is pure gain.

The critic sees the lost effort and assumes effort is inherently valuable.

Neither has done the accounting.

The developmental value of a task has to be discovered.

Sometimes the answer will embarrass tradition.

The old task was pointless.

Its defenders mistook endurance for mastery.

Automate it.

Sometimes the answer will embarrass efficiency culture.

The boring task was how people learned the system.

Replace the task if you want, but replace the learning too.

This is the competence ledger.

What are we gaining now?

What are we no longer practicing?

What future responsibility depends on that practice?

Where else will the capability be built?

The ledger can be applied to a person, school, profession, company, or society.

At the personal level, it changes daily tool use.

Suppose you are learning something.

Do not ask whether AI is allowed.

Ask which part you are trying to learn.

If you are learning source evaluation, let the model help you find sources but read enough originals to build standards.

If you are learning code architecture, use generation but sometimes design the system before seeing its proposal.

If you are learning to write, let the tool challenge and edit you but preserve stretches where language has to come from your own attempt.

If you are learning a new city, use navigation when you need arrival and occasionally orient without it when you want the map.

The same person can use different modes for different objectives.

This is mature tool use.

It is not ascetic.

It is intentional.

At the organizational level, the ledger becomes a talent strategy.

Map the competencies required for verification, anomaly detection, recovery, transfer, and future leadership.

Then inspect whether the current workflow still produces them.

If not, build practice explicitly.

Do not rely on “learning on the job” after the job has stopped containing the learning.

This may mean simulator time for knowledge workers.

It may mean case libraries.

It may mean independent-first analysis on selected tasks.

It may mean rotations through manual or lower-automation modes.

It may mean postmortems that preserve uncertainty.

It may mean red-team exercises where the AI is wrong on purpose.

It may mean teaching senior experts how to expose decision boundaries rather than merely record procedures.

The organization should know what its next generation of experts is doing this week that will make them experts in ten years.

If the answer is “reviewing the machine's work,” inspect the review.

Is it generating independent judgment?

Is it providing feedback?

Is the reviewer seeing hard cases?

Does the reviewer ever learn why the machine was wrong?

Or is the person becoming unconsciously competent at approval?

That is the nightmare version of the future.

Not humans becoming stupid.

Humans becoming excellent at the wrong layer.

A workforce can be fast, polished, and productive while its ability to reconstruct, challenge, and recover becomes thinner.

The weakness may remain hidden because machines continue performing well.

Then the environment changes.

A new regulation.

A new adversary.

A new market.

A new disease.

A new technology.

A new failure mode.

The old outputs stop being reliable.

The organization needs people who can reopen the problem.

This is why the fifth movement of competence matters.

Learn.

Practice.

Compress.

Then reopen.

The ability to reopen is what keeps expertise from becoming habit.

It is also what keeps automation from becoming dependency.

A mature expert can move down the stack when necessary.

What does the model assume?

What data produced this answer?

What causal relation is hidden inside the output?

What changed?

Which part can I verify independently?

What would make this pattern fail?

The expert does not live at the lowest level.

The expert can descend.

That is different from memorizing everything.

It is structural literacy.

The skill of finding the layer where the error lives.

As systems become more complex, this may be more important than manual reproduction.

Nobody needs to rebuild the internet in order to use it competently.

But certain people must understand protocols deeply enough to diagnose failure.

Nobody needs to fabricate a semiconductor in order to write software.

But somebody must understand the interfaces between layers.

Civilization works because competence is distributed.

Robust civilization works because not every layer is opaque to everyone.

This is the societal version of keeping the reps.

Preserve overlapping competence at critical boundaries.

Do not let one institution, one vendor, one model, or one generation become the only place where a consequential system remains understandable.

Redundancy looks inefficient in normal times.

That is why normal times continuously try to remove it.

The same economics operate inside a person.

Knowing how to estimate a result looks redundant when the calculator works.

Knowing the route looks redundant when GPS works.

Knowing the doctrine looks redundant when retrieval works.

Knowing how to diagnose looks redundant when the model works.

The retained skill appears expensive precisely because the tool is succeeding.

This is why practice has to be justified against a counterfactual failure that may never happen.

Sometimes the rational answer is to surrender the skill.

Humans cannot carry every backup.

But where consequences are high, recovery is expected, and no alternative layer exists, redundancy is not nostalgia.

It is resilience.

This book began with the phrase “unconscious competence,” but the deepest argument has become almost the reverse.

What matters is not simply making skill unconscious.

What matters is knowing what became unconscious, how it got there, and when to call it back.

Competence that cannot return to awareness can harden into routine.

Competence that never reaches automaticity remains expensive.

Mastery lives between them.

The expert can stop thinking about the stable layer without stopping the ability to think about it.

That is why a checklist can help an expert.

Why a coach can reopen one movement.

Why a postmortem can reopen one decision.

Why a simulator can reopen one emergency.

Why a model disagreement can reopen one assumption.

Consciousness comes back where the system no longer fits.

This is the circulation system of expertise.

It offers a way through the false choice that will dominate the AI era if we let it.

We do not have to choose between human skill and machine leverage.

We can use machines to remove low-value work and use the saved capacity to build higher-value judgment.

We can make production faster and training more deliberate.

We can let machines remember what machines remember well and make humans better at interpretation.

We can create simulations so rich that future experts encounter more meaningful cases in five years than older experts encountered in twenty.

We can expose rare failures safely.

We can compare human and machine reasoning.

We can make expertise less dependent on lucky access to one mentor.

That future is more ambitious than protecting old work.

It requires taking learning more seriously than old work ever did.

The obstacle is not technical.

It is economic and cultural.

Output is immediate.

Competence is delayed.

Organizations know how to count the first.

Individuals feel the second only after it is missing.

The temptation will always be to take the free-looking gain now.

That is why “keep the reps” has to become a conscious choice before the skill becomes invisible.

Keep the repetitions that build the representation you will later need.

Keep the repetitions that expose you to reality instead of only answers.

Keep the repetitions that make disagreement possible.

Keep the repetitions that teach recovery.

Keep enough of them that the machine remains leverage rather than life support.

And give the rest away.

That last part matters.

Do not become a museum of obsolete competence.

The purpose of mastery is not independence from civilization.

It is participation in a system without surrendering the ability to know where the system ends and your responsibility begins.

The best tool disappears into the work.

The best expertise does too.

But somewhere underneath the smooth surface, the structure remains available.

The river arrives.

The model fails.

The context changes.

The pattern breaks.

There is no time to begin an apprenticeship.

The reps are already there.

Or they are not.

One day you will need what they built.
