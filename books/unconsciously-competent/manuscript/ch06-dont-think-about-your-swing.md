# Chapter 6 — Don't Think About Your Swing

A golf swing can survive years of practice and collapse under one sentence.

“Keep your left arm straight.”

The instruction may be technically sound. It may even be the exact correction a golfer needs during practice. Put the same instruction into the player's head on the eighteenth tee of a tournament, with a lead, cameras, money, and reputation compressed into one shot, and the sentence can become an intruder.

The hands were supposed to know.

Now the mind is watching them.

Researchers who study choking under pressure have spent decades trying to understand why highly practiced skills sometimes degrade when the performer cares most. One influential line of work, associated with psychologist Sian Beilock and colleagues, examined what happens when attention turns inward toward the mechanics of a skill that normally runs with little conscious supervision.

The broad idea is intuitive.

A beginner often improves by paying attention to technique.

An expert can be harmed by paying attention in the same way.

The instruction is not wrong.

The timing is.

A practiced movement is fast because lower-level components have been integrated. Conscious monitoring can split the unit back into pieces. The performer starts supervising operations that no longer benefit from direct supervision.

Anyone who has thought about walking while walking knows a comic version of this. The gait does not usually require a verbal plan. The instant you inspect it too closely, the ordinary rhythm becomes strange.

Sports make the effect visible because movement is exposed and outcomes are immediate.

A basketball player who has shot thousands of free throws has a routine: breath, dribble, target, lift, release. Coaches often protect that routine because the athlete needs a stable attentional structure under pressure. An instruction introduced at the wrong moment can redirect attention toward mechanics and produce the movement equivalent of a stutter.

Musicians know the problem.

A pianist can perform a memorized passage fluently until a thought appears: What note comes next?

The question can interrupt the very memory it is trying to inspect.

Public speakers experience a verbal form. A practiced opening flows until the speaker becomes aware of remembering it. The sequence that had been retrieved as a unit suddenly becomes a search.

Writers experience a quieter version when they try to edit every sentence while drafting it. The evaluative system enters too early. Language that might have developed through forward momentum is forced to justify itself before the thought exists.

The lesson is not that conscious attention is bad.

It is that attention has levels.

Expertise depends partly on assigning attention to the level where it is most useful.

A golfer in practice may focus on a wrist position.

A golfer in competition may focus on the flight of the ball.

A pianist in slow rehearsal may isolate fingering.

A pianist in performance may hear the phrase.

A surgeon training a new maneuver may inspect hand position.

During a familiar procedure, attention may belong on anatomy, bleeding, and unexpected variation.

Practice and performance are not the same cognitive event.

Confusing them creates bad training.

One of the strangest cultural habits around mastery is the belief that great performers should be able to explain what they are doing while they are doing it. Television encourages this fantasy. We ask athletes what went through their minds during the decisive play, as if excellence were accompanied by a running strategic transcript.

Often the honest answer would be disappointing.

Not much that can be translated cleanly.

The perception arrived.

The opportunity appeared.

The body moved.

That does not make the performance thoughtless. The thought is partly embedded in the learned organization of perception and action.

A fighter does not consciously calculate the trajectory of every punch.

A driver avoiding a sudden obstacle does not solve equations.

A goalkeeper does not narrate optic flow.

Training has changed what can be acted on directly.

This creates a reporting problem after the fact.

Humans are excellent narrators.

When asked why we did something, we can often produce a plausible story even when the processes that generated the action were not available to introspection in that form. This does not mean every explanation is false. Experts can provide valuable accounts of strategy, preparation, cues, and decision criteria. But the verbal story should not automatically be treated as a complete causal trace of the performance.

The person who did the skill may not have privileged access to every process that made the skill possible.

This becomes important when organizations try to “capture” expertise by interviewing experts.

The interview asks, “How do you do it?”

The expert provides reasons.

The institution writes a procedure.

The procedure may contain useful knowledge and still omit the pattern recognition, timing, sensory discrimination, or embodied calibration that supported the original work.

Then the organization believes the expertise has been transferred because the document exists.

A recipe is not a chef.

A checklist is not a pilot.

A style guide is not an editor.

A prompt library is not judgment.

Documents can carry enormous value. They externalize memory, standardize important steps, and let knowledge travel farther than apprenticeship alone could carry it. The error is assuming that explicit representation exhausts skilled performance.

Pressure exposes the difference because pressure changes attention.

There are several theories of choking, and not every failure under pressure comes from the same mechanism. Anxiety can consume working memory. Concern about evaluation can distract. A performer can rush. Muscles can tighten. Decisions can become conservative. Sleep can be poor. Pressure changes physiology and motivation as well as attention.

Still, explicit monitoring provides a particularly revealing lens on unconscious competence.

The better integrated the skill, the less helpful it may be to decompose it during execution.

That means expertise creates a new vulnerability.

Novices are vulnerable because the skill is not built.

Experts can become vulnerable because the skill is built too well to tolerate interference at the wrong level.

This is one reason ritual matters.

Athletes, musicians, surgeons, actors, and other performers develop routines not because superstition improves physics but because stable routines protect attention. The routine decides what happens before the mind begins negotiating with itself.

Bounce the ball three times.

Check the instrument.

Set the feet.

Look at the target.

Take the breath.

Say the opening line.

The routine narrows the attentional field.

It gives pressure fewer opportunities to rewrite the control system.

There is a related phenomenon in safety work: under stress, people can revert to older, more practiced behaviors. This can be beneficial when the practiced behavior is correct and dangerous when training no longer matches the system.

The nervous system reaches for the path with the deepest groove.

That is why recurrent training matters.

A procedure learned once and rarely practiced may lose the competition against thousands of routine repetitions. In an emergency, the operator may know the rule conceptually and still initiate the familiar response.

High-reliability organizations do not assume that important knowledge remains available because employees once passed a test.

They rehearse.

The rehearsal has to preserve realism without creating reckless risk.

Simulation solves part of this. Aviation can expose crews to engine failures that should never be practiced casually in normal operations. Medicine can rehearse resuscitation, rare complications, communication failures, and technical procedures. Military units can drill responses. Cybersecurity teams can run incident simulations. Companies can conduct tabletop exercises for outages, recalls, breaches, or crises.

The value of simulation is not merely remembering the checklist.

It is allowing the person or team to build patterns under pressure before pressure is real.

A good simulation also reveals where conscious thought belongs.

During routine conditions, skilled action can stay compressed.

When the anomaly appears, the performer must reopen the right part of the system.

This switching ability is underappreciated.

People talk about being “in the zone” as if optimal performance were a trance in which consciousness disappears. That is too simple. Skilled performers can be intensely aware. The difference is what they are aware of.

A driver at speed may be highly conscious of traffic flow while barely conscious of steering corrections.

A musician may be highly conscious of sound while barely conscious of finger sequence.

A surgeon may be highly conscious of a change in tissue while barely conscious of familiar instrument handling.

Attention has moved outward toward consequence.

Choking often drags it inward toward mechanics.

This suggests a general principle for knowledge work too.

A practiced analyst does not want to be consciously thinking about spreadsheet navigation while trying to reason about a business.

A writer does not want to spend scarce attention recalling punctuation rules while testing an argument.

A programmer does not want to devote working memory to syntax while tracing a race condition.

Fluency in the lower levels creates room at the higher levels.

Tool design can either support this hierarchy or scramble it.

Good tools remove irrelevant friction.

Bad tools interrupt at the wrong level.

A notification that forces an expert to acknowledge a routine event can consume more attention than the event deserves. An interface that changes familiar controls without need can pull skill back into conscious supervision. A system that offers a suggestion before the expert has formed a judgment can shift attention from independent recognition toward evaluation of the suggestion.

That last change is especially subtle.

Evaluation feels like thinking.

It is thinking.

It is not the same thinking as generation.

Suppose a doctor sees an AI-generated differential diagnosis before forming one independently. The doctor may perform excellent critical evaluation. But the perceptual and retrieval sequence has changed. Some candidate diagnoses become salient because the system named them. Others may be suppressed because the list feels complete.

Suppose a writer receives three possible openings. Choosing among them exercises taste. It does not exercise the same generative process as creating an opening under uncertainty.

Suppose a programmer receives code completion. Accepting or rejecting the suggestion exercises review. It does not exercise the same recall and construction as producing the code.

Again, no hierarchy says generation is always superior.

In mature work, experts routinely separate generation and evaluation among people and tools. Editors improve writers. Code review improves code. Second opinions improve diagnosis. Brainstorming can benefit from stimuli. Collaboration changes cognition by design.

The question is developmental.

Which cognitive operation are you trying to automate, and which are you trying to strengthen?

Pressure gives us a way to see why the distinction matters.

When external support is removed, people fall back on internalized structure.

If the internal structure is rich, performance may survive.

If the internal structure is shallow, the person may discover that assisted fluency was not portable.

This is one reason exams have historically removed assistance, even when real life allows assistance. The point is not always moral purity. Sometimes the institution is trying to measure what remains available inside the person.

The problem is that exams themselves can be poor measures. They can reward recall that would sensibly be externalized, punish collaboration, and privilege test-taking strategies. We should not preserve bad assessment merely because AI complicates it.

But we do need ways to measure unaided capability when unaided capability matters.

A pilot must be able to fly manually under some conditions.

A clinician must be able to recognize when decision support is wrong.

An engineer responsible for a bridge must understand more than how to prompt a calculation.

A journalist must be able to identify when a source is unsupported.

A financial professional must know when a model's output is nonsense.

A citizen should not need a language model to detect the most basic contradiction in a political claim.

The relevant threshold differs by role.

The principle does not.

Supervision without competence is theater.

This is where “human in the loop” becomes a dangerously comforting phrase.

It describes an architecture, not a capability.

A human can sit in the loop and rubber-stamp.

A human can sit in the loop and lack time.

A human can sit in the loop and lack information.

A human can sit in the loop after automation has removed the repetitions that would have made the human capable of meaningful intervention.

The box on the diagram is still labeled HUMAN.

The function has vanished.

We will return to this later because it is one of the defining design problems of advanced automation. For now, pressure gives us the smaller lesson.

The pressure research contains an important warning against turning explicit monitoring into a universal explanation.

People can choke through more than one route.

Some tasks depend heavily on working memory and deliberate reasoning. Pressure can fill that working memory with worry, self-evaluation, consequences, or irrelevant thoughts. Performance suffers because the person has too little attention left for the task.

Other skills have become proceduralized enough that the problem can run in the opposite direction. Pressure causes the performer to monitor mechanics that normally operate efficiently without step-by-step control.

One failure is too little task attention.

The other can be too much attention at the wrong level.

This is why generic advice to “focus” under pressure is nearly meaningless.

Focus on what?

The answer depends on the architecture of the skill.

Beilock's experiments with golfers made this visible. Experienced golfers could perform worse when asked to attend closely to step-by-step putting mechanics, while novices could benefit from that same kind of skill-focused attention. In other experiments, distraction that would normally sound undesirable could leave expert performance intact or even help relative to intrusive self-monitoring.

The mature skill had changed the function of attention.

This has consequences for coaching language.

A coach who gives an expert the same technical cues used with a novice may be pulling attention backward.

The cue can be correct and still mistimed.

Elite coaches often respond by shifting attention toward outcome, rhythm, target, or a single external cue that organizes movement without asking the performer to consciously manage every component.

The point is not that internal technical work disappears.

It is scheduled.

Technique is opened during practice.

Performance is protected when possible.

Then the video, metrics, or coach can help reopen the movement afterward.

This creates a cycle:

perform whole,

inspect part,

modify part,

reintegrate whole.

The cycle is one of the most useful models in the book because it shows how unconscious competence and deliberate practice coexist.

The expert is not condemned to never think about technique.

The expert learns when to think about it.

This timing principle applies to intellectual skills too.

A writer drafting an argument may need momentum and broad structure. A writer revising needs ruthless attention to sentence logic, evidence, redundancy, rhythm, and factual support. Trying to perform both modes simultaneously can create paralysis.

A programmer designing an architecture may need to hold system relationships at a high level. During debugging, attention may descend into one state transition. During code review, it moves back up to interfaces, invariants, and failure modes.

A doctor taking a history may move between open attention to the patient's account and focused investigation of a specific hypothesis.

Competent work is not one attentional state.

It is controlled switching.

Pressure can damage performance by locking the person into the wrong state.

So can software.

Interfaces constantly compete for attentional control.

Autocompletion appears before the thought is finished.

Notifications arrive while the user is holding a complex representation.

Dashboards expose dozens of metrics.

AI assistants offer suggestions at the exact moment a person is generating.

Each intervention may be locally useful.

Together they can prevent sustained control over where attention sits.

This suggests another design principle: expert tools should respect attentional phase.

Do not interrupt generation with evaluation unless requested.

Do not surface every warning with equal urgency.

Do not turn every recommendation into a decision prompt.

Let users defer suggestions.

Let them create quiet modes.

Let them decide when the machine speaks.

The best tools disappear into skilled activity.

The worst tools repeatedly drag the expert's attention back to the interface.

This is why latency matters in strange ways.

A tool can be too slow and break flow.

It can also be so eager that it colonizes the pause in which the user would have thought.

The half-second before the autocomplete appears is cognitively different from a blank field that waits.

The absence of suggestion is sometimes developmental space.

Pressure narrows this space further.

Under deadline, the generated continuation is harder to resist.

Under fatigue, the default is easier to accept.

Under evaluation, users may prefer the machine's polished language to their own uncertain formulation.

Tool dependence is therefore not only a property of the user.

It is a property of the environment in which the tool is offered.

If every organizational incentive rewards speed, asking employees to preserve independent reasoning becomes ceremonial.

The worker who takes time to think loses to the worker who accepts.

A culture can deskill people while officially encouraging critical thinking.

This is why training interventions fail when production incentives point the other way.

You cannot tell reviewers to verify deeply and then measure only throughput.

You cannot tell programmers to understand generated code and then reward lines shipped.

You cannot tell students to use AI reflectively and then assign workloads that make reflection irrational.

Attention follows incentives.

Pressure makes the alignment visible.

The performer under pressure does what has been practiced, but also what the environment makes affordable.

This is one reason experts train routines under conditions that resemble competition.

The routine has to survive stress.

Knowledge workers need the same realism.

If the real job includes AI suggestions, practice evaluating them under deadline.

If the real job includes interruptions, practice recovery after interruption.

If the real job includes public challenge, rehearse explanation.

If the real job includes tool failure, simulate tool failure.

The objective is not to create stress for its own sake.

It is to keep the attentional control policy from existing only in ideal conditions.

A skill that works only when no one cares is not finished.

A skill that works only when the machine behaves normally is not finished either.

Competence includes the ability to protect the right level of attention when something important is trying to steal it.

Competence is not merely what you can do when everything supports you.

It is what remains organized when the environment begins stealing attention.

The performer under pressure has less working memory, more noise, more consequence, and less time. The value of automaticity becomes obvious because the lower-level operations continue without requesting scarce cognitive resources.

The risk of over-monitoring becomes obvious for the same reason.

A person can spend limited attention trying to supervise what practice already solved.

This is why a good coach does not always shout more instructions.

Sometimes the correct intervention is one cue.

Sometimes it is a target outside the body.

Sometimes it is silence.

The best instruction at the wrong moment can still be bad coaching.

That principle scales beyond sports.

A leader in a crisis should not force experts to narrate every familiar operation for reassurance.

A surgical team should not improvise a lecture during a routine maneuver.

A developer responding to an outage should not have to satisfy a manager's curiosity while tracing the failure.

A writer at the generative stage should not invite the editor into every sentence.

The work needs protected layers.

Consciousness is too valuable to spend everywhere.

The mature skill is partly a budget.

Practice has paid for certain operations in advance.

Pressure tests whether we remember to use the credit.
