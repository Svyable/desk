# Chapter 5 — The Curse of Expertise

Pamela Hinds gave experts a problem that should have been easy for experts.

She asked them to predict how long novices would take to perform tasks the experts themselves understood well.

The experts were bad at it.

The more knowledge a person possessed, the harder it could become to reconstruct the difficulty of not possessing it. Hinds called the phenomenon the “curse of expertise.” It is a more useful phrase than it first appears because the curse is not arrogance in the ordinary sense. The expert does not have to be impatient, vain, or dismissive. The problem can survive good intentions.

Knowledge changes the problem before the expert estimates it.

The expert looks at a task and sees a compressed object. The novice looks at the same task and sees unresolved parts.

This is why an experienced employee can say, with complete sincerity, “It only takes ten minutes,” and then watch a new hire spend an afternoon on it.

The ten minutes begin after the expert has already paid for years of invisible preparation.

A senior financial analyst opens a model and notices immediately which tabs matter, which assumptions deserve suspicion, which formulas are standard, which cells have probably been hard-coded, and where an error is most likely to propagate. A junior analyst sees a grid.

A trial lawyer reads a deposition and hears the witness change shape. A law student sees pages.

A software engineer scans an unfamiliar codebase and begins to infer architecture from names, imports, tests, directory structure, and the kinds of compromises that usually accumulate in systems of that age. A beginner sees files.

The expert's estimate of difficulty is contaminated by the expert's own perception.

The task looks shorter because the expert cannot easily see the cognitive labor that expertise has removed.

This has consequences far beyond teaching.

It shapes hiring, delegation, management, product design, public policy, medical communication, safety instructions, onboarding, documentation, and nearly every situation in which someone who knows is trying to predict the behavior of someone who does not.

A tax form can be perfectly intelligible to the people who designed it and hostile to everyone else.

A software interface can be “intuitive” to the team that has stared at prototypes for nine months.

A hospital discharge instruction can be technically complete and practically useless to a frightened family hearing the terminology for the first time.

A professor can write an exam whose difficulty comes partly from hidden assumptions rather than the knowledge being tested.

A manager can give an employee “enough context” because the manager no longer remembers how much context was required to make the task legible.

The curse of expertise is what happens when compression becomes invisible to the compressor.

It is also one reason the advice to “learn from the best” is incomplete.

The best performer is not automatically the best teacher.

Teaching requires a second expertise: the ability to model the learner.

That model includes what the learner can already retrieve, what vocabulary is available, which distinctions are visible, where working memory will overload, which errors are productive, which errors will merely confuse, and how much of the final structure should be revealed before the learner has had a chance to build any of it.

An elite performer may possess little of this knowledge.

A world-class violinist can hear an intonation problem that a student cannot yet hear. Saying “listen to the pitch” is useless if the student does not know what to listen for. An experienced programmer can say, “Just inspect the stack trace,” as if a stack trace arrives labeled with the relevant line. A physician can tell a trainee to “look at the whole patient,” a phrase whose wisdom is proportional to how little operational guidance it contains.

Experts often teach from the top of the staircase.

The learner is still looking for the first step.

This is where education research on worked examples and the expertise reversal effect becomes useful. Novices can benefit from guidance that reduces unnecessary search and directs attention toward relevant structure. Once the learner has enough knowledge, the same guidance can become redundant or even interfere. Support that is helpful at one stage can be badly matched at another.

The implication is simple and inconvenient: there is no universally optimal amount of explanation.

The correct explanation depends on who is listening.

This should be obvious. Organizations violate it constantly.

They build one training deck for everybody.

They write one manual.

They create one onboarding sequence.

They assign the same AI assistant to the beginner and the expert.

Then they are surprised that some people drown in details while others are bored by instructions that occupy attention without teaching anything.

Good instruction changes as compression changes.

At the beginning, a learner may need the invisible parts made visible. Later, the learner needs some of that scaffolding removed so the parts can be integrated. Eventually, an expert may benefit from reopening only the element that is being refined.

The teacher's job is therefore partly destructive.

A good teacher builds supports and then takes them away.

The learner has to inherit the load.

This can feel cruel when assistance has made performance smoother. Remove the worked example and errors increase. Take away the cue and the learner slows down. Ask for retrieval instead of recognition and confidence falls.

The short-term metrics get worse.

The long-term capability may improve.

That tradeoff is one reason educational systems are vulnerable to tools that optimize the visible performance of the current task. A system that makes homework cleaner, code more functional, prose more polished, and answers more accurate can appear educationally superior even if it has changed little about the learner's unaided capability.

The artifact is easy to grade.

The internal structure is not.

This was already true before generative AI. Parents helped with science projects. Tutors edited essays. Solution manuals supplied intermediate steps. Search engines made facts instantly available. Autocomplete guessed the word. Calculators executed procedures. The line between assistance and substitution has always been blurry.

Generative systems make the blur continuous.

A learner can request a hint, an explanation, a partial solution, a complete solution, a critique, a rewrite, a test, a simulation, a flash card, a worked example, or an answer that looks like the learner wrote it. The same interface can function as tutor or ghostwriter depending on the interaction.

This creates a new teaching problem: the tool itself needs a model of the learner.

A system that always answers fully is pedagogically crude even when the answer is excellent.

A system that never answers is merely annoying.

The interesting design is adaptive withholding.

Ask the learner to commit before revealing.

Offer a cue before the solution.

Expose a contradiction.

Generate a near miss.

Request an explanation.

Decrease support as competence grows.

Increase support when the task exceeds the learner's current representation rather than simply when the learner asks for relief.

Human tutors do versions of this instinctively. They wait. They watch the face. They notice the student is stuck for the wrong reason. They answer a question with a smaller question. They know when frustration has become useless.

Machines can potentially do some of this at enormous scale.

But only if the objective is learning.

If the objective is task completion, the fastest path is often to remove the learner from the difficult part.

That difference in objectives is going to define a large part of education and professional development in the AI era.

A company wants the report finished.

The employee needs the repetitions.

Those goals can align.

They can also conflict.

The manager who delegates to a junior employee is already balancing them. Giving the junior a difficult analysis may take longer and create more risk than doing it personally. Organizations absorb that inefficiency because apprenticeship has always been a form of investment. Senior people let junior people perform work inefficiently today so there will be senior people tomorrow.

Some professions formalize this.

Medical residency is built around supervised responsibility that expands with competence.

Trades use apprenticeships.

Aviation uses instruction, supervised practice, recurrent training, and check rides.

Law firms historically gave junior lawyers document review, research, drafting, and other work that was inefficient partly because the inefficiency trained them.

Accounting firms gave young accountants the work through which they learned how the numbers fit together.

Newsrooms gave young reporters routine stories.

Software teams gave junior developers bugs, tests, maintenance work, and smaller features.

The tasks were not sacred.

Their developmental function was often invisible.

Automation tends to attack exactly these tasks first because they are routine enough to specify and expensive enough to automate.

That creates an economic trap.

The organization sees a task that a machine can perform faster and more cheaply.

The novice sees a task that used to produce experience.

Both are right.

The spreadsheet rarely includes the second value.

This is how expertise pipelines can be damaged without anyone making an irrational decision.

Every local decision is sensible.

Automate the first draft.

Automate the code review.

Automate the research memo.

Automate the routine image read.

Automate the scheduling.

Automate the basic customer interaction.

Automate the initial financial analysis.

Automate the document comparison.

Then ask, ten years later, why the middle of the organization feels thin.

The missing capability was not stored in the tasks.

It was produced by doing them.

This is not an argument for preserving obsolete labor as a museum exhibit.

Many apprenticeship tasks are terrible teachers. Repetition without feedback creates boredom, not mastery. Junior professionals have often spent years performing work that could be automated with no meaningful loss. Exploitative traditions frequently disguise themselves as training because incumbents suffered through them.

The correct question is not, “Which old tasks should survive?”

It is, “What developmental function did the task serve, and where will that function move?”

If junior lawyers no longer review thousands of documents manually, perhaps training should include curated document sets designed to teach evidentiary patterns faster.

If programmers no longer write common boilerplate, perhaps they should spend more time tracing system behavior, testing assumptions, and debugging adversarial failures.

If radiologists receive machine suggestions, perhaps training should include unaided reads and deliberate comparison between human and model attention.

If writers use generative tools, perhaps workshops should shift from producing volume toward diagnosis, fact verification, structure, revision, and periods of unaided composition.

Abolish drudgery.

Keep the learning signal.

The distinction sounds clean on paper. In practice, organizations do not know the learning signal as well as they know the cost of labor.

That is another expression of the curse of expertise.

Senior leaders already possess the competence whose production process they are redesigning.

They can underestimate the importance of the work that built them because they no longer experience the work as difficult.

A partner at a law firm can use AI to eliminate junior research hours because the partner knows how to evaluate the output.

The junior does not yet.

A senior engineer can supervise generated code because years of debugging taught her what suspicious code feels like.

The new engineer has not accumulated those failures.

A professor can use an AI system to accelerate literature review because the professor knows which journals matter, which methods are weak, which claims require primary sources, and what a plausible result looks like.

The student sees fluent text.

The senior person's use case proves that the tool is useful.

It does not prove that the same use preserves the path by which the senior person became capable of using it well.

This is one of the most important asymmetries in the automation debate.

Experts are deciding which beginner tasks are no longer necessary.

They are making that decision from the far side of learning.

A thoughtful organization will therefore treat training data about humans with the same seriousness it treats performance data about systems. Which experiences correlate with later judgment? Which cases produce meaningful error correction? Which skills decay when not practiced? Which tasks are redundant once a schema exists? Which tasks look low-value but expose people to the range of reality?

These are empirical questions.

The answers will differ by field.

The common mistake is assuming there is nothing to measure because the task looks easy.

“Easy” is an expert's description of current performance.

It tells us almost nothing about developmental value.

There is a second side to the curse of expertise that matters just as much: experts can overcomplicate.

A person deeply immersed in a subject knows every caveat, exception, terminology dispute, historical wrinkle, and competing framework. Asked to explain the subject, the expert can deliver the entire warehouse.

The novice needed one shelf.

This is why the best teachers are not simply people who remember being confused. They can identify the next confusion worth solving.

They control sequence.

A textbook chapter that introduces six exceptions before the learner has grasped the rule may be accurate and pedagogically destructive. A manager who explains every edge case before assigning a simple task can make the work feel harder than it is. An AI tutor that produces an encyclopedic answer to a narrow question can bury the useful relation under completeness.

Teaching is compression in reverse, but not decompression all at once.

The expert has to unfold a structure at the speed the learner can rebuild it.

That requires humility of a very specific kind.

Not “I may be wrong.”

“I may no longer know what this looks like before it is known.”

Those are different forms of humility.

The first concerns the content.

The second concerns the learner.

Organizations need both.

Consider the phrase “common sense.”

It is one of the most expensive phrases in management.

Common sense often means knowledge whose acquisition has been forgotten. It can encode class, culture, professional exposure, local convention, or years inside an organization. Telling someone to use common sense is frequently an admission that the instruction exists only inside the people who already know it.

The phrase transfers responsibility without transferring knowledge.

A better response is to ask what cue the experienced person is using.

What did you notice?

What would make you slow down?

What are the two mistakes beginners make here?

Which part seems obvious now that did not seem obvious when you learned it?

What is the earliest sign the task is going wrong?

These questions force compressed expertise to become partially explicit.

They do not extract everything.

Some knowledge resists verbalization because it is genuinely bound to perception and action. But experts can often recover more of the path than they first believe if the inquiry begins with concrete cases rather than abstract explanations.

“Tell me how you negotiate” produces slogans.

“Why did you stop talking at this moment in yesterday's negotiation?” can produce knowledge.

“Tell me how you diagnose” produces categories.

“What did you see in this patient that made you order that test?” can reveal a cue.

“Tell me how you write” produces style advice.

“Why did you delete this sentence?” can expose judgment.

Cases pull tacit knowledge closer to the surface.

This is why apprenticeship has historically involved proximity.

The learner watches not only what the expert does but when the expert hesitates, what the expert ignores, which detail causes a return to the previous step, how an error is recovered, and which questions are asked before action.

A manual captures the canonical path.

Observation captures the deviations.

Generative systems will increasingly sit inside this relationship. They can record cases, summarize decisions, create simulations, retrieve precedent, and perhaps infer recurring cues that experts themselves struggle to name. They could become extraordinary instruments for making competence teachable.

Or they can become filters that hide the work.

The outcome will depend on what institutions ask them to optimize.

Speed is easy to measure.

Capability is slower.

That is the core difficulty.

The curse of expertise makes the future of learning especially vulnerable to good tools deployed by competent people.

The people making the decisions can use the tools safely because they already know what the tools are replacing.

The people coming after them may never know.

A civilization can lose a skill this way without anyone forgetting anything consciously.

The manuals remain.

The models remain.

The answers remain.

Only the path disappears.
