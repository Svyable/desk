# Chapter 16 — Making the Invisible Visible

Ask an expert to explain the craft and you often get philosophy.

Ask the expert to work on a real case and stop at the moment of decision.

Now you can learn something.

This distinction is one of the most practical consequences of tacit knowledge.

General questions invite compressed answers.

“How do you lead?”

“Communicate clearly.”

“How do you diagnose?”

“Listen to the patient.”

“How do you invest?”

“Protect the downside.”

“How do you write?”

“Tell the truth.”

The answers can be wise.

They are also almost useless to the learner who does not yet possess the cases that give the words meaning.

The hidden structure appears when the expert has to discriminate.

Why this patient?

Why this sentence?

Why this price?

Why this sound?

Why did you stop here?

Why did you ignore that?

Why did this exception matter when the last one did not?

Case comparison turns aphorism into evidence.

This is how the invisible can become partly visible.

Cognitive task analysis and related methods have long attempted to elicit the knowledge experts use in complex work. The challenge is that experts may omit steps that have become automatic, describe idealized procedures instead of actual practice, or rationalize decisions after the fact.

Observation helps.

So does replay.

Give the expert the video, screen recording, transcript, image, chart, or decision log.

Return to the exact moment.

“What were you noticing?”

“What did you expect next?”

“What would have surprised you?”

“What alternative were you ruling out?”

“What would a novice likely miss?”

“What would make the usual rule fail here?”

These questions recover cues.

They do not recover everything.

No interview can convert a lifetime of practice into a document.

But they can improve the transfer dramatically.

This is especially valuable in organizations that are about to automate a task.

Before removing the human work, study it.

Not the official process.

The actual work.

Watch where people hesitate.

Watch where they check another source.

Watch when they ignore the dashboard.

Watch which cases get escalated.

Watch which shortcuts are safe and which are merely tolerated.

Automation projects often begin with a process map created by people distant from the edge.

Boxes.

Arrows.

Inputs.

Outputs.

The map captures sequence.

It can miss judgment.

Then the system automates the map and discovers that the unofficial exceptions were doing more work than expected.

The organization calls them edge cases.

To the expert, they were Tuesday.

This is why process mining and observation should meet ethnography.

A log can show that a user deviated.

A conversation can reveal why.

The combination matters because tacit competence often appears as deviation from formal procedure for good reasons, bad reasons, and reasons nobody remembers.

Making the invisible visible is therefore not a celebration of experts.

It is an audit.

Some hidden practices are excellent.

Some are hacks compensating for broken systems.

Some are local superstitions.

Some are discriminatory.

Some are safety mechanisms.

Some are obsolete.

The goal is to identify which is which.

That requires evidence.

One powerful method is expert-novice comparison.

Put both people in front of the same case.

Track what they notice.

Ask for predictions.

Observe search.

Look at what each person considers important.

Then use the difference to design instruction.

In radiology, eye-tracking studies can reveal differences in visual search, though gaze alone does not contain the knowledge behind it.

In chess, board recall reveals how meaningful structure changes memory.

In programming, think-aloud studies can show how experts decompose a problem differently.

In medicine, case discussions can surface diagnostic scripts and cue weighting.

In skilled trades, video can capture sequences that were previously transmitted only by proximity.

AI expands these methods.

A system can index thousands of expert decisions.

It can identify recurring phrases before escalation.

It can cluster cases where experts disagreed.

It can generate counterexamples.

It can compare novice and expert attention.

It can replay decisions with hidden outcomes.

It can ask experts targeted questions instead of generic ones.

This may make tacit knowledge more teachable.

The danger is overclaiming capture.

A model trained on expert outputs can reproduce patterns without possessing the expert's causal understanding, and users can mistake reproduction for complete transfer.

The more convincing the imitation, the easier it is to stop apprenticeship too early.

A system that speaks like the retiring engineer may answer common questions beautifully.

The organization feels protected.

Then an unprecedented failure occurs.

The model retrieves analogies.

The absent engineer would have walked to the machine.

The difference is not mystical embodiment.

The engineer had access to context the model may not: vibration, smell, history, local modifications, relationships with technicians, and the ability to run new tests.

Expertise is often coupled to an environment.

Capturing text captures only what traveled through text.

This is why the best knowledge-transfer systems include action.

Simulation.

Shadowing.

Supervised practice.

Rotations.

Case review.

After-action analysis.

Teaching.

The learner needs chances to make the judgment.

A mentor's explanation is valuable.

A learner's prediction is more diagnostic.

This is another principle that reverses conventional training.

We often ask the expert to talk more.

Sometimes we should ask the learner to commit sooner.

“What would you do?”

Now the gap is visible.

The expert can respond to the learner's model rather than delivering a complete lecture.

This is how good coaching works.

It turns instruction into contrast.

The learner sees not only the right answer but the difference between the answer and the path the learner took.

That contrast produces information.

AI tutors can do this exceptionally well if designed for it.

They can retain a history of the learner's errors.

They can infer recurring misconceptions.

They can generate new examples that distinguish between competing models.

They can ask for confidence.

They can delay revelation.

They can show how an expert approached the same case.

They can revisit the case a week later without warning.

The technology can make invisible learning visible too.

This is an enormous opportunity.

Most education has poor longitudinal memory.

A teacher sees a student for a term.

A manager remembers a few mistakes.

A coach has notes.

A system can preserve a fine-grained record of what the learner could and could not do across years.

That record could support extraordinary personalization.

It could also become surveillance.

The governance questions are real.

Who owns the learning history?

Can an employer use it to rank people?

Does a childhood mistake follow an adult?

Can the learner delete it?

Does the system infer ability beyond what the evidence supports?

A competence record can become a permanent dossier.

The same data that enable better training can enable more invasive control.

The ethical design should separate developmental feedback from punitive monitoring where possible.

People need room to be bad while learning.

If every error becomes a performance record, learners will hide errors.

Then the training system loses its most valuable data.

Psychological safety is not softness in this context.

It is an information architecture.

A team that punishes admission of uncertainty receives less uncertainty data.

A learner who fears the permanent record asks safer questions.

An employee whose prompts are monitored may avoid revealing confusion.

The institution gains surveillance and loses learning.

This is another version of the problem we saw with team hierarchy.

Information only exists for the system if people can afford to provide it.

Making competence visible therefore requires restraint.

Measure enough to teach.

Not so much that the learner performs confidence.

The same applies to expert elicitation.

If experts believe automation is being used to replace them immediately, they have rational reasons to protect tacit knowledge. If the institution treats knowledge capture as extraction, cooperation becomes a labor negotiation.

This is often ignored in technology projects.

Knowledge does not float above incentives.

The senior employee asked to train the model may be training a substitute.

The junior employee asked to rely on the model may be losing a career path.

The organization wants efficiency.

These interests can be aligned, but not by pretending conflict does not exist.

A credible transition has to answer: what happens to the people whose expertise is being captured, and how will new people become expert after capture?

Without those questions, “knowledge transfer” becomes a euphemism.

The best organizations will likely develop a new role for experts during automation.

Not merely validation.

Curriculum design.

Experts know the failure cases.

They can help select which cases juniors must still experience.

They can identify what should become a checklist, what can be automated completely, what needs simulation, and what remains judgment-intensive.

This turns the retiring expert from data source into architect of the next apprenticeship.

It also forces experts to confront the curse of expertise.

The cases they find trivial may be the cases learners need.

The cases they find interesting may be too advanced.

Instruction requires sequencing.

A competent curriculum has its own progression from explicit to tacit.

First name the parts.

Then connect them.

Then vary them.

Then hide the labels.

Then introduce exceptions.

Then increase speed.

Then add pressure.

Then require explanation.

Then let the skill compress.

Then reopen pieces for refinement.

This is not one staircase for every domain.

It is a design rhythm.

Make visible.

Practice.

Compress.

Stress.

Reflect.

Recompress.

Expertise remains dynamic because mature performers continue cycling through these states.

They do not stay unconscious forever.

A violinist changes technique.

A surgeon adopts a new procedure.

A programmer learns a new paradigm.

A pilot transitions to a new aircraft.

A manager enters a different culture.

The expert becomes selectively novice again.

This is healthy.

The dangerous expert is not the one who knows automatically.

It is the one who cannot detect where automatic knowledge stopped applying.

The after-action review offers one of the clearest examples of how organizations can make competence visible without pretending to extract it completely.

The form is simple.

What was supposed to happen?

What actually happened?

Why was there a difference?

What should change next time?

The power is not in the four questions.

It is in asking them while the event is still rich enough to resist mythology.

Without structured review, stories simplify quickly.

The successful launch becomes evidence that the plan was right.

The failed launch becomes evidence that somebody did not execute.

The incident becomes the one mistake everyone remembers.

The negotiation becomes the clever move the winner recounts.

After-action review can preserve the uncomfortable middle.

The plan may have been good and execution weak.

The plan may have been wrong and luck favorable.

A junior person may have noticed the decisive issue.

An expert may have been right for the wrong reason.

A procedure may have worked despite a hidden vulnerability.

The review creates an institutional memory closer to the event.

This is where expertise becomes teachable.

Not as doctrine.

As contrast between expectation and reality.

A good review should recover the decision environment.

What information existed at the time?

Which cue was ignored?

Which assumption looked reasonable then?

When did the situation become recognizable?

Which action created the next constraint?

What would a competent person need to notice earlier?

These questions turn an event into training material.

They also protect against hindsight bias.

The organization knows the ending.

Future learners need access to the beginning.

This is why a case should often be taught in stages.

Do not hand over the final report first.

Give the learner the information available at minute one.

Ask for a judgment.

Reveal the next development.

Ask again.

Let the learner experience the changing situation rather than admire the postmortem.

This method is common in good professional education because it recreates uncertainty.

AI can make it extraordinarily scalable.

Take a documented incident.

Convert it into an interactive sequence.

Hide future information.

Let the learner ask questions.

Branch the scenario around choices.

Compare the learner's attention with expert attention.

Reveal the actual outcome only after commitment.

The archive becomes an apprenticeship engine.

This is more valuable than asking a model to summarize the incident.

Summary compresses.

Training often needs decompression.

The learner must spend time inside the ambiguity the expert once faced.

That is also how tacit cues become partially observable.

Suppose several experienced technicians independently pause at the same sensor pattern before a failure.

The pattern may deserve study.

Suppose expert editors repeatedly flag the same kind of structural problem before they can articulate a rule.

Collect the cases.

Compare them.

Suppose senior clinicians request one additional test in a class of cases where guidelines leave room for judgment.

Investigate the cues.

AI can search across these traces at scale.

It can propose candidate regularities humans failed to name.

This is one of the most promising forms of knowledge capture because it begins from behavior and cases rather than self-description.

But candidate regularity is not truth.

The model may discover correlation that reflects habit, bias, or local convention.

The institution has to test whether the pattern improves outcomes.

The machine can help make tacit behavior visible.

Reality still has to grade it.

This is why expertise elicitation should include counterexamples.

The expert says, “I escalate when I see X.”

Find cases with X that were not escalated.

Why not?

The exception reveals the hidden condition.

The expert says, “I never trust that vendor's estimate.”

Find a case where the estimate was right.

What was different?

The expert says, “This writing pattern usually signals weak reporting.”

Find strong reporting with the same pattern.

The boundary sharpens.

Without counterexamples, knowledge capture can turn personal habits into official rules.

This is especially dangerous when the expert is powerful.

An organization can codify one senior person's style as institutional best practice.

The person retires.

The preference remains in the software.

Automation can fossilize judgment.

This is the opposite of learning.

The tool appears modern while preserving an unexamined past.

A serious knowledge-transfer project therefore needs a falsification step.

Which expert practices survive data?

Which survive peer challenge?

Which vary by context?

Which are compensations for a broken system rather than expertise worth preserving?

Which should disappear when the system is fixed?

That last category matters.

A nurse who knows how to work around a terrible interface possesses valuable local knowledge.

The goal should not necessarily be to teach every new nurse the workaround forever.

Fix the interface.

The expertise identified a design defect.

A warehouse worker who knows a dangerous shortcut may be keeping production moving.

Do not capture the shortcut as best practice.

Remove the condition that made it necessary.

Tacit knowledge is sometimes evidence of organizational debt.

The expert has learned to carry the system's flaws invisibly.

When that person leaves, the flaws become visible.

This is why executives can be shocked by the departure of an “ordinary” employee.

The employee had become an adapter.

Every day the person corrected discrepancies the formal process did not know existed.

The work looked routine because the correction was routine to the person.

The organization measured output.

It did not measure saved failures.

AI can reveal some of these adapters by mapping exceptions.

Where do humans override recommendations?

Which cases take longer?

Where do people leave the official system and use spreadsheets, messages, or phone calls?

What information do they seek before proceeding?

These deviations are clues.

Do not automate them away before understanding them.

A deviation can be waste.

It can also be the human layer keeping the formal system connected to reality.

The same discipline applies to teaching experts to teach.

An expert demonstration is valuable, but demonstration alone can conceal the decision structure.

Pause.

Ask the learner what should happen next.

Now show the expert.

Compare.

Ask why.

Repeat with a near miss.

Change one feature.

Does the expert's decision change?

The learner begins to acquire the conditional structure.

This is better than memorizing the expert's movement.

It teaches what controls the movement.

AI can generate the perturbations endlessly.

What if the patient were twenty years older?

What if the market were illiquid?

What if the customer had already tried the obvious solution?

What if the code ran across three regions?

What if the source were anonymous?

What if the aircraft were five thousand feet lower?

One variable changes.

The decision changes or does not.

The learner sees the boundary.

This is a way to accelerate the construction of expertise without pretending experience can be downloaded.

You cannot pour tacit knowledge into a novice.

You can expose the novice to a better sequence of contrasts.

That may be enough to shorten years of accidental learning.

The technology therefore creates a strange possibility.

AI could remove the apprenticeship.

AI could also make apprenticeship far more efficient than it has ever been.

The difference is whether the system gives answers or designs experiences.

Answer systems optimize the current task.

Experience systems optimize the future person.

The distinction should become visible in product design.

Does the tool remember errors?

Does it create counterexamples?

Does it revisit after delay?

Does it reduce support as skill increases?

Does it ask for a first judgment?

Does it expose expert disagreement?

Does it allow the learner to fail privately?

Does it generate cases from real failure modes?

These are features of an apprenticeship engine.

They are not the default features of a productivity assistant.

Organizations will need both.

The confusion comes when one is purchased and assumed to provide the other.

Making competence visible also changes succession planning.

Traditional succession asks who can take the role.

A better question is who has encountered enough of the role's decision distribution.

Has the candidate seen routine operations?

Crisis?

Ambiguous cases?

Stakeholder conflict?

Failure?

Recovery?

Has the candidate made decisions with consequences and received feedback?

The résumé can list responsibilities.

The case history shows developmental exposure.

This is a more honest account of readiness.

AI can help maintain that history, but the same privacy warning returns.

A case history should not become a deterministic score of a person.

Experience is evidence, not destiny.

A learner can make a mistake once and understand it permanently.

A person can lack a case and transfer a neighboring skill brilliantly.

Human capability remains more open than a dashboard will imply.

This is why the final purpose of visibility is not ranking.

It is allocation.

What practice does this person need next?

What support should be removed?

What case has the person never seen?

What pattern is becoming too automatic?

Where has the environment changed enough to reopen the skill?

The best knowledge system ends in a new experience.

Not another document.

Making the invisible visible is therefore not only a teaching strategy.

It is a maintenance strategy for expertise itself.

Take the intuition that arrived instantly.

Interrogate it after the decision.

What cue did it use?

Was the cue valid?

What did the expert miss?

Did the environment change?

Can the pattern be taught?

Should it be automated?

Should it be checked?

The answers may improve the person, the team, and the tool simultaneously.

This is what the best human-machine learning loop could become.

The human contributes lived domain structure.

The machine contributes memory, comparison, scale, simulation, and retrieval.

The learner contributes attempts.

Reality contributes correction.

No participant gets to grade itself.

That last condition is the important one.

Competence becomes trustworthy when something outside confidence can still say: wrong.
