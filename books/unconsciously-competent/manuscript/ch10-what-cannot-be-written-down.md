# Chapter 10 — What Cannot Be Written Down

Michael Polanyi built an intellectual career around a sentence that has survived partly because it describes something people already know in their hands.

“We can know more than we can tell.”

The line has become so familiar that it risks turning into decoration. Polanyi meant something sharper. Human knowing is not exhausted by explicit propositions. A person can rely on cues, relations, skills, and forms of attention that are difficult to specify independently from the act in which they are used.

Ride a bicycle.

Now write a complete set of instructions that would allow someone who has never balanced on two wheels to ride from the document alone.

You can explain steering, momentum, balance, pedaling, braking, where to look, and how not to panic. The document may help. It will not contain balance.

The learner has to encounter instability.

The body has to make corrections.

The instructions become meaningful inside practice.

This is tacit knowledge in one of its least controversial forms. The knowledge is not secret. The expert is not refusing to share. The competence cannot be transferred completely as sentences because some of it exists in learned relations between perception and action.

Organizations have spent decades fighting this fact.

They call it knowledge capture.

A senior engineer is retiring.

A master technician is leaving.

A nurse who knows every workaround in a hospital unit moves away.

A trader who understands a niche market is replaced.

A machinist with forty years on a production line stops coming in on Monday.

Management asks the same reasonable question.

Can we write down what they know?

The answer is yes.

And no.

A great deal can be written down.

Procedures can be documented.

Thresholds can be recorded.

Failure modes can be cataloged.

Contacts can be listed.

Decision criteria can be made explicit.

Cases can be preserved.

Videos can capture movements.

Data can reveal patterns.

Stories can explain why a rule exists.

Checklists can externalize steps too important to trust to memory.

The problem begins when documentation is mistaken for equivalence.

The binder fills.

The expertise leaves.

The organization discovers that the pages contain the nominal process while the departing employee had also known which gauge lies, which supplier's “two weeks” means a month, which vibration matters, which exception is harmless, which customer request predicts a larger problem, which colleague needs a phone call instead of an email, and when the standard procedure is about to fail.

Some of that knowledge should have been formalized.

Some of it may be context-bound in ways that resist full formalization.

Most of it became visible only through cases.

This is why experts often give disappointing answers to broad questions.

“How do you know when the machine is about to fail?”

“It sounds different.”

“What does different sound like?”

“You'll hear it.”

To the learner, this is infuriating.

To the expert, it may be honest.

The sound is not one frequency with a verbal label. It may be a relation among rhythm, load, temperature, vibration, previous repairs, and the normal acoustic signature of that particular machine. The expert has heard thousands of examples. The category exists before the vocabulary.

The solution is not to accept mystery.

It is to change the extraction method.

Do not ask for “the knowledge.”

Put the expert in front of cases.

Play the recordings.

Show the image.

Recreate the fault.

Ask what changed.

Ask what would make the expert ignore the same cue.

Compare a normal case with a near miss.

Ask for counterexamples.

Watch what the hands do.

Stop at the moment of hesitation.

The closer the conversation gets to actual perception and decision, the more tacit structure can become discussable.

This is one reason apprenticeship works.

The learner sees knowledge in motion.

A craft apprentice does not merely hear a rule about material. The apprentice sees the master respond when the material violates the rule. A medical trainee watches the attending physician return to a patient after a laboratory result that seems only mildly unusual. A junior reporter sees which sentence makes the editor ask for the source document. A young mechanic sees the senior technician touch one part after hearing a noise somewhere else.

The learner acquires not only instructions but salience.

What deserves attention?

What can be ignored?

When should the routine stop?

This is hard to write down because salience is relational.

The important detail changes with context.

A number that is normal in one patient is alarming in another.

A vibration acceptable at startup is concerning under steady load.

A clause that looks standard in one contract shifts risk in another.

A word that is harmless in one interview reveals evasion in another because of what was asked just before it.

Tacit expertise lives in these relations.

That does not mean it is mystical.

It means the representation is dense.

Polanyi used the idea of “indwelling” to describe how tools and subsidiary cues can become extensions through which a person attends to something else. A blind person using a cane, for example, does not primarily attend to the pressure of the cane in the hand. The cane becomes a means of attending to the environment.

Tools disappear into skilled use.

A surgeon feels through an instrument.

A driver feels the road through a steering wheel.

A musician feels the instrument rather than a collection of keys.

A carpenter attends through a tool to the material.

This is another form of unconscious competence: the interface becomes transparent enough that attention reaches past it.

Bad tools reverse the relation.

They force attention back onto themselves.

A poorly designed interface makes the surgeon think about the interface.

A laggy instrument makes the musician think about the instrument.

An unfamiliar car makes the driver think about the controls.

A software redesign makes the employee think about where the command moved.

Expertise is partly the achievement of tool transparency.

This has profound implications for AI systems because language interfaces can become transparent unusually quickly.

A person asks.

The system answers.

There is almost no visible machinery between intention and output.

That simplicity can make the tool feel like an extension of thought before the user has learned the boundaries of the system.

The cane gives tactile feedback about the world.

The language model gives generated language about the world.

Those are different epistemic relationships.

A fluent interface can create a dangerous kind of indwelling if the person begins attending through the model without maintaining awareness of the model's uncertainties, training limitations, source gaps, and tendency to produce plausible text.

The better the interface feels, the more important competence becomes.

This is a recurring paradox of good technology.

Ease of use expands access.

Ease of use also hides mechanism.

A mature camera allows a beginner to produce a well-exposed photograph without understanding exposure.

A navigation system allows a traveler to arrive without understanding the city.

A spreadsheet allows a user to perform operations without understanding numerical analysis.

An AI system allows a user to generate domain language without possessing domain knowledge.

These are achievements.

They are also new ways for output and competence to separate.

The difference becomes visible when adaptation is required.

The automatic camera encounters a scene outside its assumptions.

The navigation system loses signal.

The spreadsheet model includes the wrong formula.

The AI answer cites a nonexistent authority.

The competent user notices because the world and the output do not fit.

The novice may notice only fluency.

Tacit knowledge matters here because verification often begins as discomfort.

Something feels off.

That phrase can irritate people trained to demand explicit reasons.

It should.

A feeling is not evidence.

But it can be a signal to look for evidence.

Experienced professionals often develop anomaly detection before they can articulate the anomaly. The correct institutional response is not to accept the feeling as proof and not to suppress it because it lacks immediate explanation.

Investigate.

The smell of smoke is not a fire report.

It is a reason to look for fire.

The same principle should govern expert intuition.

Tacit alarm starts the audit.

Explicit evidence closes it.

This protects against two opposite errors.

The first is technocratic arrogance: if the expert cannot explain the knowledge, the knowledge does not exist.

The second is romantic arrogance: because the expert cannot explain the knowledge, the knowledge is beyond analysis and should be trusted.

Neither is defensible.

Tacit knowledge can be real and wrong.

A veteran police officer can develop intuitions shaped by biased exposure.

A doctor can internalize outdated clinical patterns.

A manager can become skilled at navigating one corporate culture and mistake those habits for universal leadership.

A machinist can trust a sensory cue that was reliable on an older machine and misleading on a new one.

Experience stores the environment it experienced.

If the environment is biased, obsolete, or narrow, the competence can inherit the bias, obsolescence, or narrowness.

Documentation can help because explicit rules are easier to inspect.

Data can help.

Diverse teams can challenge local intuitions.

External benchmarks can reveal systematic errors.

Technology can detect patterns humans miss.

Again, the question is not tacit versus explicit.

It is how different forms of knowledge correct one another.

This is why checklists will matter in the next chapter.

A checklist can look insulting to expertise because it states obvious things.

Wash hands.

Confirm patient.

Confirm procedure.

Check fuel.

Verify configuration.

The expert already knows.

That is the point.

Some knowledge is too important to leave in a system optimized to stop noticing the familiar.

Automaticity frees attention by making routine actions less salient.

A checklist selectively makes a few routines salient again.

It is an external interrupt.

The best checklist does not replace judgment.

It reserves judgment for where judgment matters by protecting against omission where memory is a poor tool.

Tacit knowledge and explicit procedure are partners when designed well.

This partnership also suggests a better way to preserve expertise across generations.

Do not try to extract the expert into a database and declare victory.

Build learning environments.

Capture cases.

Preserve failures.

Create simulations.

Pair people.

Record reasoning before outcomes.

Use checklists for invariants.

Use stories for context.

Use data for calibration.

Use tools to expose rare examples.

Let novices attempt before revealing expert responses.

Ask experts to compare cases rather than summarize themselves.

The object is not to store knowledge.

It is to reproduce capability.

Those are different engineering problems.

A library stores information.

An apprenticeship changes a person.

Modern organizations are very good at the first and often accidental about the second.

This is going to become more consequential as AI makes information retrieval trivial.

If nearly every procedure, explanation, and example is available on demand, the scarce resource shifts.

It is no longer access to instructions.

It is the formation of judgment.

That formation still appears to require time spent discriminating among cases, making predictions, receiving correction, and building representations that allow a person to notice what matters before someone else labels it.

AI can help with every part of that process.

It can also help a person bypass every part.

The interface alone does not tell us which is happening.

This is why “knowledge worker” may become a misleading phrase.

Information is becoming cheap.

Generated knowledge-like artifacts are becoming cheap.

What remains scarce is the person who knows when the artifact is wrong.

That competence is partly explicit.

It is partly tacit.

And the most valuable part may be the part that first appears as a pause.

Wait.

That doesn't look right.

Civilizations depend on people who can say that sentence before they can fully explain why.

The task is not to preserve mystery around them.

The task is to make sure there are still enough people who have earned the right to notice.
