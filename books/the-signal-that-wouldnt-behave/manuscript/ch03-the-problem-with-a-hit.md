# 3 — The Problem With a Hit

A hit is not a primitive object. Someone has to manufacture it.

That sentence sounds accusatory. It is mostly statistical. A remote-viewing transcript does not arrive stamped TRUE or FALSE. It contains observations, sketches, adjectives, guesses, repetitions, hesitations and often a great deal of material with no obvious relation to the eventual target. To turn that material into a hit, an evaluator needs a comparison rule. The rule can be strict or generous. It can be numerical or narrative. It can be prospective or retrospective. Those choices decide how much freedom the evaluator has after the answer is known.

This is why the famous stories are dangerous. One viewer describes a crane-like structure at a Soviet facility. Another draws something resembling a gantry. A later event seems to match an earlier sketch. A participant remembers the officer who went pale. Stories like these can be true in the ordinary sense—people really did say things that later resembled targets—and still fail to establish anomalous information transfer. The evidentiary question is not whether there were striking correspondences. In a large archive of verbose descriptions, some correspondences are inevitable. The question is how surprising those correspondences were under the scoring system that existed before the target was revealed.

Intelligence culture makes this worse because analysts are trained to rescue fragments from noise. A HUMINT report can contain one useful detail among pages of rumor. A satellite image can become important because a tiny change in a familiar pattern means something. Signals intelligence often depends on weak pieces joined with other sources. In normal intelligence work, contextual interpretation is competence.

In a test of remote viewing, contextual interpretation can destroy the test.

The 1995 AIR evaluation understood this collision. Its operational review did not merely ask whether users could point to correct details. It asked whether the information had value. That means uniqueness, reliability and decision consequence. A source who sometimes says "water," "metal" and "large structure" may be above chance in a target-matching experiment and still be useless to a commander who needs to know which building contains the hostage.

The word "actionable" becomes important here. It is frequently abused as a synonym for "interesting." In intelligence practice it means information specific and reliable enough to affect an action. A remote-viewing report could be intriguing, even eerily so, without crossing that threshold. If it required independent collection to know which parts were right, then the independent collection was doing the decisive work.

This yields a brutal operational test: remove everything learned after the session and ask what a rational user could have done with the report at the time.

That test shrinks many miracles.

It also protects genuine anomalies. If a viewer repeatedly supplies concealed, low-base-rate details under preregistered conditions, those details should not need storytelling. They can be scored. A strong phenomenon should become more impressive as evaluator freedom decreases.

Remote viewing often moved in the opposite direction. Its most persuasive public examples are narrative. Its most defensible scientific claims are aggregate. The aggregate claims, in turn, are modest in the sense that they concern statistical deviations across trials rather than cinematic one-shot revelations. This mismatch is one reason believers and skeptics talk past each other. Believers point to improbable-seeming individual sessions; skeptics attack cueing and selection. Statisticians point to pooled effects; operational evaluators point to unreliability. Each group is answering a different question.

The archive contains a useful antidote to cherry-picking: failure reports written by the program itself. One 1982 training session, number 876, is summarized in the declassified file as showing "very little correlation." Another 1978 session is described as having "absolutely no target correlation." These are valuable documents because they prove that the bureaucracy did not preserve only triumphs. They also reveal the problem. A viewer could fail badly on one target and appear striking on another. If the effect exists, it was unstable. If it does not, instability is exactly what noisy matching would produce.

The difference between those possibilities cannot be resolved by the best story in the folder.

A clean evaluation therefore needs at least four things. First, the target and scoring rule must be fixed before output is inspected. Second, misses must survive in the dataset. Third, the judge must not receive side information that can solve the matching problem. Fourth, performance must be estimated prospectively, not only after selecting the viewers, trials or targets that looked promising.

None of this is exotic. It is ordinary experimental hygiene.

That is the recurring irony of the STAR GATE story. The farther you go into the paranormal claim, the more the decisive arguments concern file handling, randomization, customer feedback forms, target pools, effect sizes and whether one person knew something they were not supposed to know.

The ghosts, if there were ghosts, lived in the paperwork.
