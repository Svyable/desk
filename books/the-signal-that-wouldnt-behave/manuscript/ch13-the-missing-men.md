# 13 — The Missing Men

There is a list in the declassified record that should end the romance of remote viewing more effectively than any skeptical joke.

Lieutenant Colonel William Higgins. CIA station chief William Buckley. DEA agent Enrique Camarena.

All were missing, abducted or murdered. Declassified CIA material later noted that STAR GATE efforts were used in attempts to locate people in high-profile cases and that those efforts failed. Higgins, taken while serving with a United Nations peacekeeping mission in Lebanon, was killed by his captors. Buckley was abducted in Beirut and died in captivity. Camarena was kidnapped and murdered in Mexico.

These are not laboratory targets. They are where the claim encounters moral weight.

A weakly calibrated method can be entertaining when the target is a photograph in an envelope. When the target is a kidnapped person, a false location competes with real investigative leads. A method that sometimes produces striking descriptions but cannot identify which description is trustworthy may be worse than useless if confidence attaches to the wrong fragment.

This does not mean remote-viewing teams caused operational harm in these cases. The public record does not establish that. The point is narrower: the taskings offered exactly the kind of external validation a claimed intelligence capability should welcome. A missing person has a real location. Investigators act. Outcomes are eventually known. If viewers can reliably reduce the search space, repeated cases should leave a measurable signature.

Instead, the history preserved by the Agency includes failure.

That honesty is important because participants and popular accounts often gravitate toward success stories. Project mythology has a natural selection process. A dramatic target becomes a chapter in a memoir. A session that produced generic terrain and an incorrect building becomes a box in an archive. Decades later the public encounters the selected stories first.

The missing-person cases reverse the selection pressure. Failure matters more because the objective was concrete.

They also expose the difference between "correlation" and rescue. A viewer can describe something that resembles the eventual captivity environment and still fail to locate the person. From the viewpoint of consciousness research, a partial match might be significant. From the viewpoint of the family, it is not enough.

This is where proponents sometimes respond that remote viewing should never have been used alone. Fair. Intelligence sources are fused. A weak source might contribute one clue. But the fusion defense creates a measurement problem: once the clue is combined with everything else, how do we know it added value? The program needed prospective accounting. Did a remote-viewing report cause a search area to shrink? Did conventional collection later confirm a unique detail first supplied by the viewer? How often? At what false-positive cost?

Without those records, the method remains protected by ambiguity.

The ethical question is not whether unconventional methods should ever be tried in desperate situations. Desperation changes thresholds. If a child is trapped somewhere and a psychic calls police with a location, investigators may check it if the cost is low. The ethical question is whether checking one claim displaces a better lead, legitimizes an unvalidated method, or creates false hope.

Government programs should be more disciplined than desperation.

The Higgins file is especially instructive because the CIA's own later declassification-processing summary states that U.S. military and intelligence efforts to find him failed, including STAR GATE remote viewing. This is not a skeptic reconstructing failure from silence. It is institutional memory.

No single failed case disproves an effect. A radar can miss an aircraft. A drug can fail a patient. A spy can be wrong. The power comes from accumulation. A tool becomes real by surviving many opportunities to fail.

STAR GATE had opportunities.

The operational record did not converge the way a mature capability should.

There is a human tendency to protect anomalous claims from their hardest tests by moving the goalposts. If a viewer cannot locate a hostage, perhaps the target was emotionally difficult. If the description is wrong, perhaps the viewer picked up a future location. If a target moves, perhaps the session captured the previous one. Every rescue hypothesis may be possible. Together they can make the phenomenon impossible to kill.

A science that cannot lose cannot learn.

The missing men deserve a stricter legacy than being footnotes in psychic-war folklore. They show what operational validation means when the ground truth has consequences.

The signal, if present, did not arrive loudly enough.
