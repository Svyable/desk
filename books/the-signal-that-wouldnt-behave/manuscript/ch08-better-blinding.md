# 8 — Better Blinding

The remote-viewing research that reached the 1995 review was not frozen in 1974.

That matters because a criticism can become obsolete without the larger claim becoming true. Early work drew fire for transcript cues, judging procedures, possible sensory leakage, loose protocols and insufficient independent replication. Later experiments attempted to close those routes. Target selection could be randomized. The viewer could be isolated from anyone who knew the target. Responses could be compared against concealed target sets. Automated procedures reduced experimenter contact. Statistical scoring replaced some retrospective storytelling.

This is how a controversial field should respond to criticism.

It also raises the standard for the critic. Once a specific flaw is repaired, repeating the old flaw is no longer enough. The skeptic must show either that the repair failed, another artifact remains, or the aggregate evidence is not as strong as claimed. Ray Hyman's 1995 review is interesting precisely because he did not simply recycle the easy objections. He acknowledged improvements and still refused the paranormal conclusion.

The later work associated with Science Applications International Corporation used a more formal experimental architecture than the folklore surrounding psychic spying suggests. The 1995 AIR panel was asked to review this research alongside the operational program. Jessica Utts focused on the statistical pattern and argued that anomalous cognition had been established to a standard comparable with other sciences. Hyman agreed that the results were not easily dismissed as random fluctuation but argued that methodological, interpretive and replication issues prevented attribution to psychic functioning.

The disagreement turns partly on what replication means.

If the same small group of investigators repeatedly obtains an effect under improved controls, is that replication? It is internal replication. Science normally wants more: independent laboratories, independent analysts, independent incentives, and ideally adversarial teams that can agree in advance on what would count as success. Psi research has always struggled with this because the community is small and polarized. Proponents accumulate expertise that may genuinely improve protocols, but that concentration makes critics worry about laboratory-specific artifacts. Skeptics enter with less domain experience and may design replications proponents reject as insensitive. Each failure becomes interpretable through the worldview of the side that disliked it.

This dynamic is not unique to parapsychology. Small fields studying fragile effects often become dependent on tacit knowledge. Experimentalists say outsiders failed because they did not reproduce the crucial conditions. Outsiders say a phenomenon that survives only in one laboratory is not robust. Both can be right for a while. Eventually an effect that belongs to nature should become portable.

Portability is where remote viewing remained weak.

A genuine effect can be real before its mechanism is known. Mendelian inheritance preceded DNA. Anesthesia worked before anyone had a satisfactory molecular account. Continental drift was argued before plate tectonics supplied the machinery. Lack of mechanism is not fatal by itself.

But those effects had hard external structure. Pea traits segregated. Patients stopped responding to surgery. Continents fit multiple independent geological observations. Remote viewing depended on statistical scoring of subjective output. In that setting, mechanism becomes more important because it can generate independent predictions.

Does the effect decay with distance? Apparently not, according to proponents. Does shielding matter? Not in ordinary ways. Does time order matter? Some experiments claim precognitive effects. Does electromagnetic environment matter? Results are inconsistent. What physiological variable tracks performance? None has become a reliable meter. Without these constraints, "anomalous information" risks becoming a residual category: whatever remains after known channels are excluded.

Better blinding therefore accomplished something important even if it did not settle psi. It changed the problem from "the judges may have read the date on the transcript" to "what inference is warranted by a persistent but mechanism-free statistical deviation in a specialized literature?"

That is a much harder question.

It is also the question Jessica Utts believed had already been answered.
