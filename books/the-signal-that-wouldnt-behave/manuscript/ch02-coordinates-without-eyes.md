# 2 — Coordinates Without Eyes

The phrase "remote viewing" makes the method sound more visual than it was. Many sessions were not visions in the cinematic sense. They were inventories: edges, temperatures, textures, heights, colors, open spaces, enclosed spaces, water, metal, motion, pressure. A viewer might draw a line, cross it out, circle another, announce an "analytical overlay," then return to raw impressions. What looked from the outside like paranormal theater often looked on paper like a person trying not to name the thing they believed they were describing.

That distinction mattered because naming was dangerous.

Suppose the target is a suspension bridge. A viewer who says "bridge" has made a high-risk claim. A viewer who says "long horizontal element," "vertical supports," "open air," "water below," and "metal" has produced components that can later be compared with many targets. The second output may contain more information, but it also gives the evaluator more degrees of freedom. Remote-viewing methodology spent years oscillating between those poles: ask for specificity and increase obvious misses; allow low-level description and increase retrospective fit.

Coordinate remote viewing tried to discipline the process. The coordinate itself was meant to function as a pointer rather than a semantic hint. Numbers could identify a target without telling the viewer whether it was a shipyard, mountain, laboratory or empty field. In principle this severed the ordinary informational link. In practice, every stage around the coordinate mattered: who selected the target, who knew the answer, who was in the room, what the interviewer knew, how the transcript was handled, how judges saw it, what feedback the viewer received, and whether the target set contained features that made some descriptions easier to match than others.

The history of experimental psychology is full of methods that failed because the experimenter underestimated channels. Clever Hans did not perform arithmetic; the horse read human cues. Facilitated communication could feel subjectively independent while the facilitator controlled the output. Early drug trials overestimated treatment when patients and clinicians knew who received what. Science learned, often painfully, that knowledge leaks through posture, timing, expectations, file order, phrasing and analysis choices. A claim about information appearing with no known sensory channel has to be more—not less—obsessive about those ordinary channels.

The early SRI experiments brought precisely that scrutiny.

David Marks and Richard Kammann attempted replications and investigated the judging procedures. Their criticism became famous because it targeted a mundane route to an extraordinary result: transcripts could contain cues associated with session order. If judges could infer chronology and if target visits had occurred in an order, the matching problem was no longer blind in the relevant sense. Marks and Kammann reported that removing such cues collapsed the apparent success. Targ, Puthoff and Charles Tart disputed the criticism and defended the work in Nature. The argument became technical, personal and durable.

The useful lesson is not that one side won forever in 1978. It is that the criticism identified a class of contamination that later experiments had to design around. This is what progress looks like even in a field whose central claim remains disputed. A methodological attack can be valuable whether or not the phenomenon survives it.

Remote viewing eventually acquired procedures that would have looked fussy to the original sponsors: concealed target identities, randomized target selection, separation of roles, rank-order judging, automated components, defined response windows, independent evaluation. The later SAIC-era experiments reviewed in 1995 were not simply copies of the earliest SRI demonstrations. Ray Hyman, a skeptical psychologist with a long history of criticizing parapsychology, acknowledged that the later work addressed many previous objections. Jessica Utts argued that the accumulated statistics were strong enough to establish an anomalous effect. Their disagreement was no longer well described as "one person believes in ESP and one does not." It concerned what causal conclusion can be drawn from a persistent statistical deviation in a small, unusual research literature.

Before reaching that argument, however, it is worth looking at the thing the protocol was trying to protect against: us.

Human beings are very good at matching fragments after the fact. We are good at finding the sentence in a dream that resembles what happened the next morning. We remember the friend we thought about just before the phone rang and forget the hundred people we thought about who did not call. Intelligence analysis adds a professional version of the same hazard. Analysts work in rich context. They know what facilities of a certain class tend to contain. They can fit partial reports into hypotheses already under consideration. This is often a strength. It becomes a liability when the question is whether a source supplied information independently of what the analyst already knew.

The coordinate was supposed to create an informational vacuum. But a vacuum is not created by hiding the target name from the viewer. It has to extend through the entire chain.

Who talks to whom? Who knows what? What does the transcript reveal about its own history? When is feedback provided? Can viewers learn the composition of the target pool over repeated trials? Does the judge receive one transcript and several targets, several transcripts and several targets, or a score already generated by someone invested in the project? Are the targets sufficiently distinct? Is the scoring rule frozen before anybody sees the answer? Are failed sessions retained?

These questions sound pedestrian beside telepathy. That is exactly why they matter.

Every extraordinary information claim is surrounded by ordinary information channels. The job of the experiment is to kill them one at a time. Remote viewing became scientifically interesting to the extent that later work actually did so. It became operationally frustrating because intelligence operations could not kill context without also killing usefulness.

A field analyst wants context. A laboratory statistician wants isolation.

The better the experiment became, the less it resembled espionage.
