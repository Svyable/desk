# 5 — GRILL FLAME

By 1978 the question had left the cleaner world of contractor experiments and entered the Army.

A declassified INSCOM progress report dated 21 February 1979 describes the creation of a program using parapsychological phenomena for intelligence applications. Later CENTER LANE histories reconstruct the bureaucratic path: the Army Assistant Chief of Staff for Intelligence accepted lead responsibility for GRILL FLAME activities in May 1978; INSCOM became the Army's active operational element; management transferred to the INSCOM commander in February 1981; DIA retained broader Defense Department responsibility for a time. These details are more important than the code names. They show an unusual research claim acquiring institutional metabolism.

Metabolism means budgets, billets, training, approvals, lawyers, equipment and reporting chains.

One CENTER LANE memorandum records $201,000 spent in 1981, most of it on external contracts with SRI International. The following year involved another joint-services expenditure, and 1983 funds supported coordinate-remote-viewing training and work with the Monroe Institute. The numbers are small beside major weapons systems. That matters. A modest program can survive uncertainty longer because it does not need to defeat an aircraft carrier in a budget fight. It needs only to remain interesting enough that somebody thinks killing it might be premature.

The Army also encountered a revealing problem: human-subject rules. General Counsel determined in 1979 that GRILL FLAME involved experimentation on human subjects. The paper trail then winds through medical and legal review over whether activities constituted research, technology transfer or validation. That argument places the program firmly in the ordinary world of government experimentation. However strange the claimed phenomenon, people still had to sign off on what could be done to participants.

Operationalization changed the epistemic environment. A laboratory target can be selected because it scores cleanly. An intelligence target is selected because somebody needs an answer. The customer may already know partial information. The viewer may receive contextual cues. The output may be interpreted by officers who know what they hope to find. Feedback may be incomplete for years because the ground truth is itself classified or uncertain. Each operational advantage is a scientific liability.

Yet the operational program also posed the only question that ultimately mattered to the sponsor. Could remote viewing reduce uncertainty where conventional collection could not?

GRILL FLAME session reports display a bureaucratic modesty that later popular accounts often lose. Many explicitly label the output "raw intelligence data" and state that it has not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation or collation. Interpretation is left to the requester. That disclaimer is defensible tradecraft: a collector should distinguish source report from finished assessment. It also exposes the central difficulty. If the source cannot say which parts of its own output are signal, the burden migrates downstream.

A normal sensor has a calibration curve. A radar operator knows approximate range error. A satellite imager knows resolution. A HUMINT case officer grades source access and reliability. Remote viewing struggled to produce an equivalent uncertainty model. The viewer might feel confidence, but confidence did not reliably identify correctness. A long transcript could contain an unknown mixture of target-related description, imagination, inference, memory and noise.

The program attempted to manage this through training and protocol. Coordinate remote viewing formalized stages and encouraged viewers to distinguish sensory-like impressions from "analytical overlay," the mind's tendency to jump from a feature to a named object. The vocabulary is psychologically astute whether or not psi exists. Anyone who has looked at a blurry satellite photograph understands the urge to name a pattern before enough evidence has accumulated.

But formalization can create a second illusion: that a named error channel is a controlled error channel. Calling a guess analytical overlay does not prove the remaining impressions are anomalous. A procedure can improve discipline without validating its theory.

GRILL FLAME's greatest historical value may be that it forces these layers apart. The Army genuinely ran the program. Officers genuinely trained viewers. Real operational requests were tasked. Real money moved. Real classified reports were produced. None of those facts, individually or together, establish that remote viewing supplied paranormal information.

The next question is whether the work changed outcomes.

For that, the program went looking for people it desperately wanted to find.
