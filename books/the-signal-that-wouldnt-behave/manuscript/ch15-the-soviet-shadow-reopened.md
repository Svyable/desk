# 15 — The Soviet Shadow, Reopened

After the Soviet Union collapsed, the original threat logic should have become easier to test.

Archives opened unevenly. Scientists traveled. Former officials spoke. Western researchers visited institutions that had once existed mainly as intelligence-report abstractions. What emerged was not a clean revelation of a hidden psychic arsenal.

There had been real Soviet and Eastern Bloc interest in parapsychology and so-called psychotronics. There were laboratories, personalities, claims, military interest and periodic bursts of official attention. But the public record does not establish an operational Soviet remote-viewing capability comparable to the capability feared in some American assessments. The historical picture looks more like a contested research ecosystem than a secret weapon that forced the United States to catch up.

This should not surprise us.

Authoritarian systems are not immune to scientific fashion. They may be more vulnerable in some ways because patronage can protect a favored claim while secrecy blocks external correction. A military sponsor can fund an unconventional idea for the same reason on either side of an ideological border: the possibility of advantage is worth a small wager.

The Soviet case also reveals how intelligence can accidentally create prestige. If American officials believe Moscow is investing heavily in psychotronics, American researchers can cite Soviet investment when seeking funds. If Soviet officials learn the Americans have classified psychic programs, that becomes evidence that Washington takes the field seriously. Each side's investigation validates the other's concern.

This feedback loop is difficult to document completely because the relevant archives are partial, languages and institutions changed, and participants have incentives to amplify their past significance. Claims made after the Soviet collapse must therefore be classified carefully. A scientist saying a ministry funded experiments is evidence of funding if corroborated. A former officer saying operational psychics penetrated American secrets is testimony, not proof.

The American declassified archive sometimes preserves Soviet claims without resolving them. That is normal intelligence practice. Analysts collect assertions because they may matter. Decades later, however, FOIA readers encounter the assertion detached from the uncertainty that surrounded it. A typed intelligence report with classification markings looks authoritative. The visual grammar of secrecy can overpower the epistemic content.

This is one reason the STAR GATE collection is so easily mined for confirmation. Search engines surface the strangest phrase. The file exists on cia.gov. The proposition acquires a halo.

A better reading asks what kind of document it is.

Is it an experimental report? A translation of a foreign publication? A briefing note summarizing press claims? A threat assessment? A contractor proposal? A session transcript? A later retrospective? These are not interchangeable.

The Soviet shadow should therefore be retained in the book, but demoted from explanation to context. It helps explain why American agencies considered the subject. It does not establish the underlying phenomenon.

There is a broader intelligence lesson here. Threat assessment is path-dependent. Once an adversary is believed to be exploring a capability, programs appear to monitor and hedge against it. Those programs generate their own observations, contractor communities and classified vocabulary. Later historians can mistake the resulting documentary density for evidence of the capability's effectiveness.

Document density proves attention.

It does not prove success.

The same caution applies to modern technological rumors. A classified adversary program in hypersonics, AI, directed energy or biotechnology may be important because of what the adversary hopes to achieve rather than what it can currently do. States often fund the frontier precisely because the frontier is uncertain.

STAR GATE began under that uncertainty and outlived the Soviet state that helped sustain it.

By 1995, "the Russians might be ahead" could no longer do as much budgetary work. The program had to stand on its own laboratory and operational record.

That was a much less forgiving adversary.
