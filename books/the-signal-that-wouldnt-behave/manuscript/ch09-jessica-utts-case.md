# 9 — Jessica Utts's Case

Jessica Utts entered the 1995 review with a position already on the record.

That matters. She was not a statistician who wandered into STAR GATE with no prior exposure to parapsychology and emerged astonished. She had analyzed anomalous-cognition experiments for years, including work done under the same research program the government was now asking AIR to judge. Ray Hyman came with an equally public history on the other side. AIR did not solve the problem of prior belief by pretending to find two people without any. It put the disagreement in the report.

Utts's case was statistical before it was metaphysical. Her claim was not that a strange mechanism had been identified. It was that the later controlled experiments produced deviations from chance too persistent to dismiss merely because the proposed phenomenon was strange. In her view, the accumulated evidence met the standard by which other experimental sciences would normally conclude that an effect existed.

There is a number behind that confidence, and it makes the argument less cinematic.

In analyses from the program's own research stream, anomalous-cognition performance was discussed in terms of an effect size around 0.16. That is small. It is also large enough, if it is stable and the model is right, to matter statistically across a body of trials. The awkward part is what happens when one asks a single new experiment to reproduce it. A contemporary program analysis calculated that with an effect size of 0.159, a fifty-trial study would have only about a thirty-percent chance of reaching the conventional p ≤ .05 threshold. Seventy percent of such studies could therefore look like failures even if the assumed effect were real.

This was one reason Utts argued so hard against treating statistical significance as a synonym for replication. A p-value depends on sample size. A weak but stable effect can miss an arbitrary significance line in a small study; a trivial bias can cross it in a large one. The more useful question is whether independently collected experiments estimate roughly the same underlying effect.

That argument deserves more respect than the usual psychic-versus-skeptic caricature gives it.

It also creates a nasty practical problem.

The program's own records contain experiments that did not cooperate. A 1987 real-time-versus-precognition series used four viewers with favorable previous records. Across 52 real-time and 55 precognition trials, neither condition nor the combined result produced significant evidence of remote viewing. The report called the outcome disappointing and then considered possible reasons: the target photographs, the protocol, the unusually long delay before feedback.

That is exactly where a scientific anomaly and an intelligence instrument begin to part company. Explanations for a null can be perfectly reasonable. They can also multiply faster than predictions. If target type, feedback delay, viewer selection, training history, emotional salience, judging procedure and other conditions all alter performance, then the effect needs a map of those dependencies before anyone can use it.

The training record was no kinder to a simple technology story. A 1989 program report described three quantitative experiments involving eighteen novice viewers and 481 trials. Six self-selected novices produced a modest positive aggregate result, with an effect size of 0.132, but showed no evidence that training improved their performance. A second group of nine produced an aggregate effect size of -0.065 and no significant remote-viewing result. In a third group of three, one viewer showed significant improvement. The report's conclusion was restrained: evidence for remote viewing had been observed, but quantitative evidence that training improved the skill had not been established; simple practice did not appear to improve performance.

This is stronger counterevidence than saying merely that effect sizes in small literatures can wobble. The people running the research were trying to discover how to turn an apparent effect into a controllable capability, and some of their own experiments refused the conversion.

Utts's statistical conclusion does not disappear because of those nulls. In fact, her argument anticipates them: a small true effect should generate plenty of individually nonsignificant studies. The harder question is whether the distribution of positive and negative results is what a stable weak effect predicts, or whether it reflects changing protocols, selected viewers, unnoticed artifacts, analysis flexibility, or other laboratory-specific structure.

Statistics alone cannot choose among those causal stories. A low p-value says that a specified null model fits badly. It does not tell you why. If target generation contains a bias, if judging leaks information, if experiments are selected for publication, or if an analysis convention quietly changes after investigators see the data, the arithmetic can be impeccable while the interpretation is wrong.

Utts knew this. Her position was that the accumulated controls and repeated effect sizes made ordinary explanations increasingly implausible, not that a p-value opened a portal.

The intelligence problem is harsher still. Suppose her strongest conclusion is granted: some experiments contain anomalous information. The next question is not whether STAR GATE gets a star in the history of consciousness. It is how much information arrives, under what conditions, and whether anybody knows when to trust it.

A fraction of a bit can be scientifically enormous and operationally miserable.

A viewer who nudges the probability of the correct target above chance in a forced-choice laboratory can force an argument about mind and matter. A hostage search is not a five-picture target pool. The world contains too many coordinates, buildings, roads, names and plausible descriptions. Open-world intelligence punishes vagueness because almost every sufficiently rich verbal report will resemble something after the fact.

What the program needed was not another adjective for the phenomenon but an empirical transfer function. Does distance matter? Delay? Feedback? Target entropy? Viewer fatigue? Can confidence ratings predict correctness prospectively? Can two viewers be combined without cherry-picking the overlap? Does the same operator remain calibrated next month? Does training move the distribution, or merely produce more elaborate descriptions?

The surviving documents make those questions less rhetorical. They show researchers varying feedback, target sets, selection and training because the effect would not simply sit still and become a device.

That is why the 1995 disagreement is more interesting than either side's slogan. Utts could look at the laboratory record and see a small statistical signal that, in her judgment, had survived enough methodological repair to deserve recognition. Hyman could agree that the later work was better while remaining unconvinced that the anomaly had been separated from methodological and interpretive alternatives. The intelligence customer faced a different standard from both of them.

A laboratory can spend years learning the conditions under which a weak effect appears. An operations officer eventually has to decide whether today's report should move people, money or attention.

STAR GATE never learned to make that last probability legible.
