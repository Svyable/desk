# 9 — Jessica Utts's Case

Jessica Utts was not hired to make the story comfortable.

A statistician at the University of California, Davis, Utts had worked on parapsychology data before the 1995 review. That history made her an obvious target for critics who wanted a neutral outsider. AIR's design was more interesting: pair her with Ray Hyman, a psychologist and prominent skeptic, and make the disagreement explicit. The review did not pretend that expert judgment could be purified of prior belief by choosing people without priors. It put opposed priors in the room.

Utts's argument was statistical before it was metaphysical. Across a body of controlled experiments, she saw effect sizes that were too consistent and too large to be plausibly assigned to chance alone. She argued that the standards used in other areas of science would be sufficient to conclude that psychic functioning had been demonstrated.

The strongest version of her case does not depend on the word psychic.

Imagine repeated randomized trials with concealed targets. The responses are scored by rules fixed in advance. The mean performance stays above the chance expectation across experiments. The pattern appears in more than one protocol. Obvious sensory leakage has been designed out. Under ordinary statistical practice, one does not keep calling the deviation luck merely because the proposed mechanism is strange.

That argument deserves to be taken seriously.

It also has limits that are often removed when Utts is quoted by enthusiasts. Statistical significance does not tell you which causal story produced the effect. It tells you that the null model, as specified, fits poorly. If the experimental system contains an unknown bias, the p-value does not distinguish that bias from anomalous cognition. If experiments are selected for publication, if analysis flexibility is larger than reported, if investigators share procedures that encode an unnoticed artifact, or if the target-generation system behaves in ways the null model does not capture, the statistics can be real while the proposed phenomenon is not.

Utts knew this. Her position was that the accumulated controls made ordinary explanations increasingly implausible, not that a p-value opens a portal.

The argument becomes stronger when effect size is considered. A tiny effect can reach extreme significance with enough trials. Psi research did not have enormous samples, so repeated positive deviations in small studies drew attention. But small literatures bring their own instability. Effect sizes estimated from selected expert viewers can overstate what a general operator would achieve. Regression toward the mean punishes anyone who turns research subjects into a workforce.

This point matters for intelligence. Suppose Utts was entirely right that the experiments contained anomalous information. The next question is not whether a star should be placed next to the program. It is: how many bits?

Information has quantity. If a viewer slightly increases the probability that the correct target ranks first among five options, that is measurable. It may be scientifically profound. But intelligence utility depends on the open-world information rate, not the drama of violating chance. A channel capable of transmitting a fraction of a bit per trial could overturn theories of mind and remain nearly useless for locating a hostage.

This is the part of the debate I wish had been framed more explicitly in engineering language.

The physics of communication does not care whether a channel is mystical. A channel has capacity, noise, latency, stability and cost. To become an instrument, remote viewing would need an empirical transfer function. How does performance change with target type? Does distance matter? Delay? Feedback? Emotional salience? Viewer fatigue? Repetition? Can multiple viewers be averaged? Do confidence ratings predict correctness? Can the same operator be calibrated next month? Does training improve out-of-sample performance?

Utts's conclusion, even taken at full strength, answers none of these automatically.

That is not a criticism of statistics. It is a boundary around what statistics establish.

The deepest contribution of Utts's review may be that it prevents the easy ending. If a careful statistician could inspect the later database and conclude that chance was not an adequate account, then historians should not write STAR GATE as though the government spent two decades on a phenomenon that produced nothing but obvious fraud and credulity. There was a technical dispute worth having.

The equally important fact is that the dispute happened at the end of a program that had already tried to become useful.

A laboratory anomaly survived.

The spy did not.
