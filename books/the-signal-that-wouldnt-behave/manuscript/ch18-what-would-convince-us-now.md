# 18 — What Would Convince Us Now

The next remote-viewing experiment should be designed as though everyone involved expects to be accused of cheating.

Not because they are cheats. Because the claim requires a chain of custody for information.

The design begins with targets. Build a large, balanced library containing natural scenes, industrial sites, interiors, infrastructure, human activity and deliberately confusable categories. Have an external team characterize each target on preregistered features. Freeze the library. Publish hashes of the files so later substitution can be detected without revealing content.

Then separate roles absolutely. The local experimenter who interacts with the viewer does not know the target, target pool subset, randomization seed or scoring key. The central server does not reveal the target until the response is irreversibly timestamped. No person who knows the target communicates with the viewer or judge before locking.

Responses should be captured in several forms: free description, drawings, forced quantitative estimates, and confidence ratings attached to each feature. The free narrative preserves whatever proponents believe the method does best. The structured fields make calibration possible.

Judging should also be plural. One preregistered human rank-order procedure can reproduce historical methods. A second algorithmic procedure can score features without knowing which laboratory generated them. A third outcome can ask an entirely practical question: given only the viewer output, can an independent analyst identify the target from a large database better than chance?

Every trial counts.

No replacing a session because the viewer felt "off." No excluding a target because it was aesthetically poor unless the exclusion rule was triggered automatically. No post hoc creation of responder groups. If exploratory subgroups appear interesting, they become hypotheses for the next round.

The collaboration should be adversarial by design. A proponent chooses conditions they believe maximize the effect. A skeptic chooses controls most likely to expose ordinary leakage. An independent methods team arbitrates conflicts. The statistical plan includes both frequentist and Bayesian summaries, with effect-size estimates more important than ceremonial p-values.

The first decisive question is whether information transfer replicates across laboratories.

The second is whether it transfers across people.

The third is whether it transfers across time.

If a handful of viewers perform above chance, retest those same individuals prospectively over hundreds of trials. Estimate reliability. Does the best viewer remain the best? Does confidence discriminate hits from misses? Does training move the curve? Can performance be predicted before the target is revealed?

Then attack mechanism.

Vary distance while leaving every other feature fixed. Vary temporal ordering. Introduce shielding regimes. Measure environmental electromagnetic variables without assuming they matter. Record EEG and autonomic measures, but preregister which features are hypotheses and which are exploratory. Test whether target characteristics such as entropy, emotional salience or geometric complexity predict effect size.

The experiment must include a fraud model.

Modern cryptography makes this easier. Publicly commit randomization seeds after generating encrypted target assignments. Use independent logs. Mirror data to repositories controlled by opposed teams. Video-record relevant experimental spaces. Treat information leakage as an engineering threat, not an insult.

What result would convince a skeptic?

Not one dramatic drawing. Repeated prospective performance across independent sites, with locked protocols, where the correct targets are ranked above chance and the same operators remain calibrated out of sample. The effect should survive reanalysis by teams who want it to disappear.

What result should convince a proponent to reduce confidence?

A sufficiently powered multi-site null under conditions proponents approved in advance. Not necessarily that all anomalous cognition is impossible, but that the claimed protocol does not yield the effect size necessary to explain the historical literature.

And what result would matter to an intelligence service?

Something harsher: open-world information gain. Give analysts realistic search problems with thousands or millions of possibilities. Measure whether remote-viewing output reduces search cost or improves decisions compared with controls. Charge the method for false leads and analyst time.

This experiment would be expensive by academic standards and trivial by intelligence standards.

That contrast is almost painful.

The U.S. government spent more than two decades carrying the question through classified institutions. A modern, transparent adversarial collaboration could answer a narrower version better than most of the historical record.

If nobody wants to run that experiment, we should be honest about what the argument has become.

It is no longer a fight over unavailable evidence.

It is a preference for ambiguity.
