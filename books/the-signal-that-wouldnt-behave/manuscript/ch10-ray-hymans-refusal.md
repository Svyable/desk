# 10 — Ray Hyman's Refusal

Ray Hyman's skepticism is more useful when it is not caricatured.

He did not need to prove that every remote-viewing result was fraudulent. He did not need to identify a magician's trick hidden in every experiment. The scientific burden runs the other way. An extraordinary causal claim must survive plausible alternatives strongly enough that the residual cannot simply be labeled with the desired mechanism.

Hyman had spent years studying how people generate conviction from ambiguous evidence. He was therefore sensitive not only to overt sensory leakage but to the ecology of a small research field: repeated work by related investigators, limited independent replication, flexible interpretation, and the possibility that a stable artifact can survive improved protocols if the artifact is built into assumptions shared across those protocols.

His 1995 position is sometimes summarized unfairly as "the statistics are positive, but I refuse to believe them." A stronger version is this: the data may contain an effect, but the inferential chain from effect to paranormal information transfer remains incomplete.

That is a normal scientific distinction.

A randomized clinical trial can show that an intervention changes an outcome without revealing the mechanism. But if the outcome measure is subjective, the investigators are unblinded, the effect appears mainly in one laboratory, and independent teams cannot reproduce it, causal confidence falls. Remote viewing combined several of these difficulties in unusual proportions.

Hyman also emphasized replication. Proponents could point to multiple positive experiments, but many were conducted within the same research lineage. Utts saw consistency as cumulative evidence. Hyman saw dependence among studies as a reason not to treat each as a fresh independent confirmation. Both intuitions are statistically legitimate. Repeated measurement increases evidence, but correlation among measurements reduces how much.

The dispute therefore contains a hidden variable: how independent is the evidence?

Independence is not binary. Two experiments run by the same team with different targets are more independent than two analyses of the same dataset, less independent than experiments by laboratories that share no personnel or software. A replication by a friendly laboratory using the same target pool may reproduce both phenomenon and artifact. A hostile laboratory may accidentally remove the conditions required for a real fragile effect. Science deals with this by gradually widening the network until a phenomenon becomes portable.

Remote viewing never achieved ordinary portability.

Hyman's refusal to cross the causal line is therefore defensible even if one accepts Utts's statistical description. The unusual thing is that he could not make the anomaly disappear simply by pointing to the defects that had plagued the early SRI era. The later work forced skepticism to become more sophisticated.

That should be counted as progress.

A field does not advance only when believers win. It advances when the available skeptical explanations become narrower and testable. If transcript cueing explains an early effect, remove the cues. If single-judge procedures are vulnerable, use independent judges. If target selection can leak, automate it. If publication bias is suspected, preregister. If laboratory dependence is the objection, conduct adversarial multi-site replication.

The tragedy of the government program is that it ended before this experimental logic reached its modern form. In 1995 preregistration was not standard across psychology. Open data were not routine. Registered reports did not exist. Large adversarial collaborations were rare. The replication crisis that later shook mainstream psychology had not yet made researchers broadly suspicious of small-study literatures, hidden researcher degrees of freedom and selective publication.

Remote viewing was fighting those problems early because its claim was disreputable enough to attract aggressive scrutiny.

This does not mean the field was ahead of psychology. It means controversial claims sometimes receive methodological stress tests that respectable claims evade.

Hyman's most important role in the story is therefore not executioner. He is the person standing at the line between "something in these numbers needs explanation" and "we know what it is."

Science needs people willing to stand there longer than everyone else finds comfortable.

Intelligence agencies do not have that luxury. They eventually have to decide whether to buy the next year.
