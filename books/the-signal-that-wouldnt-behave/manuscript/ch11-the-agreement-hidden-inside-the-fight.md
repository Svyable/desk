# 11 — The Agreement Hidden Inside the Fight

The famous Utts–Hyman disagreement obscures how much common ground had accumulated by 1995.

Neither reviewer thought the early literature could simply be carried forward without criticism. Both understood that the later experiments were methodologically stronger than the first SRI work. Both treated the statistical pattern as something requiring serious analysis rather than a carnival trick. Both recognized that operational usefulness was a separate question from laboratory evidence. Their split concerned the strength of the inference from the observed deviations to anomalous cognition.

This matters because polarized fields routinely mistake disagreement about inference for disagreement about data.

A believer says the skeptic "admits the effect is real." A skeptic says the believer "admits there is no mechanism." Both statements can be technically true and rhetorically misleading. The useful task is to map which proposition each person accepts.

For remote viewing, the proposition ladder looks something like this:

1. Some experiments produced scores above their stated chance expectation.
2. The aggregate pattern is unlikely under the statistical null models used.
3. Known methodological artifacts are insufficient to explain the full pattern.
4. The remaining effect reflects information transfer through no recognized sensory channel.
5. That information transfer is stable enough to characterize scientifically.
6. It can be trained, calibrated and deployed.
7. It provides intelligence value exceeding its costs and risks.

One can accept proposition one and reject two if the null models are wrong. Accept two and reject three if hidden artifacts remain. Accept three and reject four if an unknown conventional process exists. Accept four and reject five if the phenomenon is unstable. Accept five and reject six if it cannot be controlled. Accept six and reject seven if the information rate is too low for operations.

Public argument usually compresses the entire ladder into one word: psychic.

The 1995 review partially uncompressed it. Utts climbed higher on the scientific rungs than Hyman. AIR's operational review stopped much lower on the utility ladder. The CIA could therefore terminate the program without resolving the metaphysics.

This is an underrated institutional achievement.

Governments often wait for scientific certainty when they need a management decision. They also sometimes use management decisions as though they were scientific verdicts. The STAR GATE termination is vulnerable to both misreadings. The government did not need to prove that anomalous cognition was impossible. It needed to determine whether the program, as an intelligence capability, justified continuation.

That distinction protects us from two bad histories.

In the believer's bad history, CIA secretly proved psi, then shut the program for political reasons or because the capability moved deeper into black compartments. Evidence for such continuations must stand on its own; program closure does not prove relocation. In the skeptic's bad history, the 1995 closure proved that the entire research record was worthless. AIR's own structure was more nuanced than that.

There is also a lesson here for frontier science. A disputed effect can deserve research without deserving deployment. Institutions need intermediate states between ridicule and adoption. Remote viewing had one for too long and then lost it all at once.

Modern research governance should do better. An effect with provocative data but poor utility can move into transparent, preregistered basic research. Operational agencies can exit while science continues. That separation reduces the incentives for advocates to inflate practical claims merely to preserve funding.

STAR GATE did not enjoy that clean transition. Once intelligence sponsors withdrew, the field returned largely to small academic and private communities. Declassification then transformed a technical controversy into cultural mythology. Code names escaped their filing cabinets. Participants became public personalities. Individual sessions detached from their denominators. The program's very strangeness made it more famous after death than during life.

The best response is to return to the proposition ladder.

Do not ask whether remote viewing was "real." Ask which claim, under which protocol, at which time, with which evidentiary standard.

The answer changes as you climb.
