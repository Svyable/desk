# 12 — The Customer Is the Experiment

Laboratory researchers usually treat the participant as the experimental subject. Intelligence agencies have another subject: the customer.

The American Institutes for Research understood this in 1995. Its evaluation did not stop at statistical review. AIR interviewed actual and potential users of remote-viewing information and examined operational tasking. The question was not whether a session could later be scored against a target. It was whether a user could do anything useful with the product.

This is the chapter where the program becomes most ordinary.

Every intelligence collection system has customers. Imagery analysts request coverage. Military commands request indications and warning. Counterintelligence officers ask for identities and locations. The usefulness of a source depends not only on accuracy but on timeliness, specificity, novelty and fit with the decision. A perfectly true fact can be useless if the customer already knows it. A partially wrong report can be dangerous if the wrong portion drives action.

Remote viewing was especially vulnerable to this distinction because its output often mixed correct-seeming and incorrect details. A customer who already possessed substantial target knowledge could identify correspondences and discard nonsense. But that same process made it difficult to measure incremental value.

Suppose a viewer reports an industrial site, water nearby, a large vertical structure, underground space and a red vehicle. The analyst knows the target is a shipyard. Three details fit; two do not. Was information gained? Perhaps. But if the analyst had not known the target class, could the report have identified it? If the report was used only after other sources narrowed the answer, remote viewing may have functioned as confirmation rather than collection. Confirmation has value only if it is independent and calibrated.

AIR's operational conclusion was harsh. Evidence for operational value was not available after years of attempts. The report argued that even if remote viewing existed as a phenomenon, its suspected characteristics made intelligence use doubtful. This is a stronger criticism than "some sessions failed." It says the form of the alleged signal may be mismatched to the task.

That idea deserves emphasis.

An effect can fail operationally because it is too weak, too inconsistent, too ambiguous, too difficult to task, too hard to score in real time, or too easy to contaminate with context. None of those failures disproves the effect. They do disqualify a tool.

The customer study also reveals a methodological trap in operational evaluation. Users who receive unusual reports may remember the successes disproportionately. A dramatic match is discussed in corridors; a vague failure vanishes into the file. Unless every tasking is logged and every customer rates utility before outcome feedback, anecdotes dominate.

The AIR review tried to reconstruct this record after the program had already evolved through multiple organizations and code names. That limits what can be concluded. Missing or inconsistent feedback does not mean every operation failed. But it is itself evidence about maturity. A program that cannot demonstrate its value after two decades has a measurement problem even before it has a psi problem.

Real capabilities leave performance records. Pilots log sorties. Sensors record detections and false alarms. Cryptanalytic systems can be tested on known traffic. HUMINT remains messier, but agencies still grade sources and reports. If remote viewing had become a mature intelligence discipline, it should have developed comparable bookkeeping.

Instead, the archive contains a mixture of formal sessions, participant memories, scattered evaluations and famous stories.

The absence of a clean operational denominator is not neutral. It makes extraordinary success claims harder to sustain.

The customer's problem was simple: should I trust this enough to spend something?

Time is something. Analyst attention is something. Surveillance coverage is something. A helicopter mission is something. Political confidence is something. A source that cannot tell you when it is wrong can consume all of them.

This is why the 1995 decision does not require us to solve consciousness. The program failed a procurement question.

The customer was the final experiment, and the customer did not get a reliable instrument.
