# 14 — Information Without a Channel

Science is more tolerant of unexplained effects than scientists sometimes admit.

You do not need a complete mechanism before you can measure a regularity. Kepler had orbital laws before Newton supplied gravity. Physicians used aspirin before its molecular targets were understood. The demand that remote viewing provide a mechanism before anyone can take the data seriously is therefore too strong.

The opposite error is to treat lack of mechanism as a virtue.

A mechanism does more than make a story intellectually satisfying. It constrains what should happen next. If information is carried electromagnetically, shielding, distance, frequency and power matter. If a chemical signal is involved, concentration and transport matter. If a neurological pathway mediates perception, lesions and stimulation should alter performance. Mechanisms generate failure conditions.

Remote viewing became stranger as attempts to constrain it accumulated. Proponents reported little meaningful attenuation with distance. Ordinary electromagnetic shielding did not obviously abolish performance. Some experiments suggested targets could be perceived across time as well as space. If those claims are accepted together, the candidate phenomenon does not resemble a weak undiscovered radio.

That is scientifically exciting and experimentally dangerous.

The less the alleged effect depends on known physical variables, the fewer knobs the experimenter can turn to test it. The theory risks becoming content-free: information somehow becomes available because consciousness has properties not represented in current models. That may be true, but it does not yet tell an experimentalist what variable to manipulate.

The government research occasionally entertained models. Later papers in the STAR GATE archive include attempts to formalize anomalous cognition and decision effects. These models are historically important because they show investigators knew description was not enough. But no model matured into a predictive engineering theory accepted outside the field.

This matters for the intelligence program because a mechanism might have solved calibration. If performance depended on a measurable brain state, operators could be screened. If target entropy predicted accuracy, tasking could be optimized. If geomagnetic conditions mattered, sessions could be scheduled. If distance imposed a known cost, confidence could be adjusted. A mechanism could turn a mystery into an instrument.

Instead, viewer performance remained stubbornly individual and variable.

Claims of talented subjects created another statistical problem. Selecting "gifted" viewers after observing many people invites winner's-curse effects. The best apparent performer in a noisy population will tend to regress toward the mean. A real individual-difference effect is possible, but it must be validated prospectively on new trials. Intelligence programs are especially vulnerable to elite-operator mythology because exceptional people make good institutional stories.

The mechanism vacuum also encouraged metaphysical overreach outside the program. Quantum mechanics became a frequent rhetorical refuge. Words such as nonlocality and entanglement appeared in popular explanations, usually without a quantitative mapping from quantum states to macroscopic target descriptions. Quantum theory contains nonclassical correlations. That fact does not license arbitrary information transfer through human intention.

A useful rule is brutal: if a proposed mechanism cannot predict a result different from the competing mechanism, it is not yet doing experimental work.

For remote viewing, the decisive mechanism experiment has never arrived.

This leaves three broad possibilities. The first is that the statistical effects arise from methodological and publication processes not fully identified. The second is that a genuine psychological effect is being misclassified as anomalous information—for example, subtle inference from uncontrolled cues. The third is that some form of information transfer exists outside current sensory models.

The data should decide among them. Mechanism is how the hypotheses become separable.

Until then, "information without a channel" is a description of the claim, not an explanation.

That distinction is easy to lose because the phrase itself feels profound.

The intelligence community could live with mystery if the output worked. Engineering often precedes theory. But when a tool is unreliable and the mechanism unknown, each problem amplifies the other. You cannot improve the system because you do not know what variable controls it. You cannot infer the mechanism from operations because the operations are too noisy.

Remote viewing became trapped between two forms of ignorance.

The government could not explain how it might work.

More importantly, it could not tell when it had worked until afterward.
