# Evidence ledger — THE SKY WE BROKE

## Working thesis

Project Argus is a documented control case for reasoning about extraordinary physical programs. The program was secret, yet its intended mechanism coupled to a shared planetary environment and produced instrumentally observable consequences. The book asks how secrecy changes as a claimed technology's physical coupling, scale, repetition, infrastructure and external dependencies increase.

This is not a universal anti-secrecy argument. The strongest countermodel is that Argus is unusually detectable and therefore weak evidence about compact, passive, underground, rare or sub-threshold systems.

## Class 1 — verified / declassified / documented

- The United States conducted three high-altitude nuclear detonations over the South Atlantic in August–September 1958 under Project/Operation Argus.
- The experiment tested Nicholas Christofilos's proposal concerning injection and trapping of energetic electrons in Earth's magnetic field.
- Explorer IV, launched 26 July 1958, carried instruments used to observe the artificial radiation populations. James Van Allen's retrospective account says its apparatus supplied the principal observations of artificial belts produced by the Argus bursts.
- NASA/GSFC historical material states that the three explosions created artificial radiation belts and artificial aurora; the Argus belts persisted on the order of weeks.
- Contemporary and later scientific literature exists, including the August 1959 *Journal of Geophysical Research* Argus collection and Christofilos's PNAS treatment.
- The project remained secret during execution and became public in 1959.
- Soviet instruments independently recorded geomagnetic disturbances associated with the tests; contemporary reporting on V. A. Troitskaya is a lead requiring primary Soviet-paper recovery before precise timing claims enter final prose.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- Detailed accounts of Task Force 88 cover arrangements, ship movements, exact operational secrecy measures and observation stations are credible in historical literature but should be reconstructed from Navy/DoD archival material before publication.
- Exact intended military utility varies by source: communications disruption, missile/warhead effects, and broader weapons implications need to be attributed to specific proposals rather than collapsed into one purpose.
- Claims about what Soviet authorities inferred from their measurements require primary Soviet publications or archival intelligence reporting.

## Class 3 — disputed / unconfirmed

Do not promote without independent evidence:

- claims that Argus demonstrated a practical planetary electromagnetic weapon;
- claims that Argus was primarily weather control;
- claims that Argus establishes later HAARP-style weather or mind-control capabilities;
- claims that the experiment produced durable operational control of the ionosphere/magnetosphere;
- claims of hidden continuation merely because later high-altitude nuclear tests occurred;
- any claim that a patent or proposal proves operational efficacy.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis / open conjecture

### Detectability budget

A physical program's concealability should be analyzed against the external traces implied by its own claimed mechanism: energy, material, emissions, logistics, test environment, calibration, failures, independent sensors, environmental persistence, industrial dependencies and operational repetition. This is a heuristic, not a conservation law.

Defeat condition: if historical comparison shows that large, strongly coupled physical programs can routinely operate for long periods without contemporaneous or retrospective independent traces, the heuristic must narrow.

### Meaning can be secret while consequence is public

Classification can conceal purpose, relationships and interpretation even when outsiders measure an effect. Argus is a clean case: public knowledge lagged the event while the physical effects existed immediately.

Defeat condition: if the supposedly independent traces depended entirely on later U.S. disclosure for identification and no genuinely independent contemporary detection survives, narrow the claim from independent observability to retrospective physical traceability.

### Adversarial peer review

Shared physical environments recruit uninvited observers, including adversaries. This can function like involuntary peer review, though adversaries may withhold their own measurements.

Defeat condition: evidence that Soviet observations were derived from leaked U.S. timing/data rather than independent instrumentation would weaken the case.

## Contradictions / publication holds

1. **Yield:** secondary accounts vary around roughly 1–2 kt per Argus shot. Do not lock exact values until primary test records are checked.
2. **Altitude:** published summaries vary in exact altitude by shot. Preserve shot-level values only after reconciliation.
3. **Persistence:** descriptions range from noticeable effects for weeks to traces over longer periods. Define which particle population/instrument threshold each duration refers to.
4. **Purpose:** distinguish Christofilos's scientific/weapon hypotheses, DoD test objectives, and later retrospective descriptions.
5. **Soviet timing:** locate Troitskaya's original Academy publication before quoting the reported precise timestamp.
6. **Secrecy chronology:** distinguish contemporaneous detection, internal U.S. declassification decisions, press disclosure in March 1959, and publication of technical results.

## Whole-book defeat test

The book fails if it turns “real programs leave traces” into a slogan rather than a calibrated inference. The finished manuscript must show how trace expectation changes with mechanism and scale, include historical programs that remained hidden despite substantial infrastructure, and identify cases where outsiders observed an effect but misidentified its cause. If those comparisons do not discriminate among claims better than ordinary source criticism, the detectability-budget idea should be demoted to a chapter observation rather than carried as the book's spine.
