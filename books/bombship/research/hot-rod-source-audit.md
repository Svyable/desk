# Hot Rod source audit: five charges, six charges, and what the model actually proved

This note is a bounded evidence check for Chapter 1. It does not authorize a manuscript change by itself. The opening currently relies on the Smithsonian object record for the surviving Project Orion propulsion test vehicle (“Hot Rod”). A NASA-hosted historical chapter introduces a useful contradiction that should be resolved before publication rather than silently choosing the cleaner number.

## Primary/authoritative records checked

### Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum object record

Object: **Propulsion Test Vehicle, Project Orion**, inventory **A19721008000**.

URL: https://www.si.edu/object/nasm_A19721008000

The Smithsonian describes Hot Rod as the largest surviving Project Orion hardware. It dates the test to **1959 at Point Loma, California** and says the vehicle’s **five rapid successive explosions** sent shock waves against a protected pusher plate and drove the vehicle forward; it was recovered by parachute. The record also says the model used conventional high explosives, not nuclear pulse units. This supports Chapter 1’s current five-explosion opening at the museum-object level.

### NASA-hosted Project Orion history, *The ARPA Years (1958–1960)*

NASA NTRS PDF: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000096503/downloads/20000096503.pdf

The historical chapter says the Orion team built a series of flight models called **Putt-Putts** to test whether aluminum pusher plates could survive the momentary temperatures and pressures created by chemical explosives. It then describes **a 100-meter flight in November 1959, propelled by six charges**, and says the test demonstrated that impulsive flight could be stable. It also reports a design lesson: the plate should be thick in the middle and tapered toward the edges to improve strength-to-weight ratio.

That is not the same numerical account as the Smithsonian’s five-explosion description.

## Publication hold: do not collapse five and six into one fact yet

Until the contemporaneous test report, test log, film, engineering memorandum, or an archival caption securely identifies whether these two descriptions refer to the same flight article and same flight, Chapter 1 should not upgrade either secondary institutional history into an uncontested event count.

The current title **Five Explosions** can remain as a working title because it accurately reflects the Smithsonian’s description of the museum object. In eventual reader-facing prose, however, the source should be named or the discrepancy resolved. If the six-charge NASA account describes the same Hot Rod flight, the title itself becomes an evidentiary choice and should be reconsidered.

Required extraction:

- exact date and location of each 1959 Orion model flight;
- flight-model identity / serial or nickname;
- number of charges loaded, fired, and successfully contributing impulse;
- explosive type and mass per charge;
- pulse interval;
- achieved altitude / trajectory and whether “100 meters” means altitude, path length, or another measure;
- pusher-plate dimensions, material, taper, and post-flight condition;
- shock-isolation configuration;
- recovery method;
- whether the Smithsonian object is the same vehicle in the NASA-described six-charge flight;
- provenance of both institutional summaries.

## What the model flight establishes

Even if the six-charge/100-meter account survives primary reconstruction, it establishes something narrower and more interesting than “Orion worked.” Under the tested chemical-explosive, model-scale conditions, a pulse-driven vehicle could achieve stable flight and the experiment could constrain pusher-plate geometry. That is genuine hardware evidence.

It does **not** establish:

- survival under nuclear pulse spectra, fluence, debris, or radiation;
- full-scale pusher-plate ablation behavior;
- nuclear pulse-unit ejection and positioning;
- thousands of repeated pulses;
- crew acceleration history or radiation dose;
- atmospheric fallout acceptability;
- integrated launch readiness;
- mission economics.

The useful narrative move is therefore not to minimize Hot Rod as a toy or inflate it into a prototype starship. It is to show exactly what one ugly experiment bought the team: a real reduction in uncertainty about impulsive flight, alongside a much larger set of uncertainties it could not touch.

## Strongest counterargument

It would be too skeptical to treat the five-versus-six discrepancy as evidence that the flight itself is doubtful. Both authoritative institutional accounts agree on the core event: General Atomics built a model Orion vehicle, drove it with a rapid sequence of conventional explosive pulses in 1959, achieved flight, and recovered a surviving article associated with the program. The disagreement is presently about event-level detail and perhaps about which test in a series is being summarized.

Conversely, institutional agreement on the broad event does not license importing the NASA history’s 100-meter and six-charge details into the Smithsonian object’s particular flight without proving identity. This is exactly the kind of small provenance problem the book asks extraordinary-technology claims to survive.

## Why this matters to the book

Chapter 1 argues that real engineering makes extraordinary claims inspectable. The opening should submit itself to that standard. A museum label says five explosions; a NASA-hosted history says six and 100 meters. Rather than sanding away the contradiction, the manuscript can use it to distinguish **hardware existence**, **test identity**, **test parameters**, and **what the test constrained**.

That strengthens the larger argument: evidence does not become weaker when its boundaries are made visible. It becomes useful.
