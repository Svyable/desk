# BURY IT FOREVER

*The Secret Missile Base That Became a Climate Archive*

Sven Hardy Benson

Camp Century is useful because several different stories occupy the same hole in the Greenland ice and they do not have the same evidentiary status.

The United States Army built a real subsurface base there. It installed a portable nuclear reactor. Army engineers studied whether the ice sheet could support a much larger military system. Project Iceworm, a plan for mobile ballistic missiles under Greenland, was real planning but was never built. The Danish government was not presented with the operational Iceworm proposal. The camp's tunnels deformed. The base was abandoned. A deep core drilled there in 1966 became scientifically important far beyond the military problem that paid for it. Decades later, sediment from beneath the ice yielded evidence that northwestern Greenland had once been ice-free. Those statements can be tied to records and measurements.

Other statements require different verbs.

This book therefore keeps four evidence classes visible in its working method even when the prose does not stop to announce them.

**Documented.** Declassified or archival records, official histories, engineering reports, contemporaneous scientific work, physical measurements, peer-reviewed papers, and other evidence strong enough to establish the bounded claim being made.

**Credible but incomplete.** Serious historical or scientific claims with good provenance but missing records, uncertain denominators, unresolved chronology, model dependence, or evidence that does not yet support the strongest available interpretation.

**Disputed or unconfirmed.** Claims that circulate in later histories, political argument, Cold War mythology, or extrapolation but lack independent confirmation adequate to state them as fact. A secret program can be real without every story attached to it becoming real by association.

**Speculative synthesis.** My argument about what the case means: that physical systems can become archives outside the institution that created them; that environmental change can alter the practical durability of secrecy; that engineering assumptions about permanence are political assumptions when waste, sovereignty and future climate are involved. These are interpretations. They have to survive the cases rather than being protected from them.

There is an obvious temptation here. Camp Century contains nearly every ingredient from which bad secret-history writing is made: nuclear weapons, a hidden strategic plan, an allied government kept outside part of the discussion, a nuclear reactor under ice, abandoned waste, declassification decades later, and a physical landscape now changing in ways the original engineers did not expect. The temptation is to make the secrecy total and the foresight sinister.

The record is stranger and more useful if left uneven. Camp Century itself was public enough to be filmed and visited. Iceworm was not. Scientific work at the site was genuine even though military feasibility mattered. The core was both an engineering product and a climate archive. Later researchers did not need to prove a conspiracy in order to discover that the old military project had preserved evidence nobody in 1966 knew how to read.

That is the book's governing constraint: never let a later consequence become an invented earlier intention.
