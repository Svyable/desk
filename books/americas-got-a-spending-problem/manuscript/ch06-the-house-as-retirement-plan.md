# The House as Retirement Plan

At the end of 2025, the monthly cost of buying the median-priced American home had climbed to roughly $3,120 once mortgage insurance, property insurance, and property taxes were included.

The Joint Center for Housing Studies at Harvard estimated that a household would need about $120,800 in annual income to carry that payment using a typical lender debt-to-income threshold. Five years earlier, the required income had been about $68,700.

The house had not discovered electricity twice.

The shelter did not become seventy-five percent better.

The financial arrangement around the shelter changed.

Prices rose. Mortgage rates rose. Insurance rose. Taxes rose. The monthly payment reassembled all of those forces into one number and presented it to the buyer as the admission price for a fairly ordinary American ambition.

There is an additional complication.

The person who already owns the house often wants that number to stay high.

Not the monthly payment, exactly. Existing owners with old fixed-rate mortgages may have remarkably low payments relative to what a new buyer would face. What many owners want protected is the asset value underneath the mortgage.

This produces one of the strangest contradictions in American economic life.

Housing is expected to become more affordable and more valuable at the same time.

A young family wants prices low enough to buy.

The same family, ten years later, may want prices to rise because the home has become its largest asset.

A retiree may have spent decades paying down a mortgage and now possess substantial equity but modest liquid income. A fall in home prices is not an abstract improvement in affordability to that household. It can feel like the destruction of savings.

The country therefore asks one object to perform too many jobs.

A house is shelter.

It is a leveraged investment.

It is collateral.

It is an inflation hedge of sorts.

It is a school-district ticket.

It is a neighborhood membership.

It is a forced-savings mechanism for households that pay down principal.

It is a source of retirement security.

It is an inheritance.

It is a tax base for local government.

It is also land, and land is where the politics gets difficult.

The Federal Reserve estimated owner-occupied real estate at about $48.7 trillion in the first quarter of 2026. Home mortgages were about $13.8 trillion in the same household-sector accounts. Those totals are not a measure of pure housing profit; they sit inside a much larger household balance sheet and include enormous differences across households.

Still, the scale makes the political economy easier to understand.

When tens of trillions of dollars of household wealth are tied to residential property, homeowners do not experience housing policy only as consumers.

They experience it as asset owners.

That changes what “build more housing” sounds like.

To a renter in a high-cost city, another apartment building may mean a better chance of finding a home.

To an owner next door, the same building may mean construction noise, a changed view, more traffic, crowded schools, pressure on parking, or a possible effect on the scarcity value of the existing home.

Some objections are real externalities.

Some are aesthetic preferences.

Some are distributional conflicts disguised as planning principles.

Some are simply the predictable behavior of somebody protecting a large investment.

Calling all homeowners NIMBYs does not solve the conflict.

It avoids the reason the conflict is durable.

American housing policy has spent generations encouraging households to accumulate wealth in homes. The tax code has favored ownership in various ways. Federal mortgage institutions helped create deep long-term financing markets. Local schools are often tied to property values and property taxes. Retirement advice has treated a paid-off house as a major source of security. Families have been told, with reason, that ownership can be a path to wealth.

Then policymakers act surprised when owners organize around the value of the asset.

The contradiction is structural.

If the national wealth-building strategy depends partly on home appreciation, affordability has an enemy built into the success metric.

This does not mean home prices should fall everywhere.

A collapse would damage households, banks, builders, local governments, and the wider economy. The United States has already seen what happens when leverage, falling prices, and fragile mortgage finance meet at scale. No serious housing policy should aim to recreate that experience merely to make homes cheaper.

The better target is less dramatic: make housing supply more elastic so that rising demand produces more homes instead of mostly higher land values.

That sentence sounds technocratic until it reaches a zoning hearing.

Supply is not a national abstraction.

It is a parcel.

A setback.

A height limit.

A parking requirement.

A sewer line.

A fire code.

A school-capacity argument.

A neighborhood meeting on a Tuesday night attended disproportionately by people who already live there.

Housing is financed through national and global capital markets but permitted street by street.

That mismatch is one reason the price signal can become so powerful without producing a quick supply response.

When the price of a manufactured good rises, factories can expand, competitors can enter, imports can arrive, designs can change, and consumers can substitute.

Housing can respond too, but the response is slower. Land must be assembled. Financing must be secured. Plans must be approved. Infrastructure must exist. Construction labor and materials must be available. The project may face litigation or political delay. In some places the legal envelope simply does not allow enough units on the land where people most want to live.

The price therefore has time to capitalize scarcity.

Then financing adds another layer.

The Federal Reserve noted in its July 2026 Monetary Policy Report that most outstanding mortgages still carried rates below 4 percent while the prevailing thirty-year fixed rate was around 6.4 percent.

That gap makes moving expensive for existing owners.

A household that sells a home financed at 3 percent and buys another at 6.4 percent may face a much larger payment even if the new home is not much more expensive. The old mortgage becomes an asset attached to staying put.

Researchers at the Federal Reserve estimated that mortgage-rate lock-in explained 44 percent of the decline in mortgage-borrower mobility from 2021 to 2022. In the unusually tight market of that period, they estimated the lock-in shock reduced time on market and pushed prices higher than they otherwise would have been.

The fixed-rate mortgage protected households exactly as designed.

The protection created a market consequence.

This is not a reason to regret fixed-rate mortgages. Their stability is enormously valuable. It is another example of a theme running through this book: a good institution can create a second-order spending problem somewhere else.

The cheap mortgage lowered the owner's financing cost.

When rates rose, the same contract reduced the owner's willingness to move.

Lower mobility reduced listings.

Tight supply helped preserve prices.

New buyers confronted the new interest rate and the old scarcity at once.

The monthly payment became punitive.

By 2026, the housing market contained a strange combination of weak transaction volume and high prices. Existing-home sales had been sitting near three-decade lows. New construction had softened. Rents had cooled in some markets. Yet affordability remained severe because the level of prices and financing costs was still high.

This is an important reminder that a market can be slow without being cheap.

Sellers can refuse to sell.

Owners with favorable mortgages can wait.

Builders can slow new projects when buyers cannot support the required prices.

Would-be buyers can remain renters.

The market clears partly through reduced mobility and delayed household formation instead of an immediate price collapse.

Harvard's 2026 housing report described cost burdens at record levels for renters and rising burdens for homeowners. It counted 22.7 million renter households spending more than 30 percent of income on housing in 2024, including 12.1 million spending more than half.

Those are not merely housing statistics.

They are spending statistics.

A household paying half its income for shelter has less room for saving, child care, education, health care, transportation, emergencies, and consumption. A high housing bill migrates into every other category of the household budget.

This is why the debate over whether Americans spend too much often becomes absurd when it ignores housing.

A family paying $3,000 a month for a modest home does not need a lecture about a twelve-dollar subscription before the housing system is examined.

The subscription may still be wasteful.

Scale comes first.

The same applies to public policy.

Down-payment assistance can help a particular buyer.

A mortgage-interest subsidy can lower effective financing cost for eligible households.

A tax credit can increase purchasing power.

A government-backed mortgage can expand access to credit.

All of those interventions may be defensible for distributional or social reasons.

But if they operate in a place where very little new housing can be built, some of the benefit can be captured in price.

The policy helps the buyer bid.

It does not necessarily help the city build.

The seller sees the bid.

This is the danger of confusing access to financing with affordability of the underlying asset.

The United States has repeatedly been better at financing homes than permitting them.

That imbalance creates a spending machine.

More credit supports higher bids.

Higher bids increase asset values.

Higher asset values reward incumbents.

Rewarded incumbents become more motivated to protect scarcity.

Scarcity makes the next generation need more credit.

Nobody has to design the loop intentionally.

The incentives are enough.

Breaking the loop is politically difficult because a large drop in housing wealth is not a neutral event.

The policy objective therefore cannot simply be “make homes cheaper.”

It should be something closer to “make the supply of useful homes grow faster than the scarcity value of existing ones.”

That allows affordability to improve through construction, density, smaller units, better use of land, and more geographic options rather than through a crash.

It also requires acknowledging that not every housing shortage is identical.

Some metros need more apartments near jobs.

Some need starter homes.

Some need infrastructure before land can support growth.

Some have plenty of units but not enough low-rent units affordable to poor households.

Some face climate or insurance risks that make the existing stock more expensive to maintain.

Some shrinking regions have houses nobody wants at the price required to restore them.

A national slogan cannot substitute for local diagnosis.

The retirement dimension makes this even harder.

A homeowner reaching old age may be wealthy on paper and cash-poor in practice. The house can be sold, borrowed against, or passed to heirs, but each option has consequences. Selling may mean leaving a community. Borrowing against equity reintroduces debt. Remaining in place can become expensive as insurance, taxes, utilities, and maintenance rise.

Harvard's housing researchers reported that property taxes increased sharply between 2019 and 2025 and average monthly insurance premiums rose even faster as disaster risk and rebuilding costs increased.

The paid-off mortgage does not produce a paid-off house.

This changes the political constituency around local costs. Older owners may resist tax increases because income is fixed. They may resist development because the home is a store of wealth. They may support exemptions that shift the tax burden elsewhere. None of these positions requires hypocrisy. They follow from an asset that society encouraged them to treat as retirement capital.

The deeper problem is concentration of purpose.

When retirement security depends too heavily on one house, housing policy becomes retirement policy.

When school quality depends heavily on local property wealth, housing policy becomes education policy.

When local government depends heavily on property taxes, housing policy becomes fiscal policy.

When access to employment depends on proximity to expensive metros, housing policy becomes labor-market policy.

When the house is collateral, housing policy becomes credit policy.

The object carries too much institutional weight to behave like a simple consumer good.

That is why the spending problem cannot be solved by asking households to be more rational buyers.

The price is not produced by buyers alone.

It is produced by land-use rules, interest rates, credit institutions, construction capacity, insurance markets, tax systems, geography, schools, migration, local politics, and the accumulated belief that a house should appreciate.

A buyer can shop carefully inside that system.

The system still sets the menu.

The most useful reform is therefore not anti-homeowner.

It is pro-option.

More homes create options for renters.

More homes create options for buyers.

More senior housing creates options for older owners who might prefer to move but cannot find an attractive alternative.

More small units create options for households that do not need a detached house.

More construction near jobs creates options for workers who otherwise pay in money or commuting time.

A more fluid market also reduces the political need to solve scarcity with ever-larger subsidies.

The house can remain an asset.

It just cannot be the only asset in the country whose public policy objective is permanently rising price and permanent affordability at the same time.

At some point one promise has to yield.

The least destructive way is to let abundance do the work.
