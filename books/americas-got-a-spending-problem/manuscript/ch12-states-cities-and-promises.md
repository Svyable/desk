# States, Cities, and Promises

The federal government can run a trillion-dollar deficit and call it a fiscal year.

Most states cannot.

Every state except Vermont has some formal balanced-budget requirement, although the rules vary in when and how balance must be achieved. Vermont traditionally balances its budget without a formal constitutional or statutory requirement of the same kind.

This sounds like the obvious solution to America's spending problem.

Make Washington live like the states.

Require the budget to balance.

Stop borrowing for ordinary operations.

The states provide useful evidence for this instinct.

They also provide a warning against taking it too literally.

A balanced budget is a rule about a period of time.

A promise can live longer than the period.

A state can balance this year's operating budget while issuing bonds for infrastructure, underfunding a pension relative to the actuarial value of benefits already earned, postponing maintenance, drawing down reserves, shifting costs to local governments, using temporary federal aid, changing the timing of payments, or assuming investment returns that determine how expensive a future obligation looks today.

None of those actions is automatically improper.

Some are ordinary public finance.

A bridge expected to serve several generations should not necessarily be paid entirely by this year's taxpayers. A pension is deferred compensation and is supposed to be financed across the worker's career. A rainy day fund exists to be drawn down when the rain arrives. Federal aid can prevent state budget cuts from worsening a national recession.

The lesson is not that balanced-budget rules are fake.

It is that balance depends on the boundary of the accounting period.

States and cities show what happens when current cash constraints are stronger than federal constraints.

They save more visibly.

They cut more abruptly.

They use capital budgets.

They borrow against specific revenue streams.

They create special districts and authorities.

They build reserves because recessions can destroy tax receipts while demand for services rises.

They negotiate with workers over pension formulas and contributions because they cannot simply finance every shortfall with Treasury securities.

They discover the price earlier.

Earlier is not always better.

During a recession, a requirement that a state close a sudden revenue gap immediately can be economically perverse. Tax receipts fall because households and businesses are already under stress. A state that responds by laying off workers, cutting aid, cancelling projects, and raising taxes can deepen the contraction.

This is one reason federal fiscal capacity matters.

Washington can borrow through a national downturn and send money to governments that cannot easily do the same.

The federal government's weak current-budget constraint can therefore stabilize a federation whose state governments face stronger constraints.

A national government that behaved exactly like a state would give up one of its macroeconomic advantages.

The relevant question is not whether every government should face the same rule.

It is whether each level has a credible mechanism for recognizing promises before the only remaining adjustment is crisis.

States have developed one such mechanism in rainy day funds.

The funds are not glamorous.

That is part of their value.

A governor does not cut a ribbon on a reserve balance.

A legislature does not hold a groundbreaking ceremony for cash not spent.

The political reward arrives only when the downturn comes and the reserve prevents a tax increase, service cut, or borrowing scramble.

Saving creates an invisible benefit.

For much of the period after the pandemic, states accumulated unusually large fiscal cushions. By the end of fiscal year 2025, Pew estimated from state-reported data that rainy day funds totaled about $174 billion. The median state had enough set aside to cover roughly 48 days of operating spending. That capacity was down from the prior year's record but remained well above the pre-pandemic norm.

The variation was enormous.

Some resource-dependent states held reserves large enough to cover months of operations because their revenues can swing with commodity prices. Other states held far less.

The right reserve is not the same everywhere.

A state dependent on volatile capital-gains taxes, severance taxes, or highly cyclical revenue needs a different cushion from a state with a more stable tax base.

This is fiscal honesty as contingency planning.

A rainy day fund says: the good year is not the baseline.

It recognizes volatility before volatility becomes an excuse for emergency borrowing or abrupt cuts.

The federal government has no equivalent rule that operates with the same force.

It has fiscal space, monetary sovereignty, and a global debt market instead.

Those are more powerful tools.

They are also easier to treat as permanent substitutes for saving.

States reveal the discipline that appears when the stop signal is closer.

Then pensions reveal how even a strong stop signal can be routed around by time.

A public pension is a promise made today and paid over decades.

The worker earns part of the benefit each year. The government and worker contribute to a fund. The fund invests. Future investment returns, contributions, and public revenues finance payments after retirement.

If the plan is properly funded, the promise and the assets intended to support the promise grow together.

If funding falls short, the missing asset is effectively a claim on the future sponsor.

The Federal Reserve's Financial Accounts makes this unusually explicit.

For state and local defined-benefit pension funds, the Fed records unfunded pension entitlements as a "claim of pension fund on sponsor."

At the end of the first quarter of 2026, that claim was about $2.7 trillion under the Fed's national-accounting methodology. Accrued defined-benefit pension entitlements were about $9.6 trillion.

Those numbers require caution.

Pension accounting is unusually sensitive to assumptions.

Different systems use different discount-rate conventions. Asset values move with markets. Actuarial liabilities change with wage growth, mortality, benefit formulas, inflation adjustments, employee turnover, and assumptions about the future. A national-accounting estimate is not the same thing as the funding number a particular state plan reports in its annual actuarial valuation.

The $2.7 trillion should not be read as a check governments must write tomorrow.

It should be read as a map of timing.

Workers have already earned benefits whose actuarial value exceeds the funded assets available under the Fed's methodology.

The difference belongs to future sponsors.

That is price distance in its purest local-government form.

The service was received when the teacher taught, the firefighter responded, the police officer patrolled, the engineer inspected, or the public employee performed years of work.

Part of the compensation arrives later.

If the government does not fund that compensation while the service is being received, future taxpayers inherit part of the labor bill.

This is not a reason to demonize pensions.

Defined-benefit pensions can provide retirement security efficiently by pooling longevity risk, investing at scale, and protecting workers from having to convert individual savings into uncertain lifetime income.

For workers who accepted lower cash wages in exchange for promised benefits, the pension is not a gift from future taxpayers.

It is compensation already earned.

That is precisely why underfunding is serious.

The promise has moral and often legal weight even if the asset was never set aside.

A government can hide a salary increase less easily than it can hide an insufficient pension contribution.

The salary hits this year's budget.

The pension shortfall may not become painful for years.

The time gap changes bargaining.

Politicians can improve compensation without raising current taxes by promising more later.

Workers can rationally accept because the benefit is valuable.

Taxpayers may not perceive the full cost.

Future officials inherit the contribution requirements.

Nobody involved needs to be corrupt.

The structure itself makes deferral attractive.

Good pension governance tries to close the distance.

Use realistic actuarial assumptions.

Make required contributions on schedule.

Avoid treating strong investment years as proof that contributions can be permanently reduced.

Avoid making benefit promises based on returns the fund must earn in order to make the politics work.

Separate debates about benefit adequacy from the obligation to fund benefits already earned.

The most important principle is simple.

Compensation should be priced when the labor is received.

Pensions are not the only local promise with a delayed invoice.

Infrastructure may be larger.

A city can balance its operating budget while the water main underneath the street ages another year.

The pipe does not appear as a deficit.

The bridge corrosion does not appear as a missed bond payment.

The school roof does not show up as pension underfunding.

Deferred maintenance can therefore produce the appearance of fiscal discipline.

The budget balances because the asset is quietly being consumed.

This is the public-sector version of a homeowner claiming the household budget is healthy because no money was spent fixing the leaking roof.

The balance is real on a cash basis.

The deterioration is real on an economic basis.

Eventually the asset forces itself into the budget.

A water main breaks.

A transit system slows service.

A bridge needs emergency repair.

A school closes rooms.

An electrical system fails inspection.

The delayed maintenance becomes capital spending at crisis prices.

This is why a responsible spending policy cannot use annual cash outlays as the only measure of thrift.

Sometimes spending less today is exactly how a government spends more tomorrow.

The same principle applies to staffing.

A city can hold vacancies open and report salary savings while permit backlogs grow, inspections slow, emergency overtime rises, or institutional knowledge disappears.

The current budget improves.

The service debt accumulates somewhere else.

Not every vacancy should be filled.

Not every maintenance project is urgent.

The point is that a balanced budget can move a cost into a queue instead of eliminating it.

Municipal bonds make the timing even more explicit.

States, cities, counties, school districts, utilities, transit agencies, hospitals, universities, and special-purpose authorities borrow for public projects through a vast municipal securities market.

The federal tax code supports part of this market by excluding interest on qualifying public-purpose state and local bonds from federal income tax.

The arrangement can spread the cost of a long-lived asset across the people who benefit from it over time.

That is often economically sensible.

A sewer system serving a city for fifty years need not be paid entirely by residents who happen to live there during construction.

Borrowing can improve intergenerational fairness.

It can also make a project easier to approve because the tax or fee increase arrives later.

The question is whether the debt is attached to a productive or durable public asset, a credible revenue stream, and a realistic repayment plan.

A bond does not make an expensive project cheap.

It changes who pays when.

That sentence describes much of state and local public finance.

Special districts add another layer.

A metropolitan area may contain cities, counties, school districts, transit authorities, water districts, fire districts, redevelopment authorities, hospital districts, port authorities, and other entities with different tax bases, borrowing powers, and governance structures.

Each boundary may have a legitimate administrative purpose.

Together they can make the total public balance sheet difficult for a resident to see.

The household receives one property-tax bill but may be financing several governments.

A developer negotiates with one authority while infrastructure belongs to another.

A transit agency carries debt separate from the city it serves.

A school district makes pension contributions under statewide rules.

The spending problem fragments across institutions.

This is the local version of the federal tax expenditure shadow budget.

The cost is visible somewhere.

It is not visible in one place.

Federal transfers make the picture still more interesting.

State and local governments receive large flows of federal money for health care, transportation, education, disaster response, housing, infrastructure, public safety, and many other purposes.

The grants can solve a genuine coordination problem.

A national government can redistribute across states, respond to regional disasters, finance projects with benefits that cross borders, and prevent poorer jurisdictions from having to provide nationally desired services entirely from narrow local tax bases.

The grants also create price distance.

A state legislature can support a program more easily when Washington pays part of the marginal cost.

A local government can pursue a capital project more easily when a federal grant covers a large share.

The project may be worthwhile.

The matching structure still changes behavior.

The person voting for the local expenditure does not face the full national tax price.

Federalism disperses both benefits and bills.

That can improve risk sharing.

It can weaken accountability.

Again, the mechanism is not inherently bad.

The question is what replaces the missing price signal.

Matching requirements can force local commitment.

Maintenance-of-effort rules can prevent governments from substituting federal dollars for spending they would have done anyway.

Performance requirements can tie money to outcomes.

Formula grants can reduce political bargaining.

Competitive grants can introduce comparison, though they can also reward jurisdictions with the best grant-writing staffs rather than the highest social return.

Every design solves one incentive problem and creates another.

This is why state and local government cannot be reduced to the comforting phrase laboratories of democracy.

They are laboratories.

Some experiments work.

Some create long liabilities.

Some benefit from competition among jurisdictions.

Some suffer from it.

A city that raises taxes too far may lose residents or businesses. That mobility can discipline policy.

The same mobility can make it difficult to finance services for people who cannot move as easily. A wealthy suburb can protect its tax base and exclude housing while the central city carries regional service burdens. A state can offer subsidies to attract a company from another state, transferring value between taxpayers without changing much national investment.

Competition is a price signal.

It is not always a social optimum.

The local level also makes distribution impossible to ignore.

A property-tax increase lands differently on a retiree with a paid-off house and low cash income than on a high-income homeowner. A sales tax reaches a different base from an income tax. Utility fees can finance infrastructure efficiently while burdening low-income households. Transit debt may be repaid by people who never ride but benefit from congestion relief and land values.

There is no neutral payer.

Every balanced budget chooses incidence.

This is one advantage states and cities have over Washington.

The choice is harder to hide.

A local government cannot routinely send the entire incremental bill to national taxpayers twenty years from now.

It usually has to raise a tax, cut a service, increase a fee, issue debt tied to a legal repayment structure, use reserves, obtain aid, or defer something tangible.

The menu is narrower.

That produces more fiscal feedback.

It also produces more visible pain.

The federal government often becomes the institution that absorbs the pain states cannot.

This is not accidental.

It is one of the functions of a fiscal union.

The danger appears when the levels of government begin using one another to hide the full price.

Washington creates a matching program and counts the federal share.

The state counts the local benefit.

The city counts the grant.

The contractor counts the project.

The resident sees the ribbon cutting.

Nobody shows the consolidated taxpayer the entire stack.

The same person may be financing every layer through different taxes.

This does not mean consolidation would be better.

Federalism has real advantages: local knowledge, experimentation, competition, diversity, and the ability to match decisions to geographic effects.

It means the accounting should follow the citizen across levels of government rather than stopping at the institutional boundary.

A promise is still a promise when three governments split it.

The strongest state and local institutions understand this.

They stress-test revenues.

They build reserves tied to volatility.

They separate operating and capital budgets while still showing debt service.

They publish pension valuations.

They maintain asset inventories.

They use multiyear forecasts.

They ask whether a new recurring program has recurring revenue.

They avoid spending one-time windfalls on permanent commitments.

None of these practices eliminates politics.

They improve the timing of politics.

That may be the most realistic goal of fiscal reform.

A budget process cannot make voters stop wanting services.

It cannot make workers stop wanting compensation.

It cannot make politicians stop preferring benefits to taxes.

It cannot know the future perfectly.

It can move the argument closer to the moment when the promise is made.

States and cities show both the value and the limit of that achievement.

Their annual budgets are often more constrained than Washington's.

Their long-term promises can still drift.

The current account can balance while the pension is underfunded.

The debt payment can be current while the water system deteriorates.

The rainy day fund can be full while a structural revenue problem approaches.

The capital project can be financed while the future maintenance is not.

The federal grant can arrive while the local tax base remains weak.

Balance is a useful discipline.

It is not a substitute for a balance sheet.

The spending problem begins wherever a government can make today's statement look complete by moving tomorrow's obligation outside the frame.

Washington does it with debt.

States can do it with pensions and timing.

Cities can do it with maintenance and fragmented authorities.

All levels can do it by treating temporary money as permanent.

The answer is not one constitutional amendment copied across fifty states and the federal government.

The answer is a fiscal culture that distinguishes three questions.

Did the cash budget balance?

Did the government fund the promises created this year?

Did the public assets become stronger or weaker?

A government can pass the first test and fail the other two.

That is how a balanced budget can still spend the future.
