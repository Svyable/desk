# The Honest Bill

A spending problem does not require an austerity cult.

It requires a bill that tells the truth.

That sounds simpler than it is because modern economies have good reasons to make prices complicated. Insurance spreads risk. Taxes pool resources. Debt moves costs across time. Financial aid redistributes opportunity. Public procurement buys things ordinary consumers cannot. Social insurance turns working-age contributions into protection against old age, disability, illness, and bad luck.

An honest bill cannot mean forcing every person to pay the full cash price of everything at the moment of use.

That would make the country poorer and harsher.

The honest bill has to do something more difficult. It has to preserve the useful distance between beneficiary and price without allowing the price to become nobody's responsibility.

Consider health care.

Suppose a patient needs an emergency appendectomy. The answer to high medical spending is not to roll the gurney to the curb while the patient opens a price-comparison app. In emergencies, consumer shopping is mostly fiction. Even for planned care, clinical complexity and negotiated contracts make ordinary retail comparison difficult.

Somebody still has to care what the procedure costs.

If the patient cannot discipline the price, the insurer can. If the private insurer lacks bargaining power, a public payer may have it. If a dominant hospital system can dictate rates because every employer needs it in-network, competition policy matters. If the same infusion costs radically more simply because it occurred in a hospital-owned facility rather than a physician's office, payment rules deserve scrutiny. If nobody can explain why one clinically similar service is multiples of another, transparency is useful not because every patient becomes a shopper but because employers, payers, journalists, regulators, and competitors can see the spread.

The bill can remain socialized without becoming imaginary.

Housing requires a different kind of honesty.

A renter facing eviction this week may need immediate assistance. A family that has saved for years but cannot clear a down-payment barrier may benefit from targeted help. There is no virtue in making a household homeless while waiting for a five-year planning reform to work through the construction pipeline.

But assistance is not the same thing as abundance.

If a city allows too few homes, helping more people bid for them does not create the missing bedrooms. It changes who can bid and how much money enters the auction. A housing policy that distributes purchasing power while defending scarcity has told only half the truth about the bill.

The honest version pairs relief with supply.

Allow more homes where demand is high. Make permitting predictable. Build infrastructure that lets land support more people. Preserve safety standards that matter and remove rituals whose main function is delay. Support vulnerable households during the transition. Let existing residents argue about real externalities without granting every incumbent a private option on somebody else's future address.

That is not a pure market prescription or a pure government prescription.

It is an attempt to make the physical constraint visible.

College offers another variation.

A school's published tuition may be a negotiating anchor rather than the amount a particular student pays. Grants, scholarships, tax benefits, family contributions, loans, work, housing, food, books, and transportation all alter the real cost. The student then has to compare that cost with something even less certain: what the education is worth.

No disclosure can solve the uncertainty.

A seventeen-year-old cannot know exactly what she will earn at thirty. A degree can change a life in ways that do not fit inside a wage regression. Some fields have high social value without high private pay. A student may change majors. A recession may hit after graduation. Networks and geography matter. So do completion risk and the possibility that the student borrows but never receives the credential.

The uncertainty is not an excuse to withhold the numbers we do know.

An honest college bill would make the full expected cost of attendance more prominent than the sticker tuition. It would show likely borrowing, the terms of that borrowing, completion rates, and program-level outcomes where the data are meaningful enough to use. It would not present one median salary as a promise. It would make uncertainty part of the price rather than burying it under a financial-aid celebration.

The institution should also have some reason to care about the financing outcome.

That reason need not be a crude rule that punishes schools for admitting low-income students or serving difficult populations. Bad incentives can be created in either direction. But a system in which a school receives its revenue at enrollment while the student and taxpayer carry nearly all the long-term financing risk invites a question: who has the strongest reason to ask whether the program is worth what it costs?

Some version of that question belongs everywhere price distance grows.

Who can say no?

Who benefits from saying yes?

Who pays if the optimistic assumptions fail?

Who receives the upside if they work?

Who has the information?

Who is still around when the bill arrives?

These are not a branded framework. They are the ordinary questions that financing can make easy to forget.

The federal budget is where forgetting becomes generational.

The United States can borrow more cheaply, at greater scale, and for longer than almost any household, business, city, or country. That capacity is a strategic asset. It should be protected for exactly the same reason a family values unused credit or a company values cash on the balance sheet: options are worth more before you need them.

A pandemic is a reason to borrow.

A major war is a reason to borrow.

A severe recession can be a reason to borrow.

A long-lived investment may be a reason to borrow when the benefits and costs sensibly span generations.

A routine inability to reconcile recurring spending with recurring revenue is a less persuasive reason.

The distinction cannot be enforced with a bumper sticker.

Governments do not have a clean accounting line between consumption and investment. A road can be a productive asset or a bridge to nowhere. Education spending can create human capital or preserve an ineffective institution. Defense research can generate spillovers; a weapons program can also become a costly failure. Preventive maintenance looks like current spending while deferred maintenance becomes a larger future capital expense.

Any fiscal rule pretending these judgments can be automated perfectly will eventually meet politics, accounting creativity, or a real emergency.

That does not make rules useless.

It means rules should create friction rather than fantasy.

A credible budget process could force lawmakers to display the long-run financing of major permanent changes. It could make tax expenditures as visible as comparable spending programs. It could require clearer explanations of what happens after temporary provisions expire. It could create automatic review points when debt service or trust-fund imbalances cross thresholds, while preserving escape clauses for genuine emergencies.

The point is not to replace elected judgment with an equation.

It is to stop elected judgment from pretending a cost did not occur because it was placed outside the headline number.

Tax expenditures are a good test.

Imagine two policies designed to encourage the same activity.

Policy A sends a qualifying household a $2,000 government payment.

Policy B reduces the household's tax bill by $2,000.

The administrative mechanics differ. The distribution may differ. Eligibility may differ. The timing may differ. The political language certainly differs.

From the federal budget's perspective, both can widen the gap between revenue and spending commitments relative to a world without the policy.

Yet one sounds like spending and the other sounds like keeping your own money.

That rhetorical difference can hide the economic comparison.

An honest bill would put similar interventions next to each other long enough to ask which one works better.

The same discipline belongs on the spending side.

Cutting waste is worthwhile.

It is not a fiscal theory.

No competent organization should tolerate fraud, obsolete programs, procurement games, duplicate systems, idle property, preventable payment errors, or contractors who charge monopoly prices because the buyer is disorganized. A government spending trillions should have extraordinary capacity to audit, measure, negotiate, and stop paying for things that fail.

There are large gains available in competence.

But the arithmetic of the long-run federal budget cannot be balanced entirely by renaming popular spending as waste and assuming somebody else will identify enough of it later.

The biggest commitments are visible.

Retirement benefits are visible. Health programs are visible. Defense is visible. Interest is visible. The tax code's major preferences are visible. The revenue level is visible.

A serious fiscal argument eventually has to enter those rooms.

The mirror image is the claim that the problem can be solved entirely by making a small group of wealthy households pay more.

The United States can choose a more progressive tax system. It can tax high incomes, wealth-related flows, estates, capital gains, corporations, or consumption differently. Enforcement can reduce evasion. Base-broadening can raise revenue while changing rates. These are legitimate choices, and some can raise substantial sums.

But the larger the permanent spending commitment, the harder it becomes to pretend the financing question can be confined forever to people whom the median voter does not resemble.

Broad social insurance often ends up with broad financing for a reason.

That does not dictate a particular tax mix.

It does make one political promise suspect: everyone can receive more while someone else, narrowly defined, will always pay.

The honest bill names the payer.

Sometimes the payer should be richer households.

Sometimes it should be consumers.

Sometimes workers.

Sometimes property owners.

Sometimes businesses, understanding that legal incidence and economic incidence are not always the same.

Sometimes current beneficiaries.

Sometimes future beneficiaries.

Sometimes the entire tax base.

Sometimes borrowing is justified.

The point is not that every policy must make everybody hurt immediately.

It is that the pain should not be erased from the analysis simply because it has been moved.

Growth is the least painful payer and therefore the most frequently overpromised.

A faster-growing economy makes almost every fiscal problem easier. More output supports higher wages, larger tax bases, stronger household balance sheets, and greater capacity to finance public goods. Productivity growth can turn a burdensome debt ratio into a manageable one even when the nominal debt continues to rise.

That makes growth policy central to spending policy.

Housing reform can improve labor mobility. Energy abundance can lower production constraints. Infrastructure can reduce bottlenecks. Skilled immigration can expand the workforce and entrepreneurship. Basic research can create options private firms underinvest in. Better health can increase productive years. Competent education can raise human capital. Rules that make business formation and construction easier can allow capital to respond to opportunity.

Those are not side conversations.

They affect the denominator underneath the debt ratio and the incomes underneath household affordability.

Growth is still not a coupon code.

A budget cannot simply assume that a favored tax cut will pay for itself or that a favored public investment will generate enough growth to erase its cost. Some policies do raise growth. Some merely redistribute income while wearing a growth label. Some investments produce returns too diffuse or too delayed to appear cleanly in tax receipts. Some tax reductions improve incentives but still reduce revenue relative to plausible alternatives.

The honest bill gives growth credit without spending the credit before it exists.

This is where the deepest spending problem becomes political rather than financial.

Visible pain loses elections.

A tax increase has a date and a number. A benefit cut has a person standing behind it. A new apartment building has neighbors who can see the crane. A hospital closure has employees and patients. A rejected insurance claim has a family. A denied grant has an institution. A procurement cancellation has a contractor. A pension reform has workers who planned around the old rules.

The benefit of restraint is often diffuse.

The apartment building lowers pressure on a housing market nobody can observe in the counterfactual. The avoided debt service appears years later. The program that was never created has no constituency. The crisis made less likely by fiscal space does not hold a press conference. The price increase prevented by competition is invisible because consumers only see what they paid, not the monopoly price that might have emerged.

Politics therefore has a structural bias toward visible yeses and invisible noes.

The bias cannot be eliminated.

It can be counterweighted.

Institutions can expose long-run costs before votes. Independent scorekeepers can make assumptions contestable. Sunset clauses can force reauthorization, though they can also create games in which everyone assumes the extension. Competitive procurement can make alternatives visible. Housing plans can move some decisions from parcel-by-parcel political combat to broader rules. Benefit formulas can adjust gradually rather than waiting for crisis. Taxes can be designed with fewer hidden preferences and broader bases, though every simplification creates losers who can identify themselves immediately.

The recurring goal is earlier feedback.

A system should discover that something is too expensive before the only remaining choices are cruel.

That is what a price does in an ordinary market. It conveys scarcity early enough for behavior to change.

Where ordinary prices cannot do the job because risk is pooled, markets are concentrated, benefits are public, or payments are collective, institutions have to recreate the feedback deliberately.

That may mean a budget cap.

It may mean a negotiated rate.

It may mean more supply.

It may mean a tax.

It may mean an eligibility rule.

It may mean telling a voter that the benefit is worth having and therefore worth paying for.

That last sentence is strangely rare in American politics.

Programs are sold as benefits. Tax cuts are sold as relief. Subsidies are sold as affordability. Borrowing is sold as financing. Growth is sold as the future. Each word isolates one side of a transaction.

The honest bill puts the sides back together.

If we want more retirement security, what financing keeps the promise credible?

If we want universal access to some form of care, what mechanism prevents providers from treating pooled payment as an unlimited price?

If we want housing assistance, what creates more housing?

If we want college access, what prevents financing from becoming permission for costs to drift away from value?

If we want lower taxes, which spending commitments are we willing to reduce or which future deficits are we willing to own?

If we want more public investment, which projects actually expand capacity and how do we stop the word investment from becoming a ceremonial label attached to ordinary spending?

Those questions are not hostile to government.

They are how government earns durability.

A benefit financed honestly is harder to demagogue because its supporters have acknowledged the cost. A tax system with fewer disguises is easier to compare. A housing program that produces units rather than merely bidding up scarcity can become more generous without defeating itself. A health system that pressures underlying prices can protect patients from direct cost while still protecting the public purse.

The alternative is the politics of permanent surprise.

Premiums rise again.

Tuition rises again.

Rent rises again.

Interest consumes more of the budget.

The monthly payment stretches farther into the future.

Another subsidy is proposed because the thing has become unaffordable.

Another borrowing measure is justified because cutting the benefit would hurt.

Every individual decision can be defended. The total keeps escaping.

An honest bill would not end political conflict.

It would improve the conflict.

People would still disagree about how large government should be, how progressive taxes should be, how much insurance society owes one another, how much housing a neighborhood should absorb, what a degree is worth, what military capability is necessary, and how much debt is prudent.

Those are real arguments.

They become less real when one side of the transaction is missing.

America is rich enough to choose many expensive things.

That is different from being rich enough to make their prices irrelevant.

The first is prosperity.

The second is how prosperity gets spent before anyone notices it is gone.
