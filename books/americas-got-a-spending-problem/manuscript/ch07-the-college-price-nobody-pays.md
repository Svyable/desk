# The College Price Nobody Pays

The average published tuition and fees at a public four-year college for an in-state student were $11,950 in the 2025–26 academic year.

The average estimated net tuition and fees for first-time full-time in-state students, after grant aid, were about $2,300.

The average total student budget was about $30,990.

All three numbers can describe the same year at the same category of institution.

None is the price of college.

That is the first problem.

The sticker price is what the institution publishes.

The net price depends on aid.

The amount a family pays this year depends on income, savings, tax benefits, scholarships, work, grants, loans, living arrangements, and which expenses are counted.

The amount the student ultimately pays depends on interest, repayment, completion, earnings, forgiveness rules, and time.

The value depends on something even less observable: what the education changes in the student's life.

College therefore occupies an almost perfect position in an American spending system.

It is expensive.

It is emotionally loaded.

Its benefits arrive later.

Its financing is fragmented.

The buyer is often young.

The parent may be paying part of the bill.

The government may be paying another part.

The institution may discount the sticker price with its own aid.

The student can borrow against future income that does not yet exist.

And the product itself varies enormously in quality, field, completion probability, labor-market payoff, social value, and personal meaning.

A normal price signal would struggle in this environment even if everyone were trying to make the system transparent.

The system is not always trying very hard.

Colleges learned long ago that the published price is not necessarily the transaction price. A high sticker price can coexist with substantial institutional aid. Two students in the same classroom may generate very different net revenues. One family may see a scholarship as generosity while the institution sees it as price discrimination used to shape enrollment.

There is nothing uniquely sinister about this.

Airlines charge different passengers different prices for the same flight. Hotels discount rooms. Businesses segment customers. Universities face a stranger problem because they are also building a class, pursuing academic goals, subsidizing research, meeting access commitments, and managing reputation.

Still, the result is a market in which even sophisticated buyers can struggle to answer a basic question before applying:

What will this actually cost us?

The second question is harder.

What is it worth?

The average answer is favorable.

College graduates, on average, earn more than people whose education ended with high school. They are more likely to be employed. Education can produce benefits beyond wages: health, networks, civic participation, knowledge, mobility, and access to occupations that require credentials.

But averages are dangerous at the point of purchase.

A student does not attend the average college, study the average field, graduate with the average debt, and receive the average labor-market outcome.

The student attends one institution and one program, perhaps after changing majors, perhaps without finishing.

The noncompletion risk is especially important because the financing system can preserve the debt when the credential disappears.

A completed degree with manageable borrowing is one asset.

Borrowing for several semesters and leaving without the degree is another financial object entirely.

This is where the price-distance problem becomes severe.

The institution receives tuition while the student is enrolled.

The lender or federal government advances the money.

The student's ability to repay is tested years later.

The school therefore receives much of its revenue before the economic outcome of the education is known.

That does not mean schools are indifferent to outcomes. Reputation, accreditation, enrollment, alumni giving, public funding, mission, and professional pride all create reasons to care.

But the timing is different.

The revenue event comes first.

The return-on-investment event comes later and lands on somebody else's balance sheet.

Federal student aid exists because this timing problem would otherwise exclude many people from education.

A seventeen-year-old from a family with little wealth may have enormous potential and almost no collateral. Human capital cannot be repossessed and sold like a car. Private lenders therefore have reasons to ration education credit severely or price it in ways that exclude exactly the students public policy may want to help.

Government credit and grants can solve a real market failure.

They can also weaken the discipline that scarce financing would otherwise impose on institutions.

The popular version of this claim is the Bennett hypothesis: colleges raise tuition because federal aid gives students more money to pay it.

The evidence does not justify treating that slogan as a universal law.

College prices move for many reasons. State appropriations rise and fall. Labor costs change. Institutions add services. Housing costs change. Enrollment changes. Research missions differ. Public and private schools behave differently. Grant aid, subsidized loans, unsubsidized loans, parent loans, and graduate borrowing operate through different channels.

Some empirical work has found meaningful tuition responses to expanded federal credit. Other work finds smaller or more conditional effects. A 2016 NBER model by Grey Gordon and Aaron Hedlund attributed a large share of the historical rise in net tuition in its model to changes in the federal student-loan program, while casting doubt on Baumol's cost disease as the main explanation. That is a serious result, not a final verdict on every institution or period.

The careful conclusion is more useful.

When financing expands faster than supply or price discipline, institutions have more room to charge.

How much of that room becomes tuition depends on the market.

That is enough to make financing design matter.

It is not enough to blame every tuition increase on student aid.

The current numbers contain another complication for the easy story.

College Board reported that inflation-adjusted net tuition and fees for first-time full-time in-state students at public four-year institutions peaked in 2012–13 and had fallen to an estimated $2,300 by 2025–26.

Borrowing among recent bachelor's degree recipients had also declined from earlier peaks. Among 2023–24 bachelor's degree recipients at public and private nonprofit four-year institutions, 47 percent graduated with debt, and the average among borrowers was $29,560. In inflation-adjusted terms, average debt among borrowers was lower than a decade earlier.

Those facts do not mean college became cheap.

They mean the simple narrative of endlessly rising tuition and endlessly rising new graduate debt is incomplete.

Living costs matter.

Time out of the labor force matters.

Graduate education can involve much larger balances.

Existing borrowers can remain indebted long after current undergraduate borrowing trends improve.

Institutional variation is enormous.

And a lower average net tuition does not help a family whose particular school, housing market, or aid package remains expensive.

The system has also accumulated a stock problem from prior years.

New York Fed data put student-loan balances at about $1.65 trillion in the second quarter of 2026. The return to normal credit reporting after the pandemic payment pause made delinquency data unusually difficult to interpret cleanly, but the broad fact was clear: millions of borrowers were carrying obligations created by educational decisions made years earlier.

In the first quarter of 2026, the New York Fed reported that about 10.3 percent of student-loan balances were ninety or more days delinquent. Researchers emphasized that the figure reflected the delayed return of delinquency reporting and should not be read as a normal steady-state comparison.

The timing distortion itself is revealing.

Education finance is full of delayed signals.

A student can borrow at eighteen.

The repayment difficulty can appear at twenty-eight.

A program can recruit several cohorts before poor outcomes become obvious in public data.

A school can close while the loans remain.

A government can change repayment rules after the original contract was signed.

A borrower can make career decisions under one set of assumptions and repay under another.

The bill has a long half-life.

That makes accountability both necessary and dangerous.

One tempting solution is to rank programs entirely by graduate earnings.

The metric has obvious appeal. If taxpayers are financing education through grants or subsidized credit, programs that consistently leave graduates earning less than plausible alternatives deserve scrutiny.

But a wage-only test can punish socially valuable work.

Teachers, social workers, artists, clergy, public defenders, researchers, and many care occupations may generate value not fully captured by salary. A program serving poor students in a weak regional labor market may produce lower observed earnings than an elite institution serving already advantaged students even if the first school creates larger gains relative to where its students began.

A crude earnings rule can therefore reproduce the same abstraction problem the financing system was supposed to solve.

The answer is not to ignore outcomes.

It is to use more than one.

Completion matters.

Debt matters.

Repayment matters.

Earnings matter.

Field matters.

Student characteristics matter.

Transfer patterns matter.

Price matters.

The uncertainty should be displayed rather than collapsed into one flattering number.

The federal College Scorecard is a step in that direction because it makes institution-level information on earnings, completion, cost, debt, and repayment available to the public. In 2026 the Department of Education updated the data used in the Scorecard and in FAFSA-related earnings information.

The existence of the data does not guarantee that a teenager making an emotionally charged decision will use it well.

Information has to arrive before commitment becomes identity.

By the time a student has toured the campus, bought the sweatshirt, imagined the dorm room, met future classmates online, and received an acceptance letter framed as a personal verdict, a spreadsheet about debt and median earnings is competing against a much stronger narrative.

College admissions understands this.

Financial disclosure often arrives inside the emotional machinery rather than before it.

The honest bill would reverse the order.

Before the celebration, show the expected full cost.

Show the grant assumptions.

Show the amount the family is expected to contribute.

Show likely borrowing under several scenarios.

Show what happens if the student takes five years instead of four.

Show what happens if the student does not finish.

Show program-level outcomes where the data are credible enough.

Show the uncertainty.

Then let the acceptance letter be joyful.

This does not require turning college into a purely financial trade.

People spend money on things whose value is not captured by future income. Families choose neighborhoods for community. Governments fund parks. Individuals study history, music, astronomy, literature, and philosophy because a life contains more than wage optimization.

The problem arises when a purchase is defended as both priceless and financeable by debt.

Debt is never impressed by pricelessness.

It asks for cash flow.

If society believes some forms of education have high public value but low private earnings, the financing should reflect that openly. Scholarships, grants, public appropriations, service programs, and direct subsidies can make a political choice visible.

Loading the cost onto an eighteen-year-old borrower and later discovering that the private return was too small is a less honest way to subsidize a public good.

The same principle applies to institutional risk.

Schools need not guarantee earnings. No institution can control recessions, student effort, geographic choices, family circumstances, or every labor-market outcome.

But a financing system can give institutions more reason to care about completion and repayment without creating perverse incentives to avoid disadvantaged students.

Risk-sharing proposals have tried to do this in various forms: make institutions bear some cost when borrowers experience persistently poor repayment outcomes.

The design is difficult.

A crude penalty can make colleges stop serving students who look risky on paper.

A sophisticated formula can become another compliance industry.

Yet the underlying question will not go away.

If the school receives the money at enrollment, the government guarantees or originates the credit, and the student carries the downside years later, where exactly does price discipline enter?

Prestige is one answer.

Competition is another.

State funding can provide discipline.

Accreditation can.

Outcomes reporting can.

Borrowing limits can.

Institutional risk can.

None is sufficient alone.

What the system cannot safely rely on is the student's ability to judge a thirty-year financial and career trajectory at seventeen with perfect information.

The college spending problem is therefore not that America spends too much on education.

A rich, technologically advanced country may need to spend a great deal on education.

The problem is that the price is scattered across too many documents and too many years while the value remains uncertain until long after the seller has been paid.

A market can tolerate uncertainty.

It has more trouble tolerating uncertainty when the buyer cannot see the price either.

The sticker price nobody pays became a symbol of generosity because aid reduced it.

It also became a way of avoiding the harder question.

What did the education cost in total, and who was still holding the bill when the promise met reality?
