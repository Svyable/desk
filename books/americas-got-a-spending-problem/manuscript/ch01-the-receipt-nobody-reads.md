# The Receipt Nobody Reads

In fiscal year 2026, the United States government was projected to spend about $7.4 trillion.

The Congressional Budget Office expected federal revenues of about $5.6 trillion.

The difference was roughly $1.9 trillion.

A number that large does not feel like money. It feels like weather.

Divide it by 365 and the projected deficit works out to a little more than $5 billion per day. That is still too large to picture. Divide again and it is roughly $60,000 per second, every second of the year, in spending above revenue.

Even that exercise is slightly misleading. The federal government does not run a cash register in a basement where a red counter advances at a constant rate. Taxes arrive unevenly. Spending arrives unevenly. Treasury securities mature and are refinanced. Economic growth changes the denominator. Inflation changes nominal values. A recession can expand deficits automatically just when borrowing is most useful. A war, financial panic, pandemic, or natural disaster can make yesterday's fiscal target irrelevant by lunchtime.

But the translation is useful for one reason.

It restores scale.

Most federal spending is discussed as a collection of individual programs, and almost every individual program has a constituency able to explain why its line should survive. Social Security is a promise to retirees. Medicare is health security for older Americans. Medicaid is a state-federal compact covering low-income patients and other eligible groups. Defense is national survival. Veterans' benefits are obligations incurred when the country sent people into danger. Farm programs stabilize an industry with unusual risks. Scientific research creates public knowledge. Roads, ports, aviation, courts, food inspection, weather forecasting, embassies, intelligence agencies, disaster response, border enforcement, education aid, housing assistance, law enforcement, national parks, and thousands of less visible functions all arrive with reasons.

Many of the reasons are good.

That is what makes the total hard.

The federal budget is not mainly a warehouse full of obviously stupid purchases waiting for a sufficiently tough manager with a red pen. Waste exists. Fraud exists. Programs outlive their usefulness. Agencies duplicate work. Procurement can be grotesquely inefficient. Every large institution accumulates barnacles, and the federal government is among the largest institutions humans have ever constructed.

Waste is politically attractive because everyone is against it in theory.

It is fiscally inadequate because the biggest numbers are attached to things voters recognize and often like.

CBO's 2026 baseline put mandatory outlays around $4.5 trillion. Social Security and Medicare alone accounted for a large share of that spending and were projected to grow as the population aged and health costs increased. Discretionary outlays were projected around $1.9 trillion. Net interest was projected above $1 trillion.

That last category deserves a moment.

Interest is the purest example of a bill arriving from decisions already made.

A government can debate whether to buy a plane, expand a tax credit, raise a benefit, or build a laboratory. It cannot hold a normal appropriations hearing and decide that last year's bondholders have become an inefficient constituency. Treasury securities are contracts. The interest is owed.

CBO projected net interest outlays to rise from about $1 trillion in 2026 to about $2.1 trillion in 2036 under then-current law. Measured against the size of the economy, net interest was projected to grow from 3.3 percent of GDP to 4.6 percent. By the end of the projection period, interest would be close to the size of all federal discretionary spending.

This is how yesterday acquires a vote in tomorrow's budget.

The political system does not experience the transition as a single dramatic event. There is no morning when the country wakes to discover that the national debt has suddenly become real. The process is quieter. A larger share of revenue is precommitted. A new program becomes harder to finance without borrowing. A tax cut has a larger debt-service tail. A military emergency arrives when interest costs are already high. A recession requires fiscal support from a starting position with less room than policymakers would have preferred.

The cost is optionality.

That word matters because debt arguments often become moral arguments too quickly.

Borrowing is not inherently irresponsible. A government that refused to borrow during a depression in order to preserve a beautiful balance sheet could destroy productive capacity and tax revenue, making its fiscal position worse. A government fighting an existential war may rationally borrow enormous sums because there is no meaningful long term if the war is lost. A country with underused resources can sometimes finance infrastructure at a cost well below the value of the resulting capacity.

The United States left World War II with federal debt held by the public above 100 percent of GDP. That fact did not prevent the extraordinary postwar expansion that followed. Growth, inflation, fiscal choices, demographics, and the institutional strength of the United States changed the burden over time.

The existence of a large debt, by itself, does not tell you whether the borrowing was wise.

The trajectory does tell you something about promises.

CBO's February 2026 baseline projected debt held by the public at about 101 percent of GDP in 2026 and 120 percent in 2036. The projected annual deficit was 5.8 percent of GDP in 2026 and 6.7 percent in 2036. Those are not emergency-war numbers appearing for one terrible year and then vanishing. They describe a structural gap under laws already in place.

The most revealing pair of numbers may be simpler: outlays at 23.3 percent of GDP and revenues at 17.5 percent in 2026.

That difference is the argument.

People can reasonably prefer a larger public sector. People can reasonably prefer a smaller one. A country can choose to provide more retirement security, more health care, more defense, more industrial policy, more family benefits, or more public investment. It can also choose lower taxes, fewer programs, more private provision, or more individual responsibility.

What it cannot do indefinitely is choose the spending preferences of one coalition and the tax preferences of another and declare the compromise free.

American politics has become remarkably skilled at exactly that form of coalition government.

Tax increases are painful because the cost is visible. Benefit cuts are painful because the loss is visible. Borrowing is unusually convenient because the immediate beneficiary can be identified while the future payer is diffuse.

That asymmetry changes the bargaining table.

Imagine a town meeting deciding whether to build a swimming pool. If the proposal is a $20 million pool financed by an immediate property-tax increase, residents confront the cost and benefit together. Some people still vote yes. They may value the pool enough. Others vote no. The debate at least contains a price.

Now imagine that the town can build the same pool while sending most of the tax bill to residents who will move there twenty years from now.

The pool becomes easier to approve.

The federal government is not a town, and future taxpayers are not a separate species. Current children become future taxpayers. Current workers are future retirees. Government debt is held by investors, pension funds, mutual funds, banks, foreign institutions, and the Federal Reserve. Taxes and benefits move through the same society in complicated loops.

Still, the political timing problem is real.

A benefit delivered now is a concrete fact. A future tax increase is a forecast. A future spending cut is somebody else's election. A future interest bill is an abstraction. The temptation is to settle today's argument by spending tomorrow's money.

The United States has another advantage that makes the temptation easier to indulge: the Treasury market.

Dollar-denominated U.S. government debt sits near the center of the global financial system. Treasury securities are used as reserves, collateral, safe assets, pricing benchmarks, and liquid stores of value. The country has repeatedly demonstrated an extraordinary ability to borrow in size.

That ability is an asset.

It can also weaken feedback.

A household that becomes overleveraged eventually gets a phone call, a denied application, a rising interest rate, or a lender who refuses to extend more credit. A small country with fragile institutions can receive the sovereign version of that message brutally fast. Yields spike. Its currency falls. Foreign funding disappears. The government discovers the budget constraint in public.

The United States can go much longer without that kind of obvious stop signal.

There is no virtue in manufacturing a crisis merely to prove that constraints exist. The absence of a crisis, however, should not be mistaken for the absence of cost.

Debt changes the distribution of future choices before it produces any cinematic moment.

This is why the familiar search for the one villain in the budget is so unsatisfying.

Some people blame defense. Defense is large, and procurement discipline matters. It is not large enough by itself to explain the long-run fiscal gap.

Some blame foreign aid. Foreign aid is politically vivid and fiscally small relative to the major drivers.

Some blame undocumented immigrants, federal workers, congressional salaries, earmarks, arts grants, tax enforcement, welfare fraud, corporate subsidies, student aid, climate programs, oil subsidies, or whatever category best fits the speaker's existing politics. Many of those categories deserve scrutiny. None can repeal arithmetic.

Others say the solution is simply to tax the rich.

Higher taxes on high-income households can raise meaningful revenue, and the distribution of the tax burden is a legitimate political choice. But a fiscal system built around large retirement and health commitments ultimately intersects with the broad economy. The larger the gap, the less plausible it becomes that one narrow group can finance every promise without behavioral, political, or mathematical limits.

Others say growth will solve the problem.

Growth helps enormously. Faster productivity raises incomes and tax receipts. A richer future can carry a larger nominal debt. Policies that increase labor-force participation, investment, innovation, housing mobility, energy abundance, and productive capacity therefore belong in any serious fiscal discussion.

Growth is not a line item that can be appropriated at will.

Forecasting faster growth because the budget needs it is the fiscal equivalent of assuming a raise before signing a mortgage.

The harder conclusion is less satisfying and more useful.

A durable fiscal settlement probably requires some combination of spending restraint, revenue, economic growth, and institutional rules that force tradeoffs to become visible sooner. The mix is political. The need for a mix is arithmetic.

The word restraint should not be confused with across-the-board austerity.

A government can reduce a future deficit by spending money today on something that raises future productive capacity or prevents a larger liability. Maintenance is often cheaper than reconstruction. Early treatment can be cheaper than late treatment. A competent tax administration can collect revenue already owed. Better procurement can buy the same capability for less. Immigration policy can change the ratio of workers to retirees. Housing reform can improve geographic mobility and reduce the amount of subsidy required to make scarce homes affordable.

The right fiscal question is not always, What can we cut?

It is, What are we buying, what does it really cost, who is paying, and what future choice are we giving up?

That is a much more demanding way to read a budget.

It also makes ideological theater harder.

A tax preference can function like spending even if no check leaves the Treasury. Economists call many of these provisions tax expenditures because the government achieves a policy goal by collecting less revenue from a favored activity rather than appropriating money to it. The mortgage-interest deduction, exclusions, credits, accelerated depreciation, and dozens of narrower provisions are politically easier to describe as tax policy than as spending.

But a dollar of foregone revenue and a dollar of direct outlay can have similar effects on the deficit.

The label changes the coalition.

This is one reason the federal receipt is so hard to read. Part of government is visible as spending. Part is embedded in the tax code. Part is delegated through loans and guarantees. Part is pushed onto states. Part appears as regulation that requires private parties to spend. Part is a future benefit promise without a pile of assets sitting beside it. Part is interest on all the choices that came before.

The categories are necessary for accounting.

They can become camouflage in politics.

A serious spending book therefore has to resist the pleasure of finding one outrageous object and pretending it explains the room.

The room itself is the subject.

It is a system designed by generations of lawmakers responding to wars, recessions, demographic change, poverty, old age, disease, regional interests, business pressure, voter demand, ideology, court decisions, accidents, and genuine public needs. Every layer has a story. Every story has defenders. Every defender can often identify a real person who would be hurt if the line disappeared.

That is precisely why the budget needs a price signal stronger than outrage.

The United States does not need to become a country that refuses to spend.

It needs to become a country capable of finishing the sentence: We are choosing to spend this, which means we are choosing not to spend that, or we are choosing to tax this, or we are choosing to borrow from those future choices.

A budget is supposed to make the sentence unavoidable.

When borrowing becomes the routine punctuation between incompatible promises, the sentence stops doing its job.
