# The Hospital Price Nobody Knows

In 2024 the United States spent about $1.63 trillion on hospital care.

That was one part of a $5.3 trillion national health bill.

If a country spends that much on a category, one might expect the price of the category to be unusually well known.

It is not.

Ask what a gallon of gasoline costs and the answer may be wrong by a few cents or a few miles.

Ask what an MRI costs and the first honest answer is often another question.

Which hospital?

Which insurer?

Which plan?

Which deductible?

Which negotiated rate?

Which facility fee?

Which professional fee?

Which diagnosis code?

Which network status?

Was contrast used?

Was the scan performed in a hospital outpatient department or an independent imaging center?

Are you asking what the hospital charges, what the insurer allows, what Medicare would pay, what the patient owes, or what somebody without insurance is billed?

The procedure is one physical event.

The prices multiply around it.

This is not because hospitals forgot how numbers work.

American health care developed a payment system in which price is a relationship rather than a tag.

A hospital negotiates with insurers.

Medicare pays under administered formulas.

Medicaid pays under different state and federal arrangements.

Self-funded employers may hire insurers or third-party administrators to negotiate on their behalf.

Uninsured patients encounter another set of rules and discounts.

The same institution can therefore receive different amounts for clinically similar work depending on who pays.

The patient enters late in the chain.

Sometimes the patient has significant cost sharing.

Sometimes very little.

Sometimes the patient learns the price only after the service has been delivered and the claim has been processed.

This is price distance in its most elaborate form.

The country created the distance for good reasons.

Serious illness is financially lumpy. A household cannot sensibly save cash for every possible cancer, premature birth, accident, stroke, infection, or genetic disease. Insurance pools that risk. Medicare protects older people from a category of expense that becomes more likely precisely when labor income is declining. Medicaid protects eligible households and finances care that many could never buy at full cost.

No serious alternative health system eliminates third-party payment for expensive care.

The question is what happens to price discipline after the third party arrives.

RAND tried to make one part of the answer visible by comparing what private health plans paid hospitals with what Medicare would have paid for the same services at the same facilities.

In its study using 2022 data, employers and private insurers paid an average of about 254 percent of Medicare rates across the hospital services in the sample. Inpatient facility services averaged about 254 percent. Outpatient hospital facility services averaged about 279 percent. Associated professional services averaged less, about 184 percent.

The state variation was enormous.

Some states averaged below 200 percent of Medicare.

Others averaged above 300 percent.

The same country did not have one commercial hospital price.

It had bargaining outcomes.

Medicare is not a perfect benchmark.

Its rates are administrative prices shaped by statute, regulation, geographic adjustments, teaching-hospital rules, rural policy, political choices, and formulas that can themselves be too high or too low for particular services or institutions. A commercial payment twice Medicare does not automatically prove that half the payment is waste.

A hospital may need to finance standby capacity that Medicare's formula does not fully reflect. It may operate trauma services, neonatal intensive care, teaching programs, burn units, psychiatric care, or rural access. It may face high local wages or extraordinary capital costs. It may treat a large number of patients who cannot pay. Hospitals also cross-subsidize internally because the line between profitable and unprofitable services rarely matches the line between medically necessary and optional.

The Medicare comparison is useful for a different reason.

It holds the service and facility relatively constant and exposes how much the payer can matter.

That points away from pure cost disease and toward market power.

RAND found that hospital market power explained more of the variation in commercial prices than the share of patients covered by Medicare or Medicaid.

That finding matters because one of the most common defenses of high commercial prices is cost shifting: public programs underpay, so private insurers must make up the difference.

There are hospitals for which public payment adequacy is a genuine problem.

The broad variation in commercial prices is still difficult to explain as a mechanical pass-through from public underpayment.

A powerful hospital system can demand more because an insurer cannot sell an attractive network without it.

That is a different mechanism.

Imagine the negotiation.

An insurer wants lower prices.

The hospital system says no.

The insurer threatens to exclude the hospitals from the network.

The hospital knows employers and patients may revolt if the most prominent local system disappears from coverage.

The insurer knows it too.

The price is therefore not produced by a patient shopping for surgery.

It is produced by two large institutions bargaining over how painful exclusion would be.

The patient is the constituency each side uses against the other.

Consolidation changes that bargaining game.

A single independent hospital can be important.

A regional system controlling several hospitals, specialist groups, outpatient facilities, and physician practices can be difficult to route around.

The Federal Trade Commission has repeatedly challenged hospital and physician-practice transactions on the theory that reduced competition can lead to higher prices. The economic literature behind those challenges is substantial even though individual mergers vary and integration can sometimes improve coordination or preserve a struggling facility.

This is another place where the spending debate becomes too moral too quickly.

Hospital executives are not required to dislike revenue.

Insurers are not required to dislike margin.

Physician groups are not required to refuse attractive acquisition offers.

Employers are not required to build their own provider networks.

The system should be designed on the assumption that institutions will use bargaining power when they have it.

That is what bargaining power is for.

The policy problem is whether the market contains a counterweight.

Price transparency was supposed to create one.

Beginning in 2021, federal rules required hospitals to publish machine-readable price information and consumer-facing prices for shoppable services. Insurer transparency rules later required an even larger universe of negotiated rates to be disclosed.

This was a remarkable change in principle.

For decades, negotiated medical prices had often been treated as commercially sensitive information. Suddenly the law was saying that the price of health care was important enough to make public.

Making data public and making it usable turned out to be different tasks.

RAND noted gaps in hospital reporting and enormous insurer files filled with duplicative information. Researchers, data firms, employers, and journalists have made progress in cleaning the information, but the raw existence of a price file does not transform a patient into a competent shopper.

The patient with chest pain still should not bargain.

The patient under anesthesia cannot switch facilities.

The patient whose surgeon practices only at one hospital may not have a practical alternative.

Even a shoppable procedure can be hard to compare because quality, physician preference, network rules, and bundled versus unbundled fees complicate the transaction.

Transparency therefore works best when it changes the behavior of institutional buyers.

An employer can ask why its plan pays one hospital three times Medicare while another high-quality hospital receives much less.

A union health plan can compare rates.

A state employee plan can use reference pricing.

An insurer can steer patients toward lower-cost facilities where clinical substitution is reasonable.

A regulator can identify unusual patterns.

A journalist can expose prices that were previously protected by obscurity.

The patient benefits without personally becoming a commodities trader in appendectomies.

This is the deeper lesson of third-party payment.

If somebody else pays, somebody else must shop.

If nobody shops, the seller learns that price is mostly a negotiation over who absorbs the increase.

The increase eventually reaches the household anyway.

Employer-sponsored insurance is often described as a benefit paid partly by the employer.

Economically, compensation is a package. Over time, higher health-benefit costs can compete with wages and other compensation even when the worker never sees a line on a pay stub labeled “hospital market power.”

The cost is hidden without being absent.

A family premium rises.

The employer contribution rises.

The deductible rises.

The wage increase that might have occurred is smaller.

The company raises prices or accepts lower margin.

There is no single moment when the hospital bill lands on the kitchen table in full.

That diffusion is politically convenient.

It is economically dangerous because it weakens the constituency for controlling underlying price.

The same logic appears in site-of-service payments.

A medical service can migrate from an independent physician office to a hospital-owned outpatient department after an acquisition. The clinician may be the same. The building may be nearby. The administrative ownership changes.

The payment can change too.

This is why site-neutral payment has become a recurring policy argument. If clinically similar services receive very different payments depending on ownership or setting, the higher-paying setting can become more attractive even when it does not create equivalent additional value.

The defense is that hospital outpatient departments carry obligations independent offices do not: emergency readiness, staffing standards, uncompensated care, complex patients, and institutional overhead.

The criticism is that paying a premium for every service delivered under a hospital umbrella can reward consolidation and raise prices for routine care.

Both claims can be true at once.

The policy task is to identify which services genuinely require the expensive setting.

This is harder than declaring all facility fees abusive or all hospital overhead necessary.

Hospitals are infrastructure.

Infrastructure is expensive when idle.

A trauma center has value precisely because it is ready before the trauma arrives. An emergency department cannot be evaluated like a factory that should run at one hundred percent utilization. Spare capacity, specialized staff, and redundancy can be part of the product.

The spending problem appears when the price of readiness is attached indiscriminately to transactions that do not need it.

A country can afford standby capacity.

It has more trouble affording a payment system that cannot distinguish standby capacity from ownership.

Quality complicates the picture further.

A high-priced hospital may be excellent.

It may also be ordinary.

RAND found wide price variation within each CMS star-rating category. Five-star hospitals, on average, had higher relative prices than lower-rated hospitals, but every star group contained a broad range. High price was not a reliable synonym for high quality.

This matters because health care contains a powerful consumer intuition: expensive care must be better.

That intuition is reinforced by fear.

Nobody wants to economize on the wrong cancer treatment to save the insurer money. A family facing a serious diagnosis will often prefer the institution with the strongest brand even when evidence of superiority for the specific service is uncertain.

Hospitals understand reputation because reputation is part of bargaining power.

A famous name can be clinically meaningful for rare or complex conditions.

It can also pull routine care into a high-priced system.

The honest bill therefore needs more than a dollar figure.

It needs a value comparison.

Price without quality can steer patients toward cheap incompetence.

Quality without price can turn excellence into a blank check.

The information has to meet at the level where a decision can actually change.

That is usually not the emergency room.

It is network design.

It is employer purchasing.

It is merger review.

It is reimbursement policy.

It is where an infusion is administered.

It is whether an insurer can exclude a high-priced system without making the network useless.

It is whether a state has enough competing hospitals to create a negotiation rather than a surrender.

It is whether a public payer updates a rule that rewards the wrong setting.

The American health system is often criticized for spending too much because patients consume too much care.

Utilization matters.

So do technology, aging, chronic disease, administrative cost, wages, pharmaceuticals, and defensive practices.

But the hospital price problem cannot be reduced to patients demanding unnecessary services.

Many patients never knew the price of the necessary service they received.

Their employer may not have known it either.

That is a remarkable fact about a trillion-dollar market.

America did not eliminate the price of hospital care.

It hid the price inside a negotiation, divided the result among several payers, and mailed the household small pieces of the consequence through premiums, taxes, cost sharing, and wages.

The bill became hard to see.

The spending did not.
