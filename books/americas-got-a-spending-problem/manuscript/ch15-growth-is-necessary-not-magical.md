# Growth Is Necessary, Not Magical

If America could grow fast enough, many of its spending problems would become easier without anybody receiving a smaller check or paying a higher tax rate.

That is why growth is the most attractive fiscal policy in the room.

A larger economy produces more income.

More income produces more tax revenue at the same tax rates.

A larger denominator makes a given debt look smaller relative to national capacity.

Higher wages make fixed debts easier for households to carry.

More productivity lets workers consume more while supplying the same labor.

More housing supply can reduce the amount of subsidy required to make shelter affordable.

More energy can lower constraints across the economy.

More workers can support more retirees.

More innovation can turn an expensive problem into a cheap technology.

Growth is not a side issue in fiscal policy.

It is one of the main ways societies escape old constraints.

The danger begins when growth moves from policy objective to accounting assumption.

Then the forecast becomes whatever number the budget needs.

A tax cut will pay for itself through growth.

A spending program will pay for itself through growth.

A tariff will rebuild industry and raise growth.

A subsidy will create a strategic sector and raise growth.

A deregulation package will unlock growth.

An industrial policy will unlock growth.

Immigration will unlock growth.

Automation will unlock growth.

Every coalition eventually discovers a preferred form of free money called future GDP.

The economy is less cooperative.

CBO's 2026 baseline projected real GDP growth averaging about 1.8 percent annually from 2027 through 2036.

That estimate was not a claim that faster growth is impossible.

It reflected a set of forces moving in opposite directions.

Productivity improvements and technology can raise output.

Investment can raise the capital available per worker.

A larger labor force can raise total output.

At the same time, population aging slows labor-force growth. Lower immigration slows it further. Large federal deficits can crowd out private investment. Trade restrictions can reduce efficiency and raise input costs. Existing technologies diffuse at uneven rates. Productivity breakthroughs arrive unpredictably.

Growth is the result of many systems rather than one dial in the Oval Office.

This should make governments humble but not passive.

Policy matters enormously at the margin.

The United States can make it easier or harder to build homes, transmission lines, factories, laboratories, data centers, roads, ports, nuclear plants, renewable generation, warehouses, and businesses.

It can attract or repel workers.

It can finance or neglect basic research.

It can create a tax system that rewards investment or one that rewards the ability to navigate preferences.

It can develop skilled people or trap them behind unnecessary licensing barriers.

It can maintain infrastructure or allow the capital stock to decay.

It can protect competition or allow incumbents to turn regulation into a moat.

It can keep the macroeconomy stable or repeatedly manufacture crises around institutions that markets had assumed were durable.

Growth policy is therefore real.

Growth magic is the assumption that every favored policy has a multiplier large enough to erase its price.

The distinction begins with the denominator.

Debt-to-GDP ratios are fractions.

There are two ways to improve them.

Reduce the numerator.

Increase the denominator.

Politically, the denominator is nicer.

Nobody has to be told no.

But a denominator strategy works only if the growth is sustained and large enough relative to the debt dynamics.

A temporary burst of demand can raise GDP for a year or two without permanently increasing productive capacity.

A permanent productivity gain can compound for decades.

Those are not equivalent fiscal events.

This is why the word investment needs discipline.

Governments often call spending investment because the word sounds future-oriented.

Some public spending genuinely creates durable productive assets.

A port expansion that reduces shipping bottlenecks can increase capacity for decades.

A well-designed transmission project can connect cheap energy to places that need it.

Basic research can create knowledge private firms underproduce because the returns spill beyond the original funder.

Childhood health or education improvements can raise future productive capacity.

A public digital system can reduce compliance costs across millions of transactions.

Other spending called investment is ordinary consumption with better branding.

A grant can support a politically favored firm without creating a durable competitive industry.

A road can be built where traffic does not justify it.

A subsidy can preserve inefficient production rather than enabling learning.

A training program can graduate participants into jobs that do not exist.

The label does not determine the return.

The return does.

This creates a measurement problem similar to the one in services.

Private investment is disciplined partly by the possibility of loss.

A business that repeatedly invests in projects with poor returns eventually runs out of capital or investors.

Government can fund investments whose returns are social rather than private, which is precisely why government investment can be valuable.

It also means the feedback loop is weaker.

The bridge does not issue a quarterly earnings report.

The research grant may produce a breakthrough twenty years later—or nothing visible at all.

The school system can improve human capital in ways not captured by one year's test score.

Public investment needs evaluation strong enough to learn without demanding that every useful public good imitate venture capital.

There is another way growth gets oversold: dynamic scoring by slogan.

Every major policy changes behavior.

Tax rates affect incentives and timing.

Spending affects demand and sometimes supply.

Regulation affects costs and risks.

Immigration affects labor supply, consumption, housing demand, and public finances.

Trade policy affects domestic production, input costs, exchange rates, and foreign retaliation.

Infrastructure changes location and productivity.

A full fiscal estimate therefore should include economic effects when they are material.

But the existence of feedback does not mean the feedback is whatever advocates hope.

A policy can partly pay for itself.

It can more than pay for itself in unusual cases.

It can also cost more than its static estimate once economic effects are included.

The direction is empirical.

The temptation is ideological.

Tax-cut advocates often emphasize the growth effects of lower marginal rates while minimizing the revenue loss.

Public-investment advocates emphasize spillovers and multipliers while minimizing implementation risk and financing cost.

Industrial-policy advocates emphasize learning and strategic resilience while minimizing capture and misallocation.

Trade advocates emphasize efficiency while minimizing adjustment costs and geopolitical dependence.

Protectionists emphasize domestic capacity while minimizing higher input prices and retaliation.

All of these arguments contain real mechanisms.

None gets to choose only the mechanism that points in the preferred direction.

This matters because the fiscal outlook is already sensitive to small changes in growth.

A tenth of a percentage point of productivity growth compounded across decades is enormous.

That fact encourages every policy advocate to claim the tenth.

The arithmetic is seductive.

The attribution is hard.

Growth also has a distribution.

An economy can become richer while many households feel little improvement.

GDP can rise because high-productivity sectors expand while housing costs consume the gains in the cities where those sectors cluster.

Stock values can rise while nonowners see little benefit.

Automation can raise total output while reducing bargaining power for some workers.

Immigration can increase aggregate output while producing localized pressures on housing, schools, or infrastructure if supply does not respond.

Trade can lower consumer prices while concentrating job losses in particular communities.

A fiscal strategy based on growth needs political legitimacy as well as aggregate arithmetic.

People experience income, prices, jobs, commutes, rents, and security.

They do not live inside GDP.

The best growth policy therefore expands capacity in ways that reduce the recurring spending pressures elsewhere in this book.

Housing is a good example.

If productive cities can build more homes, workers can move toward opportunity without bidding scarce apartments to extraordinary prices.

That raises effective labor mobility and reduces the need to subsidize rents inflated by scarcity.

Energy is another.

Abundant reliable energy can lower costs across manufacturing, transportation, computing, and household budgets while enabling new industries.

Permitting reform can matter not because regulation is bad in the abstract but because a system that takes longer to approve socially useful infrastructure than to invent it has created a growth bottleneck.

Immigration is another.

A larger working-age population can raise total output, entrepreneurship, tax revenue, and the number of workers supporting an aging society.

The fiscal effect depends on the age, skills, earnings, family structure, public-service use, and long-run assimilation of immigrants, as well as on whether housing and infrastructure expand alongside population.

Again the mechanism is conditional.

The slogan is easier.

Artificial intelligence offers the newest version of growth optimism.

If machine systems raise productivity across coding, research, administration, design, logistics, medicine, education, and management, the gains could materially improve long-run fiscal capacity.

CBO's 2026 economic projections already incorporated some productivity support from generative AI adoption.

The fiscal temptation is to spend the future productivity dividend before it arrives.

That would be a familiar mistake.

Railroads transformed productivity.

Electricity transformed productivity.

Computers transformed productivity.

The internet transformed productivity.

None abolished recessions, political conflict, bad investment, bubbles, or budget constraints.

Transformative technology expands the production possibilities frontier.

It does not automatically allocate the gains to the Treasury.

The tax system, ownership structure, labor market, competition, and public policy determine how much of the gain becomes wages, profits, lower prices, rents, or government revenue.

A productivity boom can improve the fiscal outlook substantially.

It is still not a budget line item until it happens.

This suggests a useful fiscal rule of thumb.

Build policy to create upside.

Budget as if the upside must be earned.

That is different from pessimism.

Pessimism would refuse to invest because future gains are uncertain.

Prudence invests where expected returns justify the risk while avoiding promises that require the optimistic scenario to survive.

A household follows a similar rule.

It may invest in education because higher future earnings are plausible.

It should be cautious about taking on fixed obligations that become impossible unless the highest salary projection comes true.

A business invests based on expected growth.

It also models downside cases.

A government with obligations lasting generations should do at least as much.

There is a final reason growth cannot replace fiscal choices.

Successful growth creates new things to spend money on.

A richer society demands better medicine, safer infrastructure, more advanced defense, cleaner environments, more education, better public spaces, greater disaster resilience, and more sophisticated government.

Prosperity raises capacity.

It also raises standards.

The United States did not become rich and then stop inventing public commitments.

It became rich enough to imagine new ones.

This is not a flaw.

It is what wealth is for.

But it means growth does not mechanically close the gap between desire and willingness to pay.

Politics can expand desire as fast as productivity expands income.

The fiscal problem is therefore partly institutional.

When growth arrives, does the government use some of the windfall to reduce debt or shore up existing promises?

Or does every additional dollar of projected revenue become the financing for a new commitment?

When interest rates fall, is the saving banked or spent?

When an economic boom increases receipts, does the budget distinguish cyclical revenue from permanent capacity?

When productivity surprises on the upside, does the country preserve room for the next recession?

A government can grow out of debt.

It can also grow into a larger set of promises.

The difference is not GDP.

It is what politics does with GDP.

Growth is necessary because without it every distributional conflict becomes harsher.

A stagnant society has to fight over slices.

A growing society can enlarge the pie while arguing about shares.

Growth makes retirement promises easier, debt easier, wages higher, investment more attractive, and public goods more affordable.

It creates room for generosity.

That makes pro-growth policy one of the most humane fiscal strategies available.

The humane part disappears when growth is used to avoid telling the truth about today's arithmetic.

The budget should not assume a miracle.

It should create conditions in which miracles are more likely and remain solvent if they are late.
