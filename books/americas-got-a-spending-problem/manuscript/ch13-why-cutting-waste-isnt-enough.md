# Why Cutting Waste Isn't Enough

Everybody wants to cut waste, which is why waste is such a convenient place to end a budget argument before it begins. A politician can promise to protect Social Security, Medicare, defense, veterans, families, and the middle class while avoiding tax increases and benefit cuts, then point to fraud, duplication, bureaucracy, consultants, empty buildings, procurement failures, and obscure programs as the source of the missing money.

The list is not imaginary. The federal government is enormous, and it makes errors at enormous scale. In fiscal year 2025, federal agencies reported an estimated $186 billion in improper payments across dozens of programs. Most were overpayments. Some reflected fraud. Others reflected missing documentation, incorrect eligibility decisions, outdated data, administrative mistakes, or payments made in the wrong amount.

One hundred eighty-six billion dollars is not pocket change. It is larger than the annual budget of many federal departments and should be attacked with better data, stronger controls, payment verification, procurement discipline, enforcement, and competent administration.

Then comes the arithmetic. CBO projected a federal deficit of about $1.9 trillion in 2026. Even if every dollar of the 2025 improper-payment estimate could somehow be eliminated instantly, permanently, and without new administrative cost, the structural budget problem would remain. No serious auditor would promise such a result anyway.

That is not an argument against cutting waste. It is an argument against using waste as a substitute for deciding what government should actually do and how those choices should be financed.

The word itself hides three different problems. The first is error: money went to the wrong person, a contractor billed incorrectly, an agency paid twice, eligibility changed, or the system lacked the data needed to verify a claim. This is the cleanest category because almost everyone agrees the expenditure should not have happened.

The second is inefficiency. Government bought something real but paid too much, took too long, duplicated another system, used obsolete technology, or imposed administrative costs larger than the value they created.

The third is disagreement. A program did exactly what the law intended, but somebody thinks the law should not exist. Foreign aid, a weapons platform, a tax credit, a retirement benefit, or a research grant can all be called waste by someone who opposes the underlying choice. That does not make the expenditure an accounting error. It makes it political.

A mature spending argument has to keep those categories separate. Real waste should be cut because it consumes resources without producing the intended value. Inefficiency should be reduced because taxpayers should not pay two dollars for one dollar of capability merely because they are diffuse. But if the fiscal gap is being produced mainly by legal commitments functioning as designed, calling them waste delays the real debate.

Imagine a federal government that became the most operationally disciplined large institution in the world. Every payment is verified. Every building is used efficiently. Every obsolete program is terminated. Agencies share data cleanly. Procurement offices negotiate like world-class buyers. Fraudulent claims are stopped before payment. Software is modern. Duplicate systems are gone.

The resulting government is cheaper. It still has Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, defense, veterans' benefits, courts, prisons, border operations, scientific research, transportation, disaster response, agricultural support, public health, diplomacy, tax administration, interest on existing debt, and whatever other lawful commitments Congress has chosen to preserve.

Competence changes the price of promises. It does not decide which promises exist.

That is why fiscal reform disappoints people who approach the budget as if it were a messy corporate expense report. A chief executive can close an unprofitable product line, consolidate vendors, cut travel, renegotiate leases, or reduce headcount because the company has a narrower mission and a more concentrated authority structure. A national government is a collection of missions created by law and politics, and its largest expenditures are often the product rather than overhead around the product.

Social Security checks are Social Security. Medicare reimbursements are Medicare. Military personnel, operations, equipment, logistics, and readiness are defense. Interest is the financing cost of earlier deficits. Calling these categories overhead does not make them disappear.

There is another complication. Some spending that looks wasteful in a static snapshot is insurance against a world that did not happen. A backup generator that never turns on, a strategic stockpile that is never used, a military unit that never fights, an epidemiological surveillance network that detects nothing dramatic, or a bridge inspection that finds no defect can all look inefficient if utilization is the only measure.

That does not give redundancy immunity. It means cost control has to distinguish slack that preserves resilience from slack that survives because nobody has enough incentive to remove it. The distinction requires judgment.

Administrative spending has the same problem. Bureaucracy is an easy villain because nobody has ever marched under a banner demanding more forms. Yet administration is how rules become real. A benefit program without eligibility administration invites error and fraud. A tax system without enforcement becomes a suggestion. A procurement system without contract oversight can save money on contracting officers and lose much more to weak purchasing.

Cuts can therefore create false economy. Reduce the people who verify spending and improper payments may rise. Defer maintenance and the current budget improves while the future repair bill grows. Remove experienced public employees and an agency may become more dependent on contractors who charge for institutional knowledge the government no longer retains.

A serious anti-waste agenda asks what happens after the cut. If a program is eliminated, does the underlying obligation disappear or move elsewhere? If staff is reduced, does workload fall or do processing times rise? If a grant ends, does the activity stop or do states and cities pick it up? If maintenance is deferred, does the asset survive without consequence? If a public laboratory closes, does private industry replace the research, or was the point of public funding that the return could not be privately captured?

The budget line is not the outcome.

This principle cuts both ways. Defenders of government should not use complexity as an excuse against simplification. A program can have real beneficiaries and still be badly designed. An agency can have an important mission and still contain offices that no longer justify themselves. A difficult error rate is not a reason to accept a $186 billion improper-payment estimate. A complicated procurement system is not entitled to buy technology so slowly that it is obsolete at award.

Competence is fiscal policy. Better purchasing can support a larger government at lower cost. Better payment integrity can protect social insurance and its legitimacy. Better data can make benefits easier for eligible people to receive and harder for ineligible claims to pass. Better digital systems can reduce administrative burden for citizens and agencies at the same time.

The useful standard is not simply that government spends less. It is that government can spend less to accomplish the same thing and prove that it did.

Proof matters because savings are often claimed before they exist. A commission identifies $20 billion of potential efficiencies and the number enters speeches as $20 billion saved. But an opportunity is not a realized budget reduction. Contracts may have termination costs. New systems may require upfront investment. Payment controls can create false positives. Savings need baselines, timelines, implementation costs, and verification or efficiency becomes another form of optimistic budgeting.

The federal budget has several moving parts that no single anti-waste campaign can replace. Waste reduction lowers spending. Taxes raise revenue. Growth expands the economy underneath the debt burden. Program reforms change legal commitments. Interest costs respond to borrowing, rates, and the maturity structure in ways Congress cannot command at will.

Waste is politically perfect because nobody is for it. It is fiscally incomplete because the largest choices have constituencies and purposes.

Household finance has the same trap. A family facing a large structural mismatch can benefit from canceling unused subscriptions and shopping more carefully. If housing, child care, medical costs, transportation, taxes, insurance, and debt service consume nearly all income, however, the streaming bill is not the whole problem. Personal-finance advice becomes unserious when every structural mismatch is reduced to latte arithmetic. Federal-budget advice becomes unserious when a trillion-dollar mismatch is reduced to fraud arithmetic.

Scale does not excuse small savings. It tells us where to place them. A billion dollars is real money. A trillion dollars is a thousand billions. Cutting the first is worthwhile without pretending it solved the second.

The United States should become much better at eliminating improper payments, reducing procurement waste, consolidating duplicative systems, selling unused assets, modernizing technology, policing fraud, simplifying administration, and ending programs whose benefits no longer justify their cost. Doing so would improve fiscal capacity and trust. Citizens are more willing to finance institutions they believe can distinguish a necessary dollar from a careless one.

Waste therefore matters beyond the amount saved. A visibly incompetent government makes every tax harder to defend. A visibly wasteful benefit program makes every beneficiary easier to caricature. A procurement scandal makes justified defense spending look suspicious. An agency that cannot explain where money went weakens the case for the next dollar.

The mistake is not caring too much about waste. The mistake is stopping there.

Once obvious waste is cut, the country still has to decide which things are worth buying, how much they are worth, who should pay, how much should be financed across time, which commitments should grow more slowly, which taxes should rise or broaden, which successful programs deserve more, and which should end.

No audit can answer those questions for a democracy. A spreadsheet can find an error. It cannot decide a value.

Waste is what remains after the values are chosen and the institution still pays too much. America has plenty of that. It also has a larger problem: it wants more legitimate government than it is currently willing to finance honestly.
