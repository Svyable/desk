# Why Cutting Waste Isn't Enough

Everybody wants to cut waste.

That is why waste is such a convenient place to end a budget argument before it begins.

A politician can promise to protect Social Security, protect Medicare, protect defense, protect veterans, protect families, avoid tax increases, avoid benefit cuts, avoid touching the middle class, and still claim to have a fiscal plan by announcing that the missing money will come from fraud, abuse, duplication, bureaucracy, consultants, foreign junkets, empty buildings, procurement failures, and programs nobody has heard of.

The list is not imaginary.

The federal government is enormous. It makes errors at enormous scale.

In fiscal year 2025, federal agencies reported an estimated $186 billion in improper payments across dozens of programs. Most were overpayments. Some reflected fraud. Others reflected missing documentation, incorrect eligibility decisions, outdated data, administrative mistakes, or payments made in the wrong amount.

One hundred eighty-six billion dollars is not pocket change.

It is larger than the annual budget of many federal departments.

It should be attacked with better data, stronger controls, competent administration, payment verification, procurement discipline, and enforcement.

Then comes the arithmetic.

CBO projected a federal deficit of about $1.9 trillion in 2026.

Even if every dollar of the 2025 improper-payment estimate could somehow be eliminated instantly, permanently, and without new administrative cost—a fantasy no serious auditor would endorse—the structural budget problem would remain.

That is not an argument against cutting waste.

It is an argument against using waste as a substitute for deciding what government should actually do and how it should actually be financed.

The distinction matters because the word waste is used for three very different things.

The first is error.

A payment went to the wrong person. A contractor billed incorrectly. A benefit continued after eligibility changed. An agency paid twice. A system lacked the data needed to verify a claim. This is the cleanest category because almost everyone agrees the expenditure should not have happened.

The second is inefficiency.

The government bought something real but paid too much, took too long, used obsolete technology, duplicated another system, employed an unnecessarily complicated process, or imposed administrative costs larger than the value they created.

The third is disagreement.

A program did exactly what the law intended, but somebody thinks the law should not exist.

That third category is where budget debates become dishonest.

Foreign aid can be described as waste by someone who opposes foreign aid.

A weapons platform can be described as waste by someone who thinks the threat does not justify the capability.

A tax credit can be described as waste by someone who thinks the subsidized activity is not worth supporting.

A retirement benefit can be called excessive by one coalition and earned by another.

A research grant can sound absurd in a thirty-second clip and look reasonable inside the scientific field that proposed it.

The fact that somebody hates an expenditure does not make it an accounting error.

It makes it a political choice.

A mature spending argument has to keep those categories separate.

Real waste should be cut because waste consumes resources without producing the intended value.

Inefficiency should be reduced because government should not pay two dollars for one dollar of capability merely because taxpayers are diffuse.

But if the fiscal gap is being produced mainly by legal commitments that are functioning as designed, calling them waste delays the real debate.

This is the central problem with the fantasy of the perfect audit.

Imagine that the federal government became the most operationally disciplined large institution in the world.

Every payment was verified.

Every building was used efficiently.

Every software contract was competitively bid.

Every obsolete program was terminated.

Every inspector general recommendation was implemented.

Every agency shared data cleanly.

Every procurement office negotiated like a world-class buyer.

Every fraudulent claim was stopped before payment.

The resulting government would be cheaper.

It would also still have Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, defense, veterans' benefits, federal salaries, interest on existing debt, courts, prisons, border operations, scientific research, transportation programs, disaster response, agricultural support, education programs, tax administration, public health, diplomacy, and the other lawful commitments Congress had chosen to preserve.

The structural gap would not disappear merely because the institution became competent.

Competence changes the price of promises.

It does not decide which promises exist.

This is why fiscal reform often disappoints people who approach the budget as if it were a messy corporate expense report.

A chief executive can cancel a retreat, renegotiate office leases, close an unprofitable product line, reduce headcount, consolidate software vendors, and demand lower travel spending.

Those actions may materially change a company's cost structure because the company has a narrower mission and a more concentrated authority structure.

A national government is a collection of missions created by law and politics.

Its largest expenditures are not accidental overhead.

They are the product.

Social Security is not overhead around the federal government.

Social Security checks are the program.

Medicare reimbursements are not administrative clutter around Medicare.

Paying for medical care is Medicare.

Military personnel, operations, equipment, logistics, and readiness are not overhead around defense.

They are defense.

Interest is not a redundant agency that can be reorganized.

It is the financing cost of earlier deficits.

The search for waste becomes misleading when it avoids this distinction.

There is another complication.

Some spending that looks wasteful in a static snapshot is insurance against a world that did not happen.

A backup generator that never turns on can look idle.

A strategic stockpile that is never used can look excessive.

A military unit that never fights can look unproductive.

An epidemiological surveillance network that detects nothing dramatic can look unnecessary.

A bridge inspection that finds no defect can look like paperwork.

Redundancy often looks inefficient because its successful output is an absent disaster.

That does not mean every reserve, backup, or preparedness program deserves immunity.

It means the standard cannot simply be utilization.

Good cost control distinguishes slack that preserves resilience from slack that survives because nobody has enough incentive to remove it.

This is hard work.

It requires judgment, not slogans.

The same is true of administrative spending.

Bureaucracy is one of the most reliable villains in American politics because nobody has ever marched under a banner demanding more forms.

Yet administration is the mechanism through which rules become real.

A benefit program without eligibility administration becomes an invitation to error and fraud.

A tax system without enforcement becomes a suggestion.

A procurement system without contract oversight can save money on contracting officers and lose far more to weak purchasing.

A hospital payment system without auditing can pay claims faster and less accurately.

Cutting the people who verify spending can increase improper spending.

Cutting maintenance can reduce today's budget and enlarge tomorrow's repair bill.

Cutting experienced public employees can reduce payroll and increase dependence on contractors who charge more for institutional knowledge the government no longer retains.

There is such a thing as false economy.

A serious anti-waste agenda has to ask what happens after the cut.

That question is surprisingly rare.

A program is eliminated.

Does the underlying obligation disappear, or does another agency inherit it?

A staff is reduced.

Does the workload fall, or do processing times rise?

A grant is canceled.

Does the activity stop, or do states and local governments pick it up?

A maintenance cycle is deferred.

Does the asset survive without consequence, or does the future repair become more expensive?

A public laboratory is closed.

Does private industry replicate the research because the opportunity is profitable, or was the entire reason for public funding that the return could not be privately captured?

The budget line is not the outcome.

This principle cuts in both directions.

Defenders of government often use complexity as an excuse against simplification.

They should not.

The fact that a program has beneficiaries does not prove it is well designed.

The fact that an agency performs an important mission does not justify every office inside it.

The fact that an error rate is difficult to eliminate does not make a $186 billion improper-payment estimate acceptable.

The fact that procurement is complicated does not excuse a process that takes years to buy technology already obsolete by award.

The fact that fraud prevention costs money does not mean every fraud-control system is cost effective.

Competence is a fiscal policy.

It may be one of the few fiscal policies with constituencies broader than ideology.

Better purchasing can support a larger government at lower cost.

Better payment integrity can support social insurance while protecting its legitimacy.

Better data can make benefits easier for eligible people to receive and harder for ineligible claims to pass.

Better digital systems can reduce administrative burden on citizens while reducing administrative cost to agencies.

The goal should be a government that can spend less to accomplish the same thing—and can prove when it has.

That last clause matters.

Waste reduction is difficult to govern because savings are often claimed before they exist.

A commission identifies $20 billion of potential efficiencies.

The number enters speeches as $20 billion saved.

But an identified opportunity is not a realized budget reduction.

A procurement office may need upfront investment to renegotiate systems.

A program may have termination costs.

A contract may run for years.

A payment-integrity system may catch overpayments while creating false positives that delay legitimate beneficiaries.

Savings need baselines, timelines, implementation costs, and verification.

Without those disciplines, efficiency becomes another form of optimistic budgeting.

There is a useful way to think about the scale.

The federal budget has a numerator problem and a denominator problem.

The numerator is what the government spends above revenue.

Waste reduction lowers the numerator.

Growth raises the denominator.

Taxes raise revenue.

Program reforms alter legal commitments.

Interest management affects the financing tail only indirectly because the government does not control market rates at will.

No one lever carries the whole problem.

The appeal of waste is that it appears to be the only lever without a constituency.

Nobody is for waste.

That makes it politically perfect and fiscally incomplete.

The same pattern appears in households.

A family facing a major structural mismatch between income and obligations can benefit from canceling unused subscriptions and shopping more carefully.

Those changes are sensible.

If the mortgage, child care, medical costs, car payment, and taxes consume nearly all income, the streaming bill is not the entire problem.

Personal-finance advice becomes unserious when it treats every structural mismatch as latte arithmetic.

Federal budget advice becomes unserious when it treats every trillion-dollar mismatch as fraud arithmetic.

Scale is not an excuse to ignore small savings.

It is the reason to place them correctly.

A billion dollars is a billion dollars.

A thousand billion dollars is a trillion.

Both statements can be true without implying that the first solves the second.

The United States should become much better at eliminating improper payments, reducing procurement waste, consolidating duplicative systems, selling unused assets, modernizing technology, policing fraud, simplifying administration, and terminating programs whose benefits no longer justify their cost.

Doing so would improve fiscal capacity.

It would also improve trust.

Citizens are more willing to finance institutions they believe can distinguish a necessary dollar from a careless one.

Waste therefore matters beyond the amount saved.

It affects legitimacy.

A visibly incompetent government makes every tax harder to defend.

A visibly wasteful benefit program makes every beneficiary easier to caricature.

A procurement scandal makes even justified defense spending look suspicious.

An agency that cannot explain where money went weakens the case for the next dollar.

Fiscal legitimacy is cumulative.

So is fiscal illusion.

The mistake is not caring too much about waste.

The mistake is stopping there.

Once the obvious waste is cut, the country still has to answer the question it was avoiding.

Which things are worth buying?

How much are they worth?

Who should pay?

How much should be financed across time?

Which commitments should grow more slowly?

Which taxes should rise, fall, or broaden?

Which programs should become more generous because they work?

Which should end because they do not?

There is no audit that can answer those questions for a democracy.

The spreadsheet can find an error.

It cannot decide a value.

Waste is what remains after the values are chosen and the institution still pays too much.

America has plenty of that.

It also has a much larger problem.

It wants more legitimate government than it is currently willing to finance honestly.
