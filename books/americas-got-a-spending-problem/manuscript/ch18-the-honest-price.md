# The Honest Price

At the grocery store, the receipt is almost insultingly specific. Eggs have a line. Coffee has a line. Dish soap has a line. The sales tax has a line. The total sits at the bottom because the transaction has reached the moment when all the small decisions become one fact.

Nobody expects the receipt to explain whether the coffee was worth buying, whether the eggs were fairly priced, whether the farmer was well paid, whether the tax is wise, or whether the family should have chosen a cheaper store. Its job is smaller. It tells the truth about what the decisions added up to.

Much of American economic life is designed to postpone that moment. A mortgage turns a house into a monthly payment. Student lending separates education from the years in which its financing is repaid. Health insurance separates treatment from most of the price of treatment. Tax preferences separate public policy from the appropriations column. Entitlement formulas separate a promise from the annual vote that finances it. Debt separates today's government from part of the tax burden created by today's choices.

Most of these arrangements are inventions of extraordinary usefulness. A thirty-year mortgage makes homeownership possible for people who cannot produce the price of a house in cash. Insurance allows a family to survive a medical event that would otherwise be financially catastrophic. Student lending can let a capable person buy education before receiving the income that education may help produce. Government borrowing can finance a war, bridge a recession, answer a disaster, or build an asset whose benefits will outlive the taxpayers who first paid for it. Civilization depends on moving costs and benefits across people and time. The trouble begins when moving the price changes our willingness to see it.

That is the spending problem this book has been circling. It is tempting to make the argument moral because moral arguments are easy to remember: consumers are profligate, politicians irresponsible, corporations greedy, the rich undertaxed, government wasteful, the welfare state overgenerous, the military-industrial complex self-protecting. Every version can point to evidence. None is large enough for the system.

A nation of more than three hundred million people cannot be explained by one vice. The more useful pattern is institutional. America is unusually capable of separating the person who says yes from the full economic consequence of yes. Sometimes the separation is compassionate, commercially ingenious, politically convenient, or unavoidable. But somebody, somewhere, still has to encounter scarcity.

If the patient cannot discipline a hospital's price, an insurer, public payer, regulator, or competitive market has to do it. If the student cannot realistically judge the lifetime consequences of a college choice, the financing system has to make price and outcomes legible before commitment becomes identity. If a city subsidizes housing demand while preventing supply from responding, the financing eventually collides with land scarcity. If a retiree receives a stable benefit independent of the year's tax collections, the political system has to maintain durable financing behind that stability. If Congress borrows to separate a current benefit from a current tax, future budgets inherit the commitment through debt service. Scarcity can be pooled, insured, financed, shifted, or shared. It cannot be repealed.

Prosperous societies are especially good at making that obvious fact difficult to see because prosperity creates buffers. A household with strong income can carry a payment that would crush another household. A corporation with valuable assets can refinance through a bad year. A city with a broad tax base can issue bonds on favorable terms. A sovereign country with deep capital markets and a trusted currency can borrow at a scale impossible for a household, company, or small nation.

Those buffers are real wealth, and they let bad arrangements survive for a long time. A poor system receives feedback quickly: the cash drawer empties, the lender refuses, the machine stops. A rich system can refinance, tax later, raise premiums next year, extend the loan, defer maintenance, shift a cost to another level of government, or offer more financing when the underlying asset has become harder to afford. America can afford many mistakes individually. The accumulation is what becomes expensive.

This is why the book has resisted catastrophe language. A fiscal crisis is possible, but it is not required for the argument. The federal government does not need to lose access to credit for rising interest costs to matter. A hospital system does not need to collapse for high negotiated prices to burden employers and workers. A housing market does not need to crash for entry to become unaffordable. A university does not need to fail for the financing risk of noncompletion to damage students. A pension plan does not need to default for higher contributions to crowd out other priorities.

A system can remain functional while becoming less free. Recurring interest payments narrow what a future budget can do. An underfunded promise does the same. A constrained housing market determines who can enter a city before any zoning board says the words social mobility. An opaque hospital price eventually travels into a premium, wage negotiation, tax bill, or public budget. A tax preference creates a constituency around a policy whose cost is harder to see than a check written by Treasury. The country does not wake up one morning with no choices. It discovers, one household budget and one appropriations bill at a time, that fewer choices remain cheap.

Fiscal capacity is better understood as freedom than as a score in a contest over virtue. A strong balance sheet matters because the future arrives with surprises. War, recession, pandemic, technological opportunity, natural disaster, or an ordinary family emergency rarely waits for the year in which the budget is most convenient. Savings, unused borrowing capacity, manageable fixed costs, and well-maintained public assets are all forms of maneuverability.

The federal government can roll debt indefinitely in a way a household cannot. It issues the world's most important safe asset. Its taxing power is enormous. Its currency and institutions give it borrowing capacity that makes household analogies dangerous when taken literally. But it still faces opportunity cost inside a real economy with workers, factories, hospitals, engineers, land, energy, materials, time, and political attention. Accounting can move claims across years. It cannot create infinite nurses, houses, shipyards, transmission lines, physicians, teachers, or skilled construction crews on command.

That is why so many of the expensive subjects in this book kept returning to supply. Financing solves a financing problem; it does not automatically solve a capacity problem. Writing a check is faster than building a neighborhood. Guaranteeing a loan is faster than creating a high-quality university seat. Appropriating medical money is faster than training a physician. Announcing a defense budget is faster than rebuilding a supplier base. The spending appears first because spending can happen first. Then the country asks why the thing is still scarce.

Public money can still be the right answer. The discipline is to finish the transaction. If the goal is more housing, count housing. If the goal is better health, measure outcomes and access alongside medical spending. If the goal is education, care about completion and learning alongside financing. If the goal is defense, measure capability rather than treating a protected procurement line as the objective. If the goal is retirement security, finance the promise in a way that can survive the retirement of the people who voted for it. Politics often treats money as the accomplishment because inputs are easier to announce than outcomes. A spending bill can be signed in an afternoon. A productive system may take a decade.

Cuts deserve the same scrutiny. Reducing an agency's budget does not prove the service became more efficient. Cutting maintenance does not prove the maintenance was wasteful. Lowering a medical payment does not guarantee the provider remains available. Reducing headcount can remove bureaucracy or remove state capacity. Numbers moving downward possess no moral direction of their own. The honest price includes what disappears.

So this book cannot end with one target: balance the budget, cap spending, raise taxes, cut taxes, eliminate waste, grow faster, or tax the rich. Each can belong inside a serious fiscal program. None substitutes for deciding what the country is buying and how the price travels through the system. A supporter of generous social insurance still has to care about provider prices, supply, and durable financing. A supporter of low taxes still has to identify which commitments a lower revenue level can support. A defense hawk still has to distinguish resilience from procurement inertia. A critic of government waste still has to acknowledge that lawful benefits functioning as designed are not accounting errors. Growth can make nearly every tradeoff easier; it cannot be booked before it happens.

Boring finance is underrated. The best pension funding policy does not wait for a crisis headline. The best debt-management system is the one most citizens never need to think about. Good institutions move conflict to the place where it can still be managed. They expose the long tail of a promise before the constituency hardens around it, make lifecycle cost visible while a procurement can still be changed, and make a housing constraint visible before an entire generation experiences the city as something owned by people who arrived earlier. An honest price arrives early enough to influence the decision.

There is a personal version of the same rule. Most people do not need to become ascetics. They need to know what is fixed before deciding what is free. A household can spend generously on travel, food, children, hobbies, education, gifts, or a home and still be resilient if the commitments fit its income and leave room for uncertainty. Another household can look frugal in every visible category while housing, medical, transportation, or debt costs consume nearly everything before discretionary spending begins.

This is why lectures about lattes age so badly. Small spending matters when small spending is the margin. It is insulting when the problem is structural. The same mistake occurs in federal politics when a trillion-dollar structural gap is discussed through a museum grant, a conference, a foolish software contract, or some other photogenic example of bad spending. The example may deserve ridicule. The arithmetic survives it.

Scale is an ethical discipline. If the problem is large, the answer has to be allowed to become large enough to touch things people care about: health care, retirement, defense, taxes, debt service, public investments, benefits that work, preferences with constituencies, and people who planned their lives around existing rules. There is no painless spreadsheet because the spreadsheet is describing society. A reform that pretends otherwise is usually hiding the payer again.

This is where the word honest earns its place. Honesty is not announcing the largest number available or waving a debt clock at a rally. It is not turning every household pleasure into a moral failure, or an agency defending its current budget as the minimum compatible with civilization. It is narrower and harder: state what a choice costs, who is likely to bear the cost, when it arrives, what uncertainty surrounds the estimate, what capacity the money actually buys, and what other choices become harder afterward.

Sometimes that accounting argues for spending more. A bridge may be cheaper to repair now than replace later. A vaccine program may prevent enormous future costs. A military stockpile may look idle until the year it is needed. A poor child may receive benefits whose value extends far beyond the current budget window. Scientific research may create options nobody can price at the time of appropriation. A narrow budget can underinvest just as easily as it can overspend. The test is not whether the number is large. It is whether the story being told about the number includes its consequences.

That also means admitting combinations that slogans avoid. A subsidy can help households while leaving supply constrained. A tax preference can serve a public purpose while still being a public choice. A popular benefit can be valuable and underfinanced. A defense capability can have real strategic value and still be purchased badly. Higher taxes can solve part of a fiscal problem without solving all of it. Cuts can remove waste and also destroy useful capacity. Growth can be essential without being guaranteed. Borrowing can be wise in one year and evasive in another.

Prices are estimates of tradeoffs, not declarations of value. A human life does not become worth less because treatment is expensive. National defense does not become more valuable because a weapons system costs more. A child's education is not captured by future earnings. A public park can be worth preserving even when its revenue is zero. The existence of things beyond price is not a reason to stop pricing resources. We use money to coordinate scarce means precisely because ends are not all commensurable. The budget cannot tell us what to love. It can tell us whether we are pretending love has no cost.

That pretense eventually damages the things we meant to protect. A retirement system that refuses adjustment until abrupt cuts are near is not kinder because it delayed the argument. A health system that hides prices until premiums become unaffordable is not more compassionate because the patient never saw the negotiated rate. A city that protects every existing homeowner's scarcity premium while subsidizing excluded buyers has not solved affordability. A government that discovers the value of fiscal capacity only after an emergency has consumed it discovered nothing in time.

The United States is wealthy enough to choose many expensive things and technologically capable enough to finance them in sophisticated ways. It can place a bill in a premium, payroll deduction, tax preference, monthly payment, unfunded promise, bond issue, federal transfer, future appropriation, or Treasury auction. Those inventions can make society more humane and more productive. They can also make the total difficult to see.

The answer is not to abolish the inventions. Put the financing beside the benefit. Put the supply response beside the subsidy. Put the long tail beside the first-year number. When the person saying yes does not bear the full price, make sure somebody with information and authority is still responsible for the no. Then choose.

A democracy is allowed to choose extravagance. It is allowed to choose redistribution, deterrence, insurance, beauty, science, redundancy, dignity, convenience, or security at prices a spreadsheet alone would not recommend. Those are political judgments. The requirement is smaller: do not call the price zero.

At the end of an ordinary shopping trip, the cashier does not make the customer defend every item. The machine simply adds. Eggs. Coffee. Dish soap. Tax. Total.

Then the receipt comes out of the printer. It contains no ideology. It simply refuses to forget what we chose.
