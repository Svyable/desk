# The Cost Disease with Lobbyists

A string quartet takes about as long to play today as it did when the music was written.

That is not a technological failure.

If the musicians finish Beethoven's Opus 59 in twelve minutes because a new scheduling system improved productivity, something has gone wrong.

A barber can use a better booking app, a dentist can use a faster scanner, a teacher can grade with better software, and a nurse can carry a more capable device. But much of the thing being purchased in labor-intensive services is still human attention delivered in real time. A lesson, an examination, a legal argument, a bedside conversation, a rehearsal, and a haircut all resist the kind of productivity gain that lets a factory make twice as many bearings with the same number of workers.

William Baumol and William Bowen made this observation famous in the 1960s. The mechanism later became known as Baumol's cost disease. When productivity rises quickly in some sectors, wages can rise across the economy because workers have alternatives. Sectors whose productivity rises slowly still have to pay competitive wages to attract labor. Their costs therefore rise relative to the output they produce.

The name makes the phenomenon sound pathological.

Much of it is a side effect of prosperity.

A richer society can afford to spend a larger share of income on things that remain stubbornly human. If manufactured goods become cheaper while skilled care remains labor-intensive, then care takes a larger share of the budget even if nobody is cheating. The economy has become better at making televisions without becoming equally better at sitting with a confused patient at three in the morning.

This is an important correction to any book with the phrase spending problem in the title.

Not every rising cost is evidence that somebody found a loophole.

Sometimes the price rises because the rest of the economy became more productive.

William Nordhaus tested the broader Baumol story using U.S. industry data from 1948 through 2001 and found a strong association between slower productivity growth and faster relative price growth. A 2026 paper in the American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics revisited one proposed complication—worker selection across sectors—and concluded that selection explained little of the service-sector cost disease.

The old mechanism still has teeth.

Then America adds its own machinery.

A slow-productivity sector becomes more expensive for understandable reasons. The sector is also politically important, professionally licensed, subsidized, insured, regulated, locally protected, difficult to compare, and staffed by organizations whose revenue depends on the rules. Every affected group learns the rules. Associations form. Consultants appear. Accreditation becomes a language. Reimbursement formulas acquire appendices. Tax preferences become permanent enough to feel natural. Temporary grants develop constituencies. A hospital system becomes the largest employer in a county. A university becomes the identity of a town. A defense plant becomes the reason a congressional district has twelve hundred well-paid jobs.

The cost disease gets lobbyists.

That phrase should not be read as a claim that lobbyists caused the cost disease.

They did not.

It means that once a labor-intensive service becomes expensive and socially important, the politics of its price become unusually complicated. The sector does not sit quietly while households and governments decide how much of it to buy. It develops institutions that influence how the bill is measured, who pays it, which competitors may enter, which inputs are required, and which cuts are described as intolerable.

This is where a natural rise in cost can become difficult to distinguish from a protected rise in cost.

Health care is the obvious example.

Some medical work is inherently labor-intensive. A nurse cannot safely monitor an unlimited number of intensive-care patients because software improved. Surgery still requires trained people, sterile facilities, standby capacity, infection control, and expensive equipment. New treatments can raise spending because they make previously untreatable diseases treatable. An MRI machine is not evidence of waste merely because medicine once existed without it.

But slow productivity does not explain why commercially insured patients can generate radically different payments for similar hospital services in different markets.

RAND's employer-led hospital price study found that in 2022 private plans in its sample paid, on average, about 254 percent of what Medicare would have paid for the same services at the same facilities. The study found states below 200 percent and states above 300 percent. That spread is difficult to explain as one national case of nurses becoming more expensive.

Market structure matters.

Bargaining power matters.

Payment design matters.

So does the fact that the person receiving the service often has almost no idea what price was negotiated.

Education provides a different mixture.

A teacher standing before twenty students has not experienced a productivity revolution merely because the projector is better. A seminar can become larger, but at some point the product changes. Research universities employ laboratories, compliance staffs, counselors, librarians, technicians, athletic departments, security, information systems, fundraisers, disability services, financial-aid offices, and administrators because a modern university is doing much more than placing a lecturer in front of a chalkboard.

Some of that expansion reflects services students and society genuinely value.

Some reflects competition for prestige.

Some reflects regulation and risk management.

Some reflects the same institutional instinct found everywhere: once a capability exists, eliminating it becomes more difficult than adding it was.

The phrase administrative bloat is satisfying because it imagines a building full of people doing nothing.

Reality is less convenient.

The staff member coordinating mental-health care is doing something. So is the Title IX office, the cybersecurity team, the grant administrator, the disability coordinator, the admissions officer, the compliance lawyer, the laboratory-safety specialist, and the employee handling veterans' benefits. One can argue about staffing levels or institutional design without pretending the work is fictional.

This is how spending accumulates in respectable organizations.

Each position has a reason.

Each rule has a history.

Each service can be defended independently.

The total does not ask permission from the reasons.

Government itself contains a version of the same problem.

A police officer cannot patrol five times as many dangerous scenes because computers became five times faster. A judge cannot hear an infinite number of cases. Inspectors, firefighters, air-traffic controllers, diplomats, social workers, soldiers, and teachers all perform work in which human judgment or presence is part of the output.

If wages elsewhere rise, government must compete for labor.

That raises the cost of providing the same apparent service.

The response can become politically perverse. A public agency facing higher labor costs may be accused of becoming less efficient even when it is buying the same number of labor hours at the new market wage. Another agency may actually be inefficient but defend itself with the same argument.

The observer needs a way to tell the difference.

This is where productivity measurement becomes more than an academic nuisance.

In manufacturing, output is often countable. A factory produces more units, fewer defects, or both. In health care, is output a visit, a procedure, a year of life, a reduction in pain, or a healthy person who never needed the procedure? In education, is output a course credit, a graduation, a test score, an earnings gain, a citizen, a researcher, or the student who learned enough to change fields ten years later?

Poor measures make expensive sectors easier to defend and easier to attack.

If a hospital is judged only by how many procedures it performs, productivity can look high when more people are treated even if many procedures were avoidable. If a university is judged only by graduation rate, it can improve the metric by admitting students who were likely to graduate anyway or lowering standards. If a government agency is judged only by cases closed, difficult cases become a threat to performance.

The problem is not merely that services are expensive.

It is that the output is difficult to price cleanly.

Into that ambiguity walks politics.

Industries that depend on public money become skilled at describing their preferred payment as an investment and an alternative payment as a cut. Industries that depend on tax preferences become skilled at describing those preferences as neutral features of the code. Professions facing competition often discover a public-safety vocabulary. Local incumbents facing new entry discover neighborhood character. Contractors facing procurement reform discover readiness, resilience, and jobs.

Sometimes those claims are true.

That is why they work.

A country that treats every organized constituency as a rent seeker will dismantle useful institutions. A country that treats every organized constituency as the guardian of a vital public interest will eventually discover that every dollar is vital.

The spending problem lives in the difference.

Consider licensing.

Licensing can protect patients and consumers from incompetent practitioners. Nobody wants a surgeon credentialed by a five-star rating from strangers on the internet. Some work has consequences serious enough to justify high barriers to entry.

The same barrier can also reduce competition beyond what safety requires.

Scope-of-practice rules can determine which professionals may perform which tasks. Certificate requirements can determine whether facilities expand. Accreditation rules can shape who enters an occupation. Each rule may have a defensible origin. Together they can limit the ability of supply to respond when demand grows.

A slow-productivity service with constrained supply becomes especially expensive.

Then government often subsidizes demand because the service has become unaffordable.

The subsidy is humane at the household level.

It may be inflationary at the system level if capacity does not change.

This is the recurring American sequence:

something important becomes expensive;

access becomes politically painful;

financing is expanded;

supply remains difficult;

the larger pool of money meets the same bottleneck.

The result is often blamed on greed because greed is a simpler explanation than institutional capacity.

Greed is real but poorly timed as a theory. Hospitals wanted revenue before prices rose. Universities liked money before tuition increased. Landowners preferred high values before zoning became restrictive. Contractors wanted profit before procurement became cumbersome. Human appetite is too constant to explain every change in price.

What changes are constraints, bargaining positions, payment systems, technologies, rules, and the amount of money available to bid.

Lobbying matters because it can help preserve those arrangements after the original conditions change.

A subsidy introduced when a market was competitive can become a transfer when supply later tightens. A licensing rule written for a dangerous procedure can remain after technology makes the procedure routine. A reimbursement formula can reward site of service rather than clinical value. A tax preference can persist long after its stated policy rationale stops fitting the world.

Institutional memory is asymmetrical.

Governments remember how to pay more readily than how to stop paying.

Organizations remember how to comply more readily than how to delete the requirement.

Beneficiaries remember a benefit as part of the baseline. Future payers do not organize around a cost that has not arrived yet.

This does not mean the answer is a recurring purge.

Constant institutional churn has costs too. Hospitals need predictable payment to finance capacity. Universities cannot rebuild programs every election. Public agencies cannot hire skilled people if every job is permanently provisional. Businesses invest when rules have some durability.

The goal is not instability.

It is contestability.

A spending system needs places where old assumptions can be challenged without requiring collapse.

Prices can provide that challenge when buyers see them and suppliers can enter.

Competition can provide it when customers can switch.

Audits can provide it when outputs are measurable.

Sunset reviews can provide it when programs are genuinely reconsidered rather than automatically renewed.

Procurement contests can provide it when contracts are written around outcomes rather than incumbents.

Professional rules can be reexamined when technology changes the risks.

Public payers can compare sites of service and refuse to pay radically different amounts when the clinical product is similar.

None of these mechanisms is painless.

That is the point.

The spending problem grows when every mechanism capable of creating disappointment is weakened at once.

The consumer does not see the price.

The insurer passes the cost into next year's premium.

The employer treats health benefits as a necessary labor cost.

The university spreads new expenses across tuition, aid, endowment spending, grants, and fees.

The city constrains housing and then subsidizes demand.

The federal government borrows.

The institution receiving the money can point to a real service delivered.

Everyone is correct at the level of the transaction.

The aggregate is still becoming more expensive.

Baumol's great insight was that a society can get richer and nevertheless feel as if certain things are becoming less affordable. Productivity gains elsewhere raise the opportunity cost of human time. We should expect medicine, care, education, culture, and government to absorb resources.

That is not failure.

The failure is to use cost disease as an all-purpose pardon for any price increase inside a protected sector.

A string quartet is supposed to take four musicians.

A billing department is not.

A teacher may need an hour with students.

A licensing rule may not need thirty years without review.

A nurse cannot safely care for infinite patients.

A hospital merger does not therefore deserve infinite pricing power.

Some costs rise because the service is human.

Others rise because the institution learned how to charge.

The job is to tell them apart before the bill becomes too large to question.
