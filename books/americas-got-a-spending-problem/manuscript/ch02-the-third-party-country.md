# The Third-Party Country

The American health-care system spent about $5.3 trillion in 2024.

That was roughly $15,474 for every person in the country and 18 percent of gross domestic product, according to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services.

The number is large enough to produce the usual political reflexes. One side sees proof that government programs and regulation have distorted medicine. Another sees proof that private insurers and profit-seeking providers have failed. Both can find evidence.

The more interesting fact is that very few patients experience health care as a normal purchase.

You do not usually walk into an emergency department, inspect a menu, compare the price of stabilizing your heart attack with the price three blocks away, and leave because the competitor has a better weekend special. Even when the care is predictable, the person receiving it often does not know the negotiated price. The physician may not know it either. The hospital's posted charge may bear little resemblance to what an insurer pays. The patient may be responsible for a deductible, a copay, coinsurance, nothing, or a number that cannot be known until several entities finish passing claims among themselves.

This is not an accidental flaw grafted onto an otherwise ordinary market.

It is partly the reason insurance exists.

Insurance deliberately separates the person who needs care from the full cost at the moment of need. That is the point. A twenty-year-old pays premiums despite not knowing whether she will become the patient with the six-figure bill. A healthy worker helps finance a colleague with cancer. Medicare collects taxes across working lives and then finances care in old age. Medicaid spreads the cost of care for eligible populations across state and federal taxpayers.

Risk pooling is one of civilization's better inventions.

It also breaks the simplest version of price discipline.

A customer paying the entire marginal cost of a restaurant meal can decide whether the second appetizer is worth twenty dollars. A patient whose insurer is paying most of the bill may have little reason to care whether two clinically equivalent services differ by hundreds or thousands. More important, the patient may be in no position to know the difference or negotiate it.

The provider understands the payment system instead.

That changes what competition means.

Hospitals compete for physicians, insured patients, network inclusion, favorable reimbursement, specialized service lines, geographic dominance, and bargaining power. Insurers compete for employers and members while negotiating rates with providers. Drug companies negotiate with pharmacy benefit managers and payers. Employers choose benefit designs partly to attract workers. Governments set reimbursement formulas, coverage rules, and eligibility. Patients appear in the center of this machine but are not always the buyer in the ordinary economic sense.

The result is not the absence of a market.

It is a market with many buyers and many prices layered on the same medical event.

That distinction matters because it offers a clue to a much broader American spending habit.

Across several of the country's most painful cost categories, the person making a decision is separated from the full marginal price.

College is different from medicine, but it contains a version of the same mechanism.

The published tuition and fees for an in-state student at a public four-year institution averaged $11,950 in the 2025-26 academic year, according to the College Board. Yet the average net tuition and fees paid by first-time full-time in-state students after grant aid was estimated much lower, around $2,300. That sounds like a triumph of financial aid, and in one sense it is. Grant aid can make education accessible to students who otherwise could not attend.

Then the rest of the bill arrives.

The College Board estimated an average total budget of about $30,990 for an in-state student at a public four-year college once housing, food, books, transportation, and other expenses were included.

The sticker price is not the price. The net tuition is not the price. The amount borrowed is not the price. The family's out-of-pocket payment this semester is not the price. The return on the degree is not known until years later.

Everyone is looking at a different number.

That does not mean student aid caused the cost of college. The evidence on how aid affects tuition is more complicated than the slogan that schools simply capture every subsidized dollar. State appropriations, labor costs, amenities, housing, enrollment, institutional competition, academic structure, and the composition of students all matter.

But aid changes the purchasing environment.

When government grants, subsidized loans, tax benefits, institutional aid, parent borrowing, and private credit all stand between a student and the full cost of attendance, the price signal becomes harder to interpret. The school can raise or lower one component while changing another. A generous aid package can make an expensive school cheaper for one student than a lower-sticker-price school. A student may rationally focus on the monthly loan payment or the aid award because those are the numbers that determine whether attendance is possible.

The system solves an access problem and creates an information problem at the same time.

Housing supplies a third version.

Housing assistance can help a family pay rent. Mortgage subsidies can make ownership more attractive. Down-payment assistance can help a first-time buyer clear a barrier that would otherwise keep her renting. Tax preferences can lower the effective cost of ownership. These policies can be valuable, especially when households face temporary constraints or discriminatory barriers.

But a house is not manufactured instantly when another buyer receives money.

In places where zoning, permitting delays, land scarcity, infrastructure limits, construction costs, neighborhood opposition, or other constraints prevent supply from expanding, demand support can spill into price.

The new subsidy does not disappear. It may become part of the bid.

This is why housing policy can feel so maddening. One program helps a household afford today's price while another part of the political system prevents the number of homes from responding to the added purchasing power. The household is grateful for the assistance. The seller sees more qualified buyers. Existing homeowners enjoy higher values. Builders may be unable to add enough units. The next household arrives to find the new price waiting.

By 2024, Census Bureau data showed more than 21 million renter households spending over 30 percent of income on housing costs. Among renters for whom the burden could be calculated, roughly half crossed that threshold. More than 10 million renter households were spending over half their income on housing.

A society can respond to those numbers with more money.

Sometimes it should.

A person facing eviction tonight cannot live inside a zoning reform that may produce apartments four years from now.

The short-term rescue and the long-term cure are different problems.

Confusing them is expensive.

This is the third-party country: a place where many of the most important goods are paid for through arrangements designed to protect people from the full immediate cost, while the supply of those goods is often constrained by institutions that react slowly.

The combination is combustible.

Third-party payment weakens the buyer's incentive or ability to discipline price. Supply constraints weaken the seller's incentive or ability to respond with more quantity. Political pressure then rises because the good has become unaffordable. The government adds purchasing power. The added purchasing power enters the same constrained system.

Nobody involved has to be irrational.

That is the uncomfortable part.

The patient wants treatment. The doctor wants the best outcome. The insurer wants predictable claims. The hospital wants revenue adequate to cover staff, capital, uncompensated care, and debt. The student wants a degree. The university wants faculty, facilities, research, support services, and financial aid. The renter wants a home. The homeowner wants the value of the home protected. The city wants tax revenue but may also respond to residents who oppose new construction. The politician wants to help constituents without provoking the people who would bear a visible cost.

Reasonable decisions can assemble an unreasonable price.

Economists sometimes talk about moral hazard when insurance changes behavior because the insured party does not bear the full cost of a decision. The phrase is useful but can sound accusatory in contexts where nobody is doing anything morally wrong.

A patient receiving an MRI because a physician recommends one is not cheating society. A homeowner defending the value of her largest asset is not a cartoon villain. A parent using every available aid program to send a child to college is doing what the system invites.

The better phrase may be price distance.

Price distance measures how far the person deciding is from the full cost of the decision.

The distance can be financial. An insurer pays.

It can be temporal. A student borrows and pays later.

It can be political. A future taxpayer finances a current benefit.

It can be geographic. A local government approves restrictions whose costs are partly borne by people who have not yet moved there.

It can be organizational. An employee chooses care while the employer negotiates the insurance contract.

It can be legal. A regulated reimbursement schedule replaces direct bargaining.

None of these arrangements is automatically bad.

Distance is often the mechanism that makes solidarity possible.

If every elderly person had to finance the full expected cost of late-life medical care from personal savings, many would fail. If every student had to prepay the full cost of education from family cash, opportunity would depend even more heavily on birth. If every infrastructure project had to be paid from one year's tax receipts, long-lived assets would be difficult to finance fairly.

The question is what replaces the lost price signal.

This is where American policy often becomes less serious.

We create the distance and then behave as if ordinary consumer discipline will still do the work.

Price transparency is announced as a cure for hospital costs even though a person with appendicitis is not a particularly strong shopper. Students receive pages of financial disclosures that may still fail to answer the only question that matters: What is this program likely to cost me in total, and what are the plausible outcomes? Homebuyers are shown annual percentage rates and total interest, then enter a market whose supply may have been constrained for decades by decisions dispersed across zoning boards and neighborhood hearings.

Disclosure helps.

It is not a substitute for institutional design.

If the person receiving a service cannot reasonably shop, somebody else must have both the incentive and the information to discipline the price.

That might be an insurer using reference pricing. It might be Medicare refusing to pay radically different amounts for clinically similar services delivered at different sites without a defensible reason. It might be an employer steering routine care toward lower-cost providers. It might be a regulator attacking anticompetitive consolidation. It might be a state university publishing program-level outcomes in a form students can understand. It might be a city legalizing more housing where prices signal scarcity.

The exact mechanism varies.

The principle does not.

When the buyer is insulated from the full price, the system needs another force capable of saying no.

That force is often missing because saying no is politically ugly.

Health insurers are hated when they deny care. Colleges are criticized when they reject applicants or cut programs. Local governments are attacked both for approving development and for blocking it. Federal agencies are attacked for paying too much and for refusing to pay enough. The institution assigned to control spending becomes the institution that must deliver the visible disappointment.

So the system searches for a painless controller.

There usually isn't one.

A price is a mechanism for creating disappointment before resources are exhausted. It tells a buyer that the second thing is not worth what it would cost. A budget tells a government that one priority crowds out another. An insurer's reimbursement rule tells a provider that some amount will not be paid. A zoning code tells a builder that land cannot be used in a certain way, although in that case the disappointment may fall on the future resident rather than the current property owner.

Every society decides where to locate these noes.

America has a tendency to hide them until late.

We insure the cost, finance the cost, subsidize the cost, deduct the cost, cap the immediate payment, or assign the cost to a future budget. Then we are surprised that the underlying price remains stubborn.

This can make spending control look cruel because the system waits until the patient, student, renter, homeowner, retiree, or taxpayer is personally exposed.

The humane alternative is not to abolish insurance or aid.

It is to attack the price earlier in the chain.

Build the housing before scarcity becomes an emergency. Make medical markets more contestable before premiums absorb the increase. Force schools and lenders to expose total cost and outcomes before the student signs. Design benefits with financing that arrives alongside the promise instead of decades afterward. Preserve competition where competition can work and use disciplined public purchasing where it cannot.

The point is not that every problem is a spending problem.

It is that many American spending problems are really systems problems wearing dollar signs.

The money is where the failure becomes visible.

The failure often began earlier, when the person deciding stopped seeing the full price and nobody else was given a credible reason to care.
