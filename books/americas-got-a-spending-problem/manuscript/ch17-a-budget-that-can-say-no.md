# A Budget That Can Say No

A budget that never says no is not a budget.

It is a record of enthusiasm.

The distinction matters because the United States already has an extraordinary ability to measure public money. Agencies estimate, score, audit, project, certify, report, reconcile, apportion, obligate, transfer, and account for trillions of dollars. Congress receives cost estimates. Treasury publishes statements. Inspectors general investigate. CBO models ten-year windows and longer horizons. Trustees estimate the future finances of Social Security and Medicare. GAO produces enough warnings about the long-term fiscal path to furnish a small library.

The country does not lack arithmetic.

It lacks a durable way to make arithmetic interrupt desire.

That is a different institutional problem.

A family can make a spreadsheet showing that it spends more than it earns and still keep spending. A company can produce an immaculate forecast of a cash shortage and still avoid closing the unprofitable division. A city can know that a pension promise is underfunded and keep postponing contributions. A government can publish a projection of rising debt every year and then pass legislation that makes the projection worse.

Information is necessary.

Information is not a veto.

The previous chapters have described many ways prices lose their disciplinary force. Insurance separates the patient from the full medical bill. Mortgages turn a purchase price into a monthly payment. Student aid separates sticker price from net price from borrowing. Tax expenditures turn spending-like policy into foregone revenue. Federal borrowing separates today's benefit from tomorrow's taxpayer. Entitlement formulas turn annual appropriations into durable promises. Long military programs turn one procurement decision into decades of lifecycle cost. States can balance the current budget while leaving pension obligations or deferred maintenance outside the year's cash frame.

In each case, distance is created for a reason.

Insurance pools risk.

Credit moves purchasing power through time.

Tax preferences can pursue public goals without a new agency.

Social insurance creates stability.

Defense procurement builds capability that cannot be bought overnight.

Public borrowing can absorb war, recession, disaster, or investment before current revenues could finance them.

The mistake is not distance itself.

The mistake is removing the price signal and failing to install another institution capable of saying no.

That institution does not have to be one office.

It probably should not be.

The phrase fiscal rule invites fantasies of a red line painted across the Constitution: debt may never exceed this number, the budget must always balance, spending may never grow faster than that formula. The appeal is obvious. If politicians cannot discipline themselves, bind them.

The difficulty is that a national budget is not a thermostat.

A rule strict enough to prevent every ordinary excess can become destructive in an emergency. A balanced-budget requirement imposed during a severe recession can force tax increases or spending cuts precisely when private demand is collapsing. A debt ceiling can create a showdown over obligations already enacted rather than disciplining the decisions that created them. An expenditure cap can protect fiscal totals while encouraging lawmakers to hide policy in the tax code. A deficit target can be met by delaying payments, shifting costs outside the window, selling assets, or adopting economic assumptions that make the target look easier.

Rules do not abolish politics.

Politics learns the rules.

That is why the best fiscal institutions resemble engineered systems with several layers rather than a single tripwire.

One layer measures.

Another forces explanation.

Another creates friction.

Another defines what happens after a breach.

Another makes room for genuine emergencies.

And somewhere in the arrangement, somebody must be able to say: this proposal is desirable, popular, compassionate, strategically useful, or profitable for a constituency, but it is not financed under the rules we have agreed to use.

That sentence is what the United States is missing.

It is also why fiscal reform cannot begin with one numerical target.

The target is the easy part.

The hard part is deciding which decisions must feel the target before the money is committed.

Consider a new permanent tax credit.

If Congress creates it through the tax code, the policy can reduce revenue for years without appearing beside ordinary appropriations. A useful budget system would not pretend that the legal form changes the economic question. It would ask what the preference costs against a stated baseline, how long it lasts, who benefits, how it interacts with other provisions, and what happens when the scheduled expiration arrives.

Consider a new entitlement benefit.

The first-year cost may be small because eligibility phases in slowly. The tenth-year cost may be larger. The thirtieth-year cost may be the real policy. A useful budget system would display the long tail beside the near-term score rather than letting the official window become the horizon of political imagination.

Consider a weapons program.

The appropriation buys a piece of a platform. The country is also buying training, maintenance, basing, software updates, spare parts, munitions, crews, industrial capacity, and eventual replacement. The honest budget question is not simply whether this year's procurement fits. It is what future choices the platform is likely to consume.

Consider emergency borrowing.

A recession, pandemic, war, financial crisis, or natural disaster is exactly when a government may need to spend more while collecting less. A fiscal constitution that cannot distinguish those moments from ordinary politics is not disciplined. It is brittle.

The design challenge is therefore to make normal times more binding so exceptional times can remain flexible.

This is not a novel idea internationally. Fiscal rules around the world have evolved toward combinations of debt rules, balance rules, expenditure rules, independent monitoring, escape clauses, and correction mechanisms. The details vary widely, and copying another country's rule into the United States would be lazy institutional tourism. But the direction of travel is instructive. Rules that merely announce a target are weaker than rules that specify who measures compliance, what qualifies as an emergency, what happens after the emergency, and how deviations are corrected.

A rule without a return path is an aspiration.

An escape clause without an exit is a loophole.

A target without an independent scorekeeper is a negotiation over arithmetic.

A scorekeeper without consequences is a weather forecaster.

The United States already possesses much of the measurement architecture.

CBO is not perfect and cannot make uncertainty disappear. Its projections depend on assumptions about growth, inflation, interest rates, demographics, behavior, and law. But that imperfection is not an argument for letting every political coalition invent its own arithmetic. A common baseline is valuable precisely because it gives disagreement a shared starting point.

The same principle applies to the Social Security and Medicare trustees, GAO, the Joint Committee on Taxation, agency inspectors general, and the accountants who produce federal financial statements.

Independent measurement is not anti-democratic.

It is what allows democratic conflict to occur over choices rather than over whether numbers exist.

The next step is to connect measurement to friction.

Friction is an underrated public good.

In ordinary life, we often design systems to remove it. One-click purchasing, automatic renewal, instant credit, saved cards, seamless checkout, background billing, and buy-now-pay-later all make transactions easier. The reduction in friction can be productive. It can also increase the number of decisions made before the full price is considered.

Government has its own versions of one-click spending.

An expiring tax preference is extended because expiration would raise taxes on somebody. A temporary benefit becomes permanent because ending it would be called a cut. A program that was justified by one emergency acquires a successor mission. A pilot becomes an office. An office becomes a constituency. A procurement line survives because canceling it produces a visible loss while continuation produces an invisible opportunity cost.

The budget process needs carefully placed speed bumps.

Not paralysis.

Friction.

Permanent policies should generally have to explain permanent financing.

Temporary policies should not become permanent merely through repeated extensions that never confront the long-run cost.

Major tax expenditures should be reviewed as deliberately as direct spending programs.

Large procurement decisions should carry lifecycle estimates and explicit uncertainty ranges.

Long-lived benefit changes should show the effect beyond the first convenient budget window.

Emergency borrowing should identify the condition that justified the exception and the process for returning to ordinary rules.

These mechanisms do not tell Congress what to choose.

They make choosing harder to disguise.

A useful fiscal rule should also distinguish stocks from flows.

Debt is a stock.

The deficit is a flow.

Interest is the price paid on the stock and on new borrowing.

A country can have a high debt level and a manageable near-term deficit. It can have a lower debt level and a rapidly deteriorating flow. It can run a temporary large deficit for an emergency and then return to a sustainable path. It can run modest but persistent deficits in ordinary times until the accumulated stock makes interest a larger and larger claimant on revenue.

A single number therefore cannot carry the whole discipline.

The more useful question is whether debt is expected to stabilize relative to the economy under plausible assumptions once the temporary circumstances have passed.

That sentence contains several uncertainties.

It is still better than pretending uncertainty excuses inaction.

The federal government does not need to know the exact debt-to-GDP ratio in 2056 to know that a policy path in which debt persistently grows faster than the economy deserves attention. It does not need to know the exact future interest rate to recognize that larger debt makes the budget more sensitive to rate changes. It does not need to forecast the next recession to know that entering one with more fiscal capacity is valuable.

Sustainability is not a number carved in granite.

It is a margin.

A country with deep capital markets, its own currency, a productive economy, strong institutions, and high investor demand for its debt can carry obligations that would overwhelm a smaller or less trusted borrower. The United States has unusually large fiscal capacity.

That capacity is an asset.

The point of discipline is to preserve it.

A government that borrows heavily in ordinary times because markets currently allow it is using part of the insurance value of its balance sheet before the emergency arrives.

This is why a good rule should be asymmetric across the cycle.

When the economy is weak, automatic stabilizers should be allowed to work. Unemployment insurance rises. Tax receipts fall. Emergency support may be justified. Public investment may be easier to finance and more valuable when private activity is depressed.

When the economy is strong, the standard should tighten.

That is when deficits should be easier to reduce, reserves easier to build, and temporary emergency programs easier to close. A country that treats every good year as proof that it can afford another permanent promise loses the opportunity to repair its balance sheet without crisis.

The politics work in the opposite direction.

Good times make restraint feel unnecessary.

Bad times make restraint dangerous.

The result is a ratchet.

That is why the institution has to remember what politics prefers to forget.

The same logic suggests that fiscal targets should be stated over medium horizons, not merely one year at a time. One-year balance can be achieved by delaying maintenance, shifting payments, accelerating receipts, or cutting useful investment. A medium-term framework makes some of those games less attractive because tomorrow remains inside the picture.

It also allows reforms to phase in.

If the budget must improve by a credible amount over several years, policymakers can spread changes across taxes, spending growth, eligibility rules, procurement, tax expenditures, and other commitments. They can protect people close to retirement. They can give businesses time to adapt to changes in the tax code. They can let agencies redesign programs rather than simply firing people at the end of a fiscal year.

A gradual rule can be more serious than a dramatic one.

The drama is concentrated.

The arithmetic is cumulative.

Correction mechanisms matter for the same reason.

Suppose a fiscal target is missed because growth is weaker than forecast.

That is different from missing it because Congress enacted new permanent spending without financing.

Suppose a war triggers an escape clause.

That is different from declaring every politically inconvenient year an emergency.

Suppose interest rates rise unexpectedly.

That is different from designing a budget that only works if rates remain unusually low forever.

A system capable of judgment should distinguish causes.

It should also require a path back.

The restoration plan may be contingent. It may take years. It may be revised when evidence changes. But the escape cannot simply become the new baseline by inertia.

This is one place where public explanation matters as much as legal enforcement.

A fiscal council, CBO, GAO, or other independent institution cannot force Congress to act. Nor should unelected analysts decide the distribution of taxes and benefits. But they can make evasion expensive in a different currency: credibility.

They can publish whether a proposal improves or worsens the agreed fiscal path.

They can show the effect under alternative assumptions.

They can identify temporary provisions that recur so predictably they should no longer be treated as temporary.

They can expose when a policy fits inside a ten-year window only because the expensive part begins in year eleven.

They can make the long-run financing of popular promises visible before the constituency for those promises becomes politically immovable.

The goal is not government by accountant.

The goal is government unable to fire the accountant every time the numbers are annoying.

There is another design principle the country should borrow from engineering: reversibility.

Some fiscal decisions are easier to reverse than others.

A temporary rebate can end.

A new permanent benefit creates expectations.

A short emergency loan program can close.

A major weapons platform creates supply chains, facilities, training systems, and regional jobs.

A one-year grant can expire.

A new tax exclusion can reorganize compensation, investment, and lobbying around itself.

A useful budget process should require more scrutiny before creating commitments that become expensive to reverse.

This is not because permanent commitments are bad.

Social Security is valuable partly because people can plan around it. Defense readiness depends on durable industrial capacity. Infrastructure can take decades to build and serve generations. Research institutions need continuity. A tax system that changes every six months is not neutral; it destroys planning.

Durability can be the point.

But if durability is the point, durability belongs in the price.

The budget should ask how much flexibility a decision consumes.

That may be a more useful concept than cost alone.

Two programs with the same expected ten-year cost can have different fiscal consequences if one can be scaled down easily while the other creates a legal, political, or industrial commitment that persists for decades. The second policy spends more than money.

It spends future discretion.

That discretion is scarce.

Interest spends it automatically.

Demography spends it slowly.

Emergencies can consume it suddenly.

The purpose of a budget that can say no is to keep enough discretion unspent that future citizens still have a meaningful range of choices.

This is also why fiscal rules should protect useful investment from crude austerity without allowing every favored program to rename itself investment.

A bridge that reduces transportation costs for fifty years has a different intergenerational profile from a recurring subsidy that must be financed every year to preserve the same level of consumption. Research can create knowledge spillovers. Grid upgrades can increase productive capacity. Preventive maintenance can avoid larger replacement costs. Some public borrowing creates assets, resilience, or future revenue capacity.

The label does not decide the case.

The evidence does.

Investment should have to survive an investment test: does the spending plausibly create durable capacity or reduce future costs, and can that claim be evaluated later?

That last clause matters.

Every budget gimmick begins as a story about why this item is special.

The institution must be able to return and ask whether the story came true.

This book has argued for a similar discipline in health care, housing, education, and household finance. When the person making a decision does not face the full price, somebody else must have both the information and the incentive to care about it. The same principle belongs in the federal budget.

Congress will continue to choose expensive things.

It should.

A rich country should be capable of defending itself, supporting older citizens, insuring health risks, funding science, maintaining infrastructure, responding to disaster, and making investments whose benefits arrive decades later.

A budget that says no to everything is not disciplined.

It is useless.

The point is to reserve no for the moment when yes has ceased to include its price.

That means no to the permanent program with temporary financing.

No to the tax cut whose growth effects are treated as guaranteed before they exist.

No to the emergency measure that no longer has an emergency.

No to the defense program whose sunk costs have become the main argument for future costs.

No to the tax preference that survives because nobody calls it spending.

No to the benefit expansion whose financing is assigned to a future Congress.

No to the claim that eliminating fraud will solve a structural deficit.

No to the claim that one narrow tax base can finance every broad social promise.

No to the claim that growth makes arithmetic unnecessary.

And sometimes no to fiscal restraint itself, when recession, war, or catastrophe makes borrowing the least damaging choice available.

A serious budget can say that too.

The ability to say no is not the same as saying no often.

It is the ability to make yes complete its sentence.

Yes, and here is who pays.

Yes, and here is how long the commitment lasts.

Yes, and here is what assumption would make the plan fail.

Yes, and here is what happens if the emergency ends.

Yes, and here is what other future choice becomes harder because we made this one.

That is the institutional meaning of an honest bill.

The United States does not need a fiscal machine that chooses for voters.

It needs one that makes voters, lawmakers, agencies, and beneficiaries encounter the tradeoff before the tradeoff becomes a crisis.

A budget should not prevent a democracy from getting what it wants.

It should make the democracy decide whether it wants the price too.
