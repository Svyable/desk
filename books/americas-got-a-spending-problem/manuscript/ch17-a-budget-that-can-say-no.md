# A Budget That Can Say No

A budget that never says no is not a budget.

It is a record of enthusiasm.

The distinction matters because the United States already has an extraordinary ability to measure public money. Agencies estimate, score, audit, project, certify, report, reconcile, apportion, obligate, transfer, and account for trillions of dollars. Congress receives cost estimates. Treasury publishes statements. Inspectors general investigate. CBO models ten-year windows and longer horizons. The Social Security and Medicare trustees publish actuarial projections. GAO has warned about the long-term fiscal path often enough to furnish a small library.

The country does not lack arithmetic.

It lacks a durable way to make arithmetic interrupt desire.

That is a different institutional problem.

A family can build a perfect spreadsheet showing that it spends more than it earns and still keep spending. A company can forecast a cash shortage and delay closing the unprofitable division. A city can know that a pension promise is underfunded and postpone the contribution. A government can publish a worsening debt path and then enact legislation that makes the path worse.

Information is necessary.

Information is not a veto.

The previous chapters described several ways prices lose their disciplinary force. Insurance separates the patient from the full medical bill. Credit turns a purchase price into a payment. Tax expenditures move public policy into foregone revenue. Benefit formulas turn present legislation into future payments. Long military programs turn one procurement decision into decades of operating cost. States can balance the cash budget while maintenance or pension obligations accumulate elsewhere.

In each case, the distance exists for a reason. Insurance pools risk. Credit moves purchasing power through time. Social insurance creates stability. Defense procurement builds capability that cannot be bought overnight. Public borrowing can absorb war, recession, disaster, or investment before current revenue could finance them.

The mistake is not distance itself.

The mistake is removing the price signal and failing to install another institution capable of saying no.

That institution does not have to be one office. It probably should not be.

The phrase fiscal rule invites fantasies of a red line painted across the Constitution: debt may never exceed this number, the budget must always balance, spending may never grow faster than that formula. The appeal is obvious. If politicians cannot discipline themselves, bind them.

The difficulty is that a national budget is not a thermostat.

A rule strict enough to prevent every ordinary excess can become destructive in an emergency. A balanced-budget requirement imposed during a severe recession can force tax increases or spending cuts while private demand is already collapsing. A debt ceiling can create a showdown over obligations already enacted rather than disciplining the choices that created them. A deficit target can encourage delayed payments, asset sales, optimistic assumptions, or costs shifted just beyond the budget window. An expenditure cap can protect the headline while policy migrates into the tax code.

Rules do not abolish politics.

Politics learns the rules.

The useful design therefore looks less like one tripwire and more like an engineered system with several layers. One layer measures. Another forces explanation. Another creates friction before durable commitments are made. Another defines what happens after a breach. Another makes room for genuine emergencies. The arrangement must be strong enough to impose a cost on ordinary evasion without becoming so rigid that a war, recession, pandemic, or financial crisis turns discipline into self-harm.

Somewhere in the system, somebody must be able to say that a proposal is desirable, compassionate, strategically useful, or popular and still not financed under the rules the country has agreed to use.

That sentence is what American fiscal politics has difficulty completing.

The target itself is usually the easy part.

The harder question is which decisions must feel the target before the money is committed.

Consider a permanent tax credit. If Congress creates it through the tax code, the policy can reduce revenue for years without appearing beside ordinary appropriations. A serious budget process would ask what the preference costs against a stated baseline, who benefits, how long it lasts, how it interacts with other provisions, and what happens when the statutory expiration arrives.

Consider a new entitlement. The first-year cost may be small because eligibility phases in slowly. The thirtieth-year cost may be the real policy. The long tail belongs beside the near-term score, even though the estimate becomes less precise with time.

Consider a weapons system. This year's appropriation buys only part of the commitment. The country is also buying crews, maintenance, basing, software, training, munitions, supplier capacity, upgrades, and eventual replacement. A program that fits inside one fiscal year can still consume a large share of budgets not yet written.

Then consider emergency borrowing. A recession, war, financial crisis, pandemic, or natural disaster is precisely when a government may need to spend more while collecting less. A fiscal constitution that cannot distinguish those moments from ordinary politics is not disciplined. It is brittle.

The design problem is to make normal times more binding so exceptional times can remain flexible.

Other countries have experimented with combinations of debt rules, balance rules, expenditure rules, independent monitoring, escape clauses, and correction mechanisms. Copying one foreign arrangement into the United States would be lazy institutional tourism. The useful lesson is more modest: a target is stronger when the system specifies who measures compliance, what qualifies as an exception, what happens when the exception ends, and how a deviation is corrected.

An escape clause without a return path becomes a loophole.

A target without a common scorekeeper becomes a negotiation over arithmetic.

A scorekeeper without consequences becomes a weather service.

The United States already possesses much of the measurement architecture.

CBO is not perfect and cannot make uncertainty disappear. Its projections depend on assumptions about growth, inflation, interest rates, demographics, behavior, and law. The trustees face similar uncertainty. So do JCT, GAO, inspectors general, and the accountants who produce federal statements.

That imperfection is not an argument for letting every coalition invent its own numbers.

A common baseline is valuable because disagreement can begin from the same page.

Independent measurement is not anti-democratic. It keeps democratic conflict focused on choices rather than on whether arithmetic exists.

The next step is to connect measurement to friction.

Friction is an underrated public good.

In ordinary commerce, removing friction makes many transactions better. One-click purchasing, automatic renewal, saved cards, instant credit, background billing, and installment plans reduce hassle. They also make it easier to say yes before the full price occupies much attention.

Government has its own versions of one-click spending. An expiring tax preference is extended because expiration would be called a tax increase. A temporary benefit becomes permanent because ending it would be called a cut. A program created for one emergency acquires a successor mission. A procurement line survives because cancellation produces an obvious local loss while continuation produces an invisible opportunity cost.

The budget process needs carefully placed speed bumps.

Permanent policies should generally have to explain permanent financing. Temporary policies should not become permanent through serial extensions that never confront the long-run cost. Major tax expenditures should face periodic comparison with direct spending alternatives. Large procurements should show lifecycle costs and uncertainty rather than only the next appropriation. Long-lived benefit changes should display consequences beyond the first convenient window. Emergency borrowing should identify both the condition that justified the exception and the route back to ordinary rules.

These mechanisms do not tell Congress what to choose.

They make choosing harder to disguise.

A useful fiscal framework also has to distinguish stocks from flows.

Debt is a stock. The deficit is a flow. Interest is the carrying price of the stock and of new borrowing.

A country can have a high debt level and a manageable near-term deficit. It can have a lower debt level and a rapidly worsening flow. It can run a large temporary deficit during an emergency and then return toward stability. It can also run modest but persistent deficits in ordinary times until the accumulated stock makes interest a larger claimant on revenue.

One number cannot carry the whole discipline.

The more useful question is whether the debt path is expected to stabilize relative to the economy under plausible assumptions after temporary conditions pass.

That sentence contains uncertainty. It is still better than pretending uncertainty excuses indifference.

The federal government does not need to know the exact debt-to-GDP ratio in 2056 to recognize that debt growing persistently faster than the economy deserves attention. It does not need the exact future interest rate to know that a larger debt stock makes the budget more sensitive to rates. It does not need to forecast the next recession to understand that entering one with more fiscal room is valuable.

Sustainability is not a number carved in granite.

It is a margin.

A country with deep capital markets, a productive economy, its own currency, strong institutions, and unusually high demand for its debt can carry obligations that would overwhelm a smaller or less trusted borrower. The United States has extraordinary fiscal capacity.

That capacity is an asset.

The point of discipline is to preserve it.

Borrowing heavily in ordinary years because markets currently permit it uses part of the insurance value of the national balance sheet before the emergency arrives.

That is why good fiscal discipline should be asymmetric across the cycle.

When the economy is weak, automatic stabilizers should be allowed to work. Tax receipts fall. Unemployment support rises. Emergency aid may be justified. Public investment may be easier to finance and especially useful when private demand is depressed.

When the economy is strong, the standard should tighten. That is when deficits are easier to reduce, temporary programs easier to close, and reserves easier to rebuild. A country that treats every good year as proof that it can afford another permanent promise loses the least painful opportunity to repair its balance sheet.

Politics tends to prefer the opposite schedule.

Good times make restraint feel unnecessary. Bad times make restraint dangerous.

Without an institution that remembers across the cycle, the result is a ratchet.

The same logic favors medium-term targets over annual theater. One-year balance can be manufactured by delaying maintenance, shifting payments, accelerating receipts, or cutting useful investment. A multiyear framework keeps more of tomorrow inside the picture.

It also creates room for humane adjustment.

If the budget must improve credibly over several years, policymakers can phase changes across taxes, spending growth, eligibility rules, procurement, and tax expenditures. Workers near retirement can be protected from abrupt rule changes. Businesses can receive time to adapt to changes in the tax code. Agencies can redesign programs instead of simply firing whichever employees happen to leave first.

A gradual rule can be more serious than a dramatic one because the arithmetic is cumulative even when the pain is not concentrated in one year.

Correction mechanisms matter for the same reason.

Missing a target because growth was weaker than forecast is different from missing it because Congress enacted a new permanent commitment without financing. A war that activates an escape clause is different from declaring every inconvenient year an emergency. A surprise rise in interest rates is different from designing a budget that only works if unusually low rates last forever.

A competent system should distinguish the cause and still require a path back.

The restoration plan may take years. It may be contingent. It may change as evidence changes. What it cannot do is let the exception become the new baseline through inertia.

Public explanation matters here as much as legal enforcement.

An independent institution cannot force Congress to choose a particular tax or benefit. Nor should unelected analysts decide distributional questions that belong to voters. They can make evasion expensive in a different currency: credibility.

They can show whether a proposal improves or worsens an agreed fiscal path. They can publish alternative assumptions. They can identify provisions that expire in law but recur so predictably that the temporary label has become implausible. They can expose a policy that looks cheap only because its expensive years begin after the scorekeeping window.

The goal is not government by accountant.

The goal is government unable to fire the accountant whenever the numbers become annoying.

There is another useful design principle: reversibility.

A temporary rebate can end. A permanent benefit creates expectations. A short emergency loan program can close. A weapons platform creates supply chains, facilities, training systems, and regional jobs. A one-year grant can expire. A tax exclusion can reorganize compensation, investment, and lobbying around itself.

A budget process should require more scrutiny before creating commitments that become expensive to reverse.

That does not mean permanence is bad. Social Security is valuable partly because people can plan around it. Defense readiness depends on durable industrial capacity. Infrastructure can serve generations. Research institutions need continuity. A tax code that changes every six months would impose costs of its own.

Durability can be the point.

If durability is the point, durability belongs in the price.

Two policies with the same expected ten-year cost can have very different consequences if one can be scaled down easily while the other creates a legal, political, or industrial commitment that persists for decades. The second spends more than money.

It spends future discretion.

Interest consumes that discretion automatically. Demography consumes it slowly. Emergencies can consume it suddenly.

The purpose of a budget that can say no is to keep enough discretion unspent that future citizens still have a meaningful range of choices.

This is also why fiscal rules should protect useful investment from crude austerity without allowing every favored program to rename itself investment.

A bridge that reduces transportation costs for fifty years has a different intergenerational profile from a recurring subsidy that must be financed every year to preserve the same level of consumption. Research can create knowledge spillovers. Grid upgrades can increase capacity. Preventive maintenance can avoid larger replacement costs. Some public borrowing leaves future taxpayers with an asset as well as a liability.

The label does not decide the case.

The evidence does.

An investment claim should be testable enough that somebody can return later and ask whether the promised capacity or savings appeared. Every budget gimmick begins as a story about why one item is special. The institution has to remember to ask whether the story came true.

Congress will continue to choose expensive things.

It should.

A rich country should be capable of defending itself, supporting older citizens, insuring catastrophic risks, funding science, maintaining infrastructure, responding to disaster, and making investments whose benefits arrive decades later. A budget that says no to everything is not disciplined. It is useless.

The important no arrives when a yes has stopped including its price: a permanent program financed temporarily, a tax preference that survives because nobody calls it spending, a benefit expansion assigned to a future Congress, a procurement whose sunk cost has become the main argument for more cost, or a fiscal plan that depends on growth not yet earned. The same discipline applies in reverse when recession, war, or catastrophe makes temporary borrowing less damaging than immediate restraint.

The ability to say no is not the same as saying no often.

It is the ability to make yes complete its sentence.

A complete yes identifies the payer, the likely duration, the assumptions that could make the plan fail, the conditions under which an exception ends, and the future choices made harder by the commitment.

That is the institutional meaning of an honest bill.

The United States does not need a fiscal machine that chooses for voters.

It needs one that makes voters, lawmakers, agencies, and beneficiaries encounter the tradeoff before the tradeoff becomes a crisis.

A budget should not prevent a democracy from getting what it wants.

It should make the democracy decide whether it wants the price too.
