# The Tax Expenditure Shadow Budget

In the federal budget, spending is supposed to look like money leaving the Treasury.

The tax code has another method.

Sometimes the government pursues a policy by not collecting money it otherwise would have collected under a specified tax baseline.

The result is called a tax expenditure.

The phrase is almost engineered to start an argument.

One person hears it and says, You cannot call my deduction government spending. The money was mine.

Another hears it and says, Of course it is spending. Congress used the tax code to subsidize a favored activity.

Both reactions point at something real.

A tax expenditure is not literally the same administrative act as a direct outlay.

No clerk necessarily sends the taxpayer a check.

The estimate depends on a baseline tax system, and reasonable analysts can disagree about what belongs in that baseline. Repealing a provision does not necessarily raise revenue equal to the published tax-expenditure estimate because behavior changes, other tax provisions interact, and the economy adapts.

The number is not cash waiting in a drawer.

It is still policy.

That is the part the vocabulary fight can obscure.

The government can encourage employer health insurance by excluding the benefit from taxable compensation.

It can encourage retirement saving by deferring or excluding tax on certain contributions and earnings.

It can encourage homeownership through deductions, exclusions, and favorable treatment of owner-occupied housing.

It can encourage charitable giving through deductions.

It can encourage state and local borrowing by excluding interest on qualifying municipal bonds from federal income tax.

It can encourage investment with accelerated depreciation, credits, special rates, and deductions.

The code becomes a spending program written in negative numbers.

The largest items are not obscure loopholes hiding behind a shell company in the Caribbean.

They are woven into ordinary American life.

The fiscal year 2027 Analytical Perspectives ranked the exclusion of employer contributions for medical insurance premiums and medical care as the largest income-tax expenditure under its methodology. The projected 2026 income-tax revenue effect was about $296 billion, with roughly $3.8 trillion of projected effect over 2026 through 2035.

That provision helps explain why American health insurance became so strongly tied to employment.

It is not merely a tax break attached to a health system that would otherwise look the same.

The tax preference helped shape the system itself.

Defined-contribution employer retirement plans were another enormous tax expenditure, with a projected 2026 income-tax effect above $150 billion in the same table. So was favorable capital-gains treatment. So was the exclusion of imputed rental income from owner-occupied housing under the normal-tax baseline. The capital-gains exclusion on home sales was projected to cost more than $80 billion of income-tax revenue in 2026 under that methodology.

The mortgage-interest deduction, famous in political debate, was much smaller than several of the preferences surrounding health, pensions, and housing that receive less public attention.

This is the shadow budget.

It is not hidden because the provisions are secret.

They are statutes.

Tables exist.

Economists estimate them every year.

The shadow comes from political perception.

A $10 billion appropriated housing program appears on the spending side of the federal ledger and is immediately described as government spending.

A $10 billion housing preference delivered through the tax code appears as lower revenue relative to a baseline and may be described as tax relief.

The fiscal effect can be similar while the political identity is opposite.

This creates one of the most durable asymmetries in American budgeting.

Politicians who dislike spending can support tax expenditures without describing themselves as spenders.

Politicians who like active government can use tax expenditures to deliver subsidies through the tax code rather than create a visible agency program.

The policy crosses party lines because the disguise is useful to everyone.

A tax credit can be sold as letting people keep more of their own money.

A deduction can be sold as fairness.

An exclusion can become so familiar that taxing the underlying income begins to sound like a new tax rather than repeal of an old preference.

The baseline becomes political memory.

This is why tax expenditures are unusually sticky.

A direct spending program usually has a name, an appropriation, an agency, and a line that can be attacked.

A tax expenditure may be embedded in millions of private transactions.

The beneficiary can stop experiencing it as a benefit at all.

An employee with employer-sponsored health insurance does not usually receive a statement saying: the federal government has chosen not to tax this portion of your compensation, and that choice has fiscal value.

The worker experiences health insurance.

A homeowner does not experience the exclusion of imputed rent as a monthly subsidy.

There is no check.

Nothing arrives.

The value is built into the tax treatment of ownership.

A retirement saver may understand the tax advantage of a 401(k), but the benefit feels like part of the account rather than a federal expenditure competing with other priorities.

The subsidy is real enough to shape behavior and invisible enough to escape ordinary spending politics.

This is price distance by omission.

The government does not pay the price directly.

It reduces the tax price of a favored action and lets the private system carry the policy forward.

That can be an elegant way to govern.

A tax expenditure can use existing private decisions instead of creating a bureaucracy to make them. It can preserve choice. It can be administratively efficient. It can encourage socially valuable behavior that produces benefits beyond the person receiving the tax preference.

The existence of a tax expenditure is not evidence that the policy is bad.

The problem is that the form can protect weak policies from comparison.

Consider employer-sponsored health insurance.

There are serious reasons to value the exclusion.

It makes coverage cheaper on an after-tax basis. It supports risk pooling through workplaces. It avoids taxing an in-kind benefit that many families regard as part of basic compensation. Abrupt repeal could disrupt coverage and impose large tax increases on households receiving employer benefits.

The same exclusion can encourage compensation to flow into more generous health benefits rather than taxable wages because the health benefit receives favored tax treatment.

It can reinforce job-based insurance even when labor markets become more mobile. It can provide larger dollar benefits to workers in higher tax brackets or with more expensive employer coverage. It can subsidize health spending without directly disciplining underlying medical prices.

A policy can be valuable and distortionary at the same time.

The tax code makes that duality easy to ignore because the spending never appears as a program administrator writing a check to an insurance company.

Retirement preferences tell a similar story.

Encouraging long-term saving can reduce old-age poverty, increase household security, and deepen capital markets. Tax deferral also tends to be more valuable to people who have money available to save and face higher marginal tax rates, though distribution depends on plan design, employer matching, refundable credits, participation, and future tax rates.

The right question is not, Is retirement saving good?

It is, What public objective are we trying to buy, and is this tax treatment the best way to buy it?

That is the question direct spending programs are forced to confront more often.

A housing grant must defend its beneficiaries and its cost.

The tax code can make a housing preference feel like the natural state of affairs.

Owner-occupied housing may be the most complete example of a tax expenditure becoming a culture.

The American tax system has historically treated housing differently from many other assets and forms of consumption. Mortgage interest has received favorable treatment under certain conditions. Gains on qualifying home sales can be excluded up to statutory limits. Owner-occupiers do not include an imputed rental value in taxable income even though economists can describe the homeowner as simultaneously landlord and tenant.

The last item is especially useful because it reveals how much judgment is hidden inside the phrase tax expenditure.

Most homeowners would find the idea of taxing themselves on the rent they hypothetically pay themselves bizarre.

Under a comprehensive income-tax concept, however, the return from living in a house one owns can be treated as economic income in a way that rent paid to a landlord cannot.

Whether that should be part of the practical tax base is a policy question.

The estimate is therefore not a pile of easy revenue waiting to be collected.

It is a measurement of departure from a defined baseline.

This is the caveat every serious use of tax-expenditure numbers needs.

The shadow budget is not a secret slush fund equal to the sum of all the estimates.

Treasury explicitly warns that individual estimates cannot simply be added to calculate the revenue that would be raised by repealing them all. Provisions interact. Behavior changes. Taxpayers rearrange transactions. Macroeconomic effects matter. Some estimates use different conceptual baselines.

The published numbers are not a repeal menu.

They are a map of policy embedded in the tax system.

The map is valuable precisely because normal budget language otherwise loses the terrain.

A government can expand without increasing an agency's appropriations.

It can reduce the tax price of education, housing, health care, retirement, energy, research, child care, business investment, charitable giving, or borrowing.

Private spending responds.

The federal budget records less revenue than it would under the chosen baseline.

The political system can then declare that it cut taxes rather than increased spending.

Sometimes that distinction is substantively important.

Sometimes it is branding.

Imagine two programs.

Program A gives a household a $5,000 refundable benefit after meeting an eligibility test.

Program B gives the same household a $5,000 reduction in taxes after meeting an identical eligibility test.

If the household owes at least $5,000 in tax, the economic result may be nearly indistinguishable from the household's perspective.

Yet the programs can occupy opposite ideological categories.

Now change one detail.

Suppose the household owes only $2,000.

The nonrefundable tax benefit cannot deliver the full $5,000. A direct payment can.

The form now changes distribution.

Change another detail.

Suppose eligibility is based on an expense the household chooses to incur. The tax benefit may alter the price of that expense and therefore behavior.

Change another.

Suppose the benefit is delivered through payroll withholding, annual filing, or an employer benefit exclusion. Timing and visibility change again.

The boundary between tax policy and spending policy is real.

It is also porous.

This is why tax expenditures deserve to be compared with direct alternatives rather than morally classified in advance.

If Congress wants to subsidize research, should it use grants, procurement, tax credits, accelerated cost recovery, prizes, or some mixture?

If it wants to support families, should it use refundable credits, direct transfers, public services, deductions, or social insurance?

If it wants to support housing, should it subsidize ownership, rental assistance, infrastructure, construction, credit, or supply reform?

Different instruments reach different people and create different incentives.

The tax code is an instrument, not an ideology.

The shadow-budget problem appears when the instrument escapes normal scrutiny because nobody wants to call it spending.

Municipal bonds show how deep this can go.

The federal government excludes interest on qualifying state and local bonds from federal income tax. The fiscal year 2027 Analytical Perspectives projected the 2026 income-tax expenditure from the exclusion of interest on public-purpose state and local bonds at roughly $40 billion.

The policy lowers the after-tax yield investors require from qualifying municipal debt and is intended to reduce borrowing costs for state and local public purposes.

The federal government does not build the local water plant.

It subsidizes part of the financing environment in which the local government borrows to build it.

The spending occurs several institutional layers away from Washington.

This can be effective.

It can also be difficult to evaluate because the benefit is divided between issuers and investors depending on market conditions and tax rates.

The federal government gives up revenue in order to alter another government's cost of capital.

That is public policy even if no federal project manager ever sees the construction site.

Tax expenditures therefore blur not only spending and taxes but levels of government.

They can subsidize employers, investors, homeowners, universities, charities, states, cities, energy producers, families, and industries without creating a conventional federal operating program.

This is one reason reform is so difficult.

A spending cut usually produces an organized beneficiary defending a visible appropriation.

Tax-expenditure reform produces taxpayers who may experience the change as a tax increase, industries that have built pricing around the preference, financial institutions that have structured products around it, and politicians who promised not to raise taxes.

The policy has acquired both a constituency and an identity shield.

That combination is formidable.

It also makes tax expenditures useful to politicians trying to hide the size of government from whichever coalition dislikes the word government.

A conservative government can pursue industrial policy through credits and depreciation rules while insisting it prefers markets to spending.

A progressive government can pursue social policy through credits and exclusions while avoiding the administrative footprint of a new agency.

Both may have good substantive reasons for the instrument.

Both benefit rhetorically from the accounting form.

The result is a state larger than the appropriations tables alone suggest.

This does not mean every dollar of forgone revenue should be counted as a dollar of government spending in ordinary political rhetoric.

That move creates its own distortion.

Taxes are not a natural constant from which every departure is a subsidy. Baseline choice matters. A pure comprehensive income tax is not the only coherent tax system. Consumption taxes, cash-flow taxes, territorial systems, progressive income taxes, and other structures define normal treatment differently.

Calling every feature that differs from one economist's preferred baseline a government expenditure would turn tax analysis into ideology by spreadsheet.

The more careful point is institutional.

When the tax code is used deliberately to achieve a programmatic objective, the policy should face comparison with direct spending and regulation.

What does it cost under a stated baseline?

Who receives the benefit?

What behavior changes?

What would a direct program cost?

What would a neutral tax treatment do?

What happens to prices and supply?

What happens if the preference becomes permanent enough that the market reorganizes around it?

These are ordinary policy questions.

The tax label should not exempt them.

The same is true for sunsets.

Temporary tax provisions are often scored as if they expire on schedule even when political expectations assume extension. Temporary spending programs can play the same game, but the tax code has become especially skilled at manufacturing temporary policies whose expiration would be described as a tax increase.

The formal budget window says one thing.

Political expectation says another.

The difference is another invoice sent beyond the visible horizon.

A policy can be made to look cheaper by ending it in law shortly after the period used for budget scoring.

If everyone expects renewal, the expiration is not a forecast.

It is punctuation.

This is why the honest bill must include current law and plausible continuation as separate concepts rather than pretending one number can settle the argument.

CBO baselines have rules.

JCT estimates have rules.

Treasury tax-expenditure estimates have rules.

Those rules are necessary because scorekeepers cannot read Congress's mind.

Politicians can exploit the gap between formal law and expected behavior.

The scorekeeper is not the problem.

The incentive is.

The tax-expenditure shadow budget therefore belongs in a spending book because it demonstrates how thoroughly government can separate a benefit from the feeling of being spent on.

The recipient may never receive a check.

The subsidy may never pass through an agency.

The policy may be described as a tax cut, a deduction, an exclusion, accelerated depreciation, a deferral, a basis rule, or a special rate.

The budgetary effect appears on the revenue side.

The economic behavior can look much like a spending program.

This is not a semantic trick.

It is a reminder to follow the incentive rather than the label.

America's spending problem is partly a problem of visible expenditures.

It is also a problem of benefits that become invisible because the government delivers them by declining to collect.

The difference matters for law.

It should matter less for judgment.

A dollar of direct spending deserves scrutiny.

A dollar of deliberate tax preference deserves scrutiny.

Neither deserves condemnation merely because of its form.

Neither deserves immunity either.

The honest budget is the one that puts them on the same table long enough to ask what the country is actually buying.
