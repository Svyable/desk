# Why Taxing the Rich Isn't Enough

There is a version of the American fiscal problem in which the entire missing bill is sitting in a penthouse.

Government can promise more, preserve every major benefit, avoid broad tax increases, reduce deficits, and protect the middle class if only very wealthy households and large corporations are required to pay more.

This argument has an obvious attraction.

The United States is rich.

Some Americans are extraordinarily rich.

The distribution of wealth is highly unequal.

The tax code contains preferential rates, exclusions, deferrals, deductions, business structures, estate-planning strategies, and enforcement problems that matter much more to people with complex finances than to workers whose income arrives on a W-2.

Higher taxes on high incomes, capital gains, estates, corporations, or other concentrated bases can raise substantial revenue.

Better enforcement can raise revenue already owed under existing law.

Closing poorly justified tax preferences can increase both revenue and perceived fairness.

None of that is trivial.

Then comes the word enough.

Enough for what?

A tax increase can be large in political terms and modest relative to a government spending more than seven trillion dollars a year.

A policy can raise hundreds of billions over a decade and still leave a multi-trillion-dollar annual gap largely intact.

The scale of the federal budget makes ordinary language dangerous.

Large is not the same as sufficient.

Meaningful is not the same as complete.

This is the revenue-side mirror of the previous chapter.

Cutting waste can save real money without balancing the budget.

Taxing the rich can raise real money without balancing the budget.

A serious fiscal strategy should do valuable things even when they are not enough by themselves.

The first reason concentrated taxation has limits is arithmetic.

Broad spending commitments eventually intersect with broad national income.

Social Security is not a boutique program for a small population.

Medicare is not a narrow discretionary grant.

Defense, interest, veterans' benefits, health programs, and other major commitments are measured in hundreds of billions or trillions.

If a country wants a large, durable social-insurance state, it eventually has to confront the financing base beneath it.

Countries with large welfare states often use broad taxes for a reason.

The politics may be progressive.

The financing is usually wider than a handful of billionaires.

That does not mean every taxpayer should pay the same rate.

It means a broad promise and a narrow tax base are naturally in tension.

There is another reason.

Income at the very top is not the same kind of object as wages spread across millions of workers.

High-income households receive business income, capital gains, dividends, partnership income, carried interests, rents, stock compensation, and other returns whose timing and legal form can respond to tax rules.

That responsiveness does not make taxation futile.

It makes design important.

Raise a tax rate and some revenue rises mechanically.

Then people adjust.

They accelerate or delay realizations.

They change business form.

They alter compensation.

They move assets.

They change charitable giving.

They borrow rather than sell.

They reorganize estates.

Some relocate.

Some invest differently.

Some do almost nothing.

The size of the behavioral response depends on the tax, the rate, enforcement, available avoidance channels, international coordination, state taxes, market conditions, and how much of the apparent response is merely timing.

This is why revenue estimates exist.

A statutory rate is not a revenue forecast.

The relationship is not linear forever.

That sentence is sometimes abused to imply that tax cuts always pay for themselves or tax increases always reduce revenue.

Neither is true as a general rule.

Many tax increases raise substantial revenue.

Many tax cuts lose substantial revenue.

Behavior simply means the amount is not equal to the tax-rate change multiplied by a frozen economy.

The third limit is valuation.

Wealth and income are related but not interchangeable.

A founder can own shares worth billions without receiving billions in cash income that year.

A privately held company can have a high estimated value and little liquidity.

A farm, building, or business can appreciate before the owner sells it.

Tax systems can reach those gains in many ways, but the administrative design matters enormously.

A tax on realized gains is easier to observe but encourages deferral.

A mark-to-market regime taxes gains before sale but raises valuation and liquidity questions for nontraded assets.

Estate taxation reaches wealth transfers but arrives late and invites planning.

A wealth tax reaches the stock rather than the flow but requires recurring valuation and enforcement across complex assets.

There are answers to each problem.

There is no answer in which the problem disappears because the taxpayer is rich.

Rich people can afford accountants precisely because the stakes are high.

That makes tax administration part of the policy rather than an afterthought.

The Internal Revenue Service's projected gross tax gap for tax year 2022 was about $696 billion.

That figure covered taxes across the system, not merely taxes owed by the wealthy, and it is not a bag of cash waiting to be collected with perfect enforcement. Some noncompliance is difficult to detect. Some estimates rely on assumptions. Enforcement has costs. Disputes take time. Some liabilities are never collectible.

Still, the scale matters.

A tax system that writes rules without investing enough in compliance creates a quiet spending program for people who are best able or most willing to evade them.

The legal tax rate becomes less relevant than the collected tax rate.

This is one place where fairness and fiscal capacity align.

Third-party reporting and withholding produce high compliance because the government receives information independently of the taxpayer.

Complex business and investment income can be harder to observe.

That makes information systems, reporting rules, audit capacity, and international cooperation fiscal infrastructure.

A country can raise statutory taxes on the wealthy and still undercollect them if the administrative system is weak.

It can also enforce aggressively and still fail to close a structural deficit if the underlying legal tax base is too narrow relative to spending.

These are separate problems.

The fourth limit is political rather than economic.

Every democracy is tempted to promise voters benefits financed by someone else.

The phrase someone else is powerful because it separates consumption from payment.

In one coalition, someone else is the rich.

In another, someone else is future taxpayers.

In another, someone else is foreigners paying tariffs.

In another, someone else is wasteful bureaucrats whose budget can supposedly disappear without any service changing.

The common structure is the same.

The voter is offered the benefit without the bill.

Progressive taxation can be justified on grounds that do not depend on this illusion.

A dollar has different marginal value to a billionaire and a low-wage worker.

Public institutions help create the legal and economic environment in which large fortunes can exist.

Economic rents can be taxed with less distortion than returns that are highly sensitive to incentives.

Inheritance raises different fairness questions from earned labor income.

A society may reasonably decide that people with greater ability to pay should bear a greater share of common costs.

Those are substantive arguments.

They become weaker when converted into the claim that broad public commitments never require broad financing.

The strongest case for progressive taxation is not that ordinary citizens can therefore ignore the cost of government.

It is that the cost should be distributed according to a defensible principle.

Distribution is not disappearance.

Consider Social Security.

One reform option is to raise or eliminate the cap on earnings subject to the payroll tax, with or without corresponding changes in future benefits.

That can improve program financing materially depending on design.

It also illustrates the limit.

The program is large because retirement is common.

Its financing problem reflects demographics, wage growth, longevity, labor-force participation, and the benefit formula across the whole system.

High earners can contribute more.

They cannot become an infinite demographic resource.

Medicare goes further.

Health costs in old age are broadly distributed across a population whose size is rising relative to the workforce.

High-income premiums and taxes can finance part of the system.

But if health spending continues to grow faster than the tax base, the financing question keeps returning.

The same pattern appears outside social insurance.

Suppose the federal government creates a permanent benefit costing one percent of GDP.

That is a large national commitment.

If the policy is popular enough to last, its supporters should be willing to explain the tax system that can last with it.

Maybe the answer is highly progressive income taxation.

Maybe it is a value-added tax with rebates.

Maybe it is payroll taxes, carbon taxes, consumption taxes, corporate taxes, land taxes, wealth-related taxes, or some mixture.

The choice matters enormously for distribution and incentives.

But a durable program should not depend on the fiction that a sufficiently imaginative definition of rich can finance every future expansion forever.

At some point rich becomes a moving threshold.

This already happens in ordinary political language.

A tax described as applying only to the wealthy may begin at an income that sounds enormous nationally but feels very different in a high-cost city or for a household with volatile business income.

A temporary surtax becomes permanent.

An income threshold remains fixed while nominal incomes rise.

A narrow levy becomes a broader one because the original base does not produce enough revenue.

None of these expansions is automatically wrong.

They demonstrate why the financing principle should be explicit before the benefit becomes politically irreversible.

There is also a growth question.

Taxes finance government.

Government can support growth through infrastructure, courts, education, research, public health, security, and macroeconomic stability.

Taxes can also reduce the after-tax return to work, saving, investment, risk-taking, or location at the margin.

The size of those effects varies by tax and context.

A serious revenue policy asks which base can raise the desired amount with the least damaging combination of distortion, avoidance, administrative burden, and unfairness.

That is a design problem, not a theology.

The left sometimes talks as if every untaxed dollar at the top is evidence of political cowardice.

The right sometimes talks as if every additional dollar of tax destroys the activity that generated it.

Neither is a serious model of a modern economy.

The United States has had periods with higher top tax rates and strong growth.

It has also had taxes whose design produced avoidance, shelters, distortions, and political games.

The lesson is not that rates do not matter.

It is that bases, enforcement, timing, incidence, and the use of revenue matter too.

Corporate taxation makes this especially clear.

A corporation writes the check.

People ultimately bear the economic burden.

Shareholders may receive lower returns.

Workers may receive lower wages.

Customers may pay higher prices.

The distribution depends on market structure, mobility of capital, international competition, and time horizon.

A corporate tax can still be progressive if the burden falls heavily on owners of capital.

But statutory incidence should not be confused with economic incidence.

The same caution applies to tariffs.

The foreign exporter may absorb some of the cost.

Domestic importers, consumers, and downstream firms may absorb some too.

A government cannot label the desired payer and guarantee the economic burden stays there.

This book is not arguing for low taxes.

It is arguing for visible taxes.

A country that wants more government should be capable of saying what broad, durable financing makes that choice possible.

A country that wants lower taxes should be capable of naming which commitments it will reduce rather than assuming growth or waste reduction will automatically close the gap.

Progressivity is one axis of an honest tax system.

Sufficiency is another.

Efficiency is another.

Simplicity is another.

Enforceability is another.

No single tax maximizes all of them.

The political temptation is to talk only about the axis on which your coalition looks best.

Progressives emphasize ability to pay.

Conservatives emphasize incentives and property rights.

Populists emphasize corporations, billionaires, foreigners, or loopholes.

Technocrats emphasize bases and incidence.

Taxpayers emphasize the check.

The Treasury experiences the total.

That total has to finance the total spending.

This is why the phrase pay their fair share is both necessary and incomplete.

A tax system should have a defensible distribution.

Fairness matters.

But even after society agrees that the rich should pay more, it must answer a second question.

How much government are we buying?

The first question allocates the bill.

The second determines its size.

Confusing them is another form of price distance.

If voters believe every new benefit can be assigned to a small group outside the room, the marginal price of government feels like zero.

Demand rises.

The tax base eventually does not.

The honest version of progressive government is harder and stronger.

It says: these public goods and social protections are worth having; wealthier households should finance a disproportionate share because they have greater capacity and have benefited greatly from the economic order; and the rest of society should still understand that durable public commitments consume real national resources.

That is a politics of contribution rather than concealment.

It leaves room for strong progressivity.

It leaves less room for magic.

Tax the rich more if the policy case is good.

Collect the taxes already owed.

Close preferences that cannot justify themselves.

Tax rents more intelligently.

Design enforcement around the income forms most difficult to observe.

Do all of that because it can improve revenue, fairness, and legitimacy.

Then look at the remaining gap.

If the country still wants the spending, the country still needs a financing plan.

The rich are part of America.

They are not a parallel country with an infinite Treasury attached.
