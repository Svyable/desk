# The Pentagon and the Entitlement State

American budget arguments are often staged as a duel between two caricatures.

On one side is the Pentagon: aircraft carriers, missile defense, overseas bases, procurement programs, secret facilities, contractors, generals, and the national-security language that can make an invoice sound like a geopolitical necessity.

On the other side is the entitlement state: Social Security checks, Medicare cards, disability benefits, Medicaid payments, retirement promises, and the social-insurance language that can make an invoice sound like a moral obligation.

The caricatures are politically useful because each side gets to choose the spending it regards as sacred and the spending it regards as indulgence.

The budget does not recognize the distinction.

In fiscal year 2026, CBO's baseline put defense discretionary funding at roughly $898 billion. Social Security was already a program of roughly $1.7 trillion, Medicare near a trillion dollars, and mandatory spending as a whole about $4.5 trillion. Net interest was above $1 trillion.

Those categories differ in law, purpose, beneficiaries, and budget process.

They also share a deeper trait.

They are commitments whose cost is easier to create than to reverse.

A weapons platform can become a thirty-year industrial ecosystem before the first unit is fully operational. A retirement benefit can become part of a household's expected future before the worker reaches middle age. A hospital payment formula can build an entire provider business model around itself. A military installation can become the economic anchor of a region. An entitlement formula can become a national promise repeated in every election.

The spending starts as policy.

Then policy becomes expectation.

Expectation becomes infrastructure.

Infrastructure becomes constituency.

This is the point at which the normal vocabulary of waste becomes inadequate.

Defense contains waste.

Entitlement programs contain waste.

Both also contain vast amounts of spending that are doing exactly what lawmakers told them to do.

A pension check sent to an eligible retiree is not waste merely because the program is expensive. A submarine that meets an approved military requirement is not waste merely because submarines cost a fortune. A Medicare reimbursement made under the law is not waste because the Treasury had to borrow part of the money used to finance the system.

A spending problem can be perfectly legal, competently administered, and broadly popular.

That is why it is a spending problem rather than an accounting error.

The Pentagon and the entitlement state reveal two different ways a democracy makes long-term promises.

Defense promises capability.

Social insurance promises security.

The first is aimed outward, toward uncertain adversaries and risks. The second is aimed inward, toward predictable demographic and household needs. Defense planners must decide what force might be required years from now against threats that may not yet exist. Retirement and health programs must decide what benefits future taxpayers will finance for populations whose broad demographic path is much easier to see.

Uncertainty therefore enters the two systems differently.

The military can reasonably argue that underpreparing for a low-probability catastrophic threat may be much more costly than overpreparing. Spare capacity, redundant systems, ammunition stockpiles, surge production, logistics networks, and alliances all look inefficient in peacetime precisely because their value appears in emergencies.

A country that optimizes defense like a discount airline may discover what the missing slack was for when the shooting starts.

Entitlement programs face almost the opposite forecasting problem.

The broad facts are not secret.

People are aging. The large baby-boom cohorts moved into retirement. Life expectancy, labor-force growth, fertility, immigration, wage growth, health costs, and productivity all affect the financing, but the direction of the demographic transition has been visible for decades.

The 2026 Social Security Trustees projected that the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund would deplete its reserves in the fourth quarter of 2032 under their intermediate assumptions. At that point, dedicated income would be sufficient for about 78 percent of scheduled OASI benefits if lawmakers made no changes. On a combined OASDI basis, the two trust funds were projected to support full scheduled benefits until 2034, with about 83 percent payable thereafter.

The Medicare Hospital Insurance trust fund carried its own financing shortfall and projected depletion date.

Those dates are often described as if a program falls off a cliff.

That is not what the trustees are saying.

Payroll taxes and other dedicated revenues do not suddenly vanish. The problem is that the scheduled benefits written into law are larger than the dedicated financing expected to support them once reserves run out.

The gap is the promise.

Congress can close it with higher taxes, lower benefits, broader revenues, changes in eligibility, changes in the benefit formula, faster growth, higher immigration, different health-cost trends, or some combination.

The menu has been visible for years.

The political incentive is to order later.

This is where defense and social insurance become surprisingly similar.

Every reform creates a visible loser before it creates an invisible future gain.

Cancel a weapons program and a factory closes, a congressional district loses work, military planners lose a capability, suppliers lose contracts, and officials can be accused of weakening national security.

Slow the growth of retirement benefits and people nearing retirement can calculate the difference. Raise the payroll tax and every paycheck records the decision. Increase the retirement age and a worker in a physically demanding job can reasonably ask whether the reform was designed by somebody who sits at a desk.

The future fiscal benefit has no lobby because it has not happened yet.

The present cost has an address.

This asymmetry makes both systems sticky.

Defense spending has an additional structural feature: the buyer is also the rule-maker.

The federal government specifies the military requirement, chooses procurement rules, defines security standards, selects contractors, controls export permissions, and often maintains a deliberately narrow supplier base because military production has high barriers to entry.

That is not an ordinary market failure.

It is partly intentional.

The United States does not want sensitive weapons production optimized solely around the cheapest global bidder. It may pay to preserve domestic shipyards, semiconductor access, secure communications suppliers, munitions production, engineering knowledge, and industrial surge capacity even when the peacetime spreadsheet dislikes the redundancy.

The spending problem begins when strategic resilience and ordinary inefficiency become impossible to distinguish.

A contractor can defend excess capacity as readiness.

A service branch can defend a familiar platform as necessary until the moment technology has moved past it.

A congressional delegation can defend a facility as national security when the local payroll is doing much of the political work.

A procurement bureaucracy can add rules to prevent yesterday's scandal and make tomorrow's acquisition slower and more expensive.

The military then pays a premium not merely for capability but for the institutional history surrounding capability.

Entitlements accumulate history in formulas rather than factories.

Social Security's core structure links benefits to a worker's earnings record. Medicare combines multiple parts, premiums, dedicated taxes, general revenues, and provider payment systems. Medicaid shares financing and administration between federal and state governments. Each program has been modified repeatedly, often to solve a real problem, fill a coverage gap, or protect a vulnerable group.

The resulting system is not a blank check.

It is a stack of promises with different financing sources.

That matters because the word entitlement is often used as if it means optional generosity.

In budget law, the important distinction is that spending follows eligibility rules written in law rather than being determined entirely by an annual appropriation.

If ten million more people become eligible under a formula, the program does not simply stop when the congressional committee's preferred dollar amount is exhausted.

The law creates the expenditure.

That design can be exactly what society wants.

Retirement insurance would not be much of a promise if each year's retirees had to hope an appropriations subcommittee felt generous. Health insurance for older people would not provide much security if coverage disappeared whenever annual bargaining became difficult.

Stability is the feature.

Stability is also what makes the spending hard to adjust.

A discretionary program can theoretically be appropriated at a lower level next year.

A mandatory benefit program requires changing the rule that creates the payment.

That generally means changing someone's legal expectation.

This is why arguments about "discretionary versus mandatory" can mislead people into thinking one category is controllable and the other is not.

Both are controllable in law.

They differ in the political mechanism required.

Defense spending is renewed through annual and supplemental decisions, but national-security commitments, contracts, force structure, personnel, and industrial capacity make much of the supposed discretion highly constrained in practice.

Social insurance is automatic under current formulas, but Congress can change those formulas.

The word mandatory describes the current legal plumbing, not a law of nature.

The useful question is how much future choice has already been consumed.

A new weapons system consumes future appropriations because the platform will need procurement, maintenance, crews, training, upgrades, spare parts, and eventually retirement costs.

A new entitlement consumes future revenues because eligible populations and formulas generate payments automatically.

Both can create obligations far beyond the year in which the political decision was made.

The first-year price is therefore rarely the price.

This is another version of America's monthly-payment instinct.

A major procurement is politically easier to announce through annual increments than as the full lifecycle commitment.

A benefit enhancement is politically easier to announce as a monthly increase than as the present value of decades of future payments.

The budget year becomes a convenient window through which long commitments can look smaller.

There are good reasons for annual budgeting.

Nobody can know the exact cost of operating an aircraft fleet thirty years from now. Nobody knows future wage growth, health technology, inflation, mortality, or migration with precision. Present-value estimates depend on discount rates and assumptions that can move enormously over time.

Uncertainty is real.

It should not become an excuse to pretend the long tail does not exist.

A more honest defense budget would make lifecycle cost and industrial assumptions harder to ignore.

A more honest entitlement debate would display scheduled benefits and dedicated financing on the same page.

Neither reform would decide the political answer.

They would make the answer harder to evade.

The Social Security trustees illustrate the value of this kind of uncomfortable accounting.

Every year the report arrives with projections under stated assumptions. The dates move. The actuarial balance changes. The numbers are uncertain because the future is uncertain.

But the report prevents one important fiction.

It prevents policymakers from claiming that the program's financing gap was unknowable.

Defense needs similar humility.

Threat forecasts fail. Cost estimates fail. Technologies arrive early or late. Wars happen in the wrong places. A weapon thought essential becomes obsolete. A capability thought unnecessary becomes urgent.

The honest answer is not perfect prediction.

It is keeping error visible.

A procurement process should be able to cancel, redesign, or scale a program when evidence changes without treating every prior dollar spent as an argument for the next one.

A retirement system should adjust gradually when its financing drifts rather than waiting until the remaining choices become abrupt.

The common enemy is sunk-cost politics.

Once a commitment acquires enough jobs, beneficiaries, institutions, and identity, the past begins to argue for the future.

We have already spent this much.

People have planned around it.

The factory exists.

The hospital exists.

The retiree was promised.

The shipyard employs the town.

The formula has been law for decades.

All of those statements can be true.

None proves the next dollar is the best use of the next dollar.

This is the fiscal discipline both hawks and social democrats need from each other.

The defense hawk is right that a country cannot price deterrence only by the wars that actually occurred. The welfare-state defender is right that a society cannot price social insurance only by the taxes collected from one beneficiary in one year.

Both systems pool risk across time.

Both therefore need strong institutions to stop risk pooling from becoming price blindness.

For defense, that means serious threat prioritization, competitive procurement where competition is possible, modularity, cost testing, independent evaluation, and the willingness to stop programs that no longer justify their lifecycle burden.

For entitlements, it means explicit long-run financing, gradual adjustment, protection for people with less ability to absorb change, and reforms made early enough that workers and households can plan.

The exact choices remain political.

The timing is not neutral.

Delay shrinks the set of humane options.

If Social Security changes are made decades before a financing shortfall becomes acute, the adjustment can be spread across revenue, benefit growth, taxable payroll, and cohorts.

If changes are made after reserves are nearly depleted, each available move has to be larger.

If a military procurement problem is caught at prototype, the program can change.

If the same problem is discovered after a production ecosystem depends on the platform, cancellation becomes a regional economic shock as well as a military decision.

Earlier feedback is cheaper feedback.

That is the recurring lesson of this book.

The United States does not have a defense problem instead of an entitlement problem.

It has a commitment problem that appears in different costumes.

Some commitments are worth every dollar.

Some are underfunded.

Some are overbuilt.

Some are strategically necessary.

Some are socially necessary.

Some survive mainly because stopping them would reveal the price that starting them concealed.

The budget cannot tell us which is which.

It can at least force us to admit that the same future cannot be promised twice.
