## Strongest counterargument

The chapter’s phrase “the code becomes a spending program written in negative numbers” is memorable, but taken literally it collapses a contested baseline choice into an accounting identity. Tax expenditures are measured relative to a specified reference tax system. Change the reference system and some items change or disappear. The imputed-rent example already exposes this problem especially well: most homeowners do not experience untaxed imputed rent as a government transfer, and a consumption-tax baseline would treat saving and investment differently from a comprehensive-income baseline.

There is a second boundary. Even when a provision is intentionally programmatic, its published tax-expenditure estimate is not the cash Congress would mechanically collect from repeal. OMB and JCT both emphasize interactions and behavioral responses. The manuscript already states this later, but the strongest caveat arrives after the “shadow budget” metaphor has done substantial argumentative work.

The counterargument should therefore be allowed to alter the mechanism rather than survive as a late disclaimer: a tax expenditure is best understood as **policy delivered through the tax base**, not as a direct outlay wearing a disguise in every economically relevant sense.

## What survives

The chapter’s institutional argument remains strong after that concession. Congress can pursue recognizable programmatic objectives through exclusions, deductions, credits, deferrals, and preferential rates; those choices can alter relative prices, distribution, compensation, financing, and market structure while receiving different political treatment from appropriated spending. The employer-health exclusion is particularly useful because the tax treatment is not merely a bookkeeping curiosity: it helped make employer-provided health benefits more attractive relative to taxable cash compensation.

The defensible boundary is:

**Tax expenditures are not literally a hidden pile of spending equal to their published estimates. They are policy choices embedded in a chosen tax base. When Congress uses those choices to subsidize an activity or beneficiary, they deserve comparison with direct spending, regulation, and neutral tax treatment—even though the legal form, distribution, visibility, and repeal revenue can differ materially.**

## Editorial implication for a later prose pass

The chapter currently repeats the same conceptual caveat several times: tax expenditures are not literally outlays; the baseline matters; estimates are not additive; repeal revenue differs; form can affect distribution. A later surgical revision should move the baseline objection earlier and let one concrete case carry it, probably imputed rent. Then the chapter can spend less space repeatedly proving that it understands the caveat and more space on the genuinely interesting consequence: tax form changes political perception and can reorganize private markets.

That would also reduce a recurring AI-like rhythm in the present chapter—many one-sentence paragraphs and paired oppositions—without flattening Sven’s useful lines such as “The number is not cash waiting in a drawer.” The aim should be to keep one or two earned aphoristic turns and let the surrounding explanation run in fuller paragraphs.

No new claim here implies that every tax preference is a subsidy, that every tax expenditure should be repealed, or that OMB/JCT estimates can be summed into a collectible shadow-budget total.
