# Chapter 9 research audit — defense lifecycle commitments and the entitlement analogy

Accessed 2026-09-15. This note stress-tests Chapter 9, “The Pentagon and the Entitlement State.” It does not change manuscript prose.

## Claim under review

Chapter 9 argues that defense and social insurance are legally and substantively different but share a fiscal mechanism: political decisions made today can create durable expectations, infrastructure, constituencies, and future claims on resources. The chapter is strongest when it compares commitment dynamics rather than pretending a weapons program and a statutory benefit are the same kind of obligation.

The current prose makes that distinction, but its defense side is mostly conceptual. Fresh 2026 evidence can make the lifecycle mechanism concrete while also exposing an important counterargument: a long-lived defense program can be expensive because readiness genuinely requires sustainment, redundancy, spares, skilled labor, and industrial capacity. Cost growth is not itself proof of waste.

## Fresh authoritative evidence

### GAO 2026 weapon-systems annual assessment

U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Weapon Systems Annual Assessment: Requiring Mature Technologies Could Enable Shift to Rapid Delivery*, GAO-26-108457 (July 2, 2026):
https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108457

GAO assessed 104 of DOD’s costliest weapon programs and reports that DOD plans to invest more than $2.4 trillion to develop and acquire its costliest weapon systems. The average time to deliver a capability increased to more than 12 years, and GAO says several major programs have delayed key events or retained delivery dates that may no longer be realistic. For the Middle Tier of Acquisition portfolio, DOD planned at least $49 billion across 23 of the most expensive programs; 18 of 40 programs entering that pathway from 2018 through 2025 began with immature technologies, and some reviewed programs ended without delivering a fieldable capability.

Boundary: this is an acquisition portfolio assessment, not a finding that $2.4 trillion is waste or that delay proves a program lacks military value. Some delays reflect difficult technology, changing threats, testing, safety, supply constraints, or deliberate redesign. The useful point for Chapter 9 is narrower: the political decision to begin a system often precedes years of development in which requirements, sunk costs, suppliers, and schedules can become progressively harder to reverse.

### GAO 2026 sustainment review

U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Weapon System Sustainment: DOD Identified Critical Cost Growth, and the Army Should Take Action to Yield Cost Savings*, GAO-26-108140 (April 23, 2026):
https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108140

GAO reports that operating and support costs historically account for about 70 percent of a weapon system’s total lifecycle cost. Those costs include repair parts, depot and field maintenance, contract services, engineering support, and personnel. DOD identified critical operating-and-support cost growth in 14 of 36 sustainment reviews for fiscal years 2023 and 2024. Under the statutory thresholds used for those reviews, “critical” meant at least 25 percent growth above the most recent independent estimate or at least 50 percent above the original baseline estimate.

This is unusually useful evidence for the chapter’s claim that the first-year or procurement price is rarely the whole price. The large tail is not an accounting abstraction: much of a weapon system’s cost arrives after acquisition, through the work required to keep the capability usable.

Boundary: the roughly 70 percent figure is a historical DOD-wide characterization, not a universal ratio for every platform. Nor is sustainment inherently a hidden cost. Readiness requires maintenance, trained personnel, spare parts, software, depots, and logistics. A lifecycle argument should therefore ask whether the sustainment burden was understood and remains justified by the capability, not imply that a large sustainment share is evidence of failure.

### F-35 as a concrete scale case

U.S. Government Accountability Office, *F-35 Sustainment: Actions Needed to Ensure Updated Strategy Improves Persistent Readiness Challenges*, GAO-26-108113 (June 11, 2026):
https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108113

GAO describes the F-35 as DOD’s most costly weapon system and reports estimated U.S. lifetime sustainment costs of about $1.6 trillion through 2088, as of 2024. DOD operates and sustains more than 800 U.S. F-35s and plans to buy roughly 1,700 more by the mid-2040s. GAO also reports that sustainment costs have continued to increase while readiness goals have not been met, and that DOD launched a $13.7 billion strategy in 2025 aimed at improving readiness by 2030.

Boundary: the $1.6 trillion figure is a decades-long estimate, not a present liability or a check due today. It depends on fleet size, operating tempo, inflation, modernization, service life, contractor arrangements, and future policy. The program also supplies capabilities DOD regards as important. The case is useful because it makes the time dimension visible, not because a large nominal lifetime estimate settles whether the platform is worth its cost.

## Social Security side rechecked

Social Security Administration, *2026 OASDI Trustees Report* and Trustees summary (June 9, 2026):
https://www.ssa.gov/oact/TR/2026/
https://www.ssa.gov/oact/TRSUM/

The chapter’s current Social Security figures remain accurate. Under the Trustees’ intermediate assumptions, OASI reserves are projected to be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032, when continuing income would cover about 78 percent of scheduled OASI benefits. On the hypothetical combined OASDI basis, full scheduled benefits are projected through 2034, with about 83 percent payable at combined reserve depletion.

One useful legal boundary should remain visible in any later prose pass: OASI and DI are separate trust funds under current law. The combined OASDI presentation is analytically useful, but it does not mean Congress can automatically transfer resources between the funds without legal authority.

## Strongest counterargument

The chapter risks making “commitment” so broad that unlike institutions become rhetorically interchangeable.

Social Security benefits are statutory transfers financed largely through dedicated taxes and paid automatically to eligible beneficiaries under formulas that Congress can amend. Defense programs depend on appropriations, acquisition decisions, contracts, force-structure choices, military requirements, and operating plans. A future Congress can cancel a platform, retire a fleet, change procurement quantities, or alter a benefit formula, but the legal mechanisms, transition costs, beneficiaries, and consequences are not equivalent.

The analogy also cuts the other way. A long defense tail can reflect the real cost of preserving a capability, just as a durable social-insurance formula can reflect the real value of giving households a promise they can plan around. Stickiness is not automatically pathology. The fiscal problem appears when the long tail is hidden, systematically underpriced, no longer justified by the benefit or capability, or politically protected from evidence that should change it.

## What survives

A stronger Chapter 9 boundary is:

> Defense acquisition and social insurance are not the same kind of promise. They are two different ways current law and institutions can consume future fiscal discretion. The relevant question is not whether a commitment is “mandatory” in some metaphysical sense, but whether its long-run cost, purpose, and reversibility remain visible as circumstances change.

The 2026 GAO evidence gives the defense half of that claim an observable mechanism: acquisition can take more than a decade, sustainment can dominate lifecycle cost, and estimates can grow after a program has accumulated infrastructure and dependence. The Social Security Trustees provide the contrasting mechanism: a statutory formula generates scheduled benefits and a visible financing gap years before reserve depletion.

## Suggested later prose use

A surgical manuscript pass should avoid adding a catalogue of GAO statistics. One developed defense example is enough. The F-35 can make the lifecycle argument tangible if the prose immediately says what the $1.6 trillion estimate is and is not: a decades-long sustainment projection through 2088, not current debt and not proof that the aircraft is unnecessary.

That example could replace some of the chapter’s repeated abstract statements that “the first-year price is rarely the price.” The reader would see the tail before receiving the principle.

## Defeat / narrowing condition

The analogy should be narrowed further if lifecycle defense estimates prove too unstable to inform cancellation, force-structure, or procurement decisions, or if the political and legal reversibility of defense programs is materially greater than the chapter implies. Likewise, if statutory social-insurance changes can be made early with little disruption to household planning, the claim that expectation itself creates major fiscal stickiness should be weakened.
