# Chapter 15 evidence audit — growth feedback is real, not free money

Checked 2026-09-15 for Chapter 15, “Growth Is Necessary, Not Magical.”

The chapter’s central distinction is good: faster real growth can improve fiscal capacity, but a growth forecast is not a substitute for financing. The strongest way to make that distinction empirical is to use a case where official scorekeepers explicitly allowed a policy to change growth and then asked how much of its conventional budget cost the resulting macroeconomic feedback actually recovered.

## Current baseline: growth helps while debt still outruns it

Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* (February 2026):
https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105

CBO projects real GDP growth of 2.2 percent in 2026 and an average of 1.8 percent per year from 2027 through 2036. The agency does not treat that path as policy-invariant: its current projection explicitly incorporates stronger investment incentives from the 2025 reconciliation act, wider adoption of generative AI, weaker labor-force growth from population aging and lower net immigration, productivity losses from higher tariffs, and crowding out from federal deficits.

That is useful counterevidence to any caricature that official baselines simply assume policy cannot affect growth. CBO already models channels through which taxes, investment incentives, trade, immigration, technology, deficits, interest rates, labor supply, and capital accumulation change output.

Yet in the same baseline, debt held by the public rises from 101 percent of GDP in 2026 to 120 percent in 2036. Faster output is part of the arithmetic, but under current-law assumptions it does not grow quickly enough to offset persistent primary deficits and rising net interest costs.

The chapter should preserve that distinction: a denominator can improve while the ratio still worsens because the numerator is moving too.

## A clean historical test: the 2017 tax act’s macroeconomic feedback

Congressional Budget Office, “How the 2017 Tax Act Affects CBO’s Projections” (April 20, 2018):
https://www.cbo.gov/publication/53787

Congressional Budget Office, “How the 2017 Tax Act Has Affected CBO’s GDP and Budget Projections Since January 2017” (January 31, 2019):
https://www.cbo.gov/publication/54994

CBO’s dynamic analysis is a useful test because it does not assume the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act had no growth effect. CBO estimated that the act would increase the cumulative level of GDP over 2018–2028 by about $2.56 trillion relative to the earlier projection. That higher taxable income generated meaningful fiscal feedback.

Before macroeconomic feedback, CBO estimated the act would increase the primary deficit by about $1.8 trillion over 2018–2028. Economic feedback reduced that primary-deficit effect by about $550 billion. But the act also increased projected interest rates and debt-service costs. After those effects, CBO estimated total deficits would still rise by about $1.9 trillion over the period.

CBO’s later summary described the macroeconomic feedback as offsetting roughly 30 percent of the projected primary-deficit effect and roughly 20 percent of the total-deficit effect.

This is a better example than simply asserting that “tax cuts do not pay for themselves.” In CBO’s model, growth feedback was large enough to matter and nowhere near large enough to erase the fiscal cost. The mechanism worked; the magical version did not.

## Strongest counterargument

This episode cannot establish a universal percentage for the self-financing share of tax cuts, spending, deregulation, immigration reform, public investment, or any other policy. Dynamic estimates depend on the policy’s design, the starting economy, monetary conditions, labor and capital responses, financing, international flows, and the model used. CBO itself emphasized substantial uncertainty around implementation and behavioral responses.

Nor should the chapter use one CBO estimate as proof that no policy can ever generate fiscal feedback exceeding its conventional cost. A narrowly targeted reform with low direct budget cost and a large supply effect could in principle be net positive; a tax base can also sit on a portion of a revenue curve where a rate change has unusually large behavioral effects. The empirical burden is to demonstrate those conditions rather than assume them.

The opposite overclaim is also worth avoiding. The 2017 act is not evidence that growth effects are trivial. Hundreds of billions of dollars of estimated primary-budget feedback is economically consequential. A chapter arguing that growth is “necessary, not magical” is stronger if it admits that the feedback can be substantial.

## What this should change in the chapter

The current opening has a useful line: “Every coalition eventually discovers a preferred form of free money called future GDP.” Keep the wit, but make the next move empirical rather than rhetorical.

The 2017 tax-act score gives the reader a concrete denominator experiment: official dynamic scoring credited a large growth response and a large revenue feedback, then showed why financing still mattered once the remaining primary deficit and higher debt-service costs were counted. That example can replace several generic sentences about favored policies “unlocking growth.”

The 2026 baseline then supplies the current version of the same mechanism. CBO explicitly credits the 2025 reconciliation act with near-term consumption and investment effects and expects AI adoption to support productivity, while simultaneously modeling tariff drag, slower labor-force growth, and crowding out. Growth is not one lever; it is the net result of interacting labor, capital, productivity, policy, and financing channels.

A defensible reader-facing boundary is:

**Growth feedback can repay part of a policy’s budget cost. Whether it repays all of it is an empirical result, not a premise.**

That formulation preserves the chapter’s argument while making room for the strongest pro-growth countercase.

## Defeat condition

The chapter’s “not magical” framing would need revision if robust dynamic estimates across relevant U.S. policy episodes showed that plausible growth feedback routinely erased or exceeded conventional fiscal costs after financing and debt-service effects. Evidence that growth feedback is sometimes large does not defeat the thesis; it is part of the thesis and should be shown rather than minimized.
