# Chapter 6 research note — mortgage lock-in is conditional, not a permanent scarcity machine

Rechecked 2026-09-06 for Chapter 6, “The House as Retirement Plan.”

## Primary / authoritative evidence

### Federal Reserve FEDS: *Locked In: Mobility, Market Tightness, and House Prices*

Aditya Aladangady, Jacob Krimmel, and Tess Scharlemann, Federal Reserve Finance and Economics Discussion Series 2024-088, revised May 2025. DOI: https://doi.org/10.17016/FEDS.2024.088r1

The manuscript accurately reports the paper’s headline mobility estimate: mortgage-rate lock-in explains 44% of the decline in mortgage-borrower mobility from 2021 to 2022. The revised paper also estimates that the 2022 lock-in shock reduced time on market by 29% and increased house prices by 8%.

The crucial qualification belongs beside those numbers. The authors say the price and market-tightness effects were entirely due to the historically tight initial housing market. In their counterfactual using a more balanced 2019 market, the same lock-in shock would have had little to no effect on prices or tightness. Lock-in therefore should not be written as a general law that fixed-rate mortgages mechanically raise house prices whenever rates rise.

The paper also finds that the mobility effect primarily reflects fewer local moves, with only modest effects on moves across labor-market areas. That limits any stronger claim that lock-in broadly immobilizes American labor.

### Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies: *The State of the Nation’s Housing 2026*

The June 17, 2026 report supports the chapter’s larger affordability setting: home prices were up 54% nationwide since 2020; existing-home sales remained near a three-decade low; and homeowner non-mortgage costs had risen sharply. JCHS’s accompanying affordability material puts the Q4 2025 payment on the median-priced home at about $3,100 and the required income above $120,000 under its stated financing and cost assumptions.

Those figures describe a severe affordability problem, but they do not identify mortgage lock-in as its sole or dominant cause. Prices, prevailing mortgage rates, construction costs, insurance, taxes, local supply constraints, household formation, and demand conditions all matter.

## Counterargument the chapter should preserve

The fixed-rate mortgage is doing something socially valuable when it shields an incumbent household from a rate shock. The resulting reluctance to move is not simply a market defect; it is partly the consequence of contractual stability working as promised. A policy that eliminated that protection could increase listings while making household finances substantially more volatile.

More importantly, the Fed paper itself shows that lock-in’s price effect depends on the state of the market into which the shock arrives. In a balanced market, the same rate-gap shock need not produce a meaningful price increase. The chapter’s strongest version is therefore conditional: fixed-rate protection can amplify scarcity when a market is already unusually tight.

## Editorial implication

Keep the existing 44% mobility figure, but when Chapter 6 next receives a prose pass, qualify the subsequent price discussion with the paper’s 2019 counterfactual. That single piece of disconfirming evidence makes the argument more credible and less mechanically causal: a good mortgage institution can create a second-order constraint, but the constraint becomes a price amplifier only in combination with other scarcity conditions.

Do not turn the Fed authors’ estimates into Board policy conclusions; the FEDS page explicitly notes that staff research does not indicate concurrence by the Board of Governors.
