# Chapter 5 hospital productivity boundary

Freshness check: 2026-09-15.

## Why this note exists

Chapter 5, “The Cost Disease with Lobbyists,” makes an important two-part claim: some service-sector cost growth is a genuine consequence of slow productivity growth, while market structure, payment rules, licensing, subsidies, and political protection can add another layer. The chapter already cites Nordhaus, a 2026 AEJ: Macroeconomics paper, and RAND’s commercial-hospital-price work. A new Bureau of Labor Statistics release gives the hospital half of the argument a much better primary-source anchor and also creates a useful boundary against overclaiming.

## Fresh primary evidence

### Bureau of Labor Statistics: private community hospitals

BLS updated its private-community-hospital labor-productivity series on August 26, 2026:

https://www.bls.gov/productivity/highlights/hospitals-labor-productivity.htm

The series covers private community hospitals (NAICS 6221 and 6223), not government hospitals, psychiatric/substance-abuse hospitals, or nursing/residential-care facilities. BLS reports that labor productivity in these hospitals grew only **0.1 percent per year on average from 1993 through 2023**. The subperiods are more revealing than the average: about 2.0 percent annual growth in 1993–2001, a 1.5 percent annual decline in 2001–2007, essentially flat productivity in 2007–2019, and a 1.2 percent annual decline in 2019–2023 amid the pandemic and recovery. In 2023, output and hours both rose about 2.1 percent, leaving measured labor productivity nearly unchanged (-0.1 percent).

BLS also supplies a qualification the manuscript should keep. Medical technology can move less complex procedures out of inpatient hospitals, leaving the remaining hospital case mix more difficult and labor intensive. A flat hospital productivity measure can therefore partly reflect **where care is delivered and which cases remain inside the measured sector**, not simply a hospital failing to adopt technology.

BLS’s August 26, 2026 selected-service-industry release provides a useful comparison:

https://www.bls.gov/news.release/prin2.nr0.htm

Across 30 selected service-providing industries, labor productivity rose in 15 in 2025 and unit labor costs rose in 24. Service work is not one productivity category. The chapter should avoid allowing “services are labor intensive” to become a universal exemption from productivity scrutiny.

### FTC: market structure remains a live mechanism

A separate current federal record supports the chapter’s claim that slow productivity is not the whole hospital-price story. On August 18, 2026, the FTC finalized a consent order requiring divestiture of seven ambulatory surgery centers in Ascension Health’s proposed acquisition of AmSurg. The Commission’s complaint alleged that the transaction would reduce competition for specified outpatient surgical services in five metropolitan areas and likely lead to higher prices while threatening quality and innovation:

https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-approves-final-consent-order-ascension-health-amsurg-deal

This is an enforcement allegation and settlement, not a causal estimate of hospital consolidation nationwide. Its value here is narrower: federal competition authorities still identify provider market structure as a plausible price mechanism in concrete local health-care markets. That mechanism is conceptually distinct from Baumol-style slow productivity.

A September 2026 FTC statement about Fairfield Medical Center provides another current example of the same institutional concern, but it is not needed in the manuscript if the Ascension case is used; one developed example is preferable to a catalogue:

https://search.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/09/ftc-bureau-competition-director-statement-regarding-fairfield-medical-centers-sale-adena-health

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The chapter can be read as if “cost disease” supplies a clean technological baseline and lobbying or market power explains the excess above it. The BLS series shows why that decomposition is not clean.

Hospital output is unusually difficult to measure. Changes in case mix, movement of procedures between inpatient and outpatient settings, quality improvements, new treatments, survival, and the amount of labor required by the patients who remain can all change measured productivity. A new technology can make the health system more productive while making the measured hospital sector look less productive if it shifts easy cases elsewhere. Conversely, a hospital can adopt expensive technology that improves outcomes while increasing measured cost. “Productivity” is therefore not an observable natural constant from which a simple political markup can be calculated.

The FTC evidence has the opposite limitation. A competition complaint can establish the agency’s theory of harm and the facts alleged in a specific transaction; it does not prove that consolidation explains every high commercial hospital price, or that breaking up providers would eliminate the labor, standby-capacity, quality, teaching, trauma, or complexity costs hospitals genuinely bear.

The strongest surviving boundary is:

**Slow measured hospital productivity is real and unusually persistent, but it is neither a complete explanation of hospital prices nor a clean estimate of unavoidable cost. Market structure and payment design can matter at the same time, and the measurement itself changes when technology moves patients and procedures across settings.**

## Manuscript opportunity

A later surgical prose pass could replace the chapter’s generic statement that nurses cannot monitor unlimited ICU patients with the BLS result: after three decades, measured labor productivity in private community hospitals has averaged only 0.1 percent annual growth. The surprising part is not merely the small number but BLS’s explanation of why technology can improve care while making the remaining hospital case mix harder.

That gives the chapter a better sequence: let the cost-disease mechanism win first; show the measurement complication; then introduce commercial-price dispersion and market power as a different mechanism rather than as an accusation layered onto an assumed technological baseline. The existing RAND 254-percent-of-Medicare comparison can remain useful, with its current sample and administered-benchmark caveats.

No manuscript change is recommended in this research-only pass. The next prose edit should remain bounded to the hospital/productivity passage in Chapter 5.
