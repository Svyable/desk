# Research trail

Working research for *America's Got a Spending Problem*. Accessed 2026-08-31 unless otherwise noted. The manuscript is a Desk draft; projections and current-year figures should be rechecked before any Shelf release.

## Core sources used in the opening draft

### Federal budget and debt

- Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* (February 11, 2026): https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882
  - Used for the 2026 federal outlay, revenue, deficit, debt, and GDP-share figures and the 2036 baseline trajectory.
  - CBO baselines are projections under specified current-law assumptions, not predictions of legislation or destiny. The manuscript should keep the conditional language intact.

- Congressional Budget Office, detailed budget projections accompanying the 2026–2036 outlook: https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105
  - Used to cross-check mandatory, discretionary, and net-interest magnitudes.
  - Future chapters should distinguish debt held by the public from gross federal debt and should not mix nominal dollars with GDP ratios without saying so.

### Household balance sheets and resilience

- Federal Reserve Bank of New York, *Household Debt and Credit Report, 2026:Q2* / August 11, 2026 release: https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20260811
  - Total household debt about $18.77 trillion; mortgages about $13.12 trillion; auto loans about $1.71 trillion; student loans about $1.65 trillion; credit-card balances about $1.26 trillion; HELOC balances about $459 billion.
  - These balances are not directly comparable to federal debt. Household liabilities are backed by different assets and incomes and operate under different legal and monetary constraints.

- Bureau of Economic Analysis, *Personal Income and Outlays, July 2026* (August 26, 2026): https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-july-2026
  - Personal saving rate estimated at 3.0 percent in July 2026.
  - The aggregate saving rate should not be used as a national character score. Distribution, asset ownership, essential costs, income growth, and prior saving all matter.

- Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, *Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025* (May 2026): https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-executive-summary.htm
  - 73 percent of adults reported doing okay financially or living comfortably; 63 percent could cover a $400 emergency expense using cash or its equivalent; 59 percent reported a major unexpected expense in the previous year; 91 percent described prices as at least a minor concern.
  - The manuscript uses these numbers together to avoid the false choice between “households are fine” and “households are collapsing.”

### Health care

- Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, *National Health Expenditure Data — NHE Fact Sheet*: https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/national-health-expenditure-data/nhe-fact-sheet
  - 2024 national health expenditures: about $5.3 trillion, $15,474 per person, 18.0 percent of GDP. Hospital expenditures were about $1.63 trillion.
  - High U.S. medical spending is not proof of one mechanism. Prices, utilization, demographics, technology, administrative structure, provider consolidation, public payment rules, insurance design, health outcomes, and standby capacity all require separate evidence.

### Housing

- U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2024 1-year table B25140, *Housing Costs as a Percentage of Household Income in the Past 12 Months*: https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT1Y2024.B25140
  - Roughly 21.3 million renter households spent more than 30 percent of income on housing costs; roughly 10.9 million spent more than 50 percent. About 3.1 million renter households were in the “not computed” category.
  - Rental burden is an affordability measure, not by itself proof of the causal contribution of zoning, subsidies, interest rates, incomes, migration, or construction costs. Those mechanisms should be sourced independently.

### Higher education

- College Board, *Trends in College Pricing and Student Aid 2025 — Highlights*: https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights
  - 2025–26 public four-year in-state published tuition and fees about $11,950; average estimated net tuition and fees for first-time full-time in-state students about $2,300; average total in-state student budget about $30,990.
  - Sticker tuition, net tuition, cost of attendance, and amount borrowed are different quantities. The manuscript should never substitute one for another.

- College Board, *Trends in Student Aid 2025 — Highlights*: https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/student-aid/highlights
  - Total annual student and parent borrowing was $102.6 billion in 2024–25. Among 2023–24 bachelor's degree recipients at public and private nonprofit institutions, 47 percent graduated with debt and average debt among borrowers was $29,560.
  - In inflation-adjusted terms, borrowing among recent bachelor's degree recipients is below earlier peaks; the manuscript should not tell an obsolete story of mechanically rising debt for every new cohort.

## Sources added for chapters 5–8

### Cost disease and service-sector prices

- William D. Nordhaus, *Baumol's Diseases: A Macroeconomic Perspective*, NBER Working Paper 12218 (2006): https://www.nber.org/papers/w12218
  - Finds strong historical evidence that sectors with slower productivity growth experienced faster relative price growth.
  - Used to establish that some rising service costs can be a consequence of differential productivity rather than waste or market power.

- Martin Shu, “Selection, Structural Transformation, and the Cost Disease of Services,” *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 18(3), July 2026: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20230241
  - Finds that worker selection contributes little to explaining the service-sector cost disease in the paper's model and U.S. evidence.
  - Used only as a recent check that the Baumol mechanism remains an active empirical question, not as proof that every health, education, or public-sector cost increase is technologically unavoidable.

### Housing as wealth, shelter, and financing

- Joint Center for Housing Studies of Harvard University, *The State of the Nation's Housing 2026*: https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/state-nations-housing-2026
  - Reports median-priced-home monthly ownership costs around $3,120 at the end of 2025 under its financing assumptions and an income requirement around $120,800, up sharply from five years earlier.
  - Also reports record renter cost burdens and rising non-mortgage homeowner costs. These are affordability indicators, not a causal decomposition.

- Joint Center for Housing Studies, “Ten Takeaways from the 2026 State of the Nation's Housing”: https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/ten-takeaways-2026-state-nations-housing
  - Used for the five-times-income home-price context and renter burden figures.

- Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, *Monetary Policy Report*, July 2026: https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/2026-07-mpr-part1.htm
  - Notes that the majority of outstanding mortgages remained below 4 percent while the prevailing 30-year fixed mortgage rate was about 6.4 percent around July 1, 2026, contributing to mortgage “rate lock.”

- Aditya Aladangady, Jacob Krimmel, and Tess Scharlemann, “Locked In: Mobility, Market Tightness, and House Prices,” Federal Reserve FEDS 2024-088, revised May 2025: https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/locked-in-rate-hikes-housing-markets-and-mobility.htm
  - Estimates mortgage rate lock-in explained 44 percent of the decline in mortgage-borrower mobility from 2021 to 2022 and, under historically tight market conditions, contributed to higher prices.
  - The authors explicitly find the price effect depends on initial market tightness; the manuscript preserves that condition rather than treating lock-in as a universal price effect.

- Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, *Financial Accounts of the United States*, June 11, 2026 release: https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/current/recent_developments.htm
  - Household-sector owner-occupied real estate about $48.7 trillion and home mortgages about $13.8 trillion in 2026:Q1; household and nonprofit net worth about $183.0 trillion.
  - Used to establish scale, not to imply that every household owns housing wealth or that gross real-estate value equals liquid retirement savings.

### College finance and outcomes

- Grey Gordon and Aaron Hedlund, *Accounting for the Rise in College Tuition*, NBER Working Paper 21967 (2016): https://www.nber.org/papers/w21967
  - A quantitative model attributing a large share of the 1987–2010 rise in net tuition to changes in the federal student loan program while finding little role for Baumol cost disease in that model.
  - This is one influential model, not a consensus estimate for every aid program, institution type, or period; the chapter explicitly keeps the Bennett-hypothesis evidence conditional.

- Federal Reserve Bank of New York, “Household Debt Balances Rise Slightly as Delinquency Transition Rates Hold Steady” (May 12, 2026): https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20260512
  - Student-loan balances about $1.66 trillion in 2026:Q1 and 10.3 percent of balances reported 90+ days delinquent.
  - The New York Fed warns that the return of delinquency reporting after the pandemic pause creates unusual timing effects; the manuscript states that caveat.

- Federal Reserve Bank of New York, “Federal Student Loan Defaults Return After Pandemic Pause” (May 12, 2026): https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/federal-student-loan-defaults-return-after-pandemic-pause/
  - Used for the repayment/reporting timeline and the lag between missed payments and federal default.

- U.S. Department of Education, College Scorecard technical documentation (September 2025): https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/files/InstitutionDataDocumentation.pdf
  - Scorecard institution-level data cover earnings, completion, cost, debt/repayment, and access. The chapter describes the Scorecard as a source of multidimensional information, not as a definitive ranking system.

- U.S. Department of Education, March 26, 2026 Scorecard/FAFSA transparency update: https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-celebrates-more-10-million-fafsar-forms-complete-and-additional-transparency-measures
  - Used only for the dated claim that the Department updated Scorecard data and FAFSA-related earnings information in March 2026. The partisan framing in the press release is not adopted by the manuscript.

### Hospital prices, bargaining, and transparency

- Christopher M. Whaley et al., RAND, *Prices Paid to Hospitals by Private Health Plans: Findings from Round 5.1 of an Employer-Led Transparency Initiative* (revised December 2024): https://www.rand.org/t/RRA1144-2-v2
  - Using 2020–2022 claims from participating employers and other contributors, finds average 2022 commercial hospital prices in the study sample at about 254 percent of simulated Medicare payments for the same services at the same facilities; outpatient facility prices averaged about 279 percent and associated professional services about 184 percent.
  - The study also reports wide state variation and finds hospital market power explains more price variation than hospitals' Medicare/Medicaid patient shares.
  - This is not a census of every privately insured claim in the United States; sample composition and Medicare's administered benchmark limit interpretation. The manuscript states both caveats.

- Federal Trade Commission, *Health Care Competition*: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/topics/competition-enforcement/health-care-competition
  - Used for the general claim that the FTC has repeatedly examined and challenged hospital, physician-practice, and other health-care consolidation where it believes competition may be harmed.

- Federal Trade Commission, “FTC Sues to Block Novant Health's Acquisition of Two Hospitals from Community Health Systems” (January 25, 2024): https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/01/ftc-sues-block-novant-healths-acquisition-two-hospitals-community-health-systems
  - One concrete example of the agency's theory that hospital consolidation can raise prices and reduce quality incentives; not evidence that every hospital merger has those effects.

## Claims to stress-test before later chapters

The draft proposes a recurring mechanism — price distance — rather than claiming a single cause for American costs. Later work should actively seek evidence that weakens or bounds the thesis.

- **Debt capacity:** How much does dollar reserve status, Treasury-market depth, U.S. monetary sovereignty, and investor demand expand sustainable federal borrowing capacity? At what point do interest costs materially reduce useful fiscal option value even without a market crisis?
- **Demand subsidies and supply:** In which housing, education, and health markets do subsidies primarily expand quantity or access rather than prices? The effect should not be assumed to be one-for-one.
- **Aid and tuition:** The empirical literature on whether federal student aid raises tuition is mixed by aid type, institution, and period. Chapter 7 intentionally treats the Bennett hypothesis as conditional rather than settled.
- **Health outcomes and innovation:** High U.S. medical spending must be compared with access, outcomes, wages, technology, pharmaceutical innovation, and population health. Administrative complexity, provider prices, and consolidation are important mechanisms, not complete explanations.
- **Hospital benchmarks:** Medicare prices are administered rates, not revealed competitive prices or a universal measure of cost. Commercial-to-Medicare ratios should be used as evidence of variation and bargaining outcomes, not as a claim that the Medicare price is always the correct price.
- **Housing supply:** Housing affordability is affected by land-use rules, construction costs, interest rates, migration, household formation, infrastructure, insurance, taxes, and local demand. Zoning is important in many high-demand markets but should not be written as a single national explanation.
- **Household solvency:** Household debt should be evaluated alongside assets, debt-service ratios, fixed versus variable rates, income distribution, delinquency, and household net worth. Gross balances alone do not establish fragility.
- **Public investment:** Borrowing for capacity-enhancing investments may improve rather than damage long-run fiscal space. Later chapters should distinguish productive investment from spending merely labeled investment.
- **Tax incidence:** A tax's legal payer is not necessarily its economic bearer. Distributional claims should distinguish statutory incidence from empirical incidence.
- **Automatic fiscal rules:** Rules can force earlier tradeoffs but can also create procyclicality, accounting games, or poor emergency responses. Any proposed rule needs escape clauses and institutional evidence.

## Falsification note

The book's thesis would need material revision if the evidence showed that price distance generally does not affect quantities, prices, political commitments, or borrowing behavior in the markets discussed; that supply responds rapidly enough to demand subsidies that added purchasing power rarely becomes price; or that federal debt service can rise indefinitely without meaningful tradeoffs in taxes, spending, growth, inflation, or crisis capacity.

The weaker and more defensible claim is the one the manuscript currently makes: separating a decision from its full price can be socially useful, but the system then needs another credible mechanism for discovering scarcity and saying no. The research should remain willing to narrow even that claim where the evidence does not travel.
