# Research notes — chapters 13–16

Accessed 2026-09-02. These notes extend the book-level research trail for the argument block on waste, progressive taxation, growth, and political adjustment. They are working notes for a Desk draft, not release-ready citations.

## Chapter 13 — Why Cutting Waste Isn't Enough

- U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Payment Integrity: Agencies' Estimated Improper Payments Increased to $186 Billion in Fiscal Year 2025* (GAO-26-108694, April 27, 2026): https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108694
  - Fifteen agencies estimated about $186 billion in improper payments across 64 programs in FY2025, up about $24 billion from the prior year; about $153 billion were estimated overpayments.
  - “Improper payment” is broader than fraud. It includes payments made in the wrong amount and payments lacking sufficient documentation, among other causes. The manuscript therefore does not equate the $186 billion estimate with recoverable fraud.
  - Even a hypothetical elimination of the full estimate would be much smaller than CBO's projected $1.9 trillion FY2026 deficit. That comparison is illustrative only: the figures cover different fiscal years and an improper-payment estimate is not an estimate of immediately realizable budget savings.

- Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* (February 2026): https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105
  - FY2026 baseline: about $7.4 trillion of outlays, $5.6 trillion of revenues, and a $1.9 trillion deficit.
  - Used to distinguish efficiency savings from the larger structural mismatch between lawful spending commitments and recurring revenue.

### Stress tests

- Eliminating waste can have second-order costs. Anti-fraud systems, auditors, procurement staff, modernized data systems, and contract management themselves cost money.
- Some low-utilization capacity is resilience rather than waste. The chapter should not judge preparedness, military redundancy, emergency stockpiles, or maintenance purely by utilization rates.
- The fiscal effect of ending a program is not always equal to its gross budget authority; termination costs, offsets, transfers to other jurisdictions, and replacement services can change net savings.

## Chapter 14 — Why Taxing the Rich Isn't Enough

- Internal Revenue Service, *The Tax Gap* / Tax Year 2022 projections: https://www.irs.gov/statistics/irs-the-tax-gap
  - Projected TY2022 gross tax gap: about $696 billion; projected net tax gap after enforced and other late payments: about $606 billion.
  - The gross gap includes nonfiling, underreporting, and underpayment across individual, corporate, employment, estate, and other federal taxes. It is not an estimate of taxes evaded by high-income households alone.
  - IRS notes important measurement limitations, especially for complex forms of income and activities for which compliance data are incomplete.

- U.S. Department of the Treasury, *Tax Expenditures*: https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/tax-policy/tax-expenditures
  - Useful background for large exclusions, preferential rates, and other tax-base choices that disproportionately matter to households with employer benefits, investment income, retirement assets, owner-occupied housing, and complex businesses.
  - Treasury explicitly warns that tax-expenditure estimates generally do not incorporate behavioral responses and should not be mechanically summed into repeal revenue.

- Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036*: https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105
  - Federal revenues are projected at 17.5 percent of GDP in 2026 and 17.8 percent in 2036 under current law, while outlays rise from 23.3 percent to 24.4 percent of GDP.
  - Used for the scale argument: substantial progressive revenue increases can materially improve the outlook without automatically financing every existing and proposed broad commitment.

### Stress tests

- “Not enough” does not mean “not useful.” The chapter deliberately rejects the inference that limits on a narrow tax base imply low taxes or weak progressivity.
- Revenue estimates must account for behavioral response, timing, avoidance, evasion, legal form, international mobility, and administrative capacity. Those responses vary by tax and do not imply that higher rates necessarily lose revenue.
- Corporate and tariff incidence should not be described solely by the entity legally remitting the tax. Workers, owners, customers, and foreign counterparties may share the economic burden.
- A strong progressive system can combine concentrated taxes at the top with broader taxes elsewhere; cross-country comparisons should control for benefits, tax mix, and institutional differences before drawing conclusions.

## Chapter 15 — Growth Is Necessary, Not Magical

- Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036*: https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105
  - CBO projects real GDP growth of 2.2 percent in 2026 and an average of about 1.8 percent per year from 2027 through 2036.
  - CBO identifies productivity gains, investment, labor supply, aging, immigration, tariffs, and federal debt/crowding out among the forces affecting growth.
  - CBO's 2026 baseline includes some productivity support from adoption of generative AI; the manuscript therefore does not treat AI-related productivity as an entirely omitted upside scenario.

- Congressional Budget Office, *Long-Term Budget Analysis* / 2026–2056 data: https://www.cbo.gov/topics/budget/long-term-budget-analysis
  - Used for the broader debt-dynamics point that higher growth improves fiscal capacity but does not by itself eliminate sustained primary deficits and rising interest costs under every scenario.

### Stress tests

- Aggregate GDP growth is not identical to household welfare. Distribution, housing costs, local infrastructure, labor-market adjustment, and ownership of productive assets affect how growth is experienced.
- A policy described as “investment” should not receive an assumed positive fiscal return. Public investments can create large spillovers, but project selection, execution, opportunity cost, and financing matter.
- Dynamic effects run both directions. Tax, spending, trade, immigration, regulatory, and industrial policies can raise or lower growth depending on design and context.
- Debt-to-GDP improvement depends on nominal growth, interest rates, primary balances, and debt maturity, not real GDP growth alone.

## Chapter 16 — The Politics of Visible Pain

This chapter is primarily institutional synthesis rather than a chapter driven by one current statistic. Its claims should be checked against public-choice, behavioral, and political-economy literature during revision.

Working mechanisms to test:

- concentrated benefits can create stronger political organization than diffuse costs;
- future taxpayers and hypothetical entrants are weakly represented in present decisions;
- loss aversion and status-quo expectations can make removal of an existing benefit feel larger than an equivalent foregone future benefit;
- gradual reforms can reduce transition shock when people have made long-lived plans under existing rules;
- automatic across-the-board cuts can avoid prioritization and therefore preserve low-value spending while damaging high-value capacity;
- compensation or grandfathering can improve political durability but can also become a new permanent cost.

Potential revision sources include classic public-choice work on concentrated benefits/diffuse costs, behavioral research on loss aversion and reference dependence, and empirical studies of pension, base-closure, trade-adjustment, and land-use reforms. The chapter currently keeps these mechanisms qualitative rather than attaching false precision to them.

## Falsification and narrowing notes for the four-chapter block

The argument should be narrowed if evidence shows any of the following:

- improper-payment reduction can close a much larger share of the structural federal gap than current estimates suggest without significant implementation costs or service tradeoffs;
- a realistically enforceable set of taxes concentrated only on a very small high-income population can durably finance the full scale of broad U.S. commitments with limited avoidance, incidence shifting, or economic response;
- plausible sustained productivity growth alone stabilizes the debt path under current-law spending and revenue commitments without requiring materially different primary balances;
- political systems do not systematically weight visible/concentrated losses differently from diffuse, delayed, or counterfactual benefits.

The book does not need all four mechanisms to be equally strong. Its narrower claim is that no single painless lever—waste reduction, concentrated taxation, or assumed growth—can substitute for an institution that makes recurring public commitments confront recurring resource costs while choices are still reversible.
