# Chapter 4 research audit: the honest bill and automatic stabilizers

Checked September 15, 2026.

## Why this passage deserves pressure

Chapter 4, “The Honest Bill,” makes a useful distinction between borrowing for a pandemic, war, severe recession, or some long-lived investments and borrowing caused by a routine inability to reconcile recurring spending with recurring revenue. The distinction is directionally right, but it can sound cleaner than the federal budget actually is. A recession does not wait for Congress to label a tranche of debt “emergency borrowing.” Parts of the budget automatically widen the deficit when the economy weakens and narrow it when the economy strengthens.

The strongest current source is CBO’s August 2026 report on automatic stabilizers. CBO defines them as revenue and spending changes that occur automatically with cyclical movements in output and unemployment: tax receipts fall when incomes and profits fall, while unemployment insurance, Medicaid, and SNAP spending can rise as more people qualify. Those movements support private spending without a new legislative vote.

Primary source: Congressional Budget Office, *Effects of Automatic Stabilizers on the Federal Budget: 2026 to 2036* (August 2026): https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62741

Baseline context: Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* (February 2026): https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882

## Current evidence

CBO estimates that automatic stabilizers increased federal deficits by an average of about 0.3 percent of potential GDP per year from 1976 through 2025, mainly because of six recessions. In its current forecast they decrease deficits by an average of 0.2 percent of potential GDP from 2026 through 2029, when projected output is above potential, then increase deficits by about 0.1 percent of potential GDP from 2030 through 2036.

That reversal is useful for the manuscript because the same institutional machinery can either widen or narrow a deficit depending on the economy. “Borrowing for a recession” is therefore partly an automatic property of the tax-and-transfer system, not only a discretionary choice Congress makes after identifying an emergency.

More importantly, CBO estimates that deficits *without* the effects of automatic stabilizers average 6.1 percent of potential GDP from 2026 through 2036, compared with a 3.7 percent average for cyclically adjusted deficits over the previous 50 years. By 2036, the cyclically adjusted deficit is 6.6 percent of potential GDP. CBO’s February baseline likewise projects a $1.9 trillion total deficit in 2026 rising to $3.1 trillion in 2036, with debt held by the public rising from 101 percent to 120 percent of GDP.

This is unusually clean evidence for the chapter’s deeper claim: stripping out the ordinary fiscal response to the business cycle does not make the projected structural gap disappear.

## Strongest counterargument

The chapter risks implying that policymakers can sort deficits into morally legible buckets—good emergency borrowing and bad routine borrowing. In practice, fiscal policy is not so separable. Automatic stabilizers deliberately allow revenue and spending to move against the cycle. Discretionary recession measures can also have effects that persist after a downturn, and policies enacted for structural reasons can become temporarily more expensive during recessions. Conversely, a balanced or shrinking deficit during an expansion is not automatically evidence of sound structural policy if cyclical strength is doing part of the work.

CBO also cautions that its cyclically adjusted estimates do not remove every cyclical influence perfectly. Large stock-market movements, for example, affect capital-gains revenue but are not treated as primarily cyclical in its automatic-stabilizer estimate. The adjusted deficit is therefore an analytical estimate, not an observable “true structural deficit.”

## Defensible boundary

The chapter should not argue that borrowing is honest only when Congress can attach an emergency label to it. The stronger boundary is:

**A useful budget lets automatic stabilizers and genuine emergency policy absorb temporary shocks while making the underlying fiscal position visible. The test of a structural spending problem is not whether the headline deficit rises in a recession; it is whether a large financing gap remains after ordinary cyclical effects are stripped away.**

That formulation lets countercyclical fiscal policy succeed at its intended job rather than treating every deficit dollar alike. It also makes the manuscript’s central concern more empirical: CBO’s current cyclically adjusted projections remain historically large even after the business cycle is removed as an explanation.

## Editorial opportunity

A later surgical prose pass could replace the current short sequence—“A pandemic is a reason to borrow. A major war is a reason to borrow. A severe recession can be a reason to borrow.”—with one compact paragraph that distinguishes automatic cyclical borrowing from discretionary emergency borrowing. The August 2026 CBO result can then carry the argument: the United States can allow the budget to cushion a downturn without pretending that a roughly 6-percent-of-potential-GDP cyclically adjusted deficit is itself a recession response.

That would also reduce the chapter’s repeated one-sentence declarative rhythm while preserving its core idea that fiscal capacity is valuable precisely because it can be used when shocks arrive.
