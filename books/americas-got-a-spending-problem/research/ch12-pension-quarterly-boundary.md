# Chapter 12 research audit — pension measurement boundary

Accessed September 15, 2026.

## Editorial question

Chapter 12 uses the Federal Reserve Financial Accounts to make a good conceptual point: a government can balance its annual cash budget while carrying compensation obligations earned in earlier years. The current manuscript cites 2026:Q1 and says the Fed's state-and-local pension-fund table records about $2.7 trillion as a "claim of pension fund on sponsor" and about $9.6 trillion in accrued defined-benefit pension entitlements.

That passage is now one quarter stale. More importantly, the newly released 2026:Q2 data supply a useful counterexample to any prose that lets the unfunded number feel like a fixed debt meter.

## Fresh Federal Reserve check

The Federal Reserve updated table S.129.a / S129.3.s on September 11, 2026. For state and local government employee pension funds, it reports:

- 2026:Q1 claims of pension fund on sponsor: $2.7736 trillion.
- 2026:Q2 claims of pension fund on sponsor: $2.3537 trillion.
- 2026:Q1 defined-benefit pension entitlements: $9.5511 trillion.
- 2026:Q2 defined-benefit pension entitlements: $9.6106 trillion.
- 2026:Q1 defined-benefit entitlements funded by assets: $6.7775 trillion.
- 2026:Q2 defined-benefit entitlements funded by assets: $7.2569 trillion.

Primary source:

- Federal Reserve Board, *Financial Accounts of the United States*, table S129.3.s, state and local government employee pension funds, last updated September 11, 2026: https://www.federalreserve.gov/Releases/z1/current/html/S129_3_s.htm

The Fed's own note defines the sponsor claim as unfunded defined-benefit pension entitlements and says negative values would indicate overfunding. It defines pension entitlements as the actuarial value of projected pension entitlements.

## Why the quarter-to-quarter move matters

The sponsor claim fell by about $420 billion between Q1 and Q2 even as defined-benefit entitlements rose by about $60 billion. The mechanical reason visible in the table is that assets funding defined-benefit promises rose sharply over the quarter. Corporate-equity values alone rose by more than $400 billion across state and local pension funds between Q1 and Q2.

This is not evidence that governments suddenly paid off $420 billion of pension promises in three months. It is evidence that this national-accounting measure is a market-sensitive balance-sheet estimate. Asset prices can move the measured unfunded amount dramatically even when benefit formulas and contribution policy barely change.

That volatility strengthens the chapter's existing warning that the Fed number is a map of timing rather than a check due tomorrow. A later prose pass should use the fresh Q2 figure if it wants a current snapshot, but it should also tell the reader that the snapshot can move hundreds of billions with asset valuations.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest objection to the chapter's pension framing is that an aggregate unfunded-liability number can exaggerate the sense of an immediate fiscal hole. Public pensions are long-duration institutions. Benefits are paid over decades; plans receive future employee and employer contributions; asset values fluctuate; and national-accounting discount conventions differ from plan actuarial assumptions. A sponsor claim is therefore not equivalent to bonded debt due on demand.

The Q1-to-Q2 move is unusually useful counterevidence because it demonstrates the point inside the same official series the chapter relies on. If the manuscript presents one quarter's unfunded amount as though it were a stable stock of unpaid invoices, it overstates what the measure can support.

That objection does not defeat the chapter's structural argument. The Fed still records an unfunded sponsor claim because accrued defined-benefit promises exceed the assets assigned to fund them under its methodology. The defensible claim is about timing and funding discipline: annual cash balance does not establish that compensation earned this year has been fully financed.

## Recommended manuscript boundary

A later surgical prose pass should:

1. update the current snapshot to 2026:Q2 if the chapter retains a latest-quarter number;
2. use the roughly $420 billion Q1-to-Q2 decline to show that the sponsor claim is market-sensitive rather than a fixed debt counter;
3. preserve the distinction between the Fed's national-accounting measure and individual plans' actuarial funding reports;
4. keep the argument focused on whether earned compensation is funded as it accrues, not on treating the aggregate sponsor claim as an immediately payable bill.

A compact reader-facing formulation could make the volatility do argumentative work: the Fed's measure fell from about $2.77 trillion to $2.35 trillion in one quarter, largely as asset values rose. No legislature found $420 billion under a sofa cushion. The balance-sheet estimate moved. The obligation to distinguish earned promises from funded assets did not.

## Claim discipline

Safe claims:

- The Fed records unfunded state and local defined-benefit pension entitlements as claims of pension funds on sponsors.
- The 2026:Q2 sponsor claim was about $2.35 trillion under the Fed's national-accounting methodology.
- The sponsor claim fell by roughly $420 billion from Q1 to Q2 while defined-benefit entitlements increased.
- The series is sensitive to asset valuations and is not equivalent to a cash payment due immediately.
- Annual balanced-budget compliance does not by itself establish that long-duration pension compensation is fully funded.

Claims to avoid:

- State and local governments "owe $2.35 trillion now" in the same sense as a matured bond payment.
- The Q2 decline proves pension funding policy improved by $420 billion.
- One national aggregate describes the solvency or funding policy of every state and local pension plan.
- Market gains eliminate the need for contribution discipline.

## Why this strengthens the chapter

The manuscript already includes caveats about discount rates and accounting assumptions, but they remain abstract beside a very large headline number. The September 2026 release turns the caveat into a concrete event: the official unfunded measure moved by hundreds of billions in one quarter. Using that movement would make the chapter both more current and more intellectually honest. It lets the strongest skeptical objection change the presentation while preserving the chapter's deeper point that a balanced annual budget can coexist with promises whose financing lives outside the annual cash frame.
