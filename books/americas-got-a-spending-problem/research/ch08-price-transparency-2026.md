# Chapter 8 research audit: price transparency after the 2026 rule changes

Accessed 2026-09-15. This is a bounded research audit for Chapter 8, “The Hospital Price Nobody Knows.” It does not change manuscript prose.

## Why this passage is worth revisiting

The chapter’s core description remains strong: hospital prices are often relationships among providers, payers, plans, settings, codes, and contractual rules rather than a single retail tag. Its transparency section, however, now understates how much the federal disclosure regime has changed since the original 2021 launch. A later prose pass should not leave the reader with the impression that the policy experiment is still merely “publish enormous files and hope somebody cleans them.”

The more interesting 2026 story is that CMS itself has responded to the usability and accuracy problem by making the files more standardized and by requiring additional actual allowed-amount information in circumstances where negotiated charges are expressed as percentages or algorithms.

## Current primary-source facts

### The scale figures in the opening remain current

CMS’s current National Health Expenditure fact sheet reports 2024 national health expenditures of $5.3 trillion and hospital expenditures of $1.6347 trillion. Hospital spending grew 8.9 percent in 2024.

Source: CMS, *NHE Fact Sheet*: https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/national-health-expenditure-data/nhe-fact-sheet

Boundary: national hospital expenditure is an aggregate spending measure. It does not by itself identify whether growth came from prices, utilization, case mix, population, wages, technology, consolidation, or another mechanism.

### Hospital transparency still requires more than a sticker price

CMS currently requires hospitals to publish a comprehensive machine-readable file containing standard charges, including gross charges, discounted cash prices, payer-specific negotiated charges, and de-identified minimum and maximum negotiated charges, as well as consumer-facing information for shoppable services.

Source: CMS, *Hospitals — Hospital Price Transparency*: https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/hospital-price-transparency/hospitals

This supports the chapter’s deeper point: the disclosure regime itself has to represent several different notions of “price.” The rule did not discover one hidden universal price and order hospitals to print it.

### The 2026 rules materially changed the data

The CY2026 OPPS/ASC final rule requires hospitals, when payer-specific negotiated charges are based on a percentage or algorithm, to publish actual-dollar allowed-amount statistics derived from remittance data: the median, 10th percentile, 90th percentile, and count of allowed amounts. CMS requires a 12-to-15-month lookback period and an affirmation concerning completeness and accuracy. The provisions took effect January 1, 2026; CMS began enforcement of the revised requirements April 1, 2026.

Source: CMS, *CY 2026 OPPS and Ambulatory Surgical Center Final Rule — Hospital Price Transparency Policy Changes*: https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/cy-2026-opps-ambulatory-surgical-center-final-rule-hospital-price-transparency-policy-changes

CMS’s current Hospital Price Transparency page also says it is already seeking comment in the CY2027 rulemaking on further standardization, comparability, accuracy, and completeness of the data.

Source: CMS, *Hospital Price Transparency*, page last modified August 21, 2026: https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/hospital-price-transparency

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter’s current framing

The strongest objection is no longer simply that transparency data are messy. The federal government has spent several years standardizing the files, tightening enforcement, requiring attestations, and, as of 2026, adding remittance-derived allowed-amount statistics for some contract structures. Researchers, employers, plans, regulators, and data vendors can therefore do things with the disclosures that were much harder at launch.

A chapter that says transparency “was supposed to create” a counterweight and then dwells mainly on unusable files risks freezing the policy at an early implementation stage. The empirical question should now be whether increasingly standardized disclosure changes bargaining, steering, employer purchasing, network design, or patient decisions enough to affect prices or spending—not whether the files exist.

There is also a conceptual counterargument. Price opacity is not always the dominant reason a patient cannot shop. Emergency status, physician affiliation, network design, clinical uncertainty, local provider concentration, and the need for standby capacity can make a perfectly disclosed price economically non-actionable. More legible data cannot manufacture a competing trauma center.

## What survives the counterargument

The chapter’s institutional-buyer thesis becomes stronger, not weaker. Hospital transparency is increasingly an information infrastructure for buyers and intermediaries rather than a plausible attempt to turn a frightened patient into a retail shopper at the point of care.

The defensible boundary is:

**Price transparency can reduce an information problem. It cannot by itself solve a bargaining-power, market-structure, clinical-substitutability, or emergency-timing problem. Its strongest use may be upstream, where employers, plans, regulators, and researchers can act on price differences before a patient is on a gurney.**

That formulation gives the reform credit for real institutional learning while preserving the chapter’s core argument about price distance.

## Suggested later prose move

Do not add another policy catalogue. Replace the current “making data public and making it usable” passage with a compact 2021-to-2026 progression: the government first forced the negotiated prices into daylight; the resulting files exposed a second problem—comparability and accuracy—so CMS standardized the format and began requiring remittance-derived allowed-amount statistics for some contracts. Then ask what transparency still cannot do when the buyer lacks a credible alternative provider.

That sequence gives the reader an observable institutional learning process and leads naturally back to market power.

## Claim boundary / defeat condition

This audit does not establish that the 2026 changes lowered hospital prices, improved patient outcomes, or materially changed employer bargaining. A later manuscript claim about effects should require outcome evidence rather than inferring success from a better disclosure rule. Conversely, evidence that transparency has limited effects would not establish that the information is worthless; it could instead show that opacity was not the binding constraint in concentrated or non-shoppable markets.
