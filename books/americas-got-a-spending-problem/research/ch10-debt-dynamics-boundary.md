# Chapter 10 research audit — debt dynamics, refinancing, and what r < g does not buy

Accessed 2026-09-15. This note stress-tests Chapter 10, “Interest: The Bill for Yesterday.” It does not change manuscript prose.

## Claim under review

The chapter correctly treats federal debt as a portfolio that reprices gradually rather than one giant loan, and it correctly introduces the relationship between the effective interest rate and nominal economic growth. The vulnerable step is the implication a reader may take from that shorthand: that if growth exceeds the effective interest rate, the debt ratio can take care of itself.

That is not the debt arithmetic. The primary balance still matters. A favorable interest-growth differential can reduce the primary surplus required to stabilize debt, or permit some primary deficit without a rising debt ratio, but it does not make an arbitrarily large recurring primary deficit sustainable.

## Fresh authoritative evidence

### CBO 2026 baseline: favorable-looking ingredients can coexist with a rising debt ratio

Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* (February 2026):
https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105

CBO projects debt held by the public rising from 101 percent of GDP in 2026 to 120 percent in 2036. Over the same period, the primary deficit remains persistent: 2.6 percent of GDP in 2026 and 2.1 percent in 2036. Net interest rises from 3.3 percent to 4.6 percent of GDP.

This is the cleanest current corrective to an overly casual r-versus-g story. Debt dynamics are jointly determined by the inherited debt stock, the effective financing rate, nominal GDP growth, the primary balance, and stock-flow adjustments. The sign of r minus g is important; it is not the whole equation.

CBO also notes that its 2026–2036 baseline contains unusually large deficits during a period in which unemployment is projected to remain below 5 percent. In the 14 years from 1976 through 2025 when unemployment was below 5 percent, primary deficits averaged 0.5 percent of GDP; the current baseline averages roughly 2.1 percent. That comparison supports the chapter’s distinction between emergency smoothing and a recurring structural gap without requiring a claim that any one debt ratio is a crisis threshold.

Boundary: CBO’s baseline is a conditional projection under laws in place on January 14, 2026, not a forecast that Congress will leave policy unchanged for a decade. Its interest and growth assumptions can be wrong in either direction.

### Treasury August 2026 refunding: the opening scene is accurate

U.S. Department of the Treasury, Quarterly Refunding Statement, August 5, 2026:
https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0590

Treasury offered $125 billion of 3-, 10-, and 30-year securities to refund approximately $96.3 billion of privately held notes and bonds maturing August 15 and raise approximately $28.7 billion of new cash. The chapter’s distinction between gross issuance and incremental new financing is therefore accurate and worth preserving.

Treasury, *Marketable Borrowing Estimates*, August 3, 2026:
https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0584

Treasury estimated $739 billion of privately held net marketable borrowing for July–September 2026 and $628 billion for October–December 2026, conditional on specified quarter-end cash balances. Treasury’s own footnote distinguishes net marketable borrowing from rollovers associated with Federal Reserve SOMA holdings. This reinforces the chapter’s useful warning that a large gross auction is not itself evidence of a financing crisis.

Treasury’s debt-management statement describes its objective as financing the government at the lowest cost over time through regular and predictable issuance while managing uncertainty in borrowing needs and demand:
https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/financing-the-government

That is a better institutional description than treating maturity choice as a simple short-cheap/long-expensive trade. Debt managers are balancing expected cost, rollover exposure, market functioning, investor demand, and issuance regularity under uncertainty.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest objection to the chapter is not that interest costs are irrelevant. It is that the phrase “debt service places a prior claim on future national income” can sound more legally or economically rigid than the federal budget actually is.

Treasury securities are binding obligations, and default would be extraordinarily consequential. But “contractual seniority” is not a complete description of federal budget priority. Congress still controls taxes and most spending through law; the government can refinance maturing principal; inflation changes real burdens; and monetary and fiscal conditions alter the opportunity cost of servicing debt. The important constraint is not that interest literally sits ahead of every program in a corporate capital stack. It is that honoring debt absorbs fiscal resources and that the cost of doing so is increasingly outside the discretion of the Congress writing the current year’s program budget.

A later prose pass should therefore prefer “less discretionary” or “a contractual financing obligation inherited from earlier borrowing” to an unqualified seniority metaphor.

## What survives

The chapter’s central argument survives and becomes more precise:

- refinancing causes market rates to reach the budget with a lag because the debt portfolio has a maturity structure;
- large gross issuance can be routine even while persistent net borrowing worsens the fiscal path;
- r relative to g changes the burden of stabilizing debt but does not substitute for the primary balance;
- there is no universal debt-to-GDP danger threshold that works independently of rates, growth, currency, institutions, maturity, investor demand, and the primary balance;
- interest is a real financing cost inherited from earlier fiscal choices, but describing it as literal corporate-style seniority over all other federal spending overstates the legal analogy.

The strongest reader-facing formulation is therefore not “growth beats interest, so debt shrinks” or “interest always comes first.” It is: **a favorable interest-growth differential buys fiscal room; the primary balance decides how much of that room is used.**

## Suggested later prose use

The chapter already has a strong opening in the August 2026 refunding. Keep it. The higher-value revision is in the middle: after introducing r versus g, add the missing primary-balance term in ordinary language and use CBO’s current baseline as the counterexample. Then soften the later “contractual seniority” passage so the metaphor does not imply a corporate payment waterfall that federal budgeting does not actually have.

This would improve the argument without adding another framework or another long example. One current baseline can do both jobs: show that debt arithmetic has more than two variables and preserve the chapter’s larger point that persistent structural deficits consume future fiscal room even when auctions remain calm.

## Defeat / narrowing condition

The chapter should be narrowed further if evidence shows that the projected rise in interest costs is mostly an artifact of a particular rate path rather than robust to plausible alternative assumptions. In that case the book should emphasize sensitivity and optionality rather than treating the baseline interest path as a likely realized bill. The structural point would still depend on the range of plausible paths, not on one point estimate.
