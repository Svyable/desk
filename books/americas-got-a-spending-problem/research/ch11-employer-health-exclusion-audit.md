# Chapter 11 research audit — employer-sponsored health exclusion

Accessed September 13, 2026.

## Editorial question

Chapter 11 is strongest when it treats tax expenditures as policy embedded in the tax system rather than as a pot of cash that could simply be recovered by repeal. The employer-sponsored health-insurance exclusion is the chapter's best test case because it has large fiscal effects, changes the relative price of health benefits and cash compensation, and is deeply entangled with how Americans obtain coverage.

The revision risk is causal overstatement. It is supportable to say the tax exclusion helps sustain and shape employment-based insurance. It is harder to say that the exclusion alone created the employment-based system, that its published tax-expenditure estimate is equivalent to obtainable repeal revenue, or that reducing it would leave insurance coverage and other federal programs unchanged.

## What current law actually does

Section 106 of the Internal Revenue Code states that, except as otherwise provided, an employee's gross income does not include employer-provided coverage under an accident or health plan. The exclusion therefore creates a tax differential between compensation delivered as qualifying health coverage and compensation delivered as ordinary taxable wages.

Primary/statutory source:

- 26 U.S.C. § 106, "Contributions by employer to accident and health plans": https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title26-section106
- Readable mirror: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/106

This supports the chapter's basic mechanism. The tax code does not merely measure an already-existing employment benefit differently; it changes the after-tax price of health benefits relative to cash compensation.

## What CBO says the incentive changes

CBO's current budget-option analysis gives the chapter a more precise causal spine than the generic claim that the preference "shapes the system." CBO says the exclusion encourages firms to offer more generous benefit packages and encourages workers to enroll in employment-based insurance rather than obtain coverage in the nongroup market. It also encourages plans with lower cost sharing, more covered services, and broader provider networks.

CBO further says that limiting the exclusion would cause some employers to offer less expensive plans, some employers to stop offering coverage, and some workers to decline employment-based coverage. In CBO and JCT's modeled option, many people leaving employer coverage would move to the nongroup market, Medicaid, or CHIP, while some would become uninsured. Those responses partly offset the federal revenue gain.

Authoritative source:

- Congressional Budget Office, "Reduce Tax Subsidies for Employment-Based Health Benefits" (December 2024): https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/60953
- Earlier extended discussion, "Reduce Tax Subsidies for Employment-Based Health Insurance" (December 2022): https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/58627

Editorial implication: do not describe the exclusion as merely a hidden transfer to people who would otherwise buy exactly the same insurance in exactly the same market. The policy changes plan design, employer behavior, worker enrollment, and the distribution of coverage across public and private systems.

## Strongest counterargument to a simple reform story

The strongest counterargument is not that the exclusion has no distortions. CBO itself identifies them. The stronger objection is that the existing distortion is now part of the coverage architecture.

A limit on the exclusion can reduce incentives for unusually expensive or generous employer plans and can increase taxable compensation. But CBO also expects fewer offers of employment-based insurance and lower take-up, with some people moving into subsidized marketplace coverage or public programs and some becoming uninsured. The budget effect is therefore a system response, not the face value of a tax-expenditure table.

That matters for the book's larger argument. A policy can create price distance and still be costly to unwind because households, employers, insurers, labor contracts, and public programs have reorganized around it. The right comparison is not "tax expenditure versus zero." It is the existing system versus a specified alternative with its own coverage, distributional, labor-market, and fiscal consequences.

## Distribution also complicates the word subsidy

CBO notes that the exclusion generally provides larger tax subsidies to people with higher incomes, higher marginal tax rates, or more expensive health plans. That supports the manuscript's concern that tax expenditures can distribute benefits differently from a flat direct program.

But the distributional critique should remain separate from the coverage critique. A preference can be regressive in tax value while still supporting risk pooling or coverage for people who would be harmed by abrupt repeal. Those propositions can both be true.

Source:

- Congressional Budget Office, "Reduce Tax Subsidies for Employment-Based Health Insurance" (2022): https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/58627

## What the tax-expenditure estimates do and do not mean

The manuscript already handles this well and should preserve the distinction. OMB defines tax expenditures relative to specified baseline tax systems and warns that estimates for individual provisions should not simply be added to infer the revenue obtainable from simultaneous repeal. Behavioral responses and interactions among provisions matter.

JCT independently estimates federal tax expenditures and likewise treats them as departures from its specified tax-law baseline, not as a mechanically collectible shadow appropriation.

Primary/official sources:

- Office of Management and Budget, *Analytical Perspectives, Budget of the U.S. Government, Fiscal Year 2027*, chapter 10, "Tax Expenditure Estimates": https://www.whitehouse.gov/omb/information-resources/budget/analytical-perspectives/
- Joint Committee on Taxation, *Estimates of Federal Tax Expenditures for Fiscal Years 2025–2029*, JCX-45-25 (December 3, 2025): https://www.jct.gov/publications/2025/jcx-45-25/

Editorial implication: retain the chapter's phrase that the estimates are a map of policy embedded in the tax system, not a repeal menu. That is both more accurate and more interesting than treating all forgone revenue as recoverable cash.

## Historical claim boundary

The draft currently says the exclusion "helps explain why American health insurance became so strongly tied to employment" and later says it "helped shape the system itself." Those formulations are appropriately narrower than the common story that one wartime tax decision created employer-sponsored insurance.

Keep them narrow. The growth of employment-based coverage reflects several interacting forces, including employer group purchasing, collective bargaining, wartime and postwar labor-market institutions, tax treatment, later federal statutes, and the development of private insurance markets. The current audit does not establish a single historical share for any one mechanism.

If a later prose pass wants to tell the origin story in detail, research the wartime wage-control record, the administrative tax rulings preceding codification, collective-bargaining developments, and the 1954 statutory history directly before assigning causal weight. Do not rely on the compressed textbook version as a scene.

## Recommended manuscript change

A later prose pass should make one bounded revision in the employer-health section rather than expand the whole chapter:

1. Keep the concrete OMB scale estimate already in the chapter.
2. Replace the broadest language about the exclusion "shaping" job-based insurance with CBO's more observable mechanisms: employer offers, plan generosity, worker take-up, and substitution toward other coverage.
3. Put the counterfactual on the page: limiting the exclusion can reduce health spending and increase revenue while also moving people across insurance arrangements and leaving some uninsured.
4. End the passage on the system-design problem rather than the moral category of "subsidy": once an incentive becomes infrastructure, reform has to price the transition as well as the preference.

## Claim discipline for the prose pass

Safe claims:

- Employer-provided accident and health coverage receives preferential federal tax treatment under current law.
- The exclusion changes incentives around compensation, employer plan design, and enrollment in employment-based coverage.
- CBO expects limits on the exclusion to affect health spending, employer offers, worker take-up, federal revenues, public-program enrollment, marketplace subsidies, and uninsurance.
- Tax-expenditure estimates are baseline-dependent and are not equivalent to mechanically obtainable repeal revenue.

Claims to avoid without additional historical work:

- Wartime wage controls alone created employer-sponsored health insurance.
- The tax exclusion alone explains the modern employment-based system.
- The published tax-expenditure estimate is the amount the Treasury would collect if the provision vanished.
- Reducing the exclusion would simply convert health benefits into equal taxable wages with no coverage or program effects.

## Why this strengthens the chapter

The chapter's best argument is not that tax expenditures are secretly identical to appropriations. It is that policy form changes visibility, incentives, constituencies, and the difficulty of comparison. The employer-health exclusion becomes a stronger example when the book admits that the preference is both distortion and infrastructure. That complication makes the chapter harder to dismiss from either ideological direction and better supports its closing demand: compare instruments by what they buy, whom they reach, and what changes when the price signal moves.
