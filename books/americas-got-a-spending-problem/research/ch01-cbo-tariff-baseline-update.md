# Chapter 1 — tariff baseline update

Checked September 5, 2026.

Chapter 1 opens with the Congressional Budget Office's February 11, 2026 baseline: about $7.4 trillion in outlays, $5.6 trillion in revenues, and a $1.9 trillion deficit for fiscal year 2026. Those figures were correct for that baseline and should remain dated rather than silently replaced.

A later CBO update is useful counterevidence for the chapter's framing because it shows that the fiscal gap is not mechanically a spending-only story.

## CBO's August 2026 update

Primary source: Congressional Budget Office, **"CBO's Updated Budgetary Projections of Tariffs as of July 31, 2026"** (August 20, 2026), https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62704

Related current-year check: Congressional Budget Office, **"Monthly Budget Review: July 2026"** (August 2026), https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61983

CBO reports that trade-policy changes through July 31, 2026 increase projected total deficits by about **$0.9 trillion over fiscal years 2027–2036** relative to the February 2026 baseline. That change includes about $0.7 trillion in larger primary deficits and $0.2 trillion in additional debt-service costs.

For fiscal year 2026 specifically, CBO's July review now estimates a deficit of about **$2.1 trillion**, roughly $200 billion larger than the February projection. CBO expects outlays to remain close to the February baseline; the revision is mainly on the revenue side. Net customs revenues are projected to be about $250 billion below the February estimate, partly offset by roughly $75 billion more in individual income and payroll tax collections.

The principal reason for the tariff revision is legal and policy change after the February baseline. On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled that tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act could not continue under that authority. The Administration terminated those tariffs and later used other statutory authorities for replacement tariffs. CBO estimates that the replacement regime raises substantially less revenue than the February baseline assumed.

## Why this matters for the chapter

The stronger version of Chapter 1's argument is not that federal deficits are simply the consequence of spending being too high. The budget is the difference between spending and revenue, and both sides can change materially because of legislation, administration, courts, economic behavior, interest rates, and tax policy.

The February baseline itself already contained this lesson. CBO estimated that higher tariffs then in force reduced projected deficits by about $3.0 trillion over 2026–2035 relative to its January 2025 baseline, even after accounting for their economic and debt-service effects. The August update then partially reversed that improvement after the tariff authority and policy mix changed.

That sequence is useful because it makes the book's broader thesis more rigorous: fiscal choices are real tradeoffs, but there is no single immutable "receipt." A budget baseline is a dated conditional model of laws and policies in force at the time.

## Strongest counterargument / boundary

Tariff revenue is not free fiscal repair. CBO estimates that higher tariffs can reduce federal borrowing while also raising prices temporarily and reducing real investment and real GDP. Revenue effects also depend on import behavior, exemptions, retaliation, legal authority, and future policy changes. The correct inference is therefore not "tariffs solve the deficit." It is narrower: **revenue policy can materially move the fiscal path, and the economic cost of raising that revenue matters.**

Likewise, the August revision does not invalidate the manuscript's February figures. It dates them. If the chapter is revised later, preserve the distinction between the February baseline and subsequent CBO updates rather than replacing one with the other as though CBO had made an error.
