# Chapter 14 — Revenue scale and the meaning of “enough”

Accessed 2026-09-15.

## Claim under test

Chapter 14 argues that substantially higher taxes on wealthy households and corporations can improve revenue and fairness without, by themselves, resolving a structural fiscal gap created by broad recurring commitments. The argument is directionally sound, but the current prose risks making “taxing the rich” sound smaller than the evidence warrants because it relies mainly on verbal scale comparisons rather than a current budget denominator.

The useful question is not whether progressive taxation can raise a lot of money. It can. The question is what claim follows from that fact.

## Current primary-source denominator

CBO's February 2026 baseline projects federal outlays of about $7.4 trillion (23.3 percent of GDP) and revenues of about $5.6 trillion (17.5 percent of GDP) in FY2026, leaving a $1.9 trillion deficit. By 2036, outlays rise to 24.4 percent of GDP while revenues reach 17.8 percent. CBO attributes the spending-side rise principally to Social Security, Medicare, and net interest, partly offset by declining discretionary outlays relative to GDP.

Source: Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* (February 2026).
https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105

The same baseline is useful on the revenue side because it shows where federal money already comes from. Individual income taxes are projected at 8.6 percent of GDP in 2026 and 9.1 percent in 2036. Payroll taxes remain about 5.7 percent of GDP; corporate income taxes are projected around 1.2–1.3 percent. Whatever distribution Congress chooses within those bases, the existing federal system already depends heavily on taxes collected across large populations and large income flows.

CBO also notes that the 2025 reconciliation act reduced projected revenues by $461 billion in 2026 and $4.9 trillion over 2026–2035 relative to the prior-law baseline, with most of that reduction coming from permanently extending provisions of the 2017 tax law that had been scheduled to expire. That is important counterevidence to any implication that the current fiscal gap is purely a spending artifact: tax-law choices can materially widen or narrow it.

## Strongest counterargument

The chapter title can invite a straw-man comparison: take one politically familiar tax increase, compare it with the entire federal deficit, declare it “not enough,” and infer that concentrated taxation is fiscally marginal. That inference would be wrong.

A sufficiently broad package aimed at high incomes, capital income, estates, business income, tax preferences, and enforcement could raise substantial revenue. The existing research trail also records a projected TY2022 gross tax gap of roughly $696 billion, although that figure covers the whole tax system and is not fully collectible revenue. Progressive revenue policy can therefore be consequential even when no single provision closes the deficit.

The opposite overstatement is also possible. The federal government's largest recurring revenue streams are broad because the government's largest recurring commitments are broad. In CBO's 2026 baseline, payroll taxes alone are about 5.7 percent of GDP and individual income taxes about 8.6 percent. A durable financing system for broad social insurance cannot be analyzed solely by asking how much can be extracted from a tiny population under one tax instrument; it has to specify the bases, rates, behavioral response, administration, incidence, and political durability of the full package.

The defensible boundary is therefore:

**Progressive taxation can do much more fiscal work than a token surtax, and current deficits reflect revenue choices as well as spending choices. But “tax the rich” is a distributional principle, not yet a financing plan. A financing plan has to name the tax bases and rules capable of producing the required recurring revenue at the scale and duration of the commitments.**

## Editorial implication

A later surgical prose pass should let the strongest progressive case get larger before applying the word “enough.” Replace some of the chapter's early succession of short declarative sentences with one current scale paragraph: $7.4 trillion of outlays, $5.6 trillion of revenues, and the existing mix of individual, payroll, and corporate taxes. Then state explicitly that the 2025 tax law itself materially reduced projected revenue, so the book is not defining every deficit increase as a spending increase.

That would improve both fairness and narrative movement. The reader first sees that tax policy can move trillions over a decade; only then does the chapter ask what durable tax architecture can finance broad durable promises. The chapter's strongest idea is not that rich households are fiscally unimportant. It is that assigning the bill and determining the size of the bill are different questions.

## Source-handling notes

- Treat CBO's February 2026 figures as a baseline projection, not a forecast immune to later policy changes.
- Do not mechanically compare the $4.9 trillion ten-year revenue effect of the 2025 reconciliation act with a one-year deficit; periods and baselines differ.
- Do not treat the IRS gross tax gap as collectible cash or as noncompliance by wealthy households alone.
- Preserve the existing corporate-incidence research note: legal remittance is not economic incidence, and the CBO/JCT 75/25 capital/labor convention is a modeling assumption rather than a universal empirical law.
- If manuscript prose is revised after a new CBO baseline or major tax law, refresh the figures before publication.
