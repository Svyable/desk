# Chapter 14 — Who actually bears the corporate income tax?

## Claim under test

Chapter 14 correctly warns that a corporation may write the tax check while the economic burden can ultimately reach shareholders, workers, or customers. That point is worth anchoring because it is often used too aggressively in both directions: either to imply that corporate taxes are secretly taxes on workers, or to imply that legal liability tells us who ultimately pays.

## Primary-source evidence

The Congressional Budget Office's distributional methodology provides a useful benchmark. Since 2012, CBO has generally allocated 75 percent of the federal corporate income tax to owners of capital and 25 percent to workers when estimating long-run household tax burdens. In its analysis of 2018 household income, CBO emphasized that this is a long-run allocation for the corporate tax system as a whole and that the incidence of a particular provision can differ.

Source: Congressional Budget Office, “The Distribution of Household Income, 2018,” August 2021.
https://www.cbo.gov/publication/57404

The Joint Committee on Taxation uses a closely related distinction between time horizons. Its business-tax incidence methodology assumes that, initially, the burden of a business tax increase falls entirely on owners of capital; only over time does some of the burden shift to labor as investment and the capital stock adjust. JCT's later overview of tax reform options states its standard long-run assumption directly: 75 percent of corporate income taxes are borne by owners of domestic capital and 25 percent by domestic labor.

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The 75/25 split is not an observed law of nature. It is a modeling convention for distributional analysis, and both CBO and JCT distinguish the incidence of the corporate tax system as a whole from the incidence of a specific tax provision. The burden can differ by time horizon, openness to international capital, market structure, financing, and the particular rule being changed. In the short run, JCT's framework places the burden on capital owners rather than labor.

That makes the manuscript's existing caution stronger, not weaker. “People ultimately bear the economic burden” is sound; “workers pay the corporate tax” would be an unjustified simplification. The best evidence-backed formulation is that ownership bears most of the modeled long-run burden in the congressional distributional frameworks, with a meaningful minority assigned to labor, while the allocation for any specific policy remains uncertain.

## Editorial implication

A future prose pass could replace the chapter's abstract list of possible payers with the concrete CBO/JCT benchmark—roughly three-quarters capital and one-quarter labor in their standard long-run distributional models—followed immediately by the caveat that this is a modeling assumption, not a universal incidence estimate. That would make the section more specific without pretending the empirical literature has settled every corporate-tax design question.
