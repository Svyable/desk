# Research notes — chapters 17–18

Working notes for the synthesis and conclusion of *America's Got a Spending Problem*. Accessed 2026-09-03. These chapters mostly synthesize evidence developed earlier in the book; the sources below support the new claims about fiscal-rule design and the current federal fiscal path.

## Federal fiscal path and strategy

- U.S. Government Accountability Office, *The Nation's Fiscal Health: Urgent and Sustained Action Needed to Improve the Fiscal Outlook* (GAO-26-108610, June 11, 2026): https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108610
  - GAO reports publicly held debt around the size of the U.S. economy in spring 2026 and projects debt to grow substantially faster than the economy over the coming decade under its current-policy simulation.
  - GAO continues to recommend that Congress develop a broad strategy for fiscal sustainability and has previously recommended considering fiscal rules and targets as part of that strategy.
  - Chapter 17 uses this as support for the narrower institutional claim that measurement alone is insufficient and that a durable fiscal strategy needs rules, targets, and explicit policy choices. GAO's simulation is not treated as a precise forecast or a crisis date.

- Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* (February 11, 2026): https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105
  - Baseline deficits rise from $1.9 trillion in 2026 to $3.1 trillion in 2036; debt held by the public rises from 101 percent to 120 percent of GDP; net interest rises from 3.3 percent to 4.6 percent of GDP.
  - CBO emphasizes that budget outcomes are uncertain and depend on economic, demographic, legislative, administrative, and judicial developments.
  - Chapters 17–18 use the direction of the baseline to motivate earlier feedback and preservation of fiscal space, not to claim that one debt ratio is an objective crisis threshold.

- Congressional Budget Office, *Long-Term Budget Analysis*: https://www.cbo.gov/topics/budget/long-term-budget-analysis
  - CBO's long-term work extends beyond the standard ten-year window and shows how aging, health spending, interest, economic growth, and policy interact over longer horizons.
  - Used for the argument that a ten-year score is a useful common window but cannot be the full political horizon for policies with materially longer tails.

## Fiscal rules, escape clauses, and correction mechanisms

- Virginia Alonso-Albarran et al., International Monetary Fund, *Fiscal Rules and Fiscal Councils: Recent Trends and Revisions since the Pandemic*, IMF Working Paper 2025/198 (October 2025): https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2025/english/wpiea2025198-source-pdf.pdf
  - The updated IMF databases cover 123 economies and 54 fiscal councils.
  - By the end of 2024, more than two-thirds of fiscal rules included escape clauses, reflecting a broad move away from rules that cannot accommodate severe shocks.
  - Correction mechanisms have become more common but remain incomplete, and independent fiscal oversight is far from universal.
  - Chapter 17 uses the evidence to support a design principle: a serious rule needs clear exceptional circumstances, accountability around activation, and a credible path back after deviation. It does not claim that cross-country institutional designs can be copied mechanically into the U.S. federal system.

- International Monetary Fund, *Fiscal Rules Dataset: 1985–2024*: https://www.imf.org/en/topics/fiscal-policies/fiscal-rules-dataset
  - The dataset distinguishes budget-balance, debt, expenditure, and revenue rules and records supporting characteristics such as legal basis, escape clauses, correction mechanisms, stabilization properties, monitoring bodies, and fiscal-responsibility laws.
  - Used to avoid treating “a fiscal rule” as one generic device. Different rules constrain different margins and create different opportunities for gaming or stabilization.

## Claims deliberately bounded in the landing

- **No magic debt ratio:** Chapters 17–18 do not identify one debt-to-GDP threshold as safe or unsafe. U.S. borrowing capacity depends on growth, rates, inflation, institutions, investor demand, currency status, policy credibility, and the composition and maturity of debt.
- **Rules can fail:** Fiscal rules may be gamed, procyclical, suspended too easily, or made so rigid that they impede useful stabilization. The proposed principle is layered discipline—measurement, medium-term targets, explicit escape clauses, correction paths, and public explanation—not constitutional numerology.
- **Investment exception can be abused:** Protecting productive investment from crude austerity can improve policy, but “investment” is an attractive label for ordinary consumption. The chapter therefore asks for evidence of durable capacity or future cost reduction and for later evaluation.
- **Independent analysis is not democratic authority:** CBO, GAO, JCT, trustees, auditors, or a fiscal council can provide common arithmetic and evaluate compliance. Distributional choices over taxes, benefits, public goods, and risk remain political choices for elected institutions.
- **Fiscal space is option value, not purity:** The manuscript treats balance-sheet capacity as useful because it preserves room for recession, war, disaster, investment, and error. It does not treat low debt as a moral end in itself.
- **Price is not value:** The final chapter explicitly distinguishes resource pricing from moral worth. Health, dignity, security, care, beauty, research, and other public purposes can justify spending even when market prices do not capture their value.

## Final falsification / narrowing test

The institutional conclusion would need revision if durable fiscal rules with independent measurement, well-defined escape clauses, and correction mechanisms showed no meaningful ability to improve transparency, reduce procyclical behavior, or influence fiscal outcomes relative to looser arrangements; or if U.S. federal borrowing capacity proved so unconstrained that rising interest costs and debt service did not materially reduce future policy flexibility.

A weaker conclusion would still survive: whenever financing, insurance, or public policy separates the person choosing from the full price, the system needs some other credible mechanism for exposing scarcity before commitments become difficult to reverse.
