# Chapter 2 research audit: price distance is not merely missing discipline

Accessed 2026-09-15.

## Passage under review

Chapter 2, “The Third-Party Country,” introduces **price distance** as the separation between the person making or receiving a decision and its full economic cost. The chapter is strongest when it treats insurance and aid as valuable institutions that deliberately create this distance. It becomes easier to caricature when the mechanism starts to sound one-directional: third-party payment weakens price discipline, so the answer is to restore a force capable of saying no.

The missing empirical complication is that the distance itself buys something measurable. In health insurance, reducing price distance can reduce utilization, but it does not reliably distinguish low-value from high-value care, and the burden can be largest for people whose care is most consequential. Conversely, increasing insurance coverage can increase utilization while materially reducing financial hardship. That tradeoff belongs inside the book's mechanism, not outside it as a moral caveat.

## Current scale check

CMS's current National Health Expenditure fact sheet continues to report 2024 U.S. health spending of about **$5.3 trillion**, **$15,474 per person**, and **18.0 percent of GDP**, matching the chapter opening.

Source: Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, *NHE Fact Sheet*:
https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/national-health-expenditure-data/nhe-fact-sheet

CMS also reports that out-of-pocket spending was $556.6 billion, about 11 percent of national health expenditures, while private insurance, Medicare, Medicaid, governments, employers, and households financed the rest through several channels. That supports the chapter's descriptive premise that the patient is often not the sole buyer, but it does not establish that more direct payment would improve welfare.

## Counterevidence 1: cost sharing can restore a price signal without selecting only waste

The RAND Health Insurance Experiment randomized families to insurance plans with different levels of cost sharing. RAND's retrospective summary reports that cost sharing reduced use and spending. The important boundary for Chapter 2 is what it did **not** do: the experiment found that cost sharing reduced both more-effective and less-effective care rather than selectively eliminating only low-value treatment. RAND also reports exceptions to the generally small average health effects: the poorest and sickest participants had better outcomes under free care for several measured conditions, including hypertension control.

Source: RAND, *The Health Insurance Experiment* research brief:
https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_briefs/2006/RAND_RB9174.pdf

This is strong counterevidence to any simple version of “put the patient closer to the marginal price and discipline returns.” A patient can respond rationally to a copayment by consuming less care while lacking the information needed to know which forgone care was clinically expendable. The price signal is real; its targeting is imperfect.

The RAND experiment is old and its medical system differs from today's, so it should not be used as a current estimate of a particular copayment's effect. Its value here is mechanistic: consumer cost exposure changes utilization, but consumer exposure is not itself a clinical value algorithm.

## Counterevidence 2: insurance can increase use and still create substantial value

The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment exploited Oregon's 2008 Medicaid lottery to create a randomized comparison among low-income uninsured adults. The study's current NBER project summary reports that Medicaid increased outpatient visits, prescriptions, hospitalizations, and emergency-department use. It also reduced financial strain, virtually eliminated catastrophic out-of-pocket medical expenditures in the study measure, reduced medical debt, and reduced depression, while producing no statistically significant improvement in the measured physical-health outcomes over the first one to two years.

Sources:

- NBER, *Oregon Health Insurance Experiment*:
  https://www.nber.org/oregon
- NBER, *Oregon Health Insurance Experiment — Results*:
  https://www.nber.org/programs-projects/projects-and-centers/oregon-health-insurance-experiment/oregon-health-insurance-experiment-results

The emergency-department result is especially useful because it defeats an easy story from either direction. Medicaid coverage increased the probability of any ED use by about 7 percentage points and the number of ED visits by roughly 40 percent over about eighteen months in the Portland-area sample. The increase did not disappear into a simple substitution from the emergency room to ordinary office care. Yet calling that merely “overuse” would ignore the experiment's simultaneous evidence of insurance value through protection from financial catastrophe.

The Oregon experiment concerns a particular low-income population, program, place, and period. It does not establish that every marginal medical service induced by insurance is worth its cost, nor that Medicaid's effects generalize mechanically to employer insurance or Medicare.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter

The strongest objection is not that price distance is imaginary. It is that **distance from price is often the product being purchased**. Insurance exists partly so a sick person does not have to solve a clinical-value problem and a liquidity problem at the same moment. Moving the patient closer to the bill can reduce spending, but the randomized evidence shows that patients do not reliably cut only ineffective care. Moving the patient farther from the bill can increase use, but randomized evidence also shows that coverage can buy substantial financial protection even when short-run physical-health effects are difficult to detect.

This means the chapter should resist treating “ordinary consumer discipline” as the missing benchmark. In medicine, the better comparison is among imperfect controllers: patient cost sharing, insurer utilization management, provider payment rules, reference pricing, negotiated networks, public administered prices, clinical guidelines, and competition where real substitution is possible. Each controller can say no; each can also say no badly.

## Defensible boundary for a later prose pass

**Price distance is not merely a loss of discipline. It is often the insurance product itself: protection from having to make a high-stakes purchase with full personal exposure. The design problem is therefore not how to eliminate distance, but how to place cost discipline where information and bargaining power are strongest without destroying the risk protection the distance was created to provide.**

That formulation preserves the chapter's core idea and makes it harder to read as a disguised argument for shifting medical bills back onto patients.

## Editorial opportunity

A later surgical manuscript pass could replace some of the chapter's long sequence of one-sentence “distance can be…” examples and its later catalogue of possible controllers with one compact empirical turn:

1. let insurance succeed first, using Oregon's randomized evidence on financial protection;
2. let the price-discipline objection succeed next, using the increased utilization result;
3. then use RAND to show why simply returning the marginal bill to the patient does not cleanly solve the problem, because cost sharing reduced effective and less-effective care together;
4. arrive at the chapter's stronger institutional question: **who is best positioned to say no to which kind of spending?**

That sequence gives the argument pressure and counterevidence rather than another framework. It also fits the book's existing premise that risk pooling is “one of civilization's better inventions” while making the later institutional-design conclusion earned rather than asserted.
