# Chapter 11 research audit — tax-expenditure baseline boundary

Accessed September 15, 2026.

## Editorial question

Chapter 11 is careful to say that tax-expenditure estimates are not cash waiting to be collected, but it can sharpen the reason. A tax expenditure is not simply a measured pile of forgone dollars. It is an estimate relative to a specified benchmark tax system, and different official scorekeepers can make different benchmark choices while still doing serious, internally coherent analysis.

That distinction matters because the chapter's argument is institutional, not semantic. The useful claim is that tax provisions can perform programmatic work and deserve comparison with direct alternatives. The weaker claim would be that every departure from one chosen tax baseline is economically identical to an appropriation.

## Current OMB scale and methodology

OMB's fiscal year 2027 *Analytical Perspectives* lists the exclusion of employer contributions for medical insurance premiums and medical care as the largest income-tax expenditure in its table ranked by projected 2026–2035 revenue effect. OMB estimates a $295.66 billion income-tax effect in 2026 and $3.813 trillion over 2026–2035 under its methodology.

The same table is useful precisely because it contains items that many taxpayers would not intuitively classify together. OMB estimates a $157.41 billion 2026 effect for the exclusion of net imputed rental income, $156.48 billion for defined-contribution employer plans, $135.34 billion for preferential capital-gains treatment, and $85.42 billion for the capital-gains exclusion on qualifying home sales.

Primary source:

- Office of Management and Budget, *Analytical Perspectives, Budget of the U.S. Government, Fiscal Year 2027*, chapter 10, Table 10-2: https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/ap_10_expenditures_fy2027.pdf

These figures support the manuscript's scale claims. They do not establish that simultaneous repeal would raise the sum of the table.

## JCT makes the baseline problem visible

The Joint Committee on Taxation's December 3, 2025 tax-expenditure report independently estimates federal tax expenditures using its own specified framework. JCT says its calculations are based on federal tax law enacted through August 31, 2025, the January 2025 CBO revenue baseline, and JCT projections of income, deductions, and expenditures.

Primary source:

- Joint Committee on Taxation, *Estimates of Federal Tax Expenditures for Fiscal Years 2025–2029*, JCX-45-25: https://www.jct.gov/publications/2025/jcx-45-25/

The important editorial point is not to force OMB and JCT into one supposedly canonical number. Their estimates are analytical objects built for related but not identical purposes. A difference between official estimates can reflect baseline definition, timing, tax-law coverage, modeling, interactions, and data rather than an error by one side.

## The 2025 reconciliation debate supplies a live counterexample

JCT's 2025 reconciliation work makes baseline choice unusually concrete. For major tax legislation, JCT published estimates relative to both a defined current-policy baseline and the present-law baseline. The same statutory change can therefore have materially different measured revenue effects depending on whether expiring provisions are assumed to expire as written or continue as policy.

Primary sources:

- JCT, JCX-36-25, distribution relative to a defined current-policy baseline: https://www.jct.gov/getattachment/30e0d08f-2b1f-4bf9-8590-7f1baf6f903e/x-36-25.pdf
- JCT, JCX-37-25, distribution relative to the present-law baseline: https://www.jct.gov/publications/2025/jcx-37-25/
- JCT, revenue-estimating methodology: https://www.jct.gov/operations/revenue-estimating/

JCT explains that its ordinary revenue baseline assumes present law remains unchanged during the budget period. The 2025 reconciliation estimates also show why political arguments about whether extension of an expiring provision is a "tax cut" can become arguments about the counterfactual before they become arguments about the policy.

This is a stronger empirical version of the manuscript's sentence that "the baseline becomes political memory." It should not be inflated into a claim that baselines are arbitrary. Scorekeepers need explicit conventions so estimates are reproducible and comparable. The political opportunity arises because current law and expected continuation can diverge.

## Strongest counterargument

Calling tax expenditures a "shadow budget" can imply that the government first owns a comprehensive tax base and then spends whatever it declines to collect. That is too strong. There is no uniquely natural tax baseline from which every exclusion, deduction, deferral, special rate, or non-taxation decision can be classified without judgment. A consumption-tax benchmark, comprehensive-income benchmark, cash-flow system, or present-law baseline can treat the same economic activity differently.

The counterargument changes the chapter rather than defeating it. The defensible claim is not that every tax expenditure is secretly an appropriation. It is that provisions deliberately used to pursue programmatic objectives should be compared with direct spending, regulation, or neutral tax treatment using an explicit counterfactual.

A second counterargument runs the other way: baseline dependence does not make tax-expenditure analysis meaningless. OMB and JCT publish definitions and methods precisely so readers can see what comparison is being made. Once the benchmark is stated, the estimates reveal the scale and location of policy embedded in the code even when they do not equal obtainable repeal revenue.

## Recommended manuscript boundary

A later prose pass should preserve the chapter's strongest existing line — that tax-expenditure estimates are a map, not a repeal menu — and add one concrete reason the map has a legend: the number depends on the benchmark tax system.

The most useful reader-facing sequence would be:

1. keep the current OMB scale example for employer health insurance;
2. show that official estimates require a defined baseline rather than treating "forgone revenue" as self-evident;
3. use JCT's present-law versus current-policy scoring as a compact live example of how the counterfactual changes the measured fiscal effect;
4. concede the strongest objection that not taxing something is not automatically equivalent to spending it;
5. return to the chapter's institutional test: when a tax provision is deliberately buying a policy outcome, compare it with the alternatives instead of letting the tax label settle the argument.

## Claim discipline

Safe claims:

- OMB and JCT define tax expenditures relative to specified tax-law baselines.
- OMB's FY2027 table estimates the employer-health exclusion at $295.66 billion for 2026 under its methodology.
- JCT has published revenue/distribution estimates relative to both present-law and defined current-policy baselines in recent legislative analysis.
- A published tax-expenditure estimate is not mechanically equivalent to revenue obtainable through repeal.
- Baseline choice can materially affect how a tax change is described and measured.

Claims to avoid:

- There is one uniquely correct economic baseline for all tax-expenditure analysis.
- Every dollar classified as a tax expenditure is economically identical to a dollar of direct federal spending.
- Differences between OMB and JCT estimates imply that one estimate is wrong.
- The sum of tax-expenditure estimates is a collectible revenue total.
- A current-policy baseline is inherently more realistic than present law, or vice versa; each answers a different counterfactual question.

## Why this strengthens the chapter

The chapter already sees the central problem: policy can hide in the revenue column. This audit makes the measurement problem part of that story. It gives the skeptical reader the strongest objection — the government does not possess a natural pre-political claim on every untaxed dollar — and still preserves the useful institutional conclusion. The interesting question is not whether a preference deserves the moral label "spending." It is what counterfactual the estimate uses, what behavior the provision changes, who benefits, and whether another instrument would buy the same objective more effectively.
