# Chapter 7 research audit: student-loan signal lag

Accessed 2026-09-15. Scope: one bounded currentness/counterevidence pass for Chapter 7, “The College Price Nobody Pays.” No manuscript prose changed in this pass.

## Why this passage is worth revisiting

Chapter 7’s core distinction is strong: college has several economically relevant prices, and financing can separate the institution’s revenue event from the borrower’s later repayment outcome. The current draft already avoids the obsolete story that every undergraduate price and debt measure is mechanically rising. Two current data developments make that argument sharper.

First, the New York Fed’s August 11, 2026 Household Debt and Credit release now supersedes the chapter’s Q1 balance snapshot. Student-loan balances fell from $1.658 trillion in 2026:Q1 to $1.651 trillion in Q2. More importantly, the annualized flow into serious delinquency fell from 10.86 percent in Q1 to 7.83 percent in Q2. The Fed still warns that student-loan delinquency measures are being distorted by the re-reporting of defaulted federal student debt after the pandemic-era reporting interruption.

Source: Federal Reserve Bank of New York, “Household Debt Balances Decreased Slightly; Credit Card Delinquency Transition Rates Remained Steady,” August 11, 2026: https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20260811

Second, the Department of Education has moved some outcome information earlier in the decision process. Effective December 7, 2025, FAFSA began showing a lower-earnings indicator when an institution’s average post-graduation earnings fall below the comparison benchmark. On March 22–23, 2026, Federal Student Aid refreshed the earnings data and College Scorecard outcomes, including four-years-after-graduation earnings for the 2017–18 and 2018–19 completion cohorts and updated graduation, cost, and field-of-study information.

Sources:

- U.S. Department of Education, “U.S. Department of Education Launches New Earnings Indicator to Support Students and Families in Making Informed College Decisions,” December 8, 2025: https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-launches-new-earnings-indicator-support-students-and-families-making-informed-college-decisions
- Federal Student Aid, “New Lower Earnings Indicator on the FAFSA form,” updated March 23, 2026: https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/library/electronic-announcements/2025-12-03/new-lower-earnings-indicator-fafsar-form
- U.S. Department of Education, March 26, 2026 Scorecard/FAFSA transparency update: https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-celebrates-more-10-million-fafsar-forms-complete-and-additional-transparency-measures

## Strongest counterargument

The chapter says that information often arrives after emotional commitment has begun and proposes that the “honest bill” should reverse the order. Federal policy has now partially moved in exactly that direction: an earnings warning can appear inside FAFSA rather than requiring a teenager or parent to discover a separate Scorecard page later.

That is real counterevidence and should not be waved away. It means the manuscript should no longer imply that outcome information is merely sitting in a public database waiting to be consulted after the admissions machinery has done its work.

But the reform does not close the chapter’s larger problem. FAFSA is still not the first moment of college choice; applicants may already have formed preferences, applied, toured, or been recruited. The indicator is also deliberately narrow. It compares an institution-level earnings measure with a benchmark; it does not turn uncertain program-level completion, borrowing, time-to-degree, field, geographic labor markets, nonpecuniary value, or individual outcomes into one knowable return. Better-timed information reduces one information failure without making college a standardized investment product.

## Measurement boundary

The New York Fed series supplies a second useful caution. The Q1 2026 stock statistic—10.3 percent of balances 90 or more days delinquent—was accurate, but it is easy for a static number to become the chapter’s emotional headline. By Q2, the flow into serious delinquency had fallen materially even while the Fed continued to describe the reporting regime as distorted by the return of defaulted loans to credit reports.

The chapter should therefore distinguish a **stock of balances already 90+ days delinquent** from the **flow of balances newly entering serious delinquency**. Neither should be used as a clean steady-state measure of the underlying quality of current college decisions during this reporting transition.

## Defensible revision boundary

A later surgical prose pass can improve Chapter 7 in two places without changing its thesis:

1. Update the student-debt snapshot to 2026:Q2 and use the improving serious-delinquency flow as counterevidence against turning the post-pause spike into a permanent trend.
2. Credit the FAFSA earnings indicator as an actual attempt to move outcome information upstream, then ask what remains unknowable even when the disclosure arrives earlier.

The stronger claim is not “students cannot get outcome information before commitment.” It is: **policy can move information earlier and make it easier to compare, but the college decision still combines a fragmented price with heterogeneous, delayed, and partly nonfinancial outcomes that no single disclosure can collapse into a reliable personal ROI.**

That version gives the reform credit, preserves uncertainty, and makes the chapter’s delayed-signal argument harder to caricature.
