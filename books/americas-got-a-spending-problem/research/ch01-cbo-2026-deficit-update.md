# Chapter 1 research note — 2026 deficit update after tariff changes

Accessed September 5, 2026.

## Primary sources

Congressional Budget Office, **Monthly Budget Review: July 2026** (August 10, 2026): https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61983

- Based on information available through the end of July, CBO estimated the fiscal year 2026 deficit at **$2.1 trillion**, $200 billion above the $1.9 trillion projection published in February 2026.
- CBO expected 2026 outlays to remain close to the February baseline amounts. The revision was primarily on the revenue side: total revenues were expected to be about $200 billion below the February projection.
- CBO estimated customs duties for 2026 would be about $250 billion below the February projection, largely after the Supreme Court's February 20 decision invalidated tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and the government began refunding collections made under that authority.
- Larger-than-expected individual income and payroll tax collections partly offset the customs shortfall.

Congressional Budget Office, **CBO's Updated Budgetary Projections of Tariffs as of July 31, 2026** (August 20, 2026): https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62704

- CBO estimated that tariff-policy changes through July 31 would make total deficits over 2027–2036 **$0.9 trillion larger** than in the February 2026 baseline: about $0.7 trillion in larger primary deficits plus $0.2 trillion in additional debt-service costs.
- CBO attributed most of the change to the removal of IEEPA tariffs after the Supreme Court ruling. Replacement tariffs were projected to raise less revenue.
- CBO estimated an effective tariff rate of about 10 percent as of the update, down from its 15 percent estimate for November 2025, and emphasized that projections remain unusually uncertain because tariff policy has changed repeatedly and comparable historical episodes are scarce.

## Manuscript use

Chapter 1 currently dates its opening fiscal figures to CBO's February 2026 baseline. That is a legitimate historical baseline, but it should not be presented as CBO's latest estimate of the fiscal year 2026 deficit after the August update.

If the opening is refreshed to the $2.1 trillion estimate, the chapter's scale translation becomes roughly **$5.75 billion per day** or **$66,600 per second**. Those conversions remain illustrative rather than descriptions of a constant cash-flow rate.

The August tariff update does **not** replace CBO's full February ten-year baseline. Chapter 1's 2036 debt, deficit, and net-interest figures should remain explicitly identified as February 2026 baseline projections unless CBO publishes a new comprehensive baseline.

## Counterevidence and argument boundary

The update is useful pressure against an overly literal reading of the book's title. A larger deficit is not automatically evidence of higher spending. In CBO's August fiscal-year update, projected outlays stayed close to the February baseline while projected revenues fell. Revenue policy can materially move the fiscal gap even when spending barely changes.

That does not make tariffs a free fiscal solution. Tariffs are taxes with economic incidence, behavioral responses, legal constraints, and macroeconomic effects beyond the customs-revenue line. The defensible use here is narrower: **the fiscal problem is the gap between durable commitments and durable financing, not a claim that every movement in the deficit originates on the spending side.**
