# Chapter 16 research audit — BRAC, visible pain, and local recovery

Accessed 2026-09-14. This is a bounded evidence audit for *America's Got a Spending Problem*, Chapter 16, “The Politics of Visible Pain.” It does not change manuscript prose.

## Claim under review

Chapter 16 uses military-base closure as a concrete example of the book's political asymmetry: the local payroll, workers, suppliers, mayors, contractors, and legislators are visible and organized, while the national taxpayer savings and alternative military uses of the resources are diffuse or hypothetical. Later, the chapter uses a base closure as an example of an efficient reform that can impose concentrated transition costs and therefore may need transition design.

That mechanism is plausible, but the current prose risks letting “base closure” quietly mean “local economic devastation.” The historical BRAC record supports a more interesting and more defensible claim.

## Primary / authoritative record

### 1. BRAC was designed as an institutional response to politically difficult, localized closure decisions

Congressional Research Service, *Base Closure and Realignment (BRAC): Background and Issues for Congress*, R45705:

https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45705/R45705.2.pdf

CRS describes the modern BRAC framework as using an independent commission, objective and uniform criteria, GAO review of DOD data, public hearings and installation visits, and a final package that must be accepted or rejected in its entirety. The President accepts the commission's recommendations as a package before transmitting them to Congress; implementation proceeds unless Congress rejects the recommendations in their entirety within the statutory window.

The institutional design matters to Chapter 16. Congress did not solve the political problem by pretending local losses were unreal. It changed the decision architecture so individual bases were harder to rescue one at a time through ordinary legislative bargaining. That is a stronger real-world example for the chapter's claim that fiscal institutions can move conflict earlier and make tradeoffs harder to evade.

Important boundary: BRAC was not a technocratic machine outside politics. The criteria, appointments, hearings, statutory authority, presidential decision, congressional rejection mechanism, environmental review, and community input all remained political. The useful claim is that the procedure changed the *unit of decision* and the opportunities for case-by-case veto, not that it removed politics.

### 2. The short-run local shock was real, but long-run “devastation” was not the typical outcome in the prior rounds

U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Military Base Closures: Observations on Prior and Current BRAC Rounds*, GAO-05-614 (May 2005):

https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-05-614

GAO reported that, as of September 30, 2004, about 110,086 of 129,649 local DOD civilian jobs lost at major locations in the earlier BRAC rounds had been replaced through redevelopment of the properties — about 85 percent. GAO also found that BRAC communities generally compared favorably with U.S. averages on unemployment and real per-capita-income growth, while stressing that recovery varied substantially and was not necessarily easy.

Earlier GAO work reached the same qualitative conclusion while preserving the difficult transitions. In GAO-02-433, officials in affected communities attributed recovery to factors including the national economy, local economic diversity, redevelopment, and government assistance. Individual places ranged from weak recovery to job creation far above the number of civilian jobs lost on the former base.

https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-02-433

A later review of the 2005 round found similarly heterogeneous outcomes. Among 21 communities surrounding 23 installations closed in that round, 52 percent had unemployment below the national average used by GAO, and 62 percent had real per-capita-income growth above the national rate over the comparison period; community outcomes still varied widely.

U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Defense Infrastructure: Communities Need Additional Guidance and Information to Improve Their Ability to Adjust to DOD Installation Closure or Growth*, GAO-13-436 (May 14, 2013):

https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-13-436

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The chapter's visible-pain mechanism should not become a story in which organized local opposition proves that the underlying economic loss is permanent or that closure necessarily destroys a town. GAO's longitudinal evidence cuts against that. The initial shock can be severe, and a few communities can suffer for a long time, but many communities replace much or all of the lost employment as property is reused and the regional economy adjusts.

That counterevidence improves the chapter's argument in two ways.

First, it separates **political visibility** from **long-run incidence**. A closure can rationally produce intense opposition because the immediate losses are specific, concentrated, and legible even when the eventual local economy adapts. Political salience does not require permanent economic ruin.

Second, it makes transition policy more precise. Redevelopment, property condition, cleanup, infrastructure, regional diversification, and the broader economy materially affect recovery. “Compensation” is therefore not just a cash transfer to make reform pass. In the BRAC case, the quality and speed of conversion from military to civilian use can determine whether a concentrated short-run loss becomes a persistent one.

## Recommended manuscript boundary for a later prose pass

Keep the base-closure example, but narrow its causal job:

- use BRAC as evidence that concentrated, geographically legible losses can make individually rational closures politically difficult;
- show that the United States responded by changing decision architecture — independent review, common criteria, package treatment, and a constrained congressional veto — rather than relying on exhortations to “be brave”;
- do **not** describe base closure as automatically devastating a town;
- distinguish immediate workers, suppliers, municipal revenue, and social disruption from longer-run regional employment and income;
- let the recovery evidence complicate the moral: a painful transition can be real even when the long-run counterfactual is better than opponents fear;
- treat redevelopment and transition capacity as part of the policy, not as an afterthought.

A compact formulation consistent with the evidence would be: the political problem is not that every closed base leaves a permanent crater. It is that the people who lose first are identifiable before the people and uses that may gain later exist in recognizable form.

## What would force further narrowing

The chapter should narrow again if stronger location-level causal work shows that the favorable post-BRAC averages mainly reflect selection into economically resilient regions, displacement into neighboring jurisdictions, or national trends that would have occurred without closure. GAO's comparisons are useful descriptive evidence, not a randomized estimate of the causal effect of closing a base.

Likewise, the institutional claim should narrow if historical work shows the package structure and independent commission did not materially reduce case-by-case political protection compared with ordinary closure authority. The current CRS record supports the institutional interpretation, but the manuscript should not present BRAC as proof that independent commissions generally solve concentrated-interest politics.

## Editorial payoff

This evidence can replace a generic “base closure devastates a town” intuition with a better sequence the reader can picture: a named installation faces closure; the local loss is immediate and organized; a national package constrains one-off rescue; the closure occurs; reuse and recovery then vary dramatically by place. The example becomes less moralistic and more useful to the book's central concern: how institutions make visible present losses commensurable with diffuse future gains without pretending either side is imaginary.
