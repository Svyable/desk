# Chapter 13 — Improper payments are not a synonym for fraud

## Claim under test

Chapter 13 uses the federal government's fiscal year 2025 improper-payment estimate to show why eliminating obvious waste matters but cannot by itself close a trillion-dollar structural deficit. The arithmetic works, but the category needs tighter boundaries: an improper-payment estimate is neither a fraud estimate nor a complete count of every payment error in government.

## Fresh primary-source evidence

On April 27, 2026, the U.S. Government Accountability Office reported that 15 agencies estimated about $186 billion in improper payments across 64 programs for fiscal year 2025. About $153 billion, or roughly 82 percent, were overpayments.

GAO also cautioned that the $186 billion figure does not represent the full extent of government-wide improper payments. Some programs that agencies had already identified as susceptible to significant improper payments did not produce an estimate for fiscal year 2025; GAO named Temporary Assistance for Needy Families as one example.

Source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Payment Integrity: Agencies' Estimated Improper Payments Increased to $186 Billion in Fiscal Year 2025*, GAO-26-108694, April 27, 2026.
https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108694

A separate GAO explainer makes the category distinction explicit. Improper payments are payments that should not have been made, were made for the wrong amount, or in some cases cannot be verified as proper because documentation is insufficient. Fraud requires willful misrepresentation. All fraudulent payments are improper, but not all improper payments are fraudulent, and the improper-payment estimation process is not designed to measure total fraud.

Source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Improper Payments and Fraud: How They Are Related but Different*, GAO-24-106608, December 7, 2023.
https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106608

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The strongest counterargument to Chapter 13 is not that waste is too small to matter. GAO's own reporting cuts the other way: $186 billion is substantial, the estimates are incomplete, and some programs report very high error rates. Better controls can protect large sums and improve program legitimacy.

But the estimate cannot honestly be turned into “$186 billion of fraud,” and it cannot be treated as $186 billion of instantly recoverable budget savings. The category includes underpayments, documentation failures, eligibility mistakes, duplicate payments, administrative errors, and fraud. Reducing errors also requires administration, data, controls, and enforcement, which have costs of their own.

## Editorial implication

The chapter's current sentence that some improper payments reflected fraud is defensible, but the surrounding argument would be stronger if a future prose pass makes the asymmetry explicit: the reported estimate is broad enough that it should not be equated with fraud, yet incomplete enough that it should not be treated as a ceiling on payment-integrity problems.

That distinction improves the chapter's central claim. “Cut waste” is not a fake priority; it is an incomplete fiscal strategy. The honest argument has to preserve both halves at once.
