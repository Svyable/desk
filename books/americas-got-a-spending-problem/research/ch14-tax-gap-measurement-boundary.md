# Chapter 14 research audit — the tax-gap measurement boundary

Checked September 15, 2026. This note is a bounded audit of the tax-gap passage in Chapter 14, “Why Taxing the Rich Isn't Enough.” It does not change manuscript prose. The purpose is to keep a useful enforcement example from turning into a claim that the IRS has directly measured a fixed pile of collectible revenue.

## Current primary record

- Internal Revenue Service, “The tax gap”: https://www.irs.gov/statistics/irs-the-tax-gap
- Internal Revenue Service, Statistics of Income, “What's new” (October 2024 tax-gap release): https://www.irs.gov/statistics/soi-tax-stats-whats-new
- Internal Revenue Service, *Federal Tax Compliance Research: Tax Gap Projections for Tax Year 2022*, Publication 5869 (Rev. 10-2024): https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5869.pdf

The IRS projects a **$696 billion gross tax gap for tax year 2022**, defined as projected true federal tax liability that was not paid voluntarily and on time. It projects **$90 billion** in enforced and other late payments, leaving a **$606 billion net tax gap**. The voluntary compliance rate is projected at **85.0 percent**; after enforcement and late payments, the projected eventual compliance rate is **86.9 percent**.

The gross gap is not one phenomenon. The IRS divides it into **$63 billion of nonfiling, $539 billion of underreporting, and $94 billion of underpayment**. By tax type, the gross gap includes **$514 billion of individual income tax, $50 billion of corporate income tax, $127 billion of employment tax, and $5 billion of estate tax**. Those figures describe the federal tax system broadly. They do not identify the tax gap with wealthy households, corporations, or any proposed “tax the rich” package.

## The measurement point the manuscript should preserve

The 2022 figure is a **projection**, not a contemporaneous census of unpaid 2022 liabilities. The IRS explains that tax-gap measurement is slow because underreporting estimates depend heavily on completed examinations. Its 2021 and 2022 projections are based largely on compliance behavior estimated from the most recent completed audit data, covering tax years **2014–2016**, and then projected forward using later return and economic information.

That matters for the chapter's language. The $696 billion gross number is useful evidence about the scale and composition of noncompliance. It is not evidence that $696 billion could have been recovered in 2022 by increasing the audit budget, nor that the $606 billion net projection is a mechanically collectible balance. The IRS itself says its estimates and projections cannot fully account for all types of noncompliance.

The distinction also improves the chapter's treatment of trend. The IRS says the larger 2022 dollar gap, compared with its 2014–2016 estimate, largely reflects growth in the economy and changes in sources of income rather than a deterioration in filing or payment behavior: the projected voluntary compliance rate remained about 85 percent. A bigger nominal tax gap therefore should not be narrated automatically as evidence of collapsing compliance.

## What the evidence does support

The IRS repeatedly finds higher compliance where income is subject to independent third-party information reporting, and still higher compliance where withholding is also present. That supports the chapter's institutional point that **tax administration and information architecture affect how much statutory liability becomes collected revenue**.

The causal claim should remain narrow. Third-party reporting and withholding are strong compliance mechanisms; they do not imply that every difficult-to-observe dollar is evaded, that every category of complex income can be made wage-like for reporting purposes, or that enforcement alone can close a structural federal deficit.

## Strongest counterargument

The chapter currently risks letting a large tax-gap number perform two jobs at once: establish that compliance matters and suggest a reservoir of recoverable fiscal capacity. The first inference is well supported. The second is much harder.

Even the net tax-gap projection is not a budget score for an enforcement proposal. Additional collection depends on which noncompliance can actually be detected, substantiated, litigated, and collected; what information-reporting rules exist; how taxpayers change legal form or timing; the marginal cost and quality of enforcement; and how projection error behaves in income categories that are intrinsically difficult to observe. Some liabilities may be legally due yet practically uncollectible. Some apparent gaps are estimated rather than directly observed.

If future evidence showed that the great majority of the net gap could be collected at low marginal cost through a realistic enforcement package concentrated on a narrow high-income population, that would weaken Chapter 14's claim that administration and a narrow tax base are separate constraints. The current IRS tax-gap publications do not establish that proposition.

## Manuscript implication

A later prose pass should keep the chapter's existing warning that the tax gap is not “a bag of cash,” but make the distinction more concrete and less rhetorical:

- identify **gross ($696B)** versus **net ($606B)** rather than citing only the larger number;
- say explicitly that the 2022 figure is a **projection built largely from 2014–2016 audit-derived compliance behavior projected forward**;
- preserve the systemwide scope rather than implying the gap belongs mainly to wealthy taxpayers;
- use the third-party-reporting/withholding result to support the chapter's administrative-design argument;
- avoid treating the tax gap as an estimate of revenue available from any particular tax or enforcement proposal.

The stronger proposition is not that enforcement is small. It is that **tax liability, tax-gap estimation, and realizable revenue are three different objects**. Chapter 14 is more persuasive when it keeps them separate.
