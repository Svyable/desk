# Chapter 12 research audit — balanced-budget discipline and the business cycle

Accessed September 15, 2026. This is a bounded research/counterevidence audit for Chapter 12, “States, Cities, and Promises.” It is stacked on the existing Chapter 12 pension-measurement audit and does not change manuscript prose.

## Why this passage is worth revisiting

Chapter 12 opens with a useful contrast: the federal government can borrow through ordinary fiscal years while states generally operate under much tighter balanced-budget constraints. The chapter also correctly warns that copying state rules into Washington would sacrifice some federal countercyclical capacity.

The argument can be sharper if it separates two things that are currently close together: the value of a state balanced-budget rule as a commitment device in normal times, and the macroeconomic cost of forcing the same accounting adjustment during a recession. The strongest evidence does not say that balanced-budget rules are simply good or bad. It says their value depends heavily on the time horizon, reserve policy, and whether another level of government can absorb a common shock.

## Current institutional check

NCSL’s current FY2027 budget-status tracker shows the practical variety behind the phrase “state budget.” Forty-six states begin their fiscal year on July 1; New York begins April 1, Texas September 1, and Alabama and Michigan October 1. States also differ between annual and biennial budgeting, and several began FY2027 without a newly enacted full budget, relying on interim or continuing arrangements.

Source: National Conference of State Legislatures, *FY 2027 State Budget Status*, updated July 1, 2026: https://www.ncsl.org/fiscal/fy-2027-state-budget-status

This does not contradict the manuscript’s general balanced-budget point. It does show why “make Washington live like the states” is less a single rule than a family of institutions operating with different calendars, legal constraints, reserves, capital budgets, and fallback procedures.

## Strong counterevidence: discipline can become procyclical

The Federal Reserve’s historical account of the Great Recession supplies the strongest mechanism-level counterargument. In a March 2, 2011 speech, Chairman Ben Bernanke noted that state revenues fell sharply as the recession hit and that, driven partly by balanced-budget requirements, governments cut programs and workforces. He also emphasized the national consequence: state and local spending cuts and tax increases were a headwind on the recovery.

Primary source: Federal Reserve Board, Ben S. Bernanke, *Challenges for State and Local Governments*, March 2, 2011: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/bernanke20110302a.htm

The same speech is unusually useful because it does not turn that evidence into an argument for abolishing state fiscal rules. Bernanke explicitly said the evidence suggested balanced-budget rules provide valuable fiscal discipline and did not advocate eliminating them. His proposed margin of improvement was reserve design: accumulate more fiscal capacity in good times so the rule does not force as much contraction in bad times.

That is almost exactly the tension Chapter 12 wants. The rule can discipline ordinary politics and still amplify a common downturn when revenues collapse simultaneously.

GAO’s review of the 2003 temporary state fiscal-relief program provides a second authoritative check on the federal role. Congress supplied $10 billion in unrestricted fiscal relief to help states close recession-related budget shortfalls and support essential services. GAO describes the aid as antirecession fiscal stimulus aimed at cyclical deficits, not as a solution to long-term structural fiscal problems.

Source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, GAO-04-736R, *Federal Assistance: Temporary State Fiscal Relief*, May 7, 2004: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-04-736r

That distinction is important. Federal borrowing capacity can be a shock absorber for state fiscal rules without becoming an excuse for either level of government to finance permanent structural gaps with temporary aid.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter

A reader could reasonably object that the chapter romanticizes “discovering the price earlier.” Earlier recognition is valuable when it forces a durable program to identify durable financing. It can be harmful when a temporary collapse in revenue forces a government to cut teachers, transit, health services, or investment at the same moment households and firms are also contracting.

The counterargument is not merely theoretical. The Great Recession record shows state and local retrenchment acting as a national economic headwind. Conversely, allowing states unlimited routine deficit finance would weaken the very commitment mechanism the chapter is trying to learn from.

The defensible boundary is therefore:

**State balanced-budget rules are useful commitment devices for ordinary fiscal politics, but they are poor substitutes for countercyclical capacity. Their strongest design pairs current-budget discipline with reserves, capital-finance rules, transparent long-term liabilities, and a federal shock absorber for genuinely cyclical stress.**

## What this changes editorially

A later surgical prose pass should let the apparent state solution succeed first: current cash constraints really do force earlier tradeoffs. Then use the Great Recession to show the cost of treating that virtue as universal. One concrete historical sequence can replace several abstract declarations about why “earlier is not always better.”

The most interesting move is not another list of state fiscal tools. It is the institutional handoff: a state rule says no to routine deficit finance; reserves buy time; when a nationwide recession overwhelms those reserves, federal borrowing capacity can prevent fifty simultaneous contractions from deepening the shock. After the shock, temporary federal relief should not be mistaken for recurring state revenue.

That sequence gives the chapter narrative movement and makes the federalism argument harder to caricature from either direction.

## Claim discipline

Safe claims:

- States operate under heterogeneous balanced-budget institutions rather than one uniform rule.
- Tight state and local fiscal constraints can force spending cuts or tax increases during recessions.
- The Federal Reserve identified state and local retrenchment after the Great Recession as a headwind on the national recovery while still describing balanced-budget rules as valuable fiscal discipline.
- Federal fiscal relief has been used explicitly to help states absorb cyclical shortfalls.
- Temporary recession relief does not solve a structural mismatch between recurring spending and recurring revenue.

Claims to avoid:

- Balanced-budget requirements alone caused the slow recovery from the Great Recession.
- Every state faces the same legal balance requirement or enforcement mechanism.
- Federal transfers are always stimulative or always prevent state cuts dollar for dollar.
- State balanced-budget rules prove that an analogous federal constitutional amendment would improve macroeconomic performance.
- Countercyclical federal aid removes the need for state reserves or long-run funding discipline.

## Why this strengthens the book

The current chapter already has the right instinct but mostly states the cyclical exception as principle. The Federal Reserve and GAO record turns it into an observed institutional conflict: fiscal discipline at one level can create contraction that another level is deliberately built to absorb. That makes the chapter’s larger point more precise. Fiscal honesty is not forcing every government to obey the same stop signal; it is making each level’s stop signal explicit, bounded, and appropriate to the risk it is supposed to manage.
