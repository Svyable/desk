# Chapter 10 — Refinancing lag and the debt's memory

## Claim under test

Chapter 10 argues that higher market rates do not hit the federal interest bill all at once because Treasury debt reprices over time as securities mature, new deficits are financed, and floating-rate instruments reset. The chapter's memorable formulation is that “the debt remembers interest rates.” The mechanism is sound, but it benefits from a concrete measure of the portfolio's maturity and an explicit warning that average maturity is not the same thing as average repricing time.

## Primary sources checked

- U.S. Department of the Treasury, Office of Debt Management, presentation to the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee for the April 2026 quarter. Treasury reported the weighted average maturity of marketable debt outstanding at **70.8 months as of April 30, 2026**, compared with a historical average of 61.4 months from 1980 to the present. https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/221/CombinedChargesforArchivesQ22026.pdf
- U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Minutes of the Meeting of the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee August 4, 2026,” published August 5, 2026. Treasury staff reported that gross interest outlays through fiscal 2026 Q3 were up $120 billion, or 10 percent, from the comparable period, attributing that increase to higher levels of debt. The same meeting discussed expected financing needs and the mix of bills and coupon securities, underscoring that debt-service changes reflect both the stock of debt and the terms on which it is financed. https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0592
- Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* and detailed projections. CBO projects net interest rising from about 3.3 percent of GDP in 2026 to 4.6 percent in 2036 and attributes the increase to both accumulating debt and higher average interest rates. https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105

Sources rechecked September 2, 2026.

## Counterevidence and limits

Weighted average maturity is useful evidence that the Treasury portfolio does not turn over instantly, but it is **not** a direct measure of the speed at which the government's effective interest rate changes. Bills mature much sooner than the average, floating-rate notes reset before maturity, coupon payments differ across securities, and new borrowing adds exposure even when existing debt does not mature. A 70.8-month weighted average maturity therefore should not be translated into a claim that it takes roughly six years for a rate shock to reach the budget.

The reverse complication also matters. Rising interest expense need not mean that refinancing at higher rates is the dominant cause in a particular period. Treasury's August 2026 TBAC minutes attributed the largest year-over-year Treasury outlay increase through Q3 to higher gross interest resulting from **higher levels of debt**. The chapter should keep debt stock and interest-rate pass-through as separate mechanisms rather than letting the vivid refinancing story crowd out principal growth.

## Editorial implication

A later manuscript pass can make “the debt remembers interest rates” more concrete by noting that Treasury's marketable portfolio had a weighted average maturity of about 71 months in spring 2026, then immediately qualifying the statistic. The stronger formulation is not that the budget waits six years to feel a rate change. It is that the federal interest bill is a layered record of many borrowing vintages: some securities reprice quickly, some remain fixed for decades, and every new deficit adds a fresh layer at current market terms.

That framing improves the argument in three ways. It gives the reader a physical sense of the lag, keeps the mechanism faithful to Treasury's actual portfolio, and preserves the strongest counterargument: interest costs rise because of both the price of money and the amount borrowed. The chapter is most persuasive when it refuses to let either explanation stand in for the other.
