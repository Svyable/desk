# Chapter 13 research audit — fraud, improper payments, and realizable savings

Checked 2026-09-15. This note stress-tests the arithmetic in Chapter 13, “Why Cutting Waste Isn't Enough,” against the strongest official counterevidence I found. It does not change manuscript prose.

## The existing $186 billion comparison is valid, but narrower than “fraud”

GAO reported that 15 agencies estimated about **$186 billion in improper payments across 64 programs in FY2025**, including about **$153 billion in overpayments**. GAO also says the reported estimate does **not** capture the full extent of government-wide improper payments because some programs susceptible to significant improper payments did not report an estimate.

Primary source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Payment Integrity: Agencies' Estimated Improper Payments Increased to $186 Billion in Fiscal Year 2025*, GAO-26-108694, April 27, 2026: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108694

The manuscript is right not to equate improper payments with fraud. Improper payments include payments that should not have been made or were made in the wrong amount, and can arise from eligibility, documentation, data, administrative, or other errors as well as fraud.

## Strongest counterevidence: GAO's fraud estimate is materially larger

A reader could reasonably object that the chapter's $186 billion improper-payment figure understates the fiscal scale of fraud. In April 2024, GAO estimated **direct annual financial losses from fraud at $233 billion to $521 billion**, or roughly **3% to 7% of average annual federal obligations**, using data from fiscal years 2018 through 2022. GAO describes that estimate as covering all federal programs and notes that the period includes both normal operations and the unusually high-risk pandemic emergency-spending environment.

Primary source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Fraud Risk Management: 2018-2022 Data Show Federal Government Loses an Estimated $233 Billion to $521 Billion Annually to Fraud*, GAO-24-105833, April 16, 2024: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-105833

Current GAO program-integrity material continues to cite that $233-$521 billion estimate while separately reporting improper-payment totals. See GAO-25-108172, March 11, 2025: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-108172

That is important counterevidence because the high end is almost three times the FY2025 improper-payment estimate. Chapter 13 should therefore avoid allowing “$186 billion” to read as a ceiling on federal fraud, waste, or recoverable loss.

## Why the two estimates cannot be added or substituted mechanically

The estimates measure different things, cover different periods, and use different methods. GAO's fraud-loss estimate is not a subset that can simply be added to the $186 billion improper-payment estimate, nor is every improper payment fraudulent. Some fraud losses may already appear within program-level improper-payment estimates; other fraud may not. GAO's fraud range is also an estimate of losses, not a score of savings that a particular enforcement package can realize.

The 2018-2022 estimation window matters. Pandemic-era emergency programs changed the federal fraud-risk environment, and GAO explicitly cautions that its range reflects different risk environments during those years. Using the high end as a normal-year recurring savings target would therefore overstate what the evidence establishes.

## Scale against the current budget gap

CBO's February 2026 baseline projects an FY2026 federal deficit of **$1.9 trillion**, with $7.4 trillion in outlays and $5.6 trillion in revenues. Even GAO's high-end $521 billion annual fraud-loss estimate is far smaller than that deficit, and eliminating every estimated fraud loss at zero cost is not a realistic policy scenario.

Primary source: Congressional Budget Office, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036*, February 2026: https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105

The stronger chapter boundary is therefore not “waste and fraud are only $186 billion.” It is: **large administrative losses can coexist with a still larger structural mismatch, and estimates of losses are not the same thing as realizable budget savings.**

## Implication for a later prose pass

A surgical revision should keep the chapter's excellent distinction among error, inefficiency, and political disagreement, but make one sentence explicit: the $186 billion figure is the reported FY2025 improper-payment estimate, not an estimate of all fraud or all waste. The GAO fraud range is the strongest official counterexample and should be acknowledged rather than left for a skeptical reader to supply.

That counterevidence does not defeat the chapter. It improves it. Even the broader fraud estimate leaves the central argument intact while forcing the arithmetic to stop doing work it cannot support: administrative competence is fiscally consequential, but neither an improper-payment estimate nor a fraud-loss estimate can be booked dollar-for-dollar as painless deficit reduction.
