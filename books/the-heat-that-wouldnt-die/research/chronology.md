# Chronology

- **1983–1989** — Fleischmann and Pons conduct private palladium–deuterium electrolysis experiments before public announcement.
- **March 23, 1989** — University of Utah press conference announces claims soon labeled cold fusion.
- **Spring–summer 1989** — global replication race produces a mixture of positive claims, null results, retractions and methodological disputes.
- **November 1989** — DOE Energy Research Advisory Board issues *Cold Fusion Research*, declining to recommend a special federal program.
- **1990s** — work continues in Japan, at SRI/EPRI-linked programs, government laboratories and small research communities under cold fusion, anomalous heat and LENR labels.
- **1994** — Fukai and Okuma publish superabundant-vacancy work in palladium hydride, illustrating how extreme hydrogen loading can restructure metals without implying nuclear reactions.
- **2004** — DOE conducts a second LENR review; evidence remains insufficient for establishment, though some reviewers see value in targeted proposals.
- **2015–2019** — Google-funded multi-institution program re-examines prominent cold-fusion claims with improved materials control and diagnostics.
- **May 27, 2019** — Berlinguette et al. publish *Revisiting the cold case of cold fusion* in *Nature*, reporting no cold-fusion evidence.
- **April 20, 2020** — NASA-led teams publish lattice-confinement-fusion theory and experiments in *Physical Review C*.
- **October 21–22, 2021** — ARPA-E holds a LENR workshop, framing the field as a reproducibility and theory stalemate.
- **February 17, 2023** — ARPA-E announces roughly $10 million for eight LENR projects.
- **2023–2027** — university, national-laboratory and small-business teams pursue calorimetry, nuclear-product detection, gas loading, nanoparticles and independent diagnostics under ARPA-E awards.
- **2026** — several original ARPA-E awards reach scheduled completion, making closeout data and publications a key target for the next research pass.
