# Contradictions, Incentives, and Failure Modes

## Priority and patents

The 1989 announcement occurred amid priority and intellectual-property pressure. University incentives to establish ownership can accelerate publicity before ordinary peer scrutiny. That does not make a result false; it changes the environment in which uncertainty was communicated.

## Replication races

The first months rewarded speed. Negative laboratories wanted to resolve a spectacular claim; positive laboratories could become part of a historic discovery. Both incentives can degrade method. Early neutrons, gamma rays and excess heat must therefore be read with the actual detector background, calibration and stopping rules rather than by institutional prestige.

## Field survival

Researchers who continued LENR work after 1989 faced career and funding costs, which can select for unusual persistence. Persistence can preserve neglected evidence; it can also create communities with strong identity investment in the effect. Neither sociological observation settles the experiment.

## Skeptical incentives

Mainstream scientists have rational reasons not to revisit a claim that repeatedly failed. But once a subject becomes reputationally toxic, a negative result often has little publication value and a positive result carries career risk. That can reduce the supply of high-quality decisive experiments. Google and ARPA-E are important partly because they changed that incentive temporarily.

## Commercial claims

Energy claims create unusually strong investor incentives. Proprietary catalysts, secret preparation steps and sealed devices are incompatible with decisive independent verification. Patent filings show that someone asserted an invention; they do not establish efficacy.

## Institutional rehabilitation

NASA, DOE or ARPA-E involvement can be misused rhetorically as federal validation of cold fusion. Their actual documents are narrower. NASA's LCF is externally driven nuclear physics in a deuterated lattice. ARPA-E's LENR program was structured to determine whether a reproducible phenomenon existed, including the possibility of a negative answer.

## The null-result asymmetry

Failure to observe an effect under well-characterized conditions strongly constrains the claim tested. It does not constrain an unspecified claim with infinitely movable preparation conditions. A scientific hypothesis must therefore state the material state and predicted observables tightly enough that a null result can injure it.

## The product-ratio problem

Large nuclear-scale heat claims ordinarily imply reaction products, radiation, isotopic changes or other accounting consequences. Any proposed mechanism that produces substantial energy while suppressing expected channels carries an additional burden. The book never treats unexplained heat alone as sufficient proof of fusion.
