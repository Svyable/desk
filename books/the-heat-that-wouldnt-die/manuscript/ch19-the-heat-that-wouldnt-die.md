# The Heat That Wouldn't Die

Cold fusion should have disappeared.

The original claim failed to become reproducible. The expected nuclear products did not match the reported heat. A Department of Energy panel rejected the case for a special program in 1989. A second DOE review in 2004 did not establish LENR. A lavishly competent Google-funded re-evaluation published in 2019 found no evidence of cold fusion.

That is enough history to kill most scientific ideas.

Yet in 2023 ARPA-E funded eight projects to look again.

The easiest interpretations are both wrong.

This is not proof that Fleischmann and Pons were secretly right. The modern federal documents say nothing of the kind. ARPA-E's own workshop stated that the field lacked a widely accepted repeatable experiment and a sound theoretical basis. Its program was designed to determine whether a reproducible effect could be established or whether leading claims could be strongly excluded.

It is also not simply proof that government wastes money on dead ideas. The experimental question had changed. Modern materials characterization, calorimetry and nuclear diagnostics made it possible to test narrower claims more cleanly. Google's null campaign showed that entering the territory could produce useful science even when the miracle failed. The potential payoff of a genuine low-energy nuclear process remained enormous relative to the cost of a modest exploratory portfolio.

The heat survived culturally because the experiment never became boring enough.

That is the central argument of this book.

A phenomenon enters science when the apparatus can make it appear under conditions specified before the outcome and disappear under controls. Cold fusion spent decades in the opposite state. The alleged active condition was difficult to produce, imperfectly characterized and often identified retrospectively. Positive observations arrived in different channels—heat here, tritium there, neutrons somewhere else—without converging into one quantitative mechanism.

That structure is almost ideal for persistence.

There is always another sample.

Another impurity.

Another loading history.

Another detector.

Another theory explaining why the last null was not relevant.

This does not mean every anomaly was imaginary. It means the field failed to make anomalies portable.

Portability is a stricter concept than replication because it includes independence from the original hands. A phenomenon portable enough to become technology survives new operators, new instruments and new institutions. It has a process window. Its output scales. Its failures can be diagnosed.

Cold fusion remained artisanal while promising industry.

The modern return is interesting because it finally treats that contradiction as the problem.

ARPA-E's projects are full of nouns that would have bored newspaper readers in 1989: nanocomposites, scintillators, mass spectrometers, gas cycling, material characterization, background analysis. This is what a revolution looks like after everyone stops believing the press release.

The program may fail completely.

I want that possibility protected.

There is a tendency in books about neglected science to reward curiosity with vindication. The strange researcher was right. The establishment laughed. History apologizes. This is satisfying and usually dishonest as a general template. Most laughed-at ideas are wrong. Some deserve laughter because their proponents refuse basic controls. A few contain a fragment of real observation buried under a bad explanation.

Cold fusion may belong to any of those categories depending on how narrowly one defines the claim.

The practical energy claim remains unestablished. No independently validated commercial LENR generator has emerged from nearly four decades of extraordinary promises. That absence is powerful evidence. A useful heat source is not a subtle statistical effect. Markets and laboratories would have many chances to measure it.

The broader condensed-matter nuclear question is less settled because it contains smaller, more specific possibilities. Electron screening in metals is real. Beam-driven nuclear reactions in deuterated targets are real. NASA has published lattice-confinement-fusion results under energetic irradiation. None validates chemical-input nuclear power.

The field becomes scientifically healthier when these statements are allowed to coexist.

It also becomes less profitable rhetorically.

Believers cannot recruit every lattice effect as cold fusion. Skeptics cannot use the failure of cold fusion to dismiss every unusual nuclear process in solids. Each mechanism gets its own burden.

This is the epistemic lesson I care about beyond LENR.

Modern science is extraordinarily good at studying phenomena that fit mature instrumentation and institutional categories. It becomes less comfortable around claims that mix weak signals, rare states, reputational hazards and enormous consequences. The default responses become overexcitement or neglect.

Both can be rational for a time.

Neither is a final method.

The way through is not permanent open-mindedness. Open-mindedness is too vague. The way through is experimental cruelty applied symmetrically.

Give the claimant the material condition he says matters. Then blind it.

Use the sensitive detector she requests. Then characterize the background for months.

Let the theory predict a new channel. Then require the product inventory.

Fund the weird experiment. Then publish the null.

This is not kindness toward heresy. It is a more efficient way to kill bad heresies and rescue good anomalies.

The distinction matters in other domains the Desk keeps returning to: UAP sensor claims, anomalous cognition, geophysical precursors, directed-energy bioeffects, unusual materials. Secrecy and stigma complicate all of them. But none escapes the same physical demand where physical evidence exists: make an observation travel.

Cold fusion is unusually generous as a case because the claimed output is energy. Energy can be counted. Nuclear products can be counted. Material states can increasingly be characterized. The field has fewer excuses now than it did in 1989.

That is why the ARPA-E experiment deserves a hard reading when its final results are available.

Do not ask whether LENR won.

Ask which cells were run. Which materials reached the claimed state. Which detectors were blind. Which nulls were published. Which anomalies replicated elsewhere. Which products balanced the heat. Which theories lost.

If the answer is that every strong claim disappeared under modern controls, the federal program will have done something useful. It will have purchased a cleaner ending than ridicule.

If one narrow effect survives, do not immediately call it cold fusion. Name the effect. Reproduce it. Measure its energy scale. Find the products. Let it grow into its own field without forcing it to carry the 1989 promise.

The most radical outcome would be modesty.

Perhaps a tiny screening enhancement becomes a useful neutron source. Perhaps a defect-dependent nuclear reaction exists at rates irrelevant to power. Perhaps hydrogen-rich metals reveal new solid-state behavior that is entirely chemical. Perhaps all the nuclear anomalies vanish.

The future does not owe the original claim a happy ending.

It owes the experiment an honest one.

Fleischmann and Pons gave the world a proposition too large for their cell. Thirty-four years later, ARPA-E gave several laboratories a smaller proposition: find something reproducible enough that people who do not want to believe it must measure it too.

That is a better ambition.

The heat that would not die was never just the heat in a calorimeter.

It was the residual uncertainty left when a scientific claim became socially dead before every experimental question became technically boring.

There is only one honorable way to remove that uncertainty now.

Make the boring experiment.
