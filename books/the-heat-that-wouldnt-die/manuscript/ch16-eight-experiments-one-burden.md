# Eight Experiments, One Burden

A portfolio is not a replication.

This is the first thing to remember when looking at ARPA-E's eight LENR awards. Eight teams can produce eight technically competent answers to eight different questions and still leave the central field exactly where it started if none of the questions are connected strongly enough to a shared claim.

The agency tried to avoid that problem by making reproducibility and diagnostic evidence part of the program's reason for existing. But the project list itself is heterogeneous. One team studies nanostructured potential wells. Another uses electrochemical co-deposition. Berkeley builds from ion-beam work in metal hydrides. MIT tests gas-loaded systems. Stanford works with nanoparticles and phonon stimulation. Michigan examines palladium-nickel nanocomposites and separately provides radiation-detection capability. Texas Tech contributes materials characterization and nuclear-product measurement.

This is a field in pieces.

That may be exactly what it needs.

Cold fusion was born as a unified interpretation before its observations had earned one. Heat, neutrons, tritium and palladium loading were assembled into fusion because fusion was the idea that made the press conference meaningful. When expected nuclear ratios failed, theory became more exotic while the umbrella stayed.

A modern program should reverse the direction. Let each observable earn its own mechanism first.

Suppose Michigan finds a reproducible thermal anomaly in a palladium-nickel nanocomposite but the independent radiation group finds no neutrons, gamma rays or ions, and mass spectrometry finds no unusual ash. That result is not partial proof of LENR. It is an anomalous calorimetry result with stronger constraints on nuclear explanations.

Suppose MIT sees a weak neutron excess in a gas-loaded system with the correct spectral signature but negligible heat. That could be interesting nuclear physics and terrible energy technology. Keep it separate.

Suppose Berkeley observes reaction-rate enhancements in a metal hydride under ion-beam stimulation that agree with conventional electron-screening models. That is a successful project even if it narrows the room available for extraordinary mechanisms.

Suppose every team reports nulls at useful sensitivity.

That is also a result if the tested state space is well documented.

The burden connecting all eight experiments is not make something strange happen. It is make a result portable enough that people outside the program know what changed.

Portability requires common language. Inputs must be expressed as actual energy, flux, loading and material state rather than proprietary stimulation recipes. Outputs must be expressed in calibrated physical units. Detector efficiencies and backgrounds must be published. Samples should have identifiers that survive fabrication, loading, measurement and destructive analysis. Control materials should be traceable through the same chain.

This sounds like laboratory administration until one notices how many historical LENR arguments are really metadata failures.

Which palladium lot was used? How was it annealed? Was the helium sample drawn before or after the cell was opened? Did the neutron detector threshold change during the run? Which calibration applies to the heat excursion? Was the apparent isotopic shift present before loading? Did the successful sample spend three months in a drawer after a previous experiment?

A sample without a biography is a rumor with mass.

The modern program has tools the 1989 investigators could barely imagine for recording that biography. High-resolution imaging can map defects before and after. In situ x-ray methods can follow hydride phase changes. Data acquisition can timestamp every temperature, voltage and detector pulse. Cloud storage makes raw data cheap to preserve. Automated analysis can be containerized and rerun. None of this guarantees correct interpretation, but it removes excuses for losing the experiment after the paper appears.

The burden also includes negative controls designed by people who do not want the effect.

This is the adversarial-collaboration principle. Let proponents identify the strongest conditions. Let skeptics identify the strongest artifacts. Agree on a protocol in which both sets of concerns are built into the apparatus. Blind the labels where possible. Decide before the run what outcome changes each side's belief.

Scientists dislike this language because belief sounds psychological. It is. Evidence changes human judgments. The formal version is to define likelihood ratios or decision thresholds. The practical version is simpler: write down what would make you stop saying the same thing.

Cold fusion has suffered from decades of experiments that could not injure the hypothesis.

A negative sample was insufficiently active. A positive sample proved something. A null laboratory lacked tacit knowledge. A successful laboratory had special material. A missing nuclear product implied a new branch. A detected product validated the nuclear interpretation. This asymmetry allows evidence to accumulate only in one direction.

A program funded to break a stalemate must make the hypothesis mortal.

For a preparation-specific claim, mortality might mean one hundred samples fabricated within the claimed active process window, characterized before testing, with no target signature above a predeclared detection threshold. For a radiation claim, mortality might mean detectors capable of seeing the product rate required by the proposed mechanism running through enough active exposure that the expected counts would be overwhelming. For an ash claim, mortality might mean sealed samples and isotope analysis whose detection limit is far below the inventory implied by measured heat.

The word expected is doing work. A hypothesis that refuses to predict magnitude remains difficult to kill.

This is why energy claims are eventually easier than subtle anomalies. If a process produces power, integrated exposure becomes enormous. The mechanism cannot remain forever below detection in every channel except one favorable instrument. The most scientifically frustrating LENR results are therefore the tiny ones: microwatts, trace isotopes, intermittent counts. They live near the scale where systematics are strongest and practical significance weakest.

ARPA-E's portfolio can perform a valuable triage by separating those regimes.

If nothing survives except low-level signals near instrument backgrounds, the energy case should be considered weaker even if scientific questions remain. If a macroscopic heat signal emerges, diagnostic effort should concentrate immediately because the product budget becomes testable. If a nuclear signal appears without energy, move it into nuclear physics rather than selling a generator.

This discipline protects public money from narrative drift.

It also protects surprising science from commercial pressure. A tiny reproducible nuclear effect could be ruined by premature claims of grid power. The fastest way to make a skeptical community stop listening is to multiply an interesting laboratory signal into a technology before scaling has been demonstrated.

The 1989 history is a warning about exactly that multiplication.

The portfolio has another burden: publication of nulls.

A program designed to answer whether a phenomenon exists cannot allow unsuccessful projects to disappear into final reports nobody reads. Null conditions need the same detail as positive ones. Which loading ratios were reached? Which temperature ranges? Which detector sensitivities? How many samples? Which predicted active states were traversed? What artifacts were discovered?

Negative maps prevent the field from returning to the same territory under a new name.

This is especially important because the language of LENR evolves. Cold fusion became condensed matter nuclear science, lattice-assisted nuclear reactions, solid-state fusion and other labels. Some changes reflect legitimate differences in mechanism. Others can obscure continuity. If a null experiment is described only by brand, a later proposal can claim novelty while reproducing the same physical conditions.

Parameter-space records are harder to rename.

The eight projects also create an opportunity for cross-project controls. A material fabricated at Stanford could be measured by Texas Tech or Michigan. A detector developed for one configuration could be placed near another. An anomalous sample could be shipped blind. These exchanges are scientifically more valuable than eight independent final presentations because they attack laboratory-specific artifacts.

An effect that dies in shipping is still information.

Perhaps the material state relaxes. Measure that. Perhaps the surface oxidizes. Seal it. Perhaps the originating apparatus contributes something essential. Then the apparatus, not merely the material, is part of the state and should be characterized.

Every failure can make the claim more specific if the program refuses to treat specificity as defeat.

This is a cultural difference from invention. Inventors protect flexibility. If one design fails, they change it. Science needs frozen moments where a design is allowed to fail without immediate rescue.

ARPA-E programs normally use milestones to manage this tension. Teams can explore between gates, then face quantitative criteria. LENR particularly needs gates tied to external observables rather than internal progress.

The strongest possible gate is independent replication.

Not another run by the same group. Not an effect seen by a collaborator using the same instrument. A second laboratory receives enough information or material to reproduce the result without the discoverer adjusting the apparatus after seeing failure. Ideally, a third does the same.

If an effect cannot survive this, it may still be interesting as a laboratory-specific phenomenon. It should not be treated as established nuclear energy.

The program's final burden is communication.

Every public update exists in an information environment where one phrase will be detached from its qualifiers. Nuclear anomaly will become breakthrough. Project completion will become DOE confirms. A patent will become classified technology. Skeptical caveats will become cover-up.

The only defense is excessive specificity.

Name the sample. Name the measured signal. Name the independent replication status. Name the energy balance. Name what remains unexplained.

This book uses the same rule because LENR has had enough nouns.

The eight experiments do not need one answer.

They need answers that can survive being separated from the people who want them.
