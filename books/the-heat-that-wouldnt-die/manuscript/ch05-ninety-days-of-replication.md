# Ninety Days of Replication

By the beginning of summer 1989, cold fusion had already lived several scientific lifetimes.

A normal experimental claim can take years to travel from one laboratory to enough independent groups that its weaknesses become visible. Fleischmann and Pons compressed that process into weeks. The reason was obvious. No one needed a billion-dollar accelerator to try. Every electrochemistry department could imagine a version of the cell. Every nuclear laboratory had detectors. The alleged prize was larger than almost any in postwar science.

So the world reproduced the experiment before it had agreed what the experiment was.

This sentence explains more of 1989 than the usual story of believers against skeptics.

A palladium rod in heavy water is a description, not a protocol. Dimensions matter. Purity matters. Surface treatment matters. Current density matters. electrolyte composition matters. Loading history matters. Calorimeter architecture matters. The location and efficiency of neutron detectors matter. So do weeks of operating history if proponents are right that high deuterium loading develops slowly and active states are rare.

The early laboratories did not have the luxury of knowing which variables would later be declared essential. They copied what was available from a press conference, preprints, conversations and quickly published papers. Some used palladium from different suppliers. Some changed electrode sizes. Some looked for neutrons because nuclear physics made that the cleanest early discriminator. Some focused on heat. Some drove cells hard to accelerate loading. Others used configurations optimized for detector access rather than thermal stability.

A field with a robust large effect should survive such variation. That is one reason the broad failure matters.

If room-temperature electrochemical fusion generated large reliable power under a wide range of palladium-deuterium conditions, 1989 should have ended quickly. Independent laboratories would have seen heat and products. The phenomenon would have become easier to produce as recipes converged. Instead the result fragmented. Positive reports often involved small signals, unstable cells or product ratios that disagreed with known fusion. Null results accumulated. Several celebrated confirmations disappeared.

The social temperature rose faster than the technical one.

This is where later LENR advocates make their strongest historical objection. They argue that critics treated nominal replication as decisive even when the key materials state—very high deuterium loading in palladium—had not been achieved or measured. If the active state occupied a narrow region of composition and microstructure, most rapid replications may have tested the wrong object.

The objection is not frivolous. It is incomplete.

A claim can fail because replications are poor. It can also fail because the original investigators have not specified what must be reproduced. Science cannot require skeptics to discover the hidden recipe while simultaneously crediting the claimant with an established phenomenon. If high loading is necessary, demonstrate it prospectively. Give another laboratory material that meets the condition. Predict which samples will work before the heat traces are opened.

In 1989 nobody could do that reliably.

The Department of Energy review process effectively became a national audit of this uncertainty. Panel members visited laboratories, examined calorimetry, neutron and tritium evidence, and asked whether the claimed effects survived ordinary nuclear accounting. By November the answer was no at the level required for a new federal program. The report's language was more measured than the cultural verdict that followed, but the direction was clear.

For many physicists, the case closed.

That closure had rational foundations. Known nuclear physics gave no plausible mechanism for the reported heat-product relationship. Independent replications were overwhelmingly disappointing relative to the scale of the claim. Some early positive evidence was demonstrably erroneous. Money was being requested for a phenomenon that could not be produced on demand. There were better places to spend it.

Science is allowed to allocate attention.

The more difficult question is what a minority of unresolved anomalies should mean after the main claim collapses.

Suppose ninety percent of positive reports are errors, five percent are contamination, four percent are analysis choices and one percent remain unexplained because the data are incomplete. That does not imply a real effect in the final one percent. It implies unresolved cases. The field's temptation is to collect those cases as if survival itself increases their probability. The skeptic's temptation is to treat the demonstrated errors as an explanation of every unexamined case.

Both are forms of completion by narrative.

A rigorous research program does something less satisfying. It identifies the highest-quality surviving claim, states it narrowly, and designs an experiment that can fail.

This did happen sporadically after 1989. Researchers at SRI International and elsewhere continued palladium-deuterium calorimetry, often emphasizing loading conditions and reporting excess heat in subsets of experiments. Japanese groups maintained interest through government and industrial programs. Los Alamos researchers investigated tritium claims in deuterium-loaded metals and discharges. Navy-affiliated laboratories later explored related anomalies. The work never converged into a demonstration the broader community could reproduce on demand.

That last sentence outweighs the institutional names.

Government laboratories can study bad ideas. Companies can fund anomalies. Prestigious scientists can be wrong for decades. A declassified report proves a program existed, not that its preferred interpretation was correct. This evidentiary discipline matters especially in fields where the fact of official interest is repeatedly offered as a substitute for the result.

The existence of continued work does establish something narrower: the scientific death of cold fusion in 1989 was not literal. Research persisted.

Why?

Partly because some investigators believed they had seen effects too structured to dismiss. Partly because the energy payoff remained enormous. Partly because electrochemical metal-hydrogen systems were genuinely complicated. Partly because institutional closure can increase the appeal of being the person who proves everyone wrong. And partly because a weakly reproducible anomaly is psychologically adhesive. It does not give enough evidence to settle, but it gives enough variation to preserve hope.

The same structure appears in other controversial research. A parapsychology experiment produces a small statistical effect that disappears under one protocol and returns under another. An atmospheric anomaly correlates with earthquakes in one region but not prospectively. A medical syndrome contains real suffering, heterogeneous causes and a disputed mechanism. Weak signals become harder, not easier, to resolve once communities organize around them.

Cold fusion is unusually instructive because the endpoint is physical energy.

If a real effect can be amplified, the controversy should eventually become cheap to settle. Heat can be accumulated. Nuclear products can be counted. Materials can be exchanged. The fact that this has not happened after decades is evidence. It should lower the prior probability of a useful robust phenomenon considerably.

But it does not make every new experiment irrational.

Expected value depends on cost as well as probability. A one-in-a-thousand chance of discovering a new controllable nuclear process can justify a modest, well-designed program if the experiment also advances useful measurement or materials science. The mistake is to price the research as if success were likely.

This is exactly what ARPA-E is built to do when it works properly. The agency can fund small portfolios of high-risk work whose failure does not threaten a national program. Its 2023 LENR awards are tiny beside mainstream fusion budgets. The purpose is not to declare equivalence. It is to buy information in a neglected region of hypothesis space.

The architecture is different from Utah in 1989. Instead of one charismatic claim racing outward, several teams test different hypotheses while shared diagnostic capability tries to make outcomes comparable. MIT focuses on unambiguous nuclear indicators in gas-loaded metal-hydrogen systems. Michigan combines high-resolution calorimetry with particle and mass-spectrometric measurements. Stanford explores nanoparticles and deuterium under controlled stimulation. Texas Tech provides characterization and nuclear-product detection. Berkeley works from known ion-beam interactions with metal hydrides toward systematically varied conditions.

These experiments can all fail without contradiction.

That is important. One of the old field's worst habits was treating every negative result as a failure to make the right material and every positive result as support for a common phenomenon. A portfolio should instead preserve local hypotheses. If a laser-stimulated hydride experiment sees no neutrons, it constrains that configuration. If a nanoparticle experiment sees a product, the claim belongs first to that preparation. Generalization comes after independent replication.

The ninety-day frenzy of 1989 did the opposite. Generalization came first. Everything was cold fusion.

The name itself encouraged this. It implied a mechanism before the evidence had stabilized. Later replacement terms—LENR, condensed matter nuclear science, lattice-assisted nuclear reactions—tried to broaden or soften the claim. Some are useful descriptive umbrellas. Others function rhetorically, allowing a failed mechanism to remain alive under a new label.

A mature program should be willing to abandon the umbrella.

If the only reproducible effect turns out to be electron-screening enhancement of ordinary beam-driven reactions in metal hydrides, call it that. If rare tritium appears from a discharge mechanism unrelated to calorimetric excess, separate the literatures. If anomalous heat vanishes under better calorimetry, let it vanish. If a novel isotope shift survives but produces negligible energy, do not keep selling a reactor.

The field needs disaggregation more than defense.

The first replication race also teaches a practical lesson about speed. Certain experiments cannot be accelerated merely because the question is urgent. Hydrogen diffusion has timescales. Materials anneal and crack. Background radiation needs long observation windows. Calorimeters drift across days. A detector characterized for an afternoon may show a different background distribution over a month.

The correct replication time is set by the slowest relevant variable, not by the news cycle.

This sounds obvious until money and prestige arrive. During the spring of 1989, every day without an answer felt intolerable. Institutions wanted to know whether to redirect energy research. Markets wanted to know whether fossil fuel had been made obsolete. Reporters wanted the next laboratory to vote yes or no. Science was asked to produce certainty at the speed of a political event.

It produced noise at that speed instead.

The later skeptical consensus was not wrong because of the noise. Much of the underlying evidence pointed negative. But the manner of the race left a gift to proponents: every rushed failure could be reclassified as an incompetent replication. The field learned to distrust the verdict because it had watched some laboratories sprint.

A better response to the next extraordinary but accessible claim would separate triage from adjudication. Rapid tests can identify obvious effects and gross artifacts. If they are negative, confidence falls. But a smaller number of carefully selected laboratories should then perform slow preregistered replications using shared materials and complete diagnostics. The purpose is not to indulge the claim indefinitely. It is to close the strongest version rather than a convenient version.

Google's later cold-fusion project effectively did this decades late.

The team was not trying to reproduce every 1989 cell. It chose several prominent experimental directions, built modern apparatus, learned the materials, and treated failure as publishable. That last condition matters more than it appears. In a stigmatized field, a laboratory may hesitate to spend years producing a null result nobody wants. Google could absorb the cost and publish the methods as part of the answer.

ARPA-E has now created a related opportunity with federal money.

Neither effort has erased 1989. They have changed what a serious replication can look like after 1989.

The irony is that cold fusion may become scientifically useful without ever becoming true in the form that made it famous. It has become a training ground for studying how to test rare-state claims, how to make null results valuable, and how to distinguish an underexplored parameter space from an established anomaly.

The first ninety days taught us how quickly a field can burn.

The next thirty years have been about learning what, if anything, survived the fire.
