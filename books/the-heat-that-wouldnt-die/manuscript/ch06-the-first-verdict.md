# The First Verdict

The Department of Energy did not wait for the controversy to become historical.

In April 1989, only weeks after the Utah announcement, Energy Secretary James Watkins asked the Energy Research Advisory Board to assess cold fusion. By November the panel had produced a report thick enough to contain something neither the triumphal nor suppression versions of the story particularly enjoy: detail.

The panel looked at calorimetry. It looked at neutrons, tritium, gamma rays and helium claims. It visited laboratories. It heard proponents. It examined the theoretical problem. Its conclusion was unfavorable. The evidence did not convincingly establish a useful source of energy from the claimed cold-fusion processes. The panel advised against creating special cold-fusion research centers or a targeted federal program.

This became the verdict everyone remembers.

What is less remembered is the shape of the verdict.

The report did not say no scientist should ever touch palladium and deuterium again. It recommended that proposals involving specific well-designed experiments be considered through normal peer-review channels. It distinguished between a national initiative and the possibility that individual questions might still merit investigation.

That is a subtle bureaucratic distinction with large scientific consequences.

A special program is an institutional bet that a field exists. It creates budgets, careers, workshops, review panels and a constituency. Ordinary proposal review leaves the burden on individual investigators to explain why a particular experiment is worth doing. After 1989, cold fusion largely lost the first pathway in the United States.

That was rational given the record.

The original claims implied an effect of revolutionary scale. Reproduction was poor. Expected fusion products were absent at levels commensurate with heat. Some reported nuclear signatures collapsed under scrutiny. No accepted theory bridged the enormous energy gap between chemistry and fusion. Committing large federal resources would have rewarded a claim before it had cleared the most basic experimental gate.

The panel was also operating under uncertainty created by extraordinary speed. A critic can reasonably say the review happened too early for a difficult materials phenomenon. A policymaker can reasonably answer that the review existed because extraordinary public claims were already demanding extraordinary funding.

You do not get to announce the energy revolution on Thursday and then ask the government not to evaluate it until the metallurgy is convenient.

The report is most valuable now as a template for how an institution should answer a disputed high-payoff claim. It did not need to prove impossibility. It needed to decide whether the evidence justified a program.

These are different standards.

Science often confuses them in public arguments. A negative funding decision is treated as proof a phenomenon is false. A decision to fund a small exploratory study is treated as proof the phenomenon is credible. Neither inference follows. Funding agencies allocate portfolios under uncertainty. Their job includes expected value, strategic relevance, opportunity cost and the cost of information.

The 1989 DOE panel effectively said the expected information value of a large dedicated program was too low given the state of evidence.

Thirty-five years later, ARPA-E would reach a different portfolio decision without contradicting that conclusion. Instrumentation had improved. Materials characterization had improved. The cost of a small distributed program was modest. Google had demonstrated that serious investigators could revisit the field and produce useful science from a null campaign. NASA had shown a distinct but neighboring form of lattice-assisted nuclear physics under energetic drive. The question had narrowed from build cold fusion to can we establish or decisively reject a reproducible LENR effect under modern diagnostics.

Different question, different cost, different decision.

The 1989 report also reveals how strongly theory influenced confidence. The absence of a plausible mechanism mattered because the claimed phenomenon sat far outside known nuclear reaction rates. This sometimes irritates experimentalists, who correctly insist that nature is not required to wait for theory. If a calorimeter reliably shows unexplained heat and mass spectrometry reliably shows new isotopes, a theorist's discomfort should not overrule the instruments.

But theory affects the error budget through prior probability.

When a measurement claims something common, a modest anomaly can be convincing. When it claims a process requiring many orders of magnitude departure from known rates and product channels, ordinary artifacts become much more probable than revolutionary physics. That does not license dismissal. It changes how much independent evidence is needed.

Bayesian language can make this sound more formal than it needs to be. If my kitchen scale says an apple gained two grams overnight, I suspect moisture or scale error before mass creation. If ten independent instruments in sealed chambers observe the same gain with isotopic changes, my prior objection eventually loses.

Cold fusion never reached the second stage.

The first DOE review therefore accumulated asymmetrical power over the field. Every later positive claim had to answer not only its own controls but the historical failure. This is appropriate to a point. Repeated failure should update priors. It becomes unhealthy only if historical reputation makes new evidence impossible to evaluate on its own merits.

The line is difficult.

A laboratory claiming LENR in 1995 should not receive the same prior confidence as a laboratory claiming a well-known electrochemical reaction. But if its experiment is transparent, independently reproduced and quantitatively consistent, the label cold fusion should not be allowed to reduce strong evidence to zero. Skepticism is a starting probability, not an exemption from looking.

This is where stigma enters as an epistemic variable rather than a complaint.

After the DOE report and the broader collapse of 1989 enthusiasm, mainstream career incentives changed. A young researcher could damage a reputation by working on cold fusion. Strong journals were less likely to view the subject as promising. Funding was scarce. Conferences and specialized journals increasingly became community spaces for believers and persistent investigators rather than mixed arenas containing strong skeptical groups.

That selection can degrade a field even if the initial skepticism is deserved.

When critics leave, the remaining community loses internal adversaries. Experimental practices can drift. Weak positive results receive more attention than strong nulls. Commercial actors become important sources of money. The field's language develops defenses against outsiders. Meanwhile outsiders become less familiar with the best surviving evidence because they have rationally stopped reading it.

The result is epistemic separation.

This does not mean the mainstream caused cold fusion to remain unproven. A real robust energy source would eventually overpower sociology. It means weak, difficult anomalies are especially likely to become stranded in subcultures where neither acceptance nor decisive falsification occurs.

The 2004 DOE review illustrates the persistence. Fifteen years after the first report, researchers submitted a body of LENR evidence for reconsideration. DOE assembled eighteen reviewers across relevant disciplines, using mail review and a one-day panel with presentations from several groups. The conclusion did not reverse 1989. Reviewers did not find the evidence sufficient to establish the claimed low-energy nuclear reactions.

But the distribution of opinions was not perfectly uniform. Some reviewers considered aspects of the calorimetry or nuclear-product work suggestive enough to merit further study. The report again left open the possibility that carefully designed proposals could be evaluated through normal mechanisms.

Cold fusion remained neither funded field nor forbidden field.

That middle state is more interesting than either mythology.

A suppressed technology story wants a gatekeeper who saw proof and buried it. The DOE record does not provide that. Reviewers saw inconsistent, incomplete evidence and declined a program. A triumphalist skeptical story wants a clean experiment that made every later investigation irrational. The record does not provide that either. The reports criticized the evidence severely while leaving particular questions open to proposal-level research.

Institutions rarely deliver metaphysical closure.

They deliver budgets.

This is useful when evaluating claims about government interest in anomalous science more broadly. Intelligence agencies studied remote viewing; that establishes institutional interest and expenditure, not psychic functioning. The military funds high-temperature materials for exotic propulsion; that establishes a program, not a flying machine. ARPA-E funds LENR; that establishes a wager on information, not a conclusion.

The evidentiary mistake is the same in each case: confusing what the state investigated with what nature allowed.

Government can nevertheless perform an important function in stigmatized research because it can buy adversarial infrastructure. A private believer may fund a laboratory seeking confirmation. A skeptical academic may prefer a safer topic. An agency can fund both the experiment and the detector designed to catch it lying.

That is what makes the modern LENR portfolio potentially valuable even before results are known.

One of the Michigan awards is specifically diagnostic: measure hypothetical neutron, gamma and ion emissions with modern instruments, characterize backgrounds, and help interpret signals. Texas Tech's role similarly emphasizes materials characterization and nuclear products. These projects create referees inside the experiment.

The first DOE panel had to reconstruct evidence produced by laboratories using heterogeneous equipment under frantic conditions. A modern program can decide in advance what evidence would count.

This is how a field earns another look without pretending the first verdict was corrupt.

The question is not whether DOE was wrong in 1989. Based on the evidence available, a large dedicated cold-fusion program would have been difficult to justify. The question is whether a 1989 resource decision should be treated as a permanent scientific law.

It should not.

Scientific claims remain reopenable because evidence can change. Resource decisions remain revisable because costs and tools change. What should not reset is the burden. A field with this history does not return to neutral. It returns carrying the null results, failed replications, product mismatches and commercial overclaims with it.

This is why the phrase fresh start is wrong.

There are no fresh starts in mature science. There are only better experiments performed in the presence of memory.
