# The Heat That Wouldn't Die

Cold Fusion, Metal Hydrides, and the Return of a Scientific Heresy

**Sven Hardy Benson**

On February 17, 2023, the United States Department of Energy announced ten million dollars for eight projects studying low-energy nuclear reactions. The agency making the awards was ARPA-E, which is where government goes when it wants to spend relatively small amounts of money on propositions that respectable committees are not yet ready to build a program around. The announcement contained the kind of sentence institutions learn to write only after being burned: the teams were being funded to determine whether the area showed promise, and, if not, whether they could show that it did not.

Thirty-four years earlier, on March 23, 1989, Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons had stood before reporters at the University of Utah and announced what newspapers immediately called cold fusion. Their claim was not modest. Electrochemistry performed with palladium and heavy water appeared, they said, to produce heat too large to be chemical and nuclear products consistent with fusion. If the interpretation held, one of the deepest practical barriers in energy had yielded on a laboratory bench.

It did not hold in the form announced. Laboratories raced to reproduce the result. Some reported anomalies. Many did not. Several early positive claims collapsed under calibration mistakes, contamination, statistical weakness, or retraction. By November 1989 a Department of Energy panel had concluded that the evidence did not justify a special cold-fusion research program. The field acquired something stronger than a negative result: a reputation.

Reputations are useful. Science cannot reopen every discarded claim each morning. Time, money and attention have opportunity costs, and a field that repeatedly fails to produce a reproducible effect deserves less confidence than one that does. But reputations are lossy compression. They replace a complicated record with an efficient social instruction: do not spend your career here.

This book is about what happened after that instruction worked.

The first surprise is that the underlying materials system never became boring. Palladium really does absorb extraordinary quantities of hydrogen and deuterium. Loading changes lattice dimensions, defect populations, phase behavior, stress, diffusion and surface chemistry. Hydrogen in metals remains difficult enough to matter in batteries, catalysis, embrittlement, superconductivity and nuclear technology. The second surprise is that a few serious institutions kept returning to the nuclear question, usually under narrower names and with much better diagnostics.

Google funded a multi-institution effort beginning in the 2010s to revisit prominent cold-fusion claims with modern materials characterization and calorimetry. The team published in *Nature* in 2019. It found no evidence of the claimed cold-fusion effect. That is not a footnote to the story; it is one of its central facts. The investigators also argued that the parameter space of highly hydrided metals and low-energy nuclear reactions remained scientifically underexplored.

NASA Glenn approached a neighboring territory from a different direction. Its lattice-confinement-fusion work did not claim room-temperature fusion from ordinary electrochemistry. The experiments used deuterated metals and energetic photons to create neutrons and accelerate deuterons inside a dense lattice. In 2020 a NASA-led team reported fusion-consistent neutron products in *Physical Review C*. The lattice mattered. So did an external energy source measured in megaelectronvolts. Calling this vindication of Fleischmann and Pons would erase the mechanism that made the reaction plausible.

Then ARPA-E held a LENR workshop in October 2021. Its framing was unusually clean. The agency acknowledged decades of reported empirical anomalies while stating that there was still no widely accepted, on-demand, repeatable LENR experiment and no sound theoretical basis. It proposed to attack the stalemate as an instrumentation and reproducibility problem. In 2023 it funded teams at MIT, Stanford, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Texas Tech, the University of Michigan and elsewhere. Some projects were designed to reproduce positive claims. Others were designed to detect neutrons, gamma rays, ions and isotopic products well enough to say whether anything nuclear was happening at all.

That is a different scientific culture from a press conference promising abundant energy. It is also different from pretending the entire subject vanished in 1989.

The thesis here is narrow enough to survive disappointment. Cold fusion as a practical energy source has not been established. There is no accepted mechanism by which ordinary chemical-energy inputs routinely produce nuclear-scale energy in metal hydrides. The burden of proof remains on any claimant. At the same time, the history of the field exposes a genuine problem in how science handles claims that are both extraordinary and experimentally ugly. When the claimed effect depends on loading, defects, history, geometry, contamination and rare local states, a failed reproduction can mean the claim is false, the recipe is incomplete, or the apparatus never entered the alleged state. Those possibilities are not equally likely, and they cannot be separated by sociology.

So the book follows the instruments.

The calorimeter gets more attention than the personalities. The neutron detector gets more attention than the word heresy. Palladium loading, blank controls, isotopic substitution, materials provenance, laboratory contamination, detector backgrounds and preregistered stopping rules matter because they are the places where a disputed phenomenon either becomes science or evaporates.

The evidence classes are kept separate throughout. Verified facts include the 1989 claims, the DOE reviews, the Google null campaign, NASA's published lattice-confinement experiments, and ARPA-E's public LENR awards. Credible but incomplete evidence includes some carefully reported excess-heat, tritium and materials anomalies that have not achieved broad independent replication. Disputed claims include commercial devices, large sustained heat outputs without commensurate nuclear products, and theories promoted far beyond their tests. Speculative synthesis begins only after those boundaries are visible.

There is no requirement that the ending rescue cold fusion. A good experiment may kill the thing cleanly. That would be progress.

The more interesting possibility is that the argument was framed badly from the beginning. Perhaps there is no single phenomenon called LENR, only a pile of errors plus several ordinary nuclear effects that become unusual in condensed matter. Perhaps lattice electron screening changes known reaction rates without opening an energy revolution. Perhaps a rare materials state exists but will never scale. Perhaps nothing remains once modern diagnostics close every loophole.

All of those endings are allowed.

The one ending this book refuses is the lazy one in which ridicule substitutes for measurement.
