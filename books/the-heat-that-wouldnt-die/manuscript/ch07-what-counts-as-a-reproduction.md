# What Counts as a Reproduction

There is a sentence that can keep a bad experiment alive indefinitely: you did not reproduce the conditions.

There is another sentence that can kill a real effect before anyone understands it: we followed the published method and saw nothing.

Cold fusion has spent most of its adult life between those two sentences.

The difficulty is not unique to LENR. Materials science is full of systems whose macroscopic behavior depends on a preparation history too complicated to summarize with a chemical formula. A steel alloy can have identical elemental composition and different strength because heat treatment changed grain structure and precipitates. A semiconductor wafer can have the correct nominal doping and still fail because an interface contains the wrong density of traps. A battery electrode is not defined by lithium, nickel and oxygen alone; particle morphology, binder, porosity, formation cycles and trace contamination matter.

Palladium loaded with deuterium belongs to this family before any nuclear claim enters.

So a reproduction cannot mean merely buy palladium, add heavy water and apply current. But neither can it mean reconstruct every unknowable microscopic detail of the original sample until a positive result appears. The first standard is too loose for a state-sensitive material. The second is unfalsifiable.

The only escape is to promote hidden conditions into measured variables.

This sounds bureaucratic and is therefore excellent science.

If proponents believe deuterium loading above a particular ratio is necessary, the ratio becomes part of the protocol. If grain size matters, characterize it. If a specific impurity range matters, assay it. If prolonged current history matters, log it. If mechanical stress or vacancy concentration is suspected, measure what can be measured and manufacture samples designed to vary those quantities independently.

The goal is not to reproduce an anecdote. It is to make the alleged active state portable.

Portability is the difference between craftsmanship and science. Many technologies begin as craft. Early photographic emulsions, steelmaking, crystal growth and semiconductor processing involved tacit knowledge that operators could feel before they could specify it. The existence of tacit knowledge did not make the effects unreal. Industrial maturity arrived when tacit variables were converted into controlled process windows.

LENR has often asked for the privileges of craft without completing that conversion.

A veteran experimenter says a piece of palladium feels wrong. A cathode from one supplier works while another does not. A cell needs to be conditioned for weeks. An excess-heat episode appears after a current excursion. These observations can be useful leads. They are not transferable evidence until another group can use them prospectively.

This is where sample exchange becomes more powerful than argument.

Imagine a laboratory that claims it can prepare active palladium. It manufactures twenty samples and characterizes each as deeply as practical. A second organization receives them under blind labels along with matched controls prepared to occupy nearby but allegedly inactive states. The receiving lab follows a predeclared protocol. The preparing lab predicts, before outcome data are opened, which samples should produce the target signature.

If the predictions survive, the field moves.

If they do not, the hidden-condition defense loses ground.

This design is better than having the second laboratory fabricate its own samples because it separates material preparation from measurement. If no effect appears, the first lab cannot say the second failed to create the material. If an effect appears only in predicted samples, the second lab cannot easily attribute the result to expectancy or selective analysis. A third laboratory can then repeat the exchange.

The structure resembles interlaboratory standards work more than a heroic discovery experiment. That is appropriate. Cold fusion does not need another hero.

The Google-funded re-evaluation moved in this direction by treating materials preparation as a research project in its own right. Its investigators found that producing highly hydrided palladium under controlled conditions was technically demanding. They developed electrochemical methods and diagnostics rather than assuming the older literature had specified the sample completely.

The outcome was negative for cold fusion.

That matters because it closes one rhetorical escape. A high-quality null generated after serious work on loading is more informative than a rushed 1989 cell that never entered the claimed regime. It does not prove no conceivable palladium microstructure could ever produce an anomaly. No finite experiment can prove that. It says prominent claims did not emerge under a much better-controlled traversal of their proposed territory.

A scientific hypothesis has to accept injury somewhere.

The idea that an unknown active state might exist becomes weaker each time investigators measure more of the state space and find nothing. It can remain logically possible forever. Scientific possibility is cheap.

Reproduction also has a statistical dimension often neglected in physical science. Suppose an effect is claimed in one out of ten properly loaded samples. A three-cell replication has a substantial chance of seeing none even if the effect exists. A critic who expects every cell to fire will overstate a null. A proponent who refuses to state the event probability can hide behind rarity forever.

So event rate belongs in the claim.

If active samples produce excess heat twenty percent of the time under a defined protocol, estimate that rate from an initial dataset and test it prospectively in enough new samples to distinguish twenty percent from background. Do not keep changing the expected rate after each series.

The same applies to burst-like radiation. A detector that sees one unusual cluster during a month needs a baseline long enough to estimate how often clusters appear without active material. Cosmic-ray showers, electronic glitches and environmental neutrons produce non-Poisson behavior. Background is not necessarily a flat number.

Reproduction therefore requires reproducing the measurement ecology, not only the cell.

This is one reason independent diagnostic teams in the ARPA-E portfolio are so useful. A group specialized in low-level radiation measurement can characterize detectors across multiple experiments rather than leaving each LENR team to become a nuclear metrology laboratory overnight. Shared diagnostics can reveal whether an apparent neutron signature follows the sample, the room, the detector model or the analysis pipeline.

The old literature contains too many cases where an anomaly and an instrument matured together. That is dangerous. If the detector is being tuned while the hypothesis is being tested, every change creates analyst freedom.

A modern reproduction should freeze more things.

Freeze detector thresholds before labels are revealed. Freeze calorimeter calibration functions. Freeze data exclusions. Freeze the time windows used to call radiation coincidences. Freeze the sample identity until analysis is complete. None of this prevents exploratory work. It separates exploration from confirmation.

Exploration is where a strange effect is allowed to be messy. Confirmation is where the mess has to predict something.

Cold fusion blurred these modes from the beginning. Fleischmann and Pons had years of exploratory experience but the public announcement presented a conclusion. The replication community then performed exploration under the social label of confirmation because nobody yet knew which variables mattered. Later positive groups continued modifying materials and protocols while citing each success as additional confirmation.

The result was a literature in which many papers were individually interesting but collectively difficult to score.

A good reproduction has another property: it is boring to interpret.

That may be the most important standard. If a positive result requires twenty pages explaining why this calibration drift is harmless, why this blank is not comparable, why this neutron peak appears only after filtering, and why the isotope ratio can be ignored, the experiment is not yet decisive. Frontier work can be genuinely complicated. But decisive evidence tends to simplify at least one axis.

A superconducting transition produces a sharp change in resistance and magnetic behavior that independent laboratories can reproduce. Gravitational waves appeared in a complicated interferometer, but the signal matched waveform predictions across detectors, and the field developed event populations. The Higgs boson required enormous statistical machinery, but separate experiments saw a consistent mass region and later accumulated compatible properties.

Cold fusion needs its equivalent of a feature that travels.

It might be a heat-to-helium ratio. It might be a reproducible neutron spectrum under a specific stimulation. It might be an isotopic product in sealed samples. It might be a threshold in loading that several laboratories can cross intentionally.

What it cannot be is an ever-changing family resemblance among anomalies.

This is where the term LENR creates both an opportunity and a risk. It frees investigators from defending the exact 1989 fusion mechanism. Good. But if low-energy nuclear reactions becomes a category containing electrochemical heat, gas-loading heat, glow-discharge tritium, nanoparticle transmutations, beam-enhanced fusion and unrelated lattice effects, reproduction loses its object.

The name needs to narrow at the moment evidence appears.

ARPA-E's individual project descriptions do this better than the umbrella. MIT is not being asked to prove LENR in general. It is testing prominent gas-loaded metal-hydrogen claims with neutron and isotopic diagnostics. Michigan is testing excess heat in deuterated palladium-nickel nanocomposites with a defined suite of measurements. Stanford is exploring deuterated nanoparticles under phonon stimulation. Berkeley works from ion-beam interactions and metal hydrides.

Each can produce a local answer.

The field should resist combining those answers prematurely. A positive nuclear signal under ion-beam excitation does not increase confidence in electrochemical excess heat by much unless a shared mechanism is demonstrated. A null in nanoparticles does not settle bulk palladium. Disaggregation prevents one experiment from laundering another.

The dream of reproduction is often a single recipe any competent laboratory can follow. That may come late. Earlier, the more realistic target is a shared object: active and inactive materials whose states are characterized, passed between teams, and tested blind.

If the phenomenon is too fragile even for that, energy technology can wait.

There is no shame in a scientifically real effect being impractical. Many beautiful effects never become products. The embarrassment begins when impracticality is used to preserve a commercial promise.

A reactor must eventually be reproducible by technicians who did not discover it.

Science can tolerate craft for a while. Engineering cannot.

The strongest LENR experiment of the next decade may therefore look anticlimactic. No press conference. No claim about replacing fossil fuel. A box arrives at three laboratories. The labels are hidden. Each laboratory runs the same protocol. At the end, the samples sort themselves into two populations using an observable that was named before anyone saw the data.

If that happens, people can argue about mechanism afterward.

Until it happens, the burden has not moved.
