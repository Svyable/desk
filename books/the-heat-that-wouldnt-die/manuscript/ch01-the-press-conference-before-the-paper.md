# The Press Conference Before the Paper

On Thursday, March 23, 1989, two electrochemists at the University of Utah did something that made nearly every later argument about cold fusion harder. They held a press conference.

Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons were not anonymous inventors working from a garage. Fleischmann had been a leading electrochemist. Pons chaired the chemistry department in Utah. They had spent years thinking about an old materials fact with an unreasonable implication: palladium can absorb enormous amounts of hydrogen. Replace ordinary hydrogen with deuterium, the heavy isotope whose nucleus contains a proton and a neutron, and the metal becomes a crowded three-dimensional host for nuclei that ordinarily repel one another with extraordinary force. What if the lattice changed the problem?

There is a good scientific question buried in that sentence. There is also a large distance between a good question and the claim that crossed the microphones in Salt Lake City.

The reported apparatus looked almost insulting beside the machines normally associated with fusion. A glass electrochemical cell. Heavy water. Lithium salts. A palladium cathode. A platinum anode. Electric current. No superconducting magnets. No vacuum chamber the size of a building. No imploding capsule. No plasma held at temperatures that make ordinary matter forget its chemistry. Fleischmann and Pons said the cell produced more heat than they could explain chemically and that nuclear processes were the only plausible source. They also reported nuclear products, including neutrons and tritium, although those signals never matched the heat claim in the proportions expected from familiar deuterium fusion channels.

That mismatch was visible early. It never stopped mattering.

If deuterium nuclei fuse by known pathways, the energy does not arrive anonymously. Particular reactions produce particular products at particular energies. D-D fusion can yield a proton plus tritium or a neutron plus helium-3, with well-characterized energies. Other branches and subsequent reactions exist, but the broad point is bookkeeping: nuclear energy leaves nuclear receipts. A calorimeter claiming watts of sustained nuclear heat while neutron detectors see only a whisper is not automatically evidence for a new clean branch. It is first a demand for a quantitative explanation of where the products went.

In March 1989 that problem competed with a much larger fact. If the heat was real and nuclear, energy had just changed category.

The press understood the category before the measurements had been reproduced. So did politicians. Utah was suddenly in possession of a possible energy revolution. Patents mattered. Priority mattered. Federal funding mattered. National laboratories and universities around the world began assembling cells with whatever palladium, heavy water and calorimeters they could obtain quickly. The pace was absurd by the standards of a materials experiment whose alleged behavior depended on loading a metal slowly with hydrogen isotopes.

This is where later histories often divide into morality plays. One version says Fleischmann and Pons bypassed peer review because they were reckless, triggering a deserved scientific correction. Another says university lawyers and priority disputes pushed them into publicity before the work was ready, after which hostile physicists destroyed a discovery they did not understand. Both contain pieces of the institutional situation. Neither can tell us whether a cell generated nuclear heat.

The useful question is narrower: what did the press conference do to the experiment?

It changed the clock.

A laboratory that might otherwise spend six months characterizing a palladium batch now had six days before another group appeared on television. A graduate student who would normally repeat a calibration until the residuals became boring was suddenly participating in a historical race. Journals received papers at emergency speed. Seminar rooms filled. Detector peaks acquired consequences before they acquired pedigrees. University public-relations offices learned enough nuclear vocabulary to become dangerous.

Replication under those conditions was not ordinary replication. It was a distributed emergency response to a scientific claim.

The first wave produced almost everything one would expect from such a response: apparent confirmations, clean nulls, confusing intermediate results, retractions, detector mistakes, arguments about loading and surface condition, and furious disagreement about what counted as copying the original experiment. Some teams looked primarily for neutrons because fusion should make neutrons. Others built calorimeters because the Utah claim centered on heat. Some tried to load palladium quickly. Some never reached the high deuterium-to-palladium ratios later proponents said were necessary. Some used different electrode geometries, purification methods, current histories and cell configurations. To a skeptical physicist this looked like moving the goalposts. To an electrochemist it could look like everyone had substituted a new experiment and called the failure decisive.

Both reactions are understandable. Neither is a substitute for a protocol.

The 1989 Department of Energy panel had to walk into this mess almost immediately. Energy Secretary James Watkins asked the Energy Research Advisory Board to assess the evidence. The resulting November report is worth reading now because it is more careful than either camp usually remembers. The panel did not find convincing evidence for a useful source of energy from cold fusion. It identified severe problems in calorimetry, nuclear-product evidence and reproducibility. It recommended against a special cold-fusion research program. But it did not recommend a ban. Proposals could compete through normal peer review, and the report left room for well-designed investigations of specific anomalies.

That is not suppression. It is also not vindication.

The distinction matters because cold fusion quickly acquired two competing foundation myths. In the mainstream version, the claim was tested and killed in 1989. In the dissident version, a young field was strangled before its difficult materials conditions could be learned. The archival record is less cinematic. A spectacular claim failed to become reproducible during an unusually intense worldwide test, the product ratios did not behave like known fusion, major errors were found in important positive reports, and federal reviewers declined to build a program around it. At the same time, not every experimental claim was individually resolved, not every laboratory tested the same material state, and a residue of anomalous reports survived among researchers who kept working.

That residue is where this book begins to differ from a history of scandal.

Scientific communities need forgetting. This is an underrated function. A field cannot retain every failed perpetual-motion machine, every irreproducible cancer cure, every claimed fifth force and every anomalous detector event at equal priority. The ability to lower attention after repeated failure is part of how research moves. Reputation is a computational shortcut performed by institutions.

But shortcuts create edge cases. A phenomenon that is false most of the time but true under rare conditions is especially vulnerable to being compressed into false. So is a phenomenon whose original explanation is wrong while the anomaly is real. Meteorites spent time in that category. Continental drift spent time adjacent to it, though the analogy is frequently abused by cranks who forget that plate tectonics eventually accumulated convergent evidence from seafloor spreading, paleomagnetism, earthquake belts and direct measurements. History does not award points for being mocked. It awards them when the data become harder to explain away.

Cold fusion never got that convergence.

The proper sympathy is therefore not with the claim. It is with the measurement problem.

Suppose a palladium electrode appears to generate a few watts more heat than electrical and chemical inputs can explain. Before nuclear physics enters, the calorimeter has to survive every boring explanation available: recombination of hydrogen and oxygen, changes in electrolyte inventory, calibration drift, gas losses, thermal gradients, changing heat-transfer coefficients, electrical measurement error, unaccounted stored chemical energy, sensor placement, evaporation, and analysis choices made after the data were visible. If the heat survives, nuclear products have to be sought with detectors whose backgrounds are measured in the same room over the same timescale. Materials must be characterized before and after. Controls must use ordinary hydrogen and blank samples. The electrode's deuterium loading must be measured rather than inferred from the recipe.

Now suppose the anomaly occurs in one electrode but not five nominally identical ones.

The easiest answer is noise. That answer should have high prior probability. The harder possibility is that the electrodes are not identical in the variables that matter. Palladium has grains, dislocations, vacancies, impurities, surfaces, cracks, residual stresses and phase boundaries. Hydrogen occupation changes those structures and is changed by them. A sample is not a chemical formula. It has a biography.

This is the crack through which LENR survived. Proponents said the active state was rare and material-specific. Skeptics answered, reasonably, that an effect whose conditions can be invented after every failure is not falsifiable. The argument then became sociological because the experimental object was poorly specified.

A thirty-year field can live inside that ambiguity.

The press conference made it worse by creating an implicit product before there was a stable phenomenon. Newspapers were not told that palladium-deuterium systems might contain an obscure anomaly deserving years of metrology. They were told, in effect, that fusion had arrived in a jar. When that large claim failed, smaller claims inherited its reputation. Later researchers could report an odd tritium excess or a materials-dependent heat burst and find themselves answering for the entire 1989 promise.

Some deserved the association because they kept using the promise. Commercial LENR has repeatedly produced devices whose details were proprietary, catalysts secret, demonstrations controlled by proponents, and energy balances difficult for outsiders to audit. A field asking for extraordinary evidentiary patience cannot also ask independent investigators to trust a sealed box. The moment the claimed output becomes commercially important, secrecy should lower confidence rather than raise it.

Yet the broad scientific territory is not sealed. Hydrogen in metals is ordinary enough to have textbooks and strange enough to keep surprising materials scientists. Nuclear reactions at low projectile energies are ordinary enough to be measured in accelerators and difficult enough that electron screening, target structure and detector background remain live technical subjects. The boundary between chemical and nuclear energy scales is enormous, which is precisely why a claim of crossing it is easy to formulate and hard to fake indefinitely if the energy is substantial.

This last point is the book's leverage.

A real nuclear-scale heat source cannot hide forever behind calorimetry. If it produces useful energy, the integrated output becomes large. Something in the inventory must change. Isotopes, particles, radiation, mass defects or daughter products have to carry the accounting. A new mechanism may alter branching ratios, but it does not get to abolish conservation laws. The larger the claimed power, the more generous nature becomes to the investigator looking for independent evidence.

The 1989 story is therefore not a reason to sneer at every later metal-hydrogen experiment. It is a reason to demand more channels at once.

Heat plus helium, if helium is the proposed ash. Neutrons plus calibrated spectra, if neutron-producing channels are claimed. Before-and-after mass spectrometry with blanks and contamination controls, if transmutation is claimed. Real-time loading measurements. Shared samples. Blind labels. Independent labs. A predeclared analysis before anyone knows which cell is supposed to be special.

This sounds obvious in 2026 because experimental culture has changed. Preregistration is normal in some fields. Data repositories are cheap. High-resolution mass spectrometers and neutron detectors are more accessible. Materials characterization can follow a sample from microscopy through loading through destructive analysis. Computing can fit thermal systems without pretending a convenient linear coefficient is constant forever.

In 1989 the scientific race did not wait for that apparatus to mature around the claim.

The irony is that the humiliation may eventually produce a better experiment than success would have. Had the first Utah cells worked on demand, nobody would care about the sociology. Reactors would exist. The absence of reactors forced the disputed residue into a harsher environment where every plausible artifact became part of the subject.

This is why Google could return to the field decades later without becoming a cold-fusion company. The question was no longer whether two chemists in Utah had announced the future. The question was whether modern control of materials and measurement could recreate any of the strongest claimed signatures. The team spent years learning to load palladium, build calorimeters and reproduce experimental conditions. It reported no cold-fusion effect.

That null result is not an embarrassment to the modern story. It is the modern story.

ARPA-E would later make the same intellectual move in bureaucratic form. The agency did not say cold fusion had been proven and unfairly ignored. Its 2021 workshop said the opposite: there was no widely accepted, on-demand repeatable experiment and no sound theoretical basis. The stalemate itself became the research object. What experiment could produce a result that the wider scientific community would believe, positive or negative?

That is a much smaller promise than unlimited clean energy.

It may be the first one the field can actually keep.
