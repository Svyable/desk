# Fusion in a Lattice, But Not That Fusion

The phrase cold fusion became so contaminated that a perfectly respectable nuclear reaction can now sound like a confession if it happens inside room-temperature metal.

This is unfortunate because condensed matter really can alter the environment in which nuclei interact. Electron screening is real. Dense deuterium loading is real. Nuclear reactions can be driven in deuterated targets. None of those statements requires the Fleischmann-Pons claim to be correct.

The confusion comes from collapsing three energy scales.

Chemistry lives mostly in electronvolts per atom. Nuclear binding energies live in megaelectronvolts. Accelerator experiments often occupy kiloelectronvolt or megaelectronvolt projectile energies even when the target itself is cold. A metal sample sitting at room temperature can host reactions caused by particles whose kinetic energies correspond to temperatures no macroscopic object would survive.

So room-temperature target does not mean room-temperature nuclear initiation.

This seems obvious until a headline says fusion achieved in a metal lattice and the old controversy rushes back into the sentence.

Nuclear astrophysics has long cared about fusion cross sections at low energies because stellar reactions occur through the tail of particle-energy distributions and quantum tunneling. Laboratory measurements are difficult as projectile energies fall because reaction rates become tiny. Electrons in target materials can screen nuclear charges and enhance measured reaction rates relative to bare-nucleus expectations.

The enhancement is interesting precisely because it can be measured against a known baseline.

A beam of deuterons strikes a deuterated target. Detectors count reaction products. Beam energy is known. Target composition is characterized. A model predicts a cross section without screening. The difference can be expressed quantitatively.

This is nuclear physics, not an energy mystery.

Cold fusion often borrowed the word screening to cross a much larger gap. If electrons reduce the Coulomb barrier in accelerator experiments, perhaps a palladium lattice can bring thermal deuterons close enough to fuse. The qualitative direction is right: screening helps. The quantitative leap is enormous. Ordinary measured screening enhancements do not make room-temperature reaction rates remotely comparable to power-producing levels.

The difference between helps and solves is where many exotic technologies live.

A sail helps a spacecraft accelerate; it does not solve interstellar travel. A metamaterial bends waves in unusual ways; it does not solve invisibility across all frequencies and angles. Quantum tunneling lets particles cross barriers; it does not make every forbidden reaction common. Electron screening modifies low-energy fusion; it does not automatically create an electrochemical reactor.

This is why the NASA lattice-confinement work is so useful as a conceptual control.

The target is a highly deuterated metal. The lattice is cold. Fusion reactions are reported. Yet the experiment contains an energetic external driver that old cold-fusion rhetoric often omits when borrowing the result.

NASA Glenn researchers used a high-energy electron beam to generate bremsstrahlung photons. Those photons, at energies above the deuteron photodissociation threshold, could break deuterons apart into neutrons and protons. The resulting neutrons interacted with other deuterons in the densely loaded metal, transferring enough kinetic energy to create energetic deuterons. Those deuterons could then undergo fusion or other nuclear reactions in the screened lattice environment.

The system is ingenious because the metal stores deuterium at enormous number density and provides an electron-rich environment while the radiation field seeds energetic particles internally.

It is not miraculous because the MeV arrives openly.

This changes everything about the evidentiary burden. The reaction products do not need to explain how chemical-scale inputs crossed the Coulomb barrier. An energetic path exists. The scientific questions concern reaction rates, screening, secondary processes and scaling.

The semantic danger is obvious. A public accustomed to the binary cold fusion true or false can hear NASA achieved fusion in a lattice and treat it as institutional admission that the 1989 skeptics were wrong. A skeptic can make the opposite mistake, dismissing the NASA work as rebranded cold fusion because some researchers have historical connections to LENR communities.

The experiment should be allowed to be itself.

This is an important general rule. Scientific ideas are not contaminated by neighboring bad claims. If a researcher spent years on an unpopular field, later data still rise or fall on method. If a mechanism shares vocabulary with pseudoscience, quantitative predictions still matter. Conversely, respectable institutional affiliation does not immunize an extraordinary interpretation.

Read the apparatus.

In NASA's 2020 *Physical Review C* report, deuterated erbium and titanium were exposed to bremsstrahlung radiation. The team measured neutrons in energy ranges interpreted as consistent with D-D fusion and additional processes such as Oppenheimer-Phillips stripping or boosted reactions. A companion theoretical paper described the reaction sequence and the role of screening.

There are several layers of claim here.

That the experiment irradiated deuterated metals: straightforward.

That nuclear products were measured and published after peer review: documented.

That the products support specific modeled reaction paths: scientific interpretation, open to further testing.

That the architecture can be scaled into useful power: unestablished.

That it validates electrochemical LENR: does not follow.

Keeping those layers separate makes the result more interesting, not less.

The NASA work reveals a broad design space between hot plasma fusion and the old dream of spontaneous room-temperature fusion. A fuel can be cold in bulk while a minority population of particles is energetic. A solid can confine fuel by lattice occupancy rather than magnetic fields. Nuclear reactions can occur in geometries designed around materials rather than plasma stability.

This does not necessarily improve energy efficiency. Generating the energetic photons costs energy. Reaction rates may be far too low for power. The external driver and target can suffer radiation damage. Neutron production creates shielding and activation issues.

A scientifically real neutron source is not automatically a reactor.

That sentence should be printed above every exotic-energy demonstration.

The history of fusion is full of devices that produce fusion without producing net energy. Farnsworth-Hirsch fusors can generate neutrons on a tabletop by accelerating ions electrostatically. They are genuine fusion devices. They are not power plants. Dense plasma focus machines produce fusion neutrons. Particle accelerators induce nuclear reactions every day. The hard problem is energy gain, repetition, materials survival and system efficiency.

Cold fusion rhetoric often skipped from reaction to power because the alleged environment was chemically cheap. NASA's lattice approach has to climb the same engineering ladder as any other nuclear technology.

This comparison helps rescue one useful intuition from the old field. Condensed matter can matter to nuclear processes without becoming a magic catalyst. Dense fuel, electron screening, crystalline geometry and defect structure can alter reaction probabilities. Researchers are allowed to investigate those effects aggressively.

What they are not allowed to do is round small enhancements up to a revolution.

The distinction also clarifies the ARPA-E LENR definition. At its 2021 workshop, the agency described LENR for program purposes as not-yet-understood processes characterized by nuclear-scale outputs with chemical-scale inputs. That definition intentionally preserves the hard part. If an experiment uses an external MeV beam, it may be relevant context but does not satisfy the core eV-input anomaly in the same way.

NASA's LCF therefore sits beside LENR rather than underneath it.

It demonstrates what a plausible bridge looks like when the energy source is explicit. The lattice increases target density and affects interactions. The external radiation supplies energetic particles. Nuclear detectors see products.

Any chemical-input LENR theory must provide an equally explicit bridge.

Phonons are often invoked. Collective lattice vibrations can concentrate energy locally. But ordinary phonon energies are tiny on nuclear scales. Coherent effects can change amplitudes and coupling, yet a useful theory must calculate how many orders of magnitude are gained and where conservation quantities go. Saying collective is not a calculation.

Plasmons, defect states, electron localization and nanostructures appear in other proposals. Each may change local electromagnetic environments. Each deserves the same question: what observable reaction-rate enhancement follows, and what products should be detected?

This is how frontier theory becomes experimentally polite.

The strongest possible LENR discovery may turn out to be disappointingly conventional. A particular condensed-matter environment might enhance a known reaction by a factor larger than expected but still far too small for power. Nuclear physicists would be interested. Energy investors would not.

That outcome would be intellectually healthy because it would separate the science from the promise.

It could also explain part of the literature. Some weak radiation anomalies might arise from known but under-modeled reactions in unusual targets while calorimetric claims remain artifacts. There is no requirement that one theory rescue every historical observation.

Again, disaggregate.

Fusion in a lattice is real in several conventional senses. Fusion from a chemically driven room-temperature palladium cell remains unestablished. The gap between those sentences is not semantic caution.

It is the energy source.
