# Tritium, Neutrons, and Dirty Glassware

The most dangerous phrase in low-count nuclear measurement is above background.

Background is not one thing. It is the laboratory breathing.

Cosmic rays strike the atmosphere and generate secondary particles. Building materials contain uranium, thorium and potassium isotopes. Radon enters rooms and decays. Nearby equipment produces electromagnetic interference. Photomultiplier tubes emit dark counts. Scintillators respond imperfectly to different radiation types. Electronic thresholds wander. Samples and glassware carry histories no one wrote down.

If a claimed signal is large, these details become nuisance corrections. If the signal is a handful of counts, they become the experiment.

Cold fusion spent much of 1989 in the second regime.

Neutrons were attractive because they appeared to offer a direct test. Known deuterium fusion produces 2.45 MeV neutrons in one common branch. A detector seeing a statistically convincing excess at the expected energy while a deuterated palladium cell operated would be more specific than heat. Several early groups reported neutron signals. Some claims were withdrawn. Others remained weak or controversial. Large, repeatable fluxes commensurate with the heat did not materialize.

The episode is a museum of detector failure modes.

A neutron detector rarely measures neutrons as little labeled objects. It measures secondary ionization or scintillation produced when neutrons interact with detector material. Pulse-shape discrimination may separate neutrons from gamma rays, but imperfectly. Calibration sources establish efficiency across energy ranges, but geometry matters. Moderators alter spectra. Electronics convert analog pulses into thresholds and histograms. A peak can be created by the detector system as easily as by the sample if one does not understand the chain.

The correct background measurement is not an empty room for twenty minutes. It is a distribution across the same durations, environmental conditions and electronics states used during the active experiment.

This is where low-rate anomaly research often fails. Investigators compare an interesting hour with an average background and report a sigma value as though counts were independent Poisson events. Real backgrounds can cluster. Cosmic showers create correlated particles. Radon varies with weather and ventilation. Electrical transients follow equipment cycles. A long baseline may reveal that apparently impossible clusters happen every few weeks.

Time is an instrument.

Tritium created a different kind of excitement. Unlike neutrons, tritium can accumulate. If deuterium reactions produce tritium inside a cell, one can in principle sample the electrolyte or gas and measure an isotopic increase after the event. Several laboratories reported tritium above expected levels in metal-deuterium systems, including work associated with researchers at Texas A&M and Los Alamos in the broader post-1989 period.

But tritium is also a contaminant with an institutional biography.

A laboratory that has handled tritium before can have it in places nobody expects. Glassware can carry residues. Water stocks vary. Scintillation cocktails and counting procedures require quench correction. Small changes in sample preparation can shift inferred activity. At low levels, a result needs provenance more than excitement.

This is why dirty glassware is not a cheap skeptical joke. It is a causal hypothesis.

The 1989 DOE review treated contamination and measurement ambiguity seriously because the reported product levels were small and inconsistent. One cannot infer a new nuclear mechanism merely because contamination has not been proven in every case. The burden runs in the other direction. The experiment should make contamination implausible by design.

Use virgin components where feasible. Assay them before the run. Keep active and control preparation physically separated or randomized. Include process blanks that pass through every handling step without deuterated material. Send samples to an independent analytical laboratory under blind labels. If tritium rises only in the active condition and the result repeats, the contamination hypothesis becomes expensive.

The word only matters.

A field with a history of selective reporting needs all controls reported, not only successful cells. If ten active samples and ten blanks are processed and one active sample shows tritium, the meaning depends on the distribution of the other nineteen. A single blank with a similar level can change the interpretation completely.

The same logic applies to helium, isotopic shifts and claimed transmutation products. Mass spectrometers are extraordinarily sensitive, which means they are extraordinarily good at finding things that arrived by routes other than the proposed reaction. Laboratory air, polishing compounds, electrode impurities, sputtered chamber material, previous samples and instrument memory can all become apparent products.

Modern surface analysis has made this problem both better and worse.

Better, because investigators can map composition at nanoscale resolution and distinguish isotopic ratios with much greater precision. Worse, because enough sensitivity guarantees anomalies. Every real material is dirty if the microscope becomes powerful enough.

The scientific question is not whether something unexpected is present. It is whether the unexpected thing tracks the experimental variable under controls.

This should be the central law of anomalous materials analysis.

Find boron in a spot after a run? Was boron present in the polishing slurry? Find iron where nickel was expected? Did the chamber sputter stainless steel? Find an unusual isotope ratio? What is the counting uncertainty, fractionation correction and natural variation? Was the same ratio measured before the experiment on the same instrument?

The cold-fusion literature contains transmutation claims whose evidentiary quality ranges from intriguing analytical puzzles to results that never escaped contamination concerns. Lumping them together is useless. Each requires a chain-of-custody audit.

Nuclear forensics offers the right attitude. Analysts working with safeguards or treaty verification do not treat isotope measurements as mystical signatures. They ask where the sample came from, how it was collected, what blanks say, how instruments were calibrated, and which process histories can generate the observed ratios. An extraordinary LENR claim deserves at least that level of boredom.

ARPA-E's decision to fund diagnostic capacity separately from some effect-producing teams is therefore more important than the public controversy around the program. Michigan's radiation-detection project was designed to measure hypothetical neutron, gamma and ion emissions from LENR experiments using modern instrumentation and explicit background analysis. Texas Tech's award similarly joined materials characterization with nuclear-product detection.

A diagnostic team can become an institutional skeptic without being hostile to the hypothesis.

Its incentive is to know the detector, not to make the cell work. That division of labor matters. The people who spend two years optimizing a difficult material preparation naturally become invested in seeing it produce something. The people who calibrate a neutron detector against known sources can say the apparent burst is gamma leakage without feeling that their project died.

Good programs create roles in which disappointment is a successful output.

This is the opposite of a demonstration staged for investors. In a demonstration, every instrument becomes scenery around the device. In an adversarial experiment, instruments are allowed to contradict each other.

Suppose a cell shows a thermal event while the neutron and gamma detectors remain flat. That does not prove the heat is chemical, but it narrows nuclear mechanisms. Suppose a neutron burst occurs with no heat. Perhaps the nuclear rate is too small to matter energetically; the field should resist multiplying the neutron count into an imagined reactor. Suppose mass spectrometry shows an isotope shift with no radiation or heat. That may be a contamination problem, an ordinary fractionation effect or an interesting nuclear anomaly, but it belongs first to analytical chemistry.

Disaggregation again.

The original cold-fusion story wanted all roads to point to one revolution. Modern science should let the roads end separately.

This also protects genuinely strange results. If one narrow nuclear signature survives while the energy claim dies, the community can study it without carrying the burden of free-energy rhetoric. A weak reaction-rate enhancement in a deuterated lattice could be scientifically real and economically irrelevant. That would still be worth understanding.

The NASA lattice-confinement experiments are a useful comparison because their neutron spectroscopy occupies a much higher-signal regime tied to a known energetic driver. The team used calibrated detectors and reported neutron energies consistent with D-D fusion and additional nuclear processes after bremsstrahlung irradiation of deuterated erbium and titanium. Critics can debate interpretation and scaling, but the product claim has a specified energy structure.

A neutron at the right energy is more informative than a counter clicking more often.

Energy-resolved detection should therefore be a minimum ambition for modern LENR work whenever count rates permit. So should multiple detector types. A signal that appears in one scintillator but not an independent detector with different systematics is less persuasive. Coincidence methods can reject backgrounds. Shielding configurations can test environmental sources. Moving detectors changes geometric efficiency in predictable ways for a source associated with the sample.

Spatial tests are underrated. If neutrons originate in the cell, count rates should scale with distance and shielding according to transport physics. If the anomaly follows an electrical cable instead, suspect the electronics.

This is not glamorous physics. It is how glamorous mistakes are removed.

The dirty-glassware problem has a moral equivalent too: literature contamination. Once a disputed number appears in one paper, later reviews copy it without tracing the original method. A claimed tritium ratio becomes a fact about LENR. A reported neutron burst becomes evidence that nuclear products have been repeatedly observed. The citation chain grows cleaner while the underlying sample remains dirty.

Research on anomalous subjects needs source custody as much as sample custody.

Go back to the original paper. Read the methods. Find the control counts. Check whether a later correction was published. Distinguish a conference proceeding from a peer-reviewed result. Distinguish a government report describing a claim from a government experiment confirming it.

This book follows the same rule. An ARPA-E award proves federal funding. It does not prove the proposed effect. A NASA presentation proves NASA researchers presented the work. The *Physical Review C* paper is evidence of the experimental result reported under peer review, not of a power technology. A patent proves filing.

The discipline can feel pedantic until one remembers what is being claimed.

Matter changing identity at room temperature deserves pedantry.

If LENR ever becomes accepted, I expect the breakthrough paper to devote an indecent amount of space to blanks.
