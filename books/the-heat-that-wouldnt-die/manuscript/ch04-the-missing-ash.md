# The Missing Ash

A nuclear reaction is not a mood. Something has to come out.

This elementary fact became the most persistent technical problem for cold fusion almost immediately. Fleischmann and Pons did not merely report a little neutron activity in a strange chemical cell. Their central claim was heat—sometimes at levels they believed exceeded any plausible chemical source. If that heat came from familiar deuterium fusion, the expected nuclear products should have been abundant enough to make the laboratory a very different place.

The mismatch is not subtle.

Two deuterons can fuse through known branches. One produces a proton and tritium. Another produces a neutron and helium-3. The released energies are measured in megaelectronvolts. At ordinary thermonuclear energies, the branches occur with comparable order of magnitude. A heat output corresponding to a macroscopic rate of such reactions would imply particle fluxes that standard nuclear detectors could see and, at sufficiently high power, radiation hazards nobody would confuse with background.

That did not happen in the Utah cells.

The early cold-fusion literature therefore acquired a peculiar architecture. Heat was large, when reported. Neutrons were tiny or intermittent. Tritium was sometimes reported at levels that did not reconcile with neutrons. Helium-4 later became a favored candidate for the missing nuclear ash because a hypothetical branch converting two deuterons to helium-4 and transferring roughly 24 MeV into the lattice could, in principle, account for heat without a large penetrating-radiation signature. But the ordinary free-space D-D reaction does not dispose of energy that way at useful rates. A mechanism had to be invented in which the condensed-matter environment changed not merely the rate but the reaction outcome.

There is nothing illegitimate about proposing new reaction channels. Physics progresses when measurements force theory to widen. The problem is sequence. A new channel should be demanded by reproducible evidence, not summoned to rescue a calorimetric anomaly after expected products fail to appear.

This is where the word ash becomes useful. If a cell produces nuclear-scale heat, what accumulates?

Take the helium-4 proposal seriously for a moment. If each helium nucleus corresponds to about 24 MeV of released energy, then a measured amount of integrated heat predicts a measured number of helium atoms. One can calculate the expected concentration, sample the gas or material, compare with atmospheric helium backgrounds, use blanks, and ask whether the ratio of helium to heat follows the nuclear bookkeeping. The experiment becomes less mystical because the hypothesis writes its own invoice.

The hard part is that helium is everywhere inconveniently. Atmospheric helium can contaminate samples. Seals leak. Vacuum systems have memory. Palladium and apparatus components can contain trapped gases. Sampling a tiny helium excess after a long electrochemical run requires chain of custody worthy of forensic chemistry. If the effect is small, background dominates. If the claimed heat is large, the predicted helium inventory becomes easier to test.

Again, scale is the friend of skepticism.

Tritium presents a different problem. Heavy water and deuterium systems can be contaminated with small tritium inventories because tritium exists in nuclear-industry materials and environmental traces. Laboratories handling tritium can contaminate glassware. Scintillation counting at low levels requires careful standards and quench corrections. A claimed rise in tritium concentration therefore needs blanks, independent laboratories, isotopic provenance and a mass balance. It also needs to agree with whatever reaction mechanism is supposed to be producing the heat.

Several early reports became notorious because apparently nuclear signals later traced to mundane causes. Neutron detection is especially treacherous at low count rates. Cosmic rays generate background. Electronic noise creates pulses. Gamma rays can leak into neutron channels depending on detector discrimination. A detector placed near an electrochemical apparatus can respond to electromagnetic interference. Statistical fluctuations look dramatic when the expected background is only a few counts per hour and the analyst knows exactly when the interesting cell was turned on.

A handful of neutrons is not a fusion reactor.

The 1989 replication race demonstrated this brutally. Some laboratories announced neutron peaks and then withdrew them. Others set upper limits far below what large heat claims would imply under known D-D fusion. The Department of Energy panel treated the product mismatch as a central reason the nuclear interpretation was not persuasive.

The cold-fusion community responded in several ways. One was to argue that the lattice enabled a radically different branch dominated by helium-4 without strong neutron emission. Another was to argue that nuclear activity occurred in bursts localized to microscopic sites, making average product relationships misleading. A third was to expand the phenomenon beyond fusion into low-energy nuclear reactions or condensed-matter nuclear science, loosening the requirement that one mechanism explain every observation.

The last move is scientifically defensible only if it increases precision rather than reducing it. A broader label can admit that the original theory was wrong. It can also become a bag large enough to hold every unexplained result.

LENR is useful when it means a research question: are there reproducible nuclear signatures in condensed-matter systems under conditions where conventional nuclear models predict negligible rates? It is useless when it means whatever anomalous heat, isotope, radiation or transmutation claim is currently difficult to dismiss.

The products decide.

This is why the modern ARPA-E portfolio is more interesting than its headline. Several projects are not primarily trying to build a heater. They are trying to detect nuclear products with calibrated instrumentation. The MIT project was framed around unambiguous indicators such as emitted neutrons and nuclear ash with unnatural isotopic ratios. Texas Tech was funded for materials characterization and nuclear-product detection across the program. Michigan received support for independent neutron, gamma and ion measurement.

That infrastructure acknowledges the lesson of 1989: a calorimeter alone cannot carry the claim.

Consider the hierarchy of evidence. An unexplained heat excursion in one cell is weak because calorimetry has many failure modes. Repeated isotope-dependent heat in blinded samples is stronger. Heat accompanied by a time-correlated nuclear product is stronger again. A nuclear product with the correct energy spectrum and isotopic signature is stronger than a generic radiation count. A quantitative relationship between integrated heat and accumulated ash, reproduced by an independent laboratory from shared material, would be difficult to wave away.

The hierarchy works in reverse for artifacts. If a neutron signal appears only when one electronic instrument is operating, suspect interference. If tritium appears in both active and blank cells from the same glassware batch, suspect contamination. If helium concentration scales with leak rate rather than heat, suspect air. If mass-spectrometry peaks move when the instrument is recalibrated, suspect analysis before transmutation.

This sounds severe because the claim is severe.

Nuclear physics has unusually good accounting compared with many frontier fields. Consciousness research can argue for decades over whether a weak behavioral effect represents perception, expectation or statistics. A nuclear reaction changes nuclei. The products may be hard to measure at tiny rates, but a useful energy source would generate an enormous integrated number of events. The larger the commercial promise, the less plausible permanent ambiguity becomes.

This gives the field a falsification route that is much cleaner than public debate.

Suppose a company presents a one-kilowatt LENR heater operating continuously for a week. That is more than six hundred megajoules of energy. If chemical storage is excluded by mass and known enthalpies, a nuclear explanation becomes tempting. But the energy also predicts a nuclear inventory. A mechanism based on D-D to helium-4 would imply on the order of 10^20 reaction products. That is not a few atoms lost in a leak. The fuel and ash should be measurable before and after with independent mass spectrometry. If the inventor says the ash is somehow absent, the mechanism must explain how nuclear binding energy was released without changing nuclear composition.

At that point the burden is not unfair. It is arithmetic.

Small laboratory anomalies inhabit a harsher zone because the predicted product inventory may approach contamination backgrounds. This is where experimental design matters most. The sample should be sealed where possible. Materials should be assayed before use. Isotopically unusual tracer compositions can help distinguish generated products from environmental contamination. Duplicate samples can be sent to laboratories that do not know which ones were active. Radiation detectors should run long baselines before and after the experiment to map background distributions rather than treating one quiet hour as normal.

The time structure matters too. A heat burst followed minutes later by a detector excursion can look correlated if the window was chosen after seeing the data. Define the lag before the experiment. Synchronize clocks. Publish the whole series. If a burst mechanism is proposed, state the expected particle multiplicity and duration.

These procedures do not assume the effect is fake. They assume researchers are human.

One of the strangest features of the cold-fusion story is how quickly absence of expected products became evidence for exotic new physics rather than a reason to lower confidence in the heat measurement. This is psychologically understandable. The original claim was too large to surrender easily. If the neutrons were missing, perhaps the lattice was doing something profound.

But missing evidence cannot carry both roles. It can motivate theory; it cannot verify the theory motivated by its absence.

NASA's lattice-confinement-fusion experiments provide a useful control because the nuclear products are not shy. The team used energetic bremsstrahlung photons to photodissociate deuterons, generating neutrons that then scattered from other deuterons in a dense metal deuteride. They measured neutron energies consistent with fusion and other nuclear reactions. The experimental architecture is complicated, but the logic is conventional: an energetic input creates energetic nuclear projectiles, a metal lattice modifies the local environment and density, and nuclear detectors see products in expected energy ranges.

The lattice participates without abolishing nuclear bookkeeping.

That distinction is exactly why NASA's work should not be recruited as proof of electrochemical cold fusion. It shows that condensed matter can be part of an interesting fusion geometry. It does not show that electronvolt-scale chemical inputs trigger watt-scale nuclear heat. The external photons in the NASA experiments carry megaelectronvolt energies. Remove that fact and the story becomes misleading.

The same discipline should be applied to every modern claim. What entered? What left? Which channel measured it? Which products were predicted before the run? Which alternative sources of those products were excluded?

A field matures when these questions become more interesting than the word fusion.

The missing ash has been cold fusion's most damaging absence for nearly four decades. If the field ever changes status, I suspect it will not be because a calorimeter trace becomes more beautiful. It will be because something countable appears beside the heat and refuses to go away.
