# Whose Water Is It

The Colorado River does not know who was here first.

It falls as snow, runs through canyons, disappears into reservoirs, seeps into fields, turns turbines, fills municipal pipes, crosses a national border, and evaporates under a sun that has never read a water-right decree.

People supplied the order.

Across much of the American West, one of the oldest rules is also one of the easiest to remember: first in time, first in right. A person who put water to a recognized beneficial use earlier could hold priority over someone who came later. In shortage, the junior user could be cut before the senior one.

As allocation mechanisms go, the appeal is obvious.

The rule is legible. It creates expectations. A farmer deciding whether to plant an orchard, a town deciding whether to build houses, or a lender deciding whether to finance either one can ask a practical question: how secure is this water right when the river is low?

The answer is never perfectly simple, but priority gives it structure.

It also gives history extraordinary power.

A right perfected decades ago may outrank a use that looks more valuable today. A growing city may have more residents, more tax revenue, more political visibility, and more willingness to pay than an older agricultural user and still confront a legal order shaped by earlier claims. A community that did not have the political or legal opportunity to formalize its water use on favorable terms may discover that the past has been converted into a present-day ranking.

That makes the Colorado River look, at first glance, like the purest waiting list in this book.

It is not.

The river is governed by something people in the basin call the Law of the River, which is less a single law than a stack of compacts, treaties, statutes, court decisions, contracts, federal operating rules, state water-right systems, tribal rights, conservation agreements, and emergency measures accumulated over a century.

The 1922 Colorado River Compact divided obligations between the Upper and Lower Basins. A 1944 treaty governs deliveries to Mexico. Federal reservoir rules shape how Lake Powell and Lake Mead are operated. States administer water rights under their own legal systems. Tribes hold rights that arise from distinct legal doctrines and histories. Cities, irrigation districts, farms, power users, environmental programs, and federal agencies all occupy different positions in the structure.

So the first correction is important.

The Colorado River Compact is not a numbered line of individual farmers ordered by the date they showed up.

And prior appropriation is not the only mechanism operating on the river.

The allocation system is layered.

That makes it more interesting.

In 2026, the physical system forced those layers into unusually sharp view. The Bureau of Reclamation's annual operating plan kept the Lower Basin under a formal Shortage Condition. In April, Reclamation reported that total system storage was about 36 percent of capacity. Its minimum-probable forecast for Lake Powell inflow was 2.78 million acre-feet, only 29 percent of the historical average. The agency announced emergency operational changes, including additional releases from Flaming Gorge and a reduction in the annual release from Lake Powell to Lake Mead.

These are the kinds of numbers that make law feel suddenly physical.

A compact can divide water.

A court can define a right.

A contract can promise a delivery.

None of them can make snow fall.

That fact separates water from the GPU chapter.

When cloud-computing capacity is scarce, firms can build more data centers, manufacture more accelerators, improve software, shift workloads, or add power infrastructure. The response may be slow, expensive, environmentally difficult, or technically constrained, but the supply side can move.

A river is different.

You can build storage.

You can line canals.

You can recycle wastewater.

You can desalinate in some places.

You can change crops, move water, conserve, trade, or pay users not to consume.

But you cannot scale the watershed the way a cloud provider scales a fleet.

When hydrology shrinks the available supply, the allocation rule becomes more visible because there is less slack to hide it.

This is what waiting lists do in every domain. Scarcity turns background procedure into foreground politics.

In a hospital with abundant beds, admission rules are paperwork. During a surge, they become ethics.

In a school system with empty seats, preferences are administrative details. When every family wants the same ten schools, priorities become destiny.

On a river with comfortable reservoirs, legal differences can remain dormant for years. When storage falls and runoff disappoints, people discover which promises are firm, which are contingent, which can be renegotiated, and which have merely been ignored during better times.

The phrase first in time, first in right sounds like a queue because it is a queue-shaped rule. It rewards sequence. Earlier claims receive priority over later ones.

But the moral logic is not the same as first come, first served at a bakery.

The customer first through the door did not invest millions of dollars on the assumption that her place in line would still matter forty years later.

Water users do.

A priority right can support an orchard that takes years to mature. It can support a town whose bonds assume a water supply. It can support irrigation infrastructure with a useful life measured in decades. The rule does not merely decide who receives the next gallon. It helps define whether long-lived investments are financeable at all.

This is the strongest case for seniority, and any argument about reform that skips it is unserious.

Stable property-like expectations can produce value precisely because they are difficult to reopen every year.

Imagine the alternative in its purest form.

At the beginning of every summer, a commission calculates which use of water is most socially valuable. It scores food production, municipal need, ecological value, employment, tribal claims, recreation, power generation, household affordability, future growth, and perhaps dozens of other variables. Then it reallocates the river accordingly.

At first this sounds modern. Rational. Responsive.

It would also terrify anyone asked to make a thirty-year investment.

Who chooses the weights?

What happens after an election?

Does a factory outrank a farm because it produces more revenue per acre-foot?

Does a city outrank a tribe because it has more voters?

Does a protected ecosystem receive points for existence value, and if so how many?

What happens when the model changes?

A constantly optimized allocation system can become a permanently politicized allocation system.

Durable rights protect against that.

They also freeze earlier distributions.

This is the central tradeoff.

Seniority converts history into certainty.

Reallocation converts uncertainty into discretion.

Neither is free.

The Colorado River makes this conflict harder because its legal history was written around a physical baseline that has not remained still. A priority system is easiest to defend when the amount being divided is roughly stable. Once the river persistently produces less than users planned around, the institution faces a choice it cannot avoid: enforce old priorities more sharply, pay for changes, negotiate new rules, or let shortages produce disorder.

The system has tried all four in different forms.

Water markets are one response.

They matter because they complicate the easy story that western water is simply locked into century-old uses. Rights can often be leased, transferred, conserved, or exchanged, subject to state law, contracts, third-party protections, tribal interests, environmental constraints, and the practical ability to move the water from one place to another.

This means markets and priority are not opposites.

A transferable senior right can become more valuable precisely because it is senior.

The priority establishes security. The transfer mechanism allows some reallocation.

That combination can produce flexibility without requiring the state to rewrite every ranking from scratch.

It can also produce new problems.

A transfer that looks efficient on paper may injure people who were not parties to the transaction. A farming region can lose economic activity if water leaves agriculture permanently. Return flows that downstream users relied upon can change. Tribal claims may intersect with state systems in ways that make ordinary transactions inadequate. Environmental needs may not arrive with a buyer holding a checkbook. Conveyance infrastructure may not connect the seller to the buyer.

The existence of a price does not eliminate the design problem.

It moves the design problem into the rules governing the transaction.

That lesson should be familiar by now.

Markets are mechanisms too.

The Colorado River has another complication that a simple seniority story cannot handle: reservoirs.

Lake Powell and Lake Mead are not passive buckets. They are pieces of allocation infrastructure. Federal operating rules determine how much water moves between them under different conditions. Shortage declarations affect deliveries. Conservation agreements can reduce use. Releases can be adjusted in response to changing hydrology.

A reservoir therefore inserts time into the allocation problem.

Water arriving this year can be stored for later.

Water released now can protect one reservoir while weakening another.

A policy designed to avoid an immediate crisis can change the options available next year.

This is not first come, first served.

It is intertemporal portfolio management performed with snowmelt.

The analogy is imperfect but useful. A pension fund does not simply pay claims in the order they arrive without considering future obligations. A cloud provider does not sell every available GPU-hour today if it has promised capacity next month. A reservoir operator likewise cannot treat every unit of stored water as though the only question were who wants it now.

The institution is allocating across people and across time.

This becomes more difficult when the future distribution of supply itself is uncertain.

A wet year can refill part of the system and make political conflict look exaggerated. A sequence of dry years can make yesterday's compromise look naive. Climate trends change the probability distribution beneath rules that were built from older observations.

The temptation is to respond with more precise forecasts.

But no forecast removes the underlying choice.

Even if everyone knew exactly how much water would arrive next year, they would still disagree about who should bear the shortage.

Prediction can shrink one kind of uncertainty.

It cannot decide whose claim matters more.

This is where the post-2026 process becomes unusually revealing.

For years, the basin states and federal government worked toward a successor to the operating rules that were expiring after 2026. The obvious hope was a durable agreement. Instead, long-term consensus did not materialize. After severe drought, weak snowpack, and record-low combined reservoir conditions, the Department of the Interior adopted a ten-year Decision Framework for 2027 through 2036, with operating guidelines expected in shorter two-year intervals unless a longer agreement emerges.

A two-year operating window is not simply evidence of failure.

It is a design choice under uncertainty.

Long rules create certainty but risk becoming badly mismatched to changing conditions.

Short rules adapt more easily but make planning harder.

The mechanism is choosing a time horizon.

That is an allocation decision too.

Imagine you are a city water manager deciding whether to support a new housing development. A ten-year predictable delivery schedule is easier to plan around than a rule revisited every two years. Now imagine you are a federal reservoir manager watching hydrology depart sharply from historical expectations. A rigid ten-year commitment may feel reckless.

Both concerns are legitimate.

The shorter horizon buys flexibility by selling certainty.

This trade appears in nearly every allocation system, although it is often hidden.

A college can publish admissions priorities for years or revise them annually.

A hospital can maintain a fixed transplant score or repeatedly update it as evidence changes.

A cloud provider can sell a long reservation or retain capacity for future unknown demand.

A government can guarantee a benefit indefinitely or create an emergency program with an expiration date.

Rules have maturities.

When the environment changes faster than the rule, rigidity becomes expensive.

When the rule changes faster than people can plan, flexibility becomes expensive.

The Colorado River is a particularly unforgiving place to learn this because every institutional mistake eventually encounters arithmetic.

If the system promises more water than the river reliably provides, someone must absorb the difference.

The loss can appear as reduced deliveries.

It can appear as conservation payments.

It can appear as fallowed land.

It can appear as delayed development.

It can appear as lower reservoir levels and greater future risk.

It can appear as litigation.

It can appear as political conflict displaced into the next negotiation.

The shortage cannot be abolished by renaming it.

This is one reason historical rights survive. However imperfectly distributed, they answer a question every scarcity system must answer: who loses first?

The answer may be morally uncomfortable.

But an answer known in advance has economic value.

The problem is what happens when the answer becomes impossible to live with.

Suppose a junior city grows rapidly while a senior agricultural district continues to hold secure rights. The city may be able to purchase or lease water. It may invest in conservation. It may recycle more. It may seek new infrastructure. It may challenge legal interpretations. It may use political power to pursue a negotiated settlement.

The system has pathways.

They are costly precisely because the old rule was designed not to be casually overturned.

That friction can look absurd from the outside.

Why should an old use outrank a new one merely because it is old?

Sometimes it should not.

But the inverse question is equally difficult.

Why should a later, richer, faster-growing user be able to displace an earlier one merely because it can now make a stronger case?

A mechanism that continually favors whoever has the greatest present economic value would reproduce a different kind of inequality.

The strongest bidder is not the same as the strongest claim.

This is especially important when discussing tribal water rights.

Many tribal rights arise from federal reserved-rights doctrine and legal histories that do not fit the simplistic image of nineteenth-century settlers racing to divert water first. Some claims were quantified late even though the underlying legal priority may be much earlier. Treating every water right as though it were a homogenous private entitlement would erase one of the most consequential features of the basin.

Allocation systems often look cleaner from a distance because the categories have been flattened.

Up close, different kinds of claims are doing different work.

A treaty obligation is not the same as a municipal contract.

A tribal reserved right is not the same as a speculative private purchase.

A reservoir operating rule is not the same as a state-law priority date.

A conservation agreement is not the same as permanent abandonment of a right.

Once these distinctions are restored, the Colorado River stops looking like one line and starts looking like a network of gates.

Can this user legally claim water?

What is the priority of that claim?

What compact or treaty constraints sit above it?

What reservoir conditions govern the delivery?

Has conservation altered the amount available?

Can the water physically be conveyed to the proposed use?

Would a transfer injure another user?

Does federal law impose another obligation?

What happens if the hydrology is worse than forecast?

The line is distributed across institutions.

That was true for GPUs.

It was true for refugee placement.

It is true here too.

The difference is that water carries unusually deep historical memory.

A GPU reservation may reach a few months into the future.

A water priority can reach backward more than a century.

That changes the emotional texture of reform. To one person, seniority is an obsolete accident. To another, it is the rule around which a family, a farm, a town, or a tribe has organized expectations for generations.

Institutions are hardest to change when yesterday's allocation has become today's identity.

This does not mean the old rule must win.

It means the reformer has to understand what the old rule is buying.

Predictability.

Protection against arbitrary redistribution.

A basis for investment.

A legal language for resolving conflict.

A way to turn scarcity into an administrable order.

If a new mechanism cannot provide substitutes for those functions, it may be fairer in theory and more chaotic in practice.

The same warning applies in the other direction.

Predictability can become a defense of path dependence long after the circumstances that justified the original distribution have changed. A rule can remain administratively elegant while becoming politically brittle. An entitlement can be secure and still impose large costs on people outside the original bargain.

Good mechanism design does not confuse stability with justice.

It asks what stability is for.

The post-2026 Colorado River framework is revealing because it does not settle this argument. It postpones parts of it while creating a structure for operating the system in the meantime.

There is humility in that, even if there is also frustration.

An institution confronting deep physical uncertainty may be better served by admitting that a permanent answer is unavailable than by pretending to have one.

Temporary rules can be honest.

They can also become a way to avoid distributional choices.

That is why the shorter operating intervals should not be romanticized. Flexibility is useful only if the institution can repeatedly make hard decisions. If every two-year revision merely defers conflict, provisional governance becomes another kind of queue: everyone waits for the settlement that never arrives.

This is the standard by which the framework should eventually be judged.

Does it help the basin absorb changing hydrology while preserving enough predictability for users to plan?

Does it create credible paths for conservation and transfer?

Does it treat tribal, interstate, international, municipal, agricultural, and environmental claims with enough legal specificity to avoid flattening them into one political contest?

Does it reduce the risk of catastrophic reservoir outcomes?

And when losses are unavoidable, does it make clear who bears them and why?

Those are mechanism questions.

They cannot be answered by saying the market should decide.

Markets already exist in parts of the system.

They cannot be answered by saying seniority should decide.

Seniority already exists in parts of the system.

They cannot be answered by saying experts should optimize the river.

Experts already operate reservoirs and produce models.

The institution is all of these things at once.

That is why water is such a useful case near the end of this book.

The early chapters asked us to notice the rule hiding behind scarcity.

The later chapters have made that rule harder to isolate.

By the time we reach the Colorado River, there is no single answer to the question, Who gets what?

There is law.

There is history.

There is infrastructure.

There is price.

There is politics.

There is hydrology.

There is time.

And there are people who built their lives around promises made under all of them.

The river does not care about any of this.

It simply delivers whatever water the year provides.

The waiting list is ours.
