# First Come, First Served, and Why It Isn't Fair

At an emergency department, the most comforting sign may be that nobody respects the line.

You can arrive first, fill out the forms first, take the first plastic chair beneath the television, and still watch someone who came through the door twenty minutes later disappear behind the treatment doors before you. If the later patient is struggling to breathe, bleeding heavily, confused after a head injury, or showing another sign of immediate danger, almost nobody in the room objects that the hospital has violated first come, first served.

The violation is the point.

Emergency medicine has spent decades formalizing ways to do what an ordinary queue forbids: let later arrivals move ahead. The Emergency Severity Index, one widely used five-level triage system whose development was supported by the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, sorts patients from the most urgent level to the least urgent using acuity and expected resource needs. It is not a perfect moral machine. It is a clinical instrument. But it makes an important fact visible. In some settings, treating people in arrival order would be the unfair choice.

That conclusion is obvious in a hospital and oddly difficult everywhere else.

First come, first served has a special moral status because it hides the allocator. There is no committee choosing favorites, no score whose weights can be challenged, no auctioneer raising the price. There is only time. Whoever arrived first goes first. The rule feels impartial because the clock does not know your name.

But the clock knows more about you than it seems.

It knows whether you heard about the opportunity early enough to arrive. It knows whether you could leave work. It knows whether you have a car. It knows whether someone else can pick up your children. It knows whether your internet connection failed at 10:00:03 when an application portal opened at 10:00. It knows whether you can spend a night on a sidewalk, refresh a browser for three hours, or pay someone else to do either one.

Arrival time looks like a fact. Often it is the residue of a dozen advantages that occurred before anyone started recording timestamps.

This is the central problem with first come, first served. It is admirably clear about the order after people reach the line and almost silent about who could reach it in the first place.

The distinction matters because queues do two jobs at once. They ration a scarce service, and they tell people a story about why the rationing is legitimate.

In 1987, the MIT operations researcher Richard Larson published a paper about what he called the social justice and psychology of queueing. His observation was not that people simply hate waiting. People often tolerate astonishing waits when they believe the procedure is fair. What provokes anger is the sense that someone has violated the order: a person cuts in, a second window opens and serves later arrivals first, or an insider seems to have found a shortcut. The wait has become evidence that the institution has failed to honor the bargain.

The bargain is familiar from childhood. Take a number. Wait your turn. Do not cut.

That bargain is so deeply learned that institutions can borrow legitimacy merely by making an allocation look like a line. The first person to click gets the ticket. The first hundred applicants get the permits. The first families to camp overnight get the school-registration forms. The first customers through the doors get the limited release.

Once a line forms, preserving its order can be genuinely important. Larson was right to call attention to the moral reaction people have when someone cuts. If two people have equal claims and one has been waiting longer under a published rule, suddenly moving the other person ahead can be arbitrary and humiliating. First come, first served is not foolish. It is one of civilization's cheapest technologies for preventing a scramble.

Its mistake is different. The rule takes equal claims as its starting assumption when many allocation problems are defined by unequal circumstances.

Emergency triage is the cleanest counterexample because the stakes make the flaw impossible to ignore. Imagine a hospital that proudly announced it had eliminated discretion. Every patient would be seen strictly by check-in time. No nurse could move anyone ahead. No physician could override the timestamp. The policy would be transparent, easy to audit, and almost impossible to defend.

A sprained ankle would keep its place ahead of a stroke.

Nobody needs an economics degree to see what has gone wrong. The hospital would have confused procedural equality with substantive relevance. The two patients are both people in a waiting room, but the thing that matters about them is not identical. The cost of an additional hour is radically different.

Triage therefore asks a harder question than “Who came first?” It asks, imperfectly and under pressure, “For whom does delay carry the greatest clinical risk?”

That question requires judgment. It creates opportunities for error. It can produce disagreement. It must be implemented by people and systems that may themselves contain bias. Yet the existence of those difficulties does not make arrival time a morally superior substitute. Avoiding judgment is itself a judgment when the consequences of waiting differ.

The same structure appears in less dramatic settings.

Consider a public agency with one hundred appointments and five hundred applicants. A pure first-come system opens an online portal at 9 a.m. on Monday. At first glance, every applicant has an equal chance: the same website, the same opening time, the same rules.

But one applicant is at a desk with fast broadband and a calendar reminder. Another is driving a bus. Another is working a hospital shift and cannot use a phone. Another reads English poorly and depends on a relative to help with the form. Another has a prepaid phone plan and an unstable connection. Another never saw the notice because the agency posted it online and she is not already connected to the groups where such notices circulate.

At 9:07, the hundred slots are gone.

The agency can truthfully say that it never asked about income, occupation, language, disability, or family structure. It can also have built a mechanism that sorts heavily on all of them.

This is why “neutral” procedures deserve suspicion when the ability to comply with them is unevenly distributed. A rule need not contain a discriminatory category to reproduce an unequal starting position. Sometimes it is enough to demand a resource that is itself unequally distributed: spare time.

Waiting is a price paid in hours instead of dollars.

That does not make every queue equivalent to a market. Time cannot be transferred as cleanly as money, and people experience waiting differently. But the analogy reveals something useful. A free ticket allocated by camping overnight is not costless. The buyer has paid with a night outside. A government benefit allocated through repeated office visits is not free to obtain. The applicant has paid in transportation, missed shifts, child care, attention, and uncertainty.

Those costs are easier for some people to bear than others.

A wealthy person may be unwilling to spend six hours in a line, but wealth also creates the option to turn money back into time: hire a line-sitter, pay for child care, take a taxi, book a hotel nearby, use a concierge, buy the premium tier. A low-income worker may value the scarce good more and still be less able to wait for it.

So the apparent egalitarianism of a queue can become a strange inversion. We reject price because ability to pay seems unfair, then allocate by ability to wait without asking who can afford the waiting.

Theme parks make the tradeoff visible because they have tried several versions in public.

For years, Disney parks trained visitors to understand that the visible standby line was only one of the mechanisms operating around an attraction. Earlier FastPass systems allowed guests to reserve access to a shorter queue without a separate per-day fee for the service itself. The details changed over time, but the moral arrangement was recognizable: everyone had some route to priority, though knowledge, planning, hotel status, and reservation timing could still matter.

In October 2021, Walt Disney World launched Disney Genie and the paid Genie+ service. At launch, Genie+ cost $15 per person per day at Walt Disney World and let buyers select access to Lightning Lane entrances at many attractions. Some high-demand attractions were sold separately. The standby line remained, but it was no longer the only ordinary path through the capacity of a ride. Money could purchase a different relationship with time.

Disney did not invent paying to wait less. Airlines, amusement parks, toll roads, museums, airports, and countless other institutions sell priority in one form or another. What made Genie+ interesting was the clarity of the substitution. A park built around lines had attached an explicit price to escaping some of them.

From the company's perspective, this can be defended in several ways. A paid priority product can ration a scarce shortcut when demand for the shortcut exceeds capacity. It can give families who care intensely about a few attractions a way to trade money for time. It can spread demand across rides and times of day. It can generate revenue that an across-the-board ticket increase would collect from people who never use the priority service.

From the standby guest's perspective, the same mechanism can look like purchased cutting.

The guest who has followed the oldest rule of the queue—arrive, join the end, wait—can watch people who arrived later enter through another lane because they bought access to a different rule. Nothing improper has happened. Nobody has violated the published system. The discomfort comes from the fact that the published system now contains more than one definition of whose time matters.

This is an important distinction. Queue fairness is not identical to first come, first served. It depends on what people believe the institution has promised.

At an airport, few travelers are shocked that a first-class passenger boards before someone who reached the gate earlier. The categories are explicit and have been priced into the ticket. In an emergency room, few insist that arrival order should trump medical urgency. In a grocery checkout, by contrast, a cashier waving a friend to the front would feel corrupt precisely because equal treatment by arrival is the understood rule.

The same physical act—serving a later arrival first—can therefore be fair, unfair, or merely expensive depending on the institution around it.

Disney kept changing that institution. In July 2024, Walt Disney World renamed Genie+ as Lightning Lane Multi Pass and restored advance planning features. Disney said guests had told the company they preferred to make more plans before their park day; resort-hotel guests could plan eligible Lightning Lane selections earlier than other guests. The system did not return to a single first-come standby queue. It changed which forms of planning and priority were available and when.

That evolution is more revealing than any claim that one version was objectively fair. A queue system is not just a mathematical arrangement of arrivals and servers. It is a bundle of expectations. When designers change the bundle, people recalculate what counts as a legitimate advantage.

Queueing theory traditionally gives us a language for the operational side of the problem. People or jobs arrive. They wait. Servers process them. If work arrives faster than it can be completed for long enough, the queue grows. If capacity rises, waits can shrink. A discipline determines which waiting job is served next: first in, first out; priority service; shortest job first; some other rule.

This sounds bloodless until you replace “job” with “patient,” “family,” or “student.”

Then the service discipline becomes policy.

First come, first served answers the next-in-line question with a timestamp. Triage answers it with urgency. A veteran preference answers it with status. A lottery refuses to use arrival order at all. A matching system may decide that there is no single next person because eligibility depends on the characteristics of the particular resource that became available.

The operational language is useful because it strips away one illusion: there is no natural queue. There are only choices about arrival, eligibility, priority, service, and capacity.

Even the familiar supermarket line is designed. A store can use one line feeding several cashiers or separate lines for each cashier. It can create an express lane with an item limit. It can allow customers with mobile checkout to bypass registers. It can close a lane. Each choice changes not only average waiting but the kinds of unfairness customers are likely to notice.

A single pooled line has a strong fairness advantage because people who arrive earlier are less likely to be trapped behind one unusually slow transaction while later arrivals happen to choose a faster cashier. Separate lines give customers more agency but also more regret. The person in the “wrong” line experiences delay as a consequence of a choice that was made with bad information.

The same distinction scales upward. When an institution makes people choose among several queues, it is often transferring forecasting risk to the applicants. Which office will move faster? Which school list will clear? Which visa category will advance? Which housing project will have turnover? People with better information can make better guesses.

What looks like decentralization can become an information contest.

First come, first served is strongest where three conditions roughly hold. Claims are similar. Arrival is easy and observable. The cost of waiting does not vary so dramatically that delay itself becomes dangerous or exclusionary.

A coffee shop comes close. Most customers want a similar service. The cost of waiting an extra four minutes is usually modest. The order is visible. A complicated needs assessment would be absurd.

Even there, exceptions creep in. A mobile order may have been placed before you arrived. A delivery driver may be collecting several orders. A customer with a simple black coffee may be served while a large customized order is still being prepared. The store quietly separates the order of payment from the order of production because strict sequence would waste capacity.

The more consequential the scarce good becomes, the harder it is to maintain the fiction that sequence alone contains enough information.

Housing is not coffee. Surgery is not coffee. School placement is not coffee. An organ is certainly not coffee.

Yet the appeal of first come, first served grows precisely when institutions fear accusations of favoritism. A timestamp is a shield. Administrators can point to it and say the rule treated everyone the same.

Sometimes that shield is valuable. Any alternative that ranks need creates a new problem: who gets to define need?

This is the strongest case for first come, first served, and it should not be waved away. Priority systems invite lobbying around categories. Applicants learn how points are awarded. Politicians add preferences. Administrators make judgment calls. Data errors become destiny. People who understand the bureaucracy can shape their applications to the scoring system. A simple queue limits the number of levers available to manipulate.

It is also easy to explain. If an applicant is number 317, she may dislike her position but understand why number 316 is ahead. If the same applicant receives a composite score of 71.4 produced from ten weighted factors, the system may be more responsive to genuine need and much harder to trust.

Transparency has moral value.

So does restraint. Not every inequality should be corrected inside every queue. A coffee shop does not need to investigate customers' incomes before deciding who gets the next cappuccino. A concert venue does not necessarily owe a better seat to the person for whom the music carries the deepest emotional meaning. Institutions have limited information and limited mandates.

The challenge is to identify which differences are relevant to the scarce thing being allocated.

Emergency medicine has an answer because clinical urgency is inseparable from the purpose of emergency care. A patient likely to deteriorate without immediate treatment has a stronger claim to immediate treatment than a stable patient whose condition can safely wait. The queue would fail its own purpose if it ignored that difference.

Other institutions face murkier versions.

Should a public-housing list prioritize a family sleeping in a shelter over a family doubled up with relatives, even if the second family applied years earlier? Should a day-care waiting list give priority to siblings because it reduces family logistics, or does that entrench advantage for families already inside the institution? Should a scarce research grant favor the proposal judged most promising or the scientist who has waited longest since the last award? Should a visa system reward earlier applications when geopolitical events suddenly make later applicants more vulnerable?

There is no universal answer because fairness is not a property of the timestamp. It is a relationship between the rule and the purpose of the institution.

This is why debates about queue design so often become debates about values while pretending to be debates about administration.

A person who says “just go in order” is making at least three claims, whether she knows it or not. She is saying the relevant claimants are similar enough to rank by arrival, that access to the starting line is fair enough to treat arrival as meaningful, and that the harms caused by waiting are similar enough not to justify overtaking.

Sometimes all three claims are reasonable.

Often one of them is doing hidden work.

The first failure is unequal access to arrival. The people who can monitor, travel, camp, click, or hire assistance get earlier timestamps.

The second failure is unequal cost of waiting. A delay that is irritating for one person may threaten a job, a home, a medical outcome, or a legal status for another.

The third failure is unequal fit. The scarce thing may be more useful, more compatible, or more urgent for one claimant than another.

Once any of those differences becomes important, first come, first served stops being neutral and starts becoming a choice to ignore information.

That still may be the right choice. Information can be invasive, unreliable, or too costly to collect. Need scores can be gamed. Compatibility rules can be opaque. Discretion can become corruption. A modest queue may be preferable to an elaborate bureaucracy that claims to measure every morally relevant fact and measures them badly.

But then the defense of the queue should be candid. We are not using first come, first served because time has revealed who deserves the good. We are using it because the alternatives impose costs or risks we judge worse.

That is a more mature argument, and a less comforting one.

It also explains why queues so often sprout markets beside them.

If waiting is costly and positions are valuable, people search for ways to convert one resource into another. They pay line-sitters. They buy premium access. They use brokers. They resell reservations. They trade information about when portals open. They automate clicks. They cultivate insiders. They create a shadow price for a place that the official system insisted was free.

The queue has not abolished the market. It has given the market something new to sell: time.

At the emergency department, we accept overtaking because the alternative would offend the purpose of the institution. At a theme park, we debate overtaking because the institution itself sells it. In an online application system, we may not even notice overtaking because it happened before the line became visible, in the unequal distribution of information, flexibility, and bandwidth.

These are not three different subjects. They are three ways a timestamp can mislead us.

First come, first served remains one of the best rules we have for ordinary turn-taking. It is simple, auditable, and resistant to many forms of discretionary abuse. Those virtues are real. They are also conditional.

A fair line requires more than an honest clock.

It requires a reason to believe that arriving first is the fact that should matter.

When that reason weakens, something else usually appears beside the queue: a priority rule, an exception, a paid shortcut, or an unofficial market. And when the official rule refuses to acknowledge those pressures for long enough, the unofficial market can become more important than the line itself.

That is where we go next.
