# How to Build a Better Waiting List

A waiting list looks simple from the outside.

There are more people than places.

Write down the names.

Pick an order.

Move from the top.

The difficulty begins as soon as the names become people.

One person arrived first because she had the freedom to arrive early. Another arrived late because he was caring for a child. One applicant has a medical need that makes delay unusually dangerous. Another has waited longer. A third is easier to serve. A fourth is harder to match but would be harmed more by being skipped. Some participants know the rules well enough to shape their behavior around them. Others never learn what game they are playing.

Then the institution changes the rules.

Now everybody is angry.

That sequence has appeared in almost every system in this book. The object changes—a hospital slot, a kidney, a school seat, a vaccine appointment, a housing voucher, a GPU reservation—but the underlying conflict remains remarkably stable.

Scarcity does not eliminate choice.

It concentrates choice in the rule.

That is why the first principle of building a better waiting list is also the least glamorous one: say what the list is for.

Not what the institution is for in general.

What this allocation rule is trying to accomplish.

A kidney allocation system may care about medical urgency, biological compatibility, expected benefit, pediatric priority, waiting time, geography, and fairness across populations. A school-choice mechanism may care about family preferences, school priorities, strategy resistance, diversity, travel distance, and capacity. A cloud provider may care about payment, reservation timing, hardware topology, regional supply, customer commitments, and operational reliability. A refugee placement system may care about employment prospects, family ties, language, local capacity, and the preferences of the people being placed.

Those are not interchangeable goals.

They can conflict.

A rule cannot maximize every good thing at once.

This is where bad design often begins. Institutions say they want fairness, efficiency, access, simplicity, transparency, speed, resilience, inclusion, and optimal outcomes. All of them. Then the real tradeoff appears and nobody has said which value gives way.

Suppose two patients need the same scarce treatment slot. One is sicker now. The other is expected to benefit more. Which principle governs?

There is no arithmetic trick that dissolves the ethical question.

A formula can encode the answer.

It cannot supply the answer.

The best-designed mechanisms make this visible rather than hiding it behind technical language.

This is one of the lessons of market design.

The mechanism is downstream of the objective.

The mathematics can tell us whether a rule has certain properties. It can tell us whether participants have an incentive to misstate their preferences. It can tell us whether a matching is stable under a particular definition. It can show how a lottery changes expected outcomes. It can reveal where a queue creates strategic behavior.

But the mathematics does not decide whose welfare matters most.

That remains a policy choice.

The second principle is to separate eligibility from priority.

These are frequently collapsed.

Eligibility asks whether a person belongs in the process at all.

Priority asks where that person stands relative to others who also qualify.

The distinction sounds bureaucratic until it causes a crisis.

A housing program may require income below a threshold before an applicant enters the pool. A transplant program may require medical and logistical conditions before a patient is active on a waitlist. A college may establish minimum qualifications before holistic selection. A vaccine rollout may define groups by age, occupation, medical risk, or residence before appointments are offered.

When eligibility and priority are blurred, people can mistake a place on the list for a promise.

It is not always a promise.

Being selected into a housing waitlist does not mean receiving a voucher tomorrow. Being eligible for a scarce treatment does not mean being first in line. Being allowed to request a GPU reservation does not mean the hardware exists in the needed region at the needed time.

A good system tells participants where the boundary is.

You qualify.

You are in the pool.

Here is how priority works.

Here is what can change your position.

Here is what cannot.

Here is what happens next.

This may sound like communication rather than mechanism design.

It is both.

A rule participants do not understand can produce strategic behavior that the designer never intended. People apply earlier than necessary. They flood multiple channels. They hire intermediaries. They misreport preferences. They hold backup slots they do not want because they cannot tell whether a better offer will arrive. They call repeatedly because the system provides no reliable status.

Opacity creates load.

Sometimes the load is then used as evidence that the public is irrational.

The system helped create the behavior.

The third principle is to choose the mechanism that fits the scarcity.

Not every shortage is a matching problem.

Not every matching problem needs deferred acceptance.

Not every allocation needs a lottery.

Not every queue needs to be abolished.

First-come-first-served has virtues. It is easy to explain. It can be cheap to administer. It often requires little information beyond arrival order. It can reduce discretion. It may be perfectly adequate when stakes are low, arrivals are roughly comparable, and the cost of waiting is tolerable.

A lottery has different virtues. It can neutralize advantages in speed when people are similarly situated. It can be particularly useful when demand vastly exceeds supply and there is no defensible reason to rank one eligible person above another.

Priority systems are appropriate when differences matter. Emergency medicine does not ask who entered the building first and then treat a heart attack behind a sprained ankle. Triage exists because urgency is morally and medically relevant.

Matching systems become useful when both sides have characteristics or preferences that affect whether an assignment works. A kidney is not simply a numbered unit. A school seat is not identical across schools. A medical residency applicant and a hospital program both have preferences. A refugee placement may work differently depending on language, employment history, family ties, and local services.

Exchange systems become useful when one person's incompatibility can be solved through another person's compatibility.

The mechanism should follow the structure of the problem.

This sounds obvious.

Institutions violate it constantly.

They keep a chronological queue because it already exists. They add priority points until nobody can explain them. They introduce a lottery because a lottery sounds fair, then attach so many preferences and filters that randomness is barely doing any work. They buy software before deciding what the software is supposed to optimize.

A mechanism is not improved merely because it is more elaborate.

Complexity earns its place only when it solves a real failure mode.

The fourth principle is to test for manipulation before blaming people for manipulating.

This is one of the most durable contributions of matching theory.

A mechanism that rewards strategic misrepresentation should expect strategic misrepresentation.

The old Boston school-choice mechanism famously created this problem. Families could be punished for ranking a highly desired school first if they were unlikely to receive it, because a safer school placed second might fill before their application reached it. Sophisticated families learned to game the ranking. Less-informed families were more likely to state their true preferences and lose.

The moral failure did not belong to the parents.

The rule taught them to lie.

A better mechanism tries, where possible, to make truthful participation safe.

This does not mean every system can be perfectly strategy-proof.

Real institutions have priorities, incomplete information, changing capacity, couples, ties, special cases, appeals, and political constraints. People can still try to game eligibility. They can acquire credentials, move addresses, time applications, split households, or exploit loopholes around the mechanism.

But the principle remains useful.

Ask what behavior the rule rewards.

Then assume people will notice.

If camping overnight improves your chance, people will camp.

If applying to ten colleges is safer than applying to five, applications will multiply.

If holding a backup reservation is rational, people will hold backups.

If exaggerating urgency moves you forward, pressure will grow to exaggerate urgency.

If a system's stated purpose depends on participants behaving against their own incentives, the system is fragile.

The fifth principle is to design for capacity as it actually exists.

This is where elegant models collide with ordinary administration.

A city may have a formal housing allocation process and too few units.

A transplant network may improve exchange and still face a profound shortage of organs.

A school district may improve assignment without creating another excellent school.

A cloud provider may create sophisticated reservation products without making an unavailable accelerator appear in the correct zone.

A refugee-placement model may identify a promising city that lacks enough caseworkers or affordable apartments.

Allocation does not manufacture supply.

That sentence should sit on the wall of every institution designing a queue.

A better mechanism can reduce waste, improve fit, make rules harder to manipulate, and distribute burden more defensibly.

It cannot conjure the scarce thing.

This matters politically because queue reform can become a substitute for supply reform.

The institution redesigns the list.

The list becomes more legible.

The shortage remains.

People continue waiting.

Officials point to the improved process.

Participants experience the same absence.

A mechanism can be fairer and still deliver a terrible life.

The distinction matters most in domains such as housing and transplantation, where the moral drama of allocation can distract from the scale of missing supply.

The sixth principle is to treat time as more than a timestamp.

Waiting time is attractive because it appears objective.

It is measurable.

It rewards patience.

It discourages line jumping.

It recognizes that delay itself imposes a cost.

But waiting time can also preserve old inequalities.

Who learned about the program early?

Who was able to complete the paperwork?

Who had stable internet access?

Who could remain reachable for years?

Who could afford to stay in a high-cost city while waiting?

Who was excluded by a previous version of the rules?

Chronology records history.

History may contain injustice.

That does not mean waiting time should be ignored. It means it should not be mistaken for moral neutrality.

A well-designed list asks whether delay is itself a relevant claim and, if so, how much weight it should carry relative to urgency, need, compatibility, or other priorities.

Sometimes the answer will be a lot.

Sometimes not.

The seventh principle is to preserve a way to say: this case does not fit.

Pure discretion is dangerous.

So is a rule that cannot recognize an exception.

The tension is unavoidable.

A rigid mechanism can be predictable, consistent, and auditable. It can also produce absurd outcomes when facts fall outside what the rule anticipated.

A discretionary mechanism can respond to context. It can also invite favoritism, inconsistency, and hidden bias.

The answer is not to choose one forever.

It is to design constrained discretion.

Define when an exception can be requested.

Require reasons.

Record who approved it.

Track patterns.

Review whether exceptions cluster around particular offices, populations, or decision makers.

Allow appeal without turning the appeal process into a second hidden allocation system accessible only to the persistent and well-connected.

This is one place where software can genuinely improve accountability.

A paper process may leave almost no trace.

A digital process can record the original recommendation, the override, the reason, the person responsible, and the later outcome.

The point is not automation.

The point is memory.

Institutions need to remember how they used their power.

The eighth principle is to make the disappointed person part of the design.

Every scarce allocation mechanism produces disappointment.

Often it produces grief.

Someone does not get the kidney.

Someone does not get the school.

Someone does not get the apartment.

Someone does not get the appointment.

Someone does not get the job.

The mechanism is usually designed by the institution that has the scarce resource, not by the people who will be denied it.

That asymmetry shapes what gets optimized.

The institution cares about throughput, fraud, staffing, compliance, and administrative cost.

Those concerns are legitimate.

The applicant cares about something else.

What happened to me?

Was I actually considered?

Did I make a mistake?

Can I correct it?

What happens now?

How long will this take?

Do I need to keep waiting?

Can I appeal?

What information would change the decision?

These questions are not decorative customer service.

They affect behavior, trust, and sometimes survival.

A system that cannot answer them creates secondary scarcity: scarcity of information.

People then compete for attention from staff, elected officials, lawyers, journalists, or anyone who might explain the unexplained result.

Good design reduces that desperation where it can.

The ninth principle is to audit outcomes, not just code.

The previous chapter argued that an algorithm audit can mean many different things. The same is true for any allocation mechanism.

A system can be internally consistent and still create outcomes nobody intended.

The correct question is not merely: did the algorithm follow the rule?

It is also: what did the rule do?

Who received the scarce resource?

Who waited longest?

Who dropped out?

Which groups were repeatedly screened out at eligibility?

How many offers were rejected?

Where did capacity go unused because the match was poor?

How often were decisions overridden?

What complaints recur?

Did the intervention improve the outcome it was meant to improve?

A mechanism deserves the same skepticism after deployment that it received before deployment.

This is harder than it sounds because institutions become attached to systems they have built.

The original design acquires defenders.

Changing the rule creates winners and losers.

Every revision reopens political fights that the previous rule temporarily settled.

So systems drift.

A priority category remains long after its original rationale has weakened. A threshold survives because nobody wants to own the consequences of moving it. A vendor contract becomes a policy. A temporary emergency rule becomes infrastructure.

The queue starts to look natural.

It is not.

It is accumulated decisions.

The tenth principle is therefore to give the mechanism a date with doubt.

Review it.

Not because failure has already been proved.

Because conditions change.

Demand changes.

Supply changes.

Technology changes.

Law changes.

Participant behavior changes.

The composition of the population changes.

The institution learns things it did not know when the rule was created.

A mechanism that was sensible in one environment may become indefensible in another.

The Colorado River makes this visible at geological scale. Rules built around one historical pattern of water availability confront a different hydrological reality. Cloud capacity products evolve as hardware generations and demand change. College admissions rules respond to court decisions and state laws. Automated-decision regulation is still being built while the systems being regulated keep changing.

A queue can age.

Design should include a way to notice.

There is a final principle, and it is the most uncomfortable.

Do not ask the waiting list to solve a political conflict that nobody wants to name.

Mechanism design is powerful because it forces precision.

Who is eligible?

What counts as priority?

What information matters?

Who proposes?

Who can reject?

What happens in a tie?

What makes an assignment stable?

How can a participant appeal?

What evidence is recorded?

Those questions expose choices that vague language hides.

But mechanism design can also become a refuge from politics.

A committee can spend months perfecting a ranking rule for a resource that is scandalously undersupplied.

A city can optimize access to affordable apartments while preserving the conditions that keep affordable apartments scarce.

A health system can refine organ allocation while avoiding harder questions about donation, prevention, procurement, and access to transplant centers.

A college can make admissions more defensible while the broader distribution of educational opportunity remains profoundly unequal.

A beautiful queue is still a queue.

Sometimes the best redesign is more supply.

Sometimes it is a market where a market is ethically acceptable.

Sometimes it is a lottery.

Sometimes it is a match.

Sometimes it is priority by need.

Sometimes it is a boring chronological line because the stakes do not justify anything more complicated.

The discipline is not to prefer one mechanism.

It is to refuse accidental mechanisms.

Someone should be able to explain why this rule exists.

Someone should be able to show what failure it is meant to prevent.

Someone should be able to identify who benefits and who bears the cost.

Someone should be able to test whether the rule is doing what it claims.

Someone should have the authority to change it when the evidence says it is failing.

And the people subject to the rule should not need an economist, a lawyer, or a favor from an insider merely to understand what happened to them.

That is a modest standard.

Many important systems still do not meet it.

The reason is not that we lack mechanisms.

We have decades of theory, experiments, redesigns, failures, and working institutions to learn from.

Gale and Shapley showed that the structure of preferences matters.

The medical match showed that a mechanism can stop a market from unraveling.

Kidney exchange showed that incompatibility can sometimes be turned into possibility without introducing a cash price.

School-choice reform showed that bad incentives can punish honest participants and that changing the rule can remove some of that strategic burden.

Refugee-placement research showed the promise and the limits of using prediction to improve fit.

The pandemic showed what happens when priorities must be built under pressure with incomplete evidence and enormous moral stakes.

Housing lotteries showed that randomness can be fairer than speed while doing nothing to eliminate scarcity.

GPU reservations showed that even markets contain queues once the product is specific enough and supply cannot instantly respond.

Algorithm audits showed that a rule needs a rule for inspecting the rule.

The lesson is not that allocation can be perfected.

It cannot.

Scarcity means somebody will hear no.

A serious mechanism does not make that fact disappear.

It makes the no less arbitrary.

That is what a better waiting list can do.

Not justice in a box.

Not optimization without politics.

Not a machine that discovers the correct order of human worth.

A better rule for a difficult choice, built in public enough to be challenged, specific enough to be tested, and humble enough to be changed.

That is considerably more than most waiting lists were ever designed to be.
