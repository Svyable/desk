# The Line for College

A college admissions office does not have a line.

It has something worse.

A line at least tells you where you stand.

If you are number 412, you know that 411 people are ahead of you. You may hate the wait. You may think the rule is crude. But the mechanism is legible.

Selective college admissions is not legible in that way.

There is no universal number attached to an applicant. No public queue moves forward one file at a time. No single score settles the matter. A student may have stronger grades than another student and still lose the place. Another may have weaker test scores and still be admitted because the institution is trying to build a class rather than rank a spreadsheet.

This distinction sits underneath nearly every angry argument about admissions.

Applicants often imagine a contest.

Colleges often describe a composition problem.

Those are not the same thing.

In a contest, the best performer should win.

In a composition problem, the institution is deciding what mixture of people, talents, backgrounds, programs, geographies, needs, and constraints it wants to fit into a fixed number of seats.

The language of merit becomes unstable the moment those two models collide.

Suppose a university has two thousand places and twenty thousand students who could plausibly succeed there.

The institution could rank them by grades.

It could rank them by standardized tests.

It could build a formula combining both.

It could reserve places for athletes, musicians, engineers, rural students, low-income students, first-generation students, children of alumni, donors' relatives, or applicants from underrepresented regions.

It could use essays and recommendations to distinguish among files with similar numbers.

It could use an actual lottery.

It could do some mixture of all of these.

The important point is that scarcity forces a rule whether or not the institution admits that it has chosen one.

A selective college is not discovering the natural order of applicants.

It is constructing an order for a particular purpose.

That is why college admissions belongs in a book about waiting lists.

The line is there.

It is simply hidden inside the criteria.

The first source of confusion is the admissions rate itself.

An admit rate looks like a clean statistic. If a school admits five percent of applicants, the number seems to tell us something profound about scarcity and quality.

It does tell us something about scarcity.

It tells us less about quality than people often assume.

In the 2025–2026 application cycle, Common App reported that by February 1, 2026, 1,401,214 first-year applicants had submitted 9,188,630 applications through its platform. The number of applicants was up two percent from the comparable point a year earlier.

Applications were up five percent.

The difference matters.

Students were not merely entering college admissions in greater numbers. They were also sending more applications per person.

The average applicant in that Common App snapshot had submitted 6.56 applications, compared with 6.37 a year earlier.

The prior completed cycle had already crossed ten million applications on the platform.

This changes the denominator.

If one student applies to ten colleges instead of five, five additional applications appear in institutional statistics even though no additional student has entered the system.

A university with a fixed class size can therefore become statistically more selective without becoming more selective in any deeper sense.

Its admit rate falls because the same seats are being divided by a larger application count.

That does not mean the demand is fake.

Those applications are real.

Staff must read them. Applicants pay fees or seek waivers. Families invest time. Institutions model yield. Counselors build lists. Students wait for decisions from several places at once.

Application inflation creates real congestion even when the number of distinct people grows more slowly.

The mechanism begins to resemble an arms race.

If applicants believe everyone else is applying more broadly, applying narrowly feels risky.

If applicants apply more broadly, colleges face less certainty about who will enroll.

If colleges face less certainty about yield, they may rely more heavily on waitlists, early-decision programs, predictive enrollment models, or other devices to manage the class.

Those devices then change applicant strategy again.

The result is a matching market in which both sides react to the other side's uncertainty.

This is not the clean college-admissions problem David Gale and Lloyd Shapley wrote down in 1962.

But it is recognizably related.

Applicants have preferences among colleges.

Colleges have preferences among applicants.

There are capacity constraints.

There is strategic behavior.

And there is no price high enough to simply clear the most selective part of the market because, even at private institutions charging enormous tuition, the sticker price does not determine who gets admitted.

Money matters enormously in college admissions.

It does not operate as a normal auction.

That distinction is uncomfortable because higher education likes to describe admissions as a moral and educational process while tuition reminds everyone that it is also an economic one.

A wealthy applicant may have attended better schools, hired better tutors, received sophisticated advising, accumulated expensive extracurricular experiences, visited campuses, taken tests multiple times, and applied without worrying much about fees.

None of this requires an admissions officer to ask for a bank balance.

Privilege can enter the file before the file is read.

This is one reason a purely numerical admissions system is not obviously neutral.

Grades are information.

Tests are information.

Course rigor is information.

But information is produced under unequal conditions.

A score can be standardized without making the path to that score equal.

That is the strongest argument for holistic review.

A reader can ask whether a student exhausted the opportunities available in a small rural school rather than comparing the transcript mechanically with someone whose suburban high school offers twenty Advanced Placement courses.

A committee can notice that an applicant worked twenty hours a week instead of founding a club.

It can interpret achievement in context.

It can recognize that two identical numbers may represent different levels of effort, access, adversity, or preparation.

This is not inherently corruption.

It is an attempt to recover information that a single metric loses.

But the moment discretion enters, auditability becomes harder.

A formula can be criticized because we can see the weights.

A holistic process can be defended because there are no fixed weights.

Those two facts are connected.

The flexibility that allows context also makes the mechanism harder for outsiders to reconstruct.

An applicant rejected by a point system can at least disagree with the points.

An applicant rejected by holistic review often receives something closer to a weather report.

There were many qualified candidates.

The class was unusually strong.

The committee appreciated the application.

The decision was difficult.

All of those statements can be true.

None tells the student where she stood.

That opacity has a political cost because people fill missing explanations with their own theories.

The rich think standards are being lowered for somebody else.

Poorer applicants suspect wealth is buying invisible preference.

Asian American applicants may suspect racial balancing.

Black and Latino applicants may suspect formal neutrality is preserving structural advantage.

Athletes, legacies, rural students, first-generation students, international applicants, and applicants from particular regions may all imagine that some other category received the seat that should have been theirs.

Once the mechanism is opaque, every rejection becomes an invitation to infer the hidden rule.

The Supreme Court entered this argument in 2023.

In Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard and the companion case involving the University of North Carolina, the Court sharply constrained race-conscious admissions under the Equal Protection Clause and Title VI framework.

The decision did not remove scarcity.

It changed the feasible mechanism.

That is the useful way to understand it here.

Before the ruling, the challenged institutions could consider race in ways the Court held unconstitutional.

After the ruling, they could not continue the same categorical use.

But the Court did not say that an applicant's life experience involving race becomes invisible.

Its majority opinion explicitly distinguished between treating race itself as an admissions factor and considering how an applicant's experiences affected character, achievement, leadership, hardship, or other individual qualities.

That distinction created a new design problem.

How does an institution consider context without recreating a prohibited preference through another channel?

How does it read an essay about discrimination without turning the essay into a coded racial category?

How does it pursue socioeconomic diversity, geographic diversity, first-generation access, or other institutional goals without assuming those categories are interchangeable with race?

There is no algorithmic shortcut through the legal boundary.

The college still has to decide what information matters.

The applicant still has incentives to present that information strategically.

And the public still has reasons to ask whether the stated rule is the actual rule.

This is where legal reform and mechanism design meet.

Law does not eliminate the allocation system.

It changes which variables the system may use.

California's move against legacy and donor preferences makes the same point from another direction.

In 2024, the state enacted a law barring covered private nonprofit colleges from giving admissions preferences based on alumni or donor relationships.

That policy does not make admissions egalitarian.

It removes one explicit inherited advantage.

The distinction matters.

A legacy preference is unusually easy to see as a policy choice because nobody can seriously argue that having a parent who attended the institution measures academic ability.

Its defenders usually make different arguments.

They may say alumni ties build community.

They may say family continuity strengthens institutional loyalty.

They may say donor engagement finances aid, faculty, laboratories, libraries, or other benefits that help the broader university.

Those arguments can be debated.

What they cannot do is turn the preference into a natural fact.

It is a choice about what the institution values.

Once that is admitted, other preferences become easier to see clearly.

A recruited-athlete preference is also a choice.

So is a preference for geographic diversity.

So is an institutional desire to fill an orchestra section, sustain a language program, enroll enough engineers, expand first-generation representation, or avoid admitting a class composed almost entirely of students from a small cluster of elite high schools.

Selective admissions is full of such objectives.

The word merit often conceals them because merit sounds singular.

Institutions are usually optimizing several things at once.

That can be legitimate.

It can also be abused.

The two facts coexist.

A college assembling a class has real educational interests that a simple rank order misses.

A campus with no one interested in certain programs would be a strange university.

A class drawn entirely from one region would narrow the institution's reach.

A school that ignored financial need, first-generation status, or unequal opportunity might reproduce advantage while claiming neutrality.

At the same time, every additional objective creates another place where discretion can hide favoritism.

This is the central tradeoff of holistic systems.

Rich information produces richer judgment.

Richer judgment produces a larger surface for discretion.

The obvious response is transparency.

Publish the weights.

Explain the priorities.

Show applicants exactly how the system works.

This sounds appealing until strategic behavior appears.

If a college announced that a particular extracurricular category added exactly three points, an advising industry would immediately optimize for those three points.

If one essay topic reliably produced an advantage, applicants would converge on it.

If a rural preference had a precise threshold, families near the boundary would learn how to present themselves against it.

If every weight were fixed and public, affluent applicants would not become less strategic.

They would become more efficient.

Transparency can reduce arbitrariness.

It can also make gaming easier.

This is not an argument for secrecy.

It is an argument that auditability and strategy interact.

The medical match solved one kind of strategic problem by designing a system in which applicants could report preferences more honestly.

College admissions has not solved the equivalent problem.

Applicants still have reasons to manage signals.

Early decision is one of the clearest examples.

A binding early-decision program asks a student to commit to attend if admitted, subject to the program's financial-aid terms and exceptions.

For the college, that commitment improves yield certainty.

For the student, it can turn preference into a stronger signal.

The institution learns that this applicant is not merely interested but willing to give up later comparison among offers.

That can be valuable in a market flooded with applications.

It can also advantage students who are comfortable committing before comparing aid packages or who have access to counseling sophisticated enough to use the system strategically.

The admissions process therefore contains time preferences as well as applicant preferences.

Who applies early?

Who can afford to commit early?

Who waits for regular decision?

Who gets placed on a waitlist?

Who remains willing to enroll months later?

The line changes shape over the year.

A waitlist makes this visible.

At first, a college admits fewer students than it expects to enroll because it knows some will go elsewhere.

Then the institution watches deposits.

If fewer accepted students enroll than expected, the waitlist becomes a reserve pool.

Applicants are not necessarily called in rank order.

A college may need a particular major, geography, financial profile, or other attribute to balance the remaining class.

The waitlist therefore dramatizes what was already true during regular admissions.

The institution is filling a composition, not advancing a universal queue.

This can feel maddening to an applicant because the word waitlist borrows the moral intuition of a line without providing the line's order.

You are waiting.

You do not know what number you are.

You may not even be comparable to the people called before you because the institution may be looking for something different by then.

This is why college admissions produces so much emotional heat.

The process uses the language of individual evaluation while operating under institutional constraints the individual cannot see.

A student may have done everything adults told her to do.

Get the grades.

Take hard classes.

Study for the test.

Lead something.

Write carefully.

Ask for recommendations.

Apply broadly.

None of that creates a claim enforceable against a scarce seat.

Qualification is not entitlement.

That is a hard truth because education is often narrated as a reward for effort.

Selective admissions reveals the limit of that story.

When thousands of applicants have worked hard, effort cannot distinguish them all.

The mechanism must reach for additional criteria.

Some criteria will be measurable.

Some will be contextual.

Some will be explicitly institutional.

Some will be legally prohibited.

Some will survive because they are politically powerful.

And some will change after public pressure makes them difficult to defend.

This is not unique to colleges.

The housing lottery from the previous chapter did the same thing more openly.

There were more eligible households than available assistance.

A lottery determined entry into one stage.

Preferences and eligibility rules shaped later stages.

Administrative capacity added another bottleneck.

College admissions performs a similarly layered allocation but usually avoids the word lottery.

That avoidance tells us something.

Randomness feels inconsistent with the story institutions want to tell about merit.

Imagine a university announcing the following policy:

Every applicant above a rigorous academic threshold enters a lottery for the remaining seats.

The policy would have virtues.

It would admit that many applicants are effectively indistinguishable in any morally confident sense.

It would reduce incentives to manufacture microscopic differences among already strong students.

It might limit favoritism if the eligible pool were defined carefully.

It would also be politically explosive.

Parents would ask why years of effort ended in a random draw.

Applicants would ask whether extraordinary achievement deserved more than one ticket in the drum.

Departments would ask how they were supposed to fill specialized programs.

Coaches would ask how to field teams.

Institutions would ask how to pursue educational missions that depend on assembling a class rather than merely filling chairs.

The lottery would remove one kind of discretion by discarding information the institution believes matters.

That is the difficulty.

Randomness is fair only after we decide what differences we are willing to ignore.

A housing authority may decide that among similarly eligible households, it has no defensible basis for ranking one above another at a particular stage.

A selective university usually claims that it does have relevant differences to consider.

The dispute is over which differences count.

Grades?

Tests?

Family income?

School context?

Geography?

Athletic ability?

Race-related life experience?

Legacy?

Intended field?

Essays?

Recommendations?

The argument about admissions is therefore not mainly about whether to have a mechanism.

It is about the feature set.

That phrase sounds technical.

The consequences are not.

Every variable represents a judgment about what kind of claim a person can make on a scarce opportunity.

A test score says demonstrated academic performance should matter.

A first-generation preference says family educational history should matter.

A geographic preference says place should matter.

A legacy preference says institutional lineage should matter.

A financial-aid policy says ability to pay should matter less, more, or differently depending on the institution.

A recruited-athlete slot says contribution to a team should matter.

None is neutral merely because it can be measured.

The same is true of removing a variable.

A ban is also a design choice.

The 2023 Supreme Court decision removed one method institutions had used to pursue racial diversity.

California's legacy law removed another category in covered institutions.

Each change leaves the remaining scarcity intact and pushes more weight onto other variables.

Mechanisms do not become simpler merely because one lever is taken away.

Sometimes the pressure moves elsewhere.

This is why admissions policy should be evaluated end to end rather than by slogans.

Test optional.

Holistic.

Race neutral.

Need blind.

Legacy free.

Each label tells us something.

None tells us how the full allocation works.

The interesting questions are operational.

Who enters the pool?

What information is collected?

Which criteria are permitted?

Which are weighted explicitly?

Which are handled informally?

Who reviews the file?

How much discretion does that person have?

How are disagreements resolved?

How are institutional priorities communicated?

What happens when one office wants a student another office would reject?

What data are retained after the cycle?

Who checks whether stated policy and actual outcomes align?

That last question is where the chapter has been heading.

For most of this book, the allocation mechanism has been visible enough to interrogate directly.

A queue has an order.

A lottery has a randomizer.

A stable match has an algorithm.

A vaccine priority system has tiers.

A GPU reservation has a contract and capacity boundary.

Water rights have legal priorities and operating rules.

College admissions is harder because the mechanism is partly code, partly policy, partly professional judgment, partly institutional custom, and partly law.

No single diagram captures it.

That makes oversight more important, not less.

A system this consequential should be able to answer a basic question:

Did we do what we said we would do?

Not, did everyone like the outcome.

No scarce allocation mechanism can promise that.

Not, did every rejected applicant understand exactly why.

Some decisions genuinely depend on comparisons that resist one-line explanations.

The question is narrower and more demanding.

Did the institution use the criteria it claimed to use?

Did prohibited criteria disappear in practice as well as on paper?

Did supposedly neutral tools produce patterns worth investigating?

Did readers apply standards consistently enough that the process means what the policy says it means?

Did hidden preferences survive after public preferences were abolished?

Did a change intended to widen access actually widen access?

Did application growth merely create more administrative noise?

Did the system learn from its own outcomes?

Admissions offices often ask applicants to trust the process.

Trust is not a mechanism.

It is the result of a mechanism surviving scrutiny.

That is the handoff to the next chapter.

Once an institution has decided who gets what, someone has to inspect the decision rule itself.

A queue can be audited.

A lottery can be audited.

An algorithm can be audited.

A committee can be audited too, though the tools are different and the questions are harder.

The line for college is not a line because selective admissions has chosen not to be one.

It has chosen a richer, more flexible, more contestable mechanism.

That choice may be defensible.

But flexibility is not a substitute for accountability.

When twenty thousand qualified people are competing for two thousand seats, rejection is inevitable.

Arbitrariness is not.
