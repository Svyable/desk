# A Taxonomy of Lines

The word *line* makes scarcity look simpler than it is.

A line has an order. Someone is first, someone is second, someone is last. You can usually see why. The person at the front arrived before you. If the cashier is moving slowly, you may resent the delay, but you do not need a policy manual to understand your place.

Now consider three other lines.

A patient waiting for a kidney may have been registered for years and still lose priority to someone who joined later because the newer patient is unusually difficult to match, is a child, is a better biological fit for a particular organ, or satisfies another rule embedded in the allocation system. A family waiting for a housing voucher may have submitted an application years ago, yet the local housing authority may use preferences, a lottery, or a closed waiting list that changes whether the application is even considered. A military retiree hoping to take an empty seat on a Space-Available flight can arrive before dawn, sign in correctly, and still watch an active-duty traveler on emergency leave move ahead because the Department of Defense puts the two passengers in different priority categories.

All three people are waiting. None is standing in a simple line.

This is the first distinction worth keeping through the rest of the book: a waiting list is not a queue in the ordinary sense. It is an allocation rule that happens to involve waiting.

Sometimes waiting is the main rule. Sometimes it is only a tiebreaker. Sometimes it barely matters at all.

That difference is easy to miss because institutions use the same language for very different mechanisms. “You are on the waiting list” can mean your turn is determined almost entirely by elapsed time. It can mean you have entered a pool from which a lottery will later draw. It can mean you are eligible but will be sorted by need. It can mean you will be matched against the characteristics of a scarce resource that does not yet exist. It can mean your priority will rise and fall as your circumstances change. It can even mean that the list is currently closed and your real status is waiting to be allowed to wait.

The mechanisms fall into a small number of recurring families.

The simplest is first come, first served. Arrival establishes priority. If one unit becomes available, the first eligible person gets it. This rule is attractive because it is legible. It does not require an administrator to decide who deserves the good more. It does not ask applicants to reveal much about themselves. It can be audited with a timestamp.

That simplicity is why first come, first served remains deeply appealing even when it is a poor fit for the underlying problem. We associate waiting with patience and patience with fairness. The person who has “put in the time” seems to have earned a claim.

But arrival is rarely neutral. To arrive early, a person needs information early. To remain in line, a person may need time off work, transportation, child care, internet access, a stable mailing address, or the ability to monitor a portal that opens unpredictably. A rule that never asks for income can still favor people with resources because resources determine who can get to the line first and stay there.

The next family is the lottery. Instead of rewarding arrival, the institution deliberately destroys order among eligible applicants. Everyone who clears the threshold gets a chance; a random draw selects among them.

Lotteries are often treated as a confession that the institution has no better principle. That is too dismissive. Randomness can be a positive design choice when the administrator believes the eligible claimants are too similar to rank fairly, or when ranking them would invite manipulation. A lottery can also protect against the political temptation to hand scarce benefits to favored applicants under vague discretionary criteria.

Yet a lottery solves only one problem: who among the eligible gets selected. It says nothing about how eligibility was defined, who heard about the application window, whether the window stayed open long enough, or whether some applicants received preferences before the draw. A perfectly random lottery can sit on top of a profoundly unequal pool.

A third family is priority by need.

Emergency departments are the familiar case. Nobody expects an emergency room to treat patients strictly in the order they entered the building. A person with crushing chest pain may be seen before someone who has been waiting longer with a minor injury. The moral intuition reverses: ignoring arrival order feels fairer because the stakes differ.

Once need enters, however, somebody must define it. Need sounds like a fact until institutions have to translate it into categories. Is a patient’s medical urgency the only need that counts? Should age matter? Time already suffered? Expected benefit? Probability of survival? Whether the condition was preventable? Whether the patient can adhere to treatment? Each additional criterion makes the system more responsive to real differences and less like a line.

A fourth family is priority by status or tenure.

Air Mobility Command’s Space-Available travel program makes this visible because the categories are explicit. Seats that the military does not need for its mission may be offered to eligible travelers, but eligibility does not mean equal priority. Current guidance divides travelers into six categories. Emergency-leave travelers sit at the top. Other classes follow. Retirees traveling for leisure are lower in the order. The person who signed up first within a lower category does not leapfrog a later-arriving passenger in a higher one merely by waiting longer.

The rule is not trying to discover who “values” the seat most. It is expressing an institutional hierarchy. Emergency need matters more than leisure. Active service can matter more than retired status. The empty seat is allocated according to what the organization believes it owes different classes of people.

A fifth family is matching.

This is the mechanism that will occupy much of the middle of this book because it differs most sharply from the folk picture of a line. A match is not just a ranking of people. It is a ranking of relationships between people and things.

Kidney allocation illustrates the difference. There is no generic kidney waiting in a warehouse. A deceased donor kidney appears at a particular place and time, can be preserved only for a limited period, carries biological characteristics that matter to recipients, and may be far more suitable for one patient than another. The question is not simply “Who has waited longest?” It is “Among the eligible candidates, who should receive this particular organ?”

The current U.S. kidney allocation system uses a layered set of criteria. Waiting time matters. Blood type matters. Immune sensitization matters. Geography and distance matter because organs cannot be stored indefinitely. For some kidneys and candidates, expected post-transplant survival measures matter as well. Highly sensitized patients—people whose immune systems are likely to reject most donor kidneys—receive extra priority because an organ they can accept may be unusually rare for them.

That last rule is a good example of why pure first come, first served can become perverse. Suppose two patients have both waited three years. One is compatible with a broad share of available kidneys. The other is compatible with almost none. Treating each organ offer as though the two patients have equal access to future opportunities would ignore the structure of the shortage. Priority for the harder-to-match patient is not charity. It is an attempt to account for the fact that opportunities are not evenly distributed.

Matching systems can become extraordinarily complicated because the thing being allocated has characteristics of its own. A school seat belongs to a particular school. A refugee placement belongs to a particular city with a particular labor market and service network. A medical residency is a position in a particular program sought by a particular applicant. A ride request exists in one location while drivers are moving through others. Once both sides have attributes and preferences, a single ranking is no longer enough.

The sixth family is the hybrid, which is what real institutions tend to become after ideal mechanisms collide with actual people.

Housing assistance is a useful example because the phrase “housing waiting list” hides a great deal of local discretion. Federal housing programs establish eligibility rules and broad requirements, but local public housing agencies often have meaningful authority over how they manage waiting lists, including local preferences within legal constraints. Lists may open and close. Applicants may be screened for eligibility. Some places use date and time of application. Some use lotteries when demand is overwhelming. Preferences can move certain households ahead. A family may therefore be “on the list” while its eventual position depends on several layers of rules that have little resemblance to a supermarket queue.

This hybridity is not necessarily administrative failure. It is often a response to the defects of pure mechanisms.

A pure lottery ignores differences in need. A pure need ranking invites endless disputes about how need is measured. Pure first come, first served rewards early information and the ability to wait. Pure seniority can trap institutions into privileging old claims over acute new ones. Pure matching can be opaque and computationally complex. Pure price excludes people who cannot pay.

So institutions combine them.

They may first define eligibility by income, then apply a priority for disability, then run a lottery among everyone left. They may rank candidates by medical score and use waiting time as a tiebreaker. They may divide people into categories and use sign-up time within each category. They may match on compatibility, then use geography, then waiting time. A mechanism that looks messy from the outside may be the accumulated result of designers trying to protect several legitimate values at once.

The mess becomes dangerous when nobody remembers which value each rule was meant to protect.

The kidney system offers a particularly revealing example. In 2022, the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network adopted a policy requiring transplant programs to identify Black kidney candidates whose waiting time had been disadvantaged by earlier use of a race-inclusive kidney-function calculation. By January 2024, HRSA reported that all active kidney programs had attested to the required review and that 14,849 waiting-time modifications had been completed.

A timestamp, in other words, turned out not to be merely a timestamp. The date on which some patients became eligible to accrue waiting time had been influenced by a clinical calculation that treated race differently. Correcting that inequity required changing the recorded past.

This is a profound feature of designed queues. They do not merely distribute future scarcity. They encode judgments about history.

If a person was excluded from the line by a rule later judged unfair, should the institution restore the time she would have accumulated? If a veteran received a preference because of service, is that preference a reward, compensation, or recognition of a distinct claim? If a household has been on a housing list for eight years but no longer lives in the jurisdiction, should tenure still dominate? If a patient’s medical condition worsens rapidly, should need outrank years already spent waiting?

There is no mechanical answer because each question asks what the waiting itself is supposed to mean.

Sometimes waiting is treated as sacrifice. The longer you have endured scarcity, the stronger your claim becomes.

Sometimes waiting is treated as evidence of persistence. You kept your application active. You complied with recertification. You answered letters. You stayed reachable.

Sometimes waiting is treated as morally irrelevant. A heart attack does not become less urgent because someone else reached the emergency room twenty minutes earlier.

Sometimes waiting is a cost the system is actively trying to minimize rather than reward.

These are very different philosophies hiding behind the same administrative noun.

The distinction matters because people judge fairness partly by the story a mechanism tells them. First come, first served tells a story about turn-taking. A lottery tells a story about equal chance. Need-based priority tells a story about compassion or urgency. Tenure tells a story about earned position. Matching tells a story about fit. Price tells a story about willingness and ability to pay.

Every mechanism has a moral grammar.

That grammar can be manipulated. Applicants learn where points come from. Consultants emerge. People move addresses, time applications, gather documents, split households, stay nominally enrolled, exaggerate urgency, or strategically rank preferences. None of this proves that applicants are dishonest in some unusual sense. It proves that people respond to rules when the rules determine access to valuable things.

Designers therefore face an awkward tradeoff. A transparent rule is easier to understand and audit, but also easier to game. A complex rule can account for more genuine differences, but may become illegible to the people whose lives it governs. A discretionary rule can adapt to unusual cases, but creates room for favoritism. A rigid rule protects against favoritism, but can produce absurd results at the margins.

There is no mechanism that escapes politics by becoming mathematical.

One more layer complicates every category: lists have to be maintained.

This sounds administrative, which is why it receives less attention than the dramatic question of who gets the scarce thing. In practice, maintenance can decide as much as ranking. A list must know whether an applicant still exists, still qualifies, still wants the benefit, can still be reached, and has supplied whatever documentation the institution requires. Addresses change. Phone numbers stop working. Families split or grow. Medical conditions improve or deteriorate. People die. People move. People miss letters.

A system that ignores those changes becomes a cemetery of stale applications. A system that cleans too aggressively can erase the people least able to comply with bureaucracy.

Imagine two housing applicants who entered the same day. One has stable housing for now, reliable mail, a smartphone, and an employer who allows personal calls. The other is moving between relatives’ apartments, changes phone numbers twice in a year, and works shifts that make office calls difficult. If the housing authority periodically asks both applicants to confirm continued interest and removes anyone who misses the deadline, the list still appears to operate by neutral rules. Yet “staying on the list” has become another scarce capability.

The mechanism therefore has at least two gates. The first decides how someone enters. The second decides whether she remains visible long enough to be served.

Closed lists create a stranger version of the same problem. When demand overwhelms expected supply, an institution may stop taking names altogether. That can be sensible: adding a family to a list with no plausible path to service can create false hope and administrative burden. But closing the list changes the allocation question. The contest is no longer merely among people waiting for housing. It is also among people trying to catch the brief moment when the institution agrees to record that they are waiting.

The opening itself becomes a scarce good.

That distinction matters in every system with intermittent enrollment windows. People with better information, stronger networks, more flexible work, or faster internet may be more likely to enter the pool. A lottery conducted afterward can be perfectly random and still inherit that unequal access. Random selection cannot repair an application window someone never knew existed.

Appeals create another hidden layer. A ranking system can be precise and still make mistakes because the data entering it are wrong. A birth date is mistyped. A medical record is incomplete. A veteran status is not recognized. An address falls outside a boundary because of a database error. A household preference is coded incorrectly. The fairness of the system then depends not only on the allocation formula but on whether a person can discover the error, understand it, and persuade someone to correct it before the opportunity disappears.

In a physical line, a mistake is often visible. Someone cuts in front of you and everyone can see it. In an administrative line, the equivalent event may be a field in a database. The person who lost priority may never know that anything happened.

This is why auditability matters differently from transparency. Publishing a rule tells people what should happen. Auditing asks whether it did happen. A lottery needs evidence that the draw was actually random. A priority system needs records showing how priority was calculated. A matching system needs enough traceability to reconstruct why one person was assigned and another was not. An appeals process needs authority to correct the record rather than merely explain it.

These functions are boring right up until the moment they fail.

They also show why the cleanest mechanism on paper can become the least fair in operation. A beautifully designed queue can depend on notices mailed to unstable addresses. A sophisticated match can rely on preference forms that some applicants never understood. A needs-based system can use categories that caseworkers apply inconsistently. A lottery can be fair after eligibility screening while the screening itself contains discretion.

The allocation rule is only the visible center of a larger institution. Around it sit intake, documentation, verification, maintenance, notification, appeals, and enforcement. Those surrounding systems determine who actually reaches the moment when the formal rule is applied.

The point is easiest to see in systems that explicitly use categories. Air Mobility Command does not pretend its Space-Available priorities emerged from a neutral optimization model. Category I travelers outrank Category VI travelers because the military has made a policy judgment about emergency leave and leisure. The hierarchy is visible.

Other institutions bury comparable judgments inside formulas.

Kidney allocation gives extra priority to some highly sensitized patients. That is a policy choice expressed through a clinical variable. Housing programs may prioritize particular classes of households. That is a policy choice expressed through eligibility and preference rules. School systems may reserve seats for students in geographic zones or with siblings already enrolled. That is a policy choice expressed through a matching algorithm.

The code executes the rule. It does not absolve the rule.

This is why the vocabulary in the rest of the book will matter. When an institution says it has a waiting list, the useful questions begin after the phrase.

Is it first come, first served?

Is it a lottery?

Is there priority by need, status, age, tenure, or some other characteristic?

Is the scarce item generic, or does compatibility matter?

Are preferences collected from one side or both?

Does money enter indirectly through eligibility, access, geography, or the ability to remain in the queue?

What breaks ties?

Who can appeal?

Who can see the rule?

Who can change it?

And what happens when the rule produces a result that everyone agrees is technically correct and substantively wrong?

Those questions turn a line into an institution.

They also make it possible to compare systems that otherwise seem unrelated. The kidney patient and the military retiree are not competing for the same kind of good. The housing applicant and the school applicant do not face the same stakes. Yet each sits inside a designed scarcity mechanism that translates values into order.

The mechanisms are not infinitely varied. They are combinations of a few basic choices.

Arrival.

Chance.

Need.

Status.

Fit.

Price.

Most real systems use more than one.

The interesting part is not identifying the label. It is noticing what each system has decided should count, and what it has decided should not.

Chapter 1 began with a society refusing to let price decide who receives a kidney. Once price is removed, the allocation problem does not become simpler. It becomes more explicit. Somebody has to write down another principle, then another, until the moral judgment becomes an operational sequence that a hospital, housing authority, airline terminal, or computer can actually follow.

That sequence is the line.

And before we celebrate any line as fair because everyone appears to be waiting their turn, we should look more closely at who was able to arrive first.
