# Scalpers, Bots, and the Ticketmaster Problem

At 10 a.m. on November 15, 2022, millions of people tried to stand in the same line.

The line had no sidewalk. There were no folding chairs, no wristbands, no sleeping bags, no teenager sent ahead to hold a place. There was a browser window, a login, a presale code, and a queue that existed inside someone else’s servers.

The prize was a Taylor Swift ticket.

Ticketmaster had opened the Verified Fan presale for the Eras Tour. Demand was extraordinary even by stadium-tour standards. The company later said that more than 3.5 million people had registered for the program and that 1.5 million received codes, while the rest were placed on a waiting list. When the sale began, Ticketmaster said its systems faced unprecedented traffic, including billions of system requests, many of them from bots or other attempts to reach the sale without valid codes. Fans reported hours in queues, frozen screens, error messages, tickets disappearing during checkout, and prices on resale sites that bore little resemblance to the face values that had brought people into the queue in the first place.

The public anger was so intense that a failed concert-ticket sale became a Senate hearing.

That reaction is easy to dismiss as celebrity scale. Taylor Swift has an unusually large fan base. A stadium tour is a discretionary purchase. Nobody needs a concert ticket in the sense that somebody needs a kidney, a vaccine, or a place in a public school.

But precisely because the stakes are lower, ticketing gives us a cleaner view of a mechanism that becomes harder to study when life or health is involved.

A scarce good is offered at a posted price below what at least some buyers are willing to pay.

Access is rationed through a queue.

The queue is attacked by participants with better software, more accounts, more time, or specialized tools.

A secondary market appears almost immediately.

The people who failed in the official queue discover that the very ticket they could not buy at the posted price is available elsewhere at a much higher one.

The shortage did not disappear. It changed hands.

Ticketing therefore sits in the awkward space between queue and market. The primary seller often uses a posted face price and a timed on-sale. The secondary market uses whatever price buyers will bear after the initial allocation is complete. If the first price is far below the market-clearing price, the difference becomes an invitation.

Someone will try to capture it.

Sometimes that person is a fan who bought an extra seat and later cannot attend. Sometimes it is a professional reseller. Sometimes it is a broker operating hundreds of accounts. Sometimes automated software is used to search, reserve, or purchase inventory faster than an ordinary person can. The economic function is the same: obtain a scarce ticket under the primary mechanism and resell it under a different one.

Scalping predates the internet by generations. The internet changed the speed, reach, and industrial scale of it.

A man standing outside a venue with six tickets is visible. He has only six. A reseller using software, multiple accounts, multiple payment methods, distributed IP addresses, or a network of buyers can participate in many sales at once. A digital queue can look as though it is giving every person one place in line while the surrounding system quietly allows some participants to create far more than one effective identity.

That is the central design problem.

A queue can be fair only if the system knows what one entrant is.

Is it one email address?

One phone number?

One credit card?

One household?

One device?

One verified person?

Each answer creates a new way to make mistakes. An email address is cheap. Phone numbers can be obtained in volume. Families legitimately use several cards. People share devices. A household may contain four genuine fans. Identity verification can reduce fraud and also exclude people whose names, addresses, documents, or payment methods do not fit the system cleanly.

Digital queues therefore inherit a problem that physical queues solve almost accidentally.

A body is difficult to duplicate.

You can hire a line-sitter or send a friend, but creating ten thousand physical versions of yourself is expensive. Software changes that cost structure. It can make artificial arrival cheap.

Congress responded to this problem in 2016 with the Better Online Ticket Sales Act, usually shortened to the BOTS Act. The law prohibits circumventing security measures, access controls, or other technological controls used by online ticket sellers to enforce ticket-purchase limits or ordering rules, and it also prohibits selling tickets obtained through such circumvention when the seller knows or should know how they were acquired.

The law was aimed at a recognizable fairness intuition: if a seller says four tickets per customer, one sophisticated buyer should not be able to become hundreds of customers by defeating the system that enforces the limit.

The Federal Trade Commission’s later enforcement showed what that behavior can look like in practice. In 2021, the agency brought its first cases under the BOTS Act against ticket brokers it said had used automated purchasing software, IP-address concealment, hundreds of fictitious Ticketmaster accounts, and large numbers of credit cards to evade posted ticket limits. The companies agreed to orders that included $3.7 million in civil penalties.

Four years later, the FTC was still reminding ticket sellers and resellers that the law applied.

This is the first useful lesson from ticket bots: a prohibition can raise the cost of cheating without eliminating the reward for cheating.

The reward is created by the gap between the primary price and the resale price.

Suppose a concert ticket is offered for $150 and would sell immediately for $800 on a secondary market. Whoever captures that ticket in the original sale has acquired an asset with a potential $650 gross spread before fees and costs. If software can increase the probability of getting the ticket, software has economic value. If creating more accounts increases the probability, accounts have economic value. If a presale code opens access before the general public, codes have economic value.

The queue itself becomes a commodity.

That is what connects Ticketmaster to the organ chapter before it.

The comparison has limits that matter. Buying a concert ticket from a reseller is not buying a kidney from a vulnerable donor. The health, consent, and irreversibility problems are entirely different. But the mechanism underneath is familiar. When an official system allocates something valuable under a rule that does not clear demand, access to the rule becomes valuable too.

People trade not only the good but the ability to get near the front.

Verified Fan was one attempt to manage that problem.

The idea was not simply to create a queue. It was to decide who should be allowed into the queue before the sale began. Fans registered in advance. Ticketmaster tried to distinguish likely genuine buyers from bots and suspicious accounts. Selected registrants received codes. Others waited.

This moved rationing one step upstream.

Instead of asking only who clicked first, the system asked who would receive permission to click at all.

That can improve fairness if the gate successfully reduces automated abuse. It can also create a second black box. A fan who is not selected does not necessarily know why. A person can be genuine and still lose access before inventory is ever visible. The queue has become a queue for the queue.

The Eras Tour presale exposed how fragile that structure can be when demand is extreme.

Ticketmaster’s own explanation after the sale emphasized both legitimate demand and hostile or invalid traffic. The company said that the number of people trying to purchase and the volume of requests overwhelmed parts of the system. It subsequently canceled the planned public sale because remaining ticket inventory was insufficient to meet demand.

That outcome created a peculiar form of public fury.

Fans had not merely lost an auction. There had been no single transparent auction to lose.

They had been told they were entering a controlled allocation system designed to identify real fans and manage demand. Many then experienced that system as arbitrary, unstable, and technically unreliable. Meanwhile, tickets appeared on resale platforms at enormous markups.

The visible secondary market made every failure of the primary queue look more offensive.

If a ticket simply did not exist, disappointment would be easier to explain. But when the ticket exists and is sitting on another website for six times the price, the failed queue looks less like scarcity and more like leakage.

That perception matters even when the resale listing came from a legitimate buyer rather than a bot.

Mechanisms are judged partly by what participants can observe. A primary seller may have blocked millions of automated requests and still look incompetent because the successful abuse is visible while the blocked abuse is not. A resale platform may contain mostly legitimate inventory and still look predatory because the highest prices are the ones people photograph and share.

The design problem is not only to allocate tickets. It is to make the allocation credible.

That is why the Senate Judiciary Committee’s January 2023 hearing was about more than server capacity.

Lawmakers examined competition in live entertainment, ticketing practices, bots, fees, resale, and the relationship between Live Nation and Ticketmaster. The 2010 merger of Live Nation and Ticketmaster had combined a major concert promoter with the dominant primary ticketing platform. Critics argued that the structure gave the combined company power across promotion, venues, ticketing, sponsorship, and other parts of the live-events business. Live Nation and Ticketmaster disputed many of the monopoly claims and have long argued that ticket prices are driven heavily by artists and demand, while secondary resale prices are set outside the primary platform’s control.

Those distinctions matter.

Ticketmaster is not the only actor in a ticket price. Artists, promoters, venues, primary sellers, resale platforms, sponsors, and buyers all affect the system. A face value can be set by an artist or promoter. Fees can be divided among parties. Resale prices can be set by resellers. A platform may be blamed for a price it did not unilaterally choose.

But platform structure still matters because the allocator controls the doorway.

A company that operates the primary ticketing system decides how the queue works, what identity checks exist, how ticket limits are enforced, how presales are structured, what information users see, and how much capacity the system can absorb when millions arrive at once.

These are not neutral technical details.

They determine who can participate in the market.

In May 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice and a group of states sued Live Nation and Ticketmaster, alleging monopolization and other unlawful conduct across live concert markets. The case was broader than the Eras Tour failure. The complaint challenged conduct involving venues, promotion, ticketing, and competition across the concert ecosystem.

For this chapter, the important point is what happened later.

By March 2026, the parties had reached a settlement. In June 2026, the Justice Department filed a proposed final judgment and related materials under the Tunney Act process. That means the current factual frame is no longer “the government is suing and trial is ahead.” The litigation moved into a settlement and public-interest review stage.

That update matters because ticketing debates age quickly.

A book about allocation mechanisms should not freeze a live antitrust case at its filing date simply because the complaint made a convenient dramatic endpoint.

The same caution applies to claims about bots.

Bots are an important part of ticket-market abuse, but they are not an all-purpose explanation for expensive resale. Even perfect bot enforcement would not remove the underlying price gap if tickets remained systematically underpriced relative to demand. Human buyers, professional brokers, fan-club members, season-ticket holders, credit-card presales, venue partners, and ordinary lucky purchasers can all resell.

Eliminate every bot tomorrow and there can still be a secondary market on Friday.

This is the strongest argument for letting prices rise earlier.

If a ticket would clear at $800, why sell it for $150 and create a lottery whose winners can immediately resell it? Why not use dynamic pricing, an auction, or a higher posted price and let the primary seller capture the value instead of a broker?

Economically, that argument has force.

A higher primary price can reduce the arbitrage spread. It can make automated attack less profitable. It can move revenue toward the artist, promoter, or venue rather than the reseller. And it can allocate the ticket to the person willing to pay the most rather than the person with the fastest software or best luck.

But it answers one fairness problem by choosing another allocation principle.

Willingness to pay is not the same as devotion, need, patience, or desert.

Artists know this instinctively. Many do not want the front rows of every show allocated only to the wealthiest buyers. A fan who has followed an artist for ten years but cannot spend $1,200 may look, from the artist’s perspective, like exactly the person the ticketing system ought to preserve access for.

That is why underpricing can be deliberate.

The primary price may contain a social objective as well as a revenue objective.

A musician may want some tickets available at a price ordinary fans can afford. A sports team may want families and longtime supporters in the building, not only corporate buyers. A theater may preserve rush tickets. A museum may offer free admission slots. A city may cap the price of something precisely because it does not want purchasing power to be the sole allocator.

Once price is constrained, however, the system needs a second rule.

That rule could be a queue.

It could be a lottery.

It could be verified eligibility.

It could be a fan-club presale.

It could be a nontransferable ticket tied to identity.

It could be a resale cap.

It could be a combination of several of these.

Each one closes some opportunities and opens others.

Nontransferability can deter scalpers, but it can punish legitimate buyers whose plans change. Identity checks can reduce duplicate accounts, but they create privacy and accessibility burdens. Lotteries can reduce the advantage of software speed, but they frustrate people who prefer a transparent queue. Dynamic pricing can reduce resale spreads, but it makes the official seller look like the scalper. Resale caps can protect affordability, but they may move transactions to less trustworthy markets.

There is no mechanism that preserves every virtue simultaneously.

That is the larger lesson of the Ticketmaster problem.

The public conversation often treats ticketing failures as a technology problem: the website crashed, the bots got through, the queue froze.

Those failures are real. But technology is implementing a policy choice about who should get a scarce seat.

The moment demand exceeds supply, somebody has to lose.

The system can decide losers by price, time, lottery, identity, status, membership, randomization, or some mixture. It can make resale easy or difficult. It can make the rule transparent or obscure. It can distribute the surplus to artists, platforms, brokers, or lucky buyers. It can spend heavily to enforce one identity per customer or tolerate a degree of duplication.

The code does not remove those choices.

It hides them.

A good queue makes its political decisions look operational.

A bad queue makes the politics impossible to ignore.

The Eras Tour sale did the second.

Fans did not merely discover that there were fewer tickets than people who wanted them. Everyone already knew that.

They discovered that the mechanism for deciding who would get them had become part of the scarce good itself.

A Verified Fan code had value.

A place in the queue had value.

A working browser session had value.

A ticket acquired at face value had immediate resale value.

And the entire system depended on deciding which identities were real, which requests were legitimate, and which advantages counted as cheating.

That last problem is where this book turns next.

Ticketing tries to defeat strategic behavior with better gates and enforcement.

The economists in the next chapters pursued a stranger possibility: design a mechanism in which telling the truth about what you want is not a mistake in the first place.

Their starting point was not a stadium.

It was marriage.
