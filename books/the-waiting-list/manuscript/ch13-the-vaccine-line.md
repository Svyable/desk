# The Vaccine Line

In December 2020, the United States had a vaccine before it had enough vaccine.

That distinction turned a scientific triumph into an allocation problem almost overnight.

A vial could prevent severe disease and death. But the first shipments were too small for everyone who wanted protection, and the country had no single answer to the obvious question: who should go first?

The question sounds medical.

It was also moral, administrative, political, and mathematical.

A queue had to be built before most people had even seen the product they were queuing for.

The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, the federal advisory body that recommends how vaccines should be used, had been working on the problem for months. Its November 2020 ethical framework named four principles for allocating limited initial supplies of COVID-19 vaccine: maximize benefits and minimize harms, promote justice, mitigate health inequities, and promote transparency.

Those principles are difficult to oppose in the abstract.

The difficulty begins when they point in different directions.

If the goal is to prevent the most deaths, older adults move toward the front. If the goal is to preserve the health system, doctors, nurses, aides, and other health-care workers move forward. If the goal is to reduce transmission or preserve essential services, workers who cannot stay home gain priority. If the goal is to mitigate inequity, communities carrying the heaviest burden of disease and exposure deserve special attention.

A single dose can satisfy more than one objective.

It cannot satisfy all of them at once.

This is the same problem that appeared in kidney allocation, school choice, and the medical Match, but pandemic vaccination made the tradeoff visible to almost everyone. The scarce object was standardized. One dose did not have a personality, a school boundary, or a blood type. The main design problem was priority.

Who moves ahead of whom?

On December 1, 2020, ACIP recommended that health-care personnel and residents of long-term-care facilities be offered vaccination first, in Phase 1a. On December 20, it recommended that Phase 1b include people aged seventy-five and older along with non-health-care frontline essential workers. Phase 1c would include people aged sixty-five to seventy-four, younger adults with high-risk medical conditions, and other essential workers.

The categories were broad because the country was broad.

They were also large.

ACIP estimated that Phase 1b alone covered roughly forty-nine million unique people. Phase 1c covered roughly 129 million. The labels created priority classes, but they did not create enough appointments.

Eligibility is not supply.

This sounds obvious now. It was not obvious in the lived experience of the rollout.

A person could be officially eligible and still unable to find a dose. Another could live in a county where a local system had opened a different group sooner. A hospital could vaccinate one category while a pharmacy used another. A state could adopt the federal recommendation, modify it, or simplify it.

The national waiting list fractured into fifty state systems and thousands of local queues.

That fragmentation was not entirely a policy failure. Public health authority in the United States is distributed. Local conditions differed. Vaccine supply, storage capacity, appointment infrastructure, workforce, infection rates, and population structure varied from place to place.

A flexible system could adapt.

A flexible system could also make the rule harder to understand.

By January 11, 2021, a KFF review found that thirty-five states broadly followed ACIP’s Phase 1 recommendations while sixteen varied from them. A week later, after federal guidance urged states to open vaccination to people sixty-five and older, the line was shifting again. By January 19, twenty-eight states had moved adults sixty-five and older into an early priority group, up from sixteen eight days earlier.

The national priority rule had become a moving target.

This produced an experience familiar from every badly communicated queue: people spent as much effort learning the line as standing in it.

Who counted as an essential worker?

Did a seventy-year-old qualify before a sixty-three-year-old grocery worker?

Did a teacher count in this county yet?

Could a person with diabetes register now or only after the next phase opened?

Was a correctional officer eligible before an incarcerated person living in the same outbreak-prone facility?

Was a home health aide included in “health-care personnel” if she worked independently rather than for a hospital?

The rules were trying to encode multiple kinds of risk at once.

Age approximated risk of severe illness and death.

Occupation approximated exposure and social function.

Medical conditions approximated vulnerability.

Congregate residence approximated both exposure and outbreak intensity.

Geography could approximate local epidemic pressure and access barriers.

No category was perfect.

Age is blunt but easy to verify.

Occupation can be more responsive to exposure but harder to classify.

Medical-risk rules can identify vulnerability but require definitions and documentation.

Social-vulnerability measures can target structural disadvantage but raise difficult questions about granularity, eligibility, and political legitimacy.

The more dimensions a system tries to optimize, the more complicated the line becomes.

The more complicated the line becomes, the more advantage shifts toward people who can understand and navigate it.

That was the paradox.

A sophisticated priority system intended to promote equity could become inequitable in implementation.

The National Academies had confronted the same problem before the first U.S. vaccine authorization. Its 2020 framework for equitable allocation recommended giving early priority to high-risk health workers, first responders, people with conditions that placed them at significantly higher risk, and older adults in congregate settings, followed by additional groups including high-exposure critical workers and residents of prisons, shelters, and group homes. It also recommended that equity cut across every phase, including geographic prioritization using a social-vulnerability measure.

The committee’s logic was not simply “oldest first.”

It was trying to combine risk of infection, risk of severe outcome, social function, and inequity.

That is what a serious allocation framework should do.

It is also what makes serious frameworks hard to operate at a pharmacy counter.

Imagine a vaccine clinic with one hundred doses left and two hundred people who can plausibly claim priority.

One is eighty-two and lives alone.

One is forty-seven and works every day in a meatpacking plant.

One is thirty-eight and treats COVID patients in an emergency department.

One is sixty-four with serious medical conditions.

One is seventy-six but can work safely from home.

One is a school custodian who shares a household with an immunocompromised relative.

One is incarcerated in a crowded facility experiencing an outbreak.

One is the administrator responsible for keeping the clinic open.

A principle can explain why each person matters.

A mechanism has to sort them.

This is where public debates often became misleading. People argued as though a morally persuasive claim for one group disproved the claims of everyone behind it.

It did not.

Scarcity creates morally valid losers.

That is why allocation rules matter.

A good rule does not make every outcome fair in the sense that nobody is disappointed. It makes the tradeoff legible and defensible before the individual cases arrive.

That was ACIP’s reason for including transparency as an ethical principle rather than merely a communications preference.

A hidden priority rule cannot easily earn trust.

The committee’s meetings were public. Its evidence and analytic reasoning were published. The categories were debated openly. That transparency did not eliminate disagreement, but it placed the disagreement in the right location: over the values and evidence used to construct the line.

The implementation layer was less tidy.

In many places, scarce appointments were distributed online.

That transformed priority into a second mechanism.

First, the government decided who was eligible.

Then the appointment system decided which eligible people actually got a slot.

The first mechanism might prioritize age, occupation, medical vulnerability, or exposure.

The second might reward broadband access, free time, English fluency, digital literacy, fast typing, social networks, or the ability to refresh a browser repeatedly.

A priority rule can be equitable on paper and first-come-first-served in software.

This is one of the most important distinctions in the entire book.

Eligibility and allocation are not the same thing.

A system can spend months designing fair eligibility categories and then hand the scarce appointments within each category to whoever clicks fastest.

That is not a small implementation detail.

It is the actual mechanism experienced by participants.

The same problem appears in housing lotteries that require difficult paperwork, school-choice systems that assume families understand ranking rules, and benefits programs whose formal eligibility is broader than practical access.

The queue has an entrance fee even when the product is free.

During the vaccine rollout, that fee was often time and information.

People recruited children and grandchildren to search for appointments. Volunteer groups built alert systems. Social media accounts posted openings. Pharmacies released slots at inconsistent times. Some people drove hours to clinics with available doses.

These improvisations were often generous and effective.

They also reveal a failure mode.

When ordinary citizens need informal brokers to navigate a public allocation system, the official line is only part of the allocation process.

There was a third mechanism underneath the first two: throughput.

A priority rule decides who deserves access first. An appointment system decides who can claim a place. A delivery system still has to turn inventory into injections.

Those are different jobs.

A jurisdiction could have a morally persuasive priority order and still perform poorly if doses arrived unevenly, sites lacked enough staff, appointment capacity was too low, or the people at the front of the formal line could not reach the places where doses were available. Conversely, a clinic could move doses through arms very quickly while doing a poor job of reaching the people its own priority framework said mattered most.

Speed and priority can therefore conflict.

Suppose a site can fill every appointment immediately by opening booking to a broad group. That may maximize throughput. It may also allow people with flexible schedules and better information to consume appointments before harder-to-reach high-priority residents know they exist.

Suppose instead the site reserves every slot for a narrowly defined group and verifies each claim carefully. That protects the priority rule. It can also slow the line if verification is cumbersome or if reserved appointments go unused.

The design problem is no longer simply who ranks first.

It is how much friction the system should tolerate to protect the ranking.

This is where a queue becomes an operating system rather than a list.

Someone has to decide how inventory is divided among hospitals, pharmacies, mass-vaccination sites, long-term-care facilities, and local clinics. Someone has to decide whether appointments are released all at once or in smaller batches. Someone has to decide how long a slot can remain unfilled before it is offered to somebody else. Someone has to decide what evidence proves eligibility and what happens when the evidence is ambiguous.

Each decision can change who actually gets vaccinated without changing the official priority chart at all.

That is why implementation cannot be treated as the morally neutral part that begins after ethics ends.

Implementation is ethics with clocks, forms, refrigerators, staff schedules, websites, and physical distance added.

The National Academies and ACIP could state principles at national scale. A local operator had to turn those principles into a Tuesday afternoon.

This also explains why the simplest performance metric could mislead.

Counting doses administered tells you whether inventory is moving. It does not tell you whether the people vaccinated are the people the priority rule intended to reach. Measuring adherence to priority categories tells you something about procedural fidelity. It does not tell you whether appointments sat empty or whether the rules were too difficult for eligible people to navigate.

A system can be fast and exclusionary.

It can be careful and wasteful.

The hard work lies in refusing to call either dimension the whole definition of success.

There was another problem: doses expired.

A perfectly ordered queue is useless if the product spoils while administrators protect the order.

Early COVID vaccines came with handling constraints, multidose vials, and uncertainty about daily attendance. Clinics sometimes reached the end of the day with doses that had to be used quickly.

Now the fairness question changed.

Should a clinic preserve strict priority and risk wasting a dose?

Or vaccinate the nearest available lower-priority person?

The answer seems easy when the alternative is literal disposal.

But once exceptions are permitted, people worry that the exception will become the rule. A well-connected person can always be “nearby.” A clinic can always claim urgency. An informal standby list can become a shadow priority system.

The right answer is not rigid purity.

It is rule-governed flexibility.

A system can define in advance what happens to leftover doses. It can maintain standby lists. It can randomize among eligible nearby people. It can widen priority categories at a specified time of day. It can record deviations.

The goal is not to eliminate discretion.

It is to stop discretion from becoming invisible privilege.

That distinction is easy to miss when debates are framed as rules versus judgment.

Every real allocation system needs both.

Rules protect people from arbitrary favoritism.

Judgment protects the system from absurd outcomes the rules did not anticipate.

The design problem is deciding where judgment enters and how it is audited.

The vaccine line also exposed a deeper conflict between simplicity and precision.

Suppose the sole goal were minimizing deaths. A system might rank people using detailed estimates of age, comorbidities, occupation, local infection risk, household structure, and prior exposure. In theory, such a score could be more precise than broad age bands.

In practice, it would be difficult to verify, explain, and administer at national scale.

It might also encode uncertain science as false precision.

A seventy-five-year age threshold is crude.

It is also legible.

Anyone can understand where the line is drawn.

That matters.

Transparent roughness can sometimes be fairer than opaque sophistication.

The lesson runs against the instinct of algorithmic governance, which often assumes that more variables produce a better decision.

More variables can produce a more accurate prediction.

A public allocation rule has other obligations.

People need to know why they were ranked where they were. Administrators need to verify eligibility quickly. Rules need to survive political disagreement. Errors need to be contestable. The system has to function in thousands of locations with different capacities.

A mechanism is not merely an objective function.

It is an institution.

The vaccine rollout made that painfully clear because the underlying scientific product was unusually good while the distribution system was unusually visible.

Vaccination worked.

The queue still mattered.

This is worth emphasizing because retrospective arguments can collapse the two questions. Criticism of allocation does not imply criticism of vaccination. Support for vaccination does not imply that every priority rule was optimal.

The product and the mechanism should be judged separately.

The same distinction applies to many systems in this book. A kidney transplant can be life-saving even when the waiting-list rule is contested. A school can be excellent even when its admissions mechanism is unfair. A housing subsidy can be valuable even when its application process systematically excludes people.

Good things still require allocation.

Scarcity does not disappear because the object is beneficial.

By spring 2021, vaccine supply expanded dramatically and eligibility widened. The moral intensity of the ranking problem eased because the queue changed character. When nearly everyone could become eligible, the problem shifted from who should go first to how to reach people who had not yet been vaccinated.

The bottleneck moved.

At first, demand exceeded supply.

Later, in many places, supply exceeded willing or convenient demand.

The mechanism had to change with it.

This is another important property of waiting lists: they are temporary institutions built around a particular scarcity regime.

A good allocation rule in December can become a bad rule in April.

When supply is tiny, priority matters enormously.

When supply becomes abundant, elaborate priority can slow distribution for no benefit.

The designer has to know when to dismantle the line.

That sounds straightforward.

Institutions are often better at creating eligibility rules than retiring them.

The vaccine experience therefore offers a rare full life cycle of a waiting list compressed into months. The country began with a scarce good, developed ethical principles, translated them into priority groups, delegated implementation to jurisdictions, discovered that appointment systems created new inequities, adjusted the rules repeatedly as supply and evidence changed, and eventually broadened access until the original priority structure mattered far less.

The line was designed, contested, revised, and then partly dissolved.

What survived was the underlying problem.

When there is not enough of something important, people want a reason for why someone else went first.

“Because they were faster” is sometimes a reason.

“Because they were richer” is sometimes a reason.

“Because they were sicker,” “older,” “more exposed,” “more essential,” or “more likely to benefit” are also reasons.

Mechanism design begins when we stop pretending those reasons are interchangeable.

The pandemic made the ranking rule public because the stakes were impossible to hide.

The next chapter returns to a quieter form of allocation, one in which the scarce resource is not a dose but a place.

A refugee arriving in a new country does not merely need permission to enter.

Someone must decide where that person will have the best chance to begin again.
