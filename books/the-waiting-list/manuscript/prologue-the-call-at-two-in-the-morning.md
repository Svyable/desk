# Prologue: The Call at Two in the Morning

Somewhere tonight, a phone will ring in the dark, and a stranger's death will become the most hopeful event in another family's year.

The call comes from a transplant coordinator, not a doctor, and it comes with a script that has been refined over forty years of American organ allocation: a kidney has become available, the computer has ranked you near the top of a list built from blood type, tissue match, time on dialysis, and a dozen other variables you have never seen calculated, and you have perhaps four hours to get to the hospital before the answer changes to no. Pack a bag. Do not eat. Bring your medications. Drive carefully; a car accident on the way would be its own kind of tragedy inside a tragedy.

For the roughly ninety-five thousand Americans waiting for a kidney on any given day in 2026, according to data maintained by the federal Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, that phone call is the entire architecture of hope. There is no other way in. You cannot apply harder. You cannot, in the ordinary case, pay more. You cannot found a startup that queue-jumps the line, though people have certainly tried. Roughly a dozen people on the national transplant waiting list die every day still waiting, federal data compiled by the Health Resources and Services Administration shows, which means that for a meaningful fraction of the people reading their name on that list tonight, the call will simply never come in time.

This book is not, in the end, a book about kidneys. It uses the kidney waiting list because nothing clarifies a mechanism like watching it operate where the stakes are highest and the shortcuts are fewest. But the argument travels much further than nephrology.

Picture a parent in Brooklyn in March, filling out a twelve-page form ranking public high schools in order of preference for a child who is about to become a teenager, with no idea whether the algorithm on the other end of that form will reward honesty or punish it. Picture a Kurdish family who spent three years in a refugee camp in Jordan, about to be told which country will resettle them and which city inside that country, a decision that a small number of governments have started outsourcing, in part, to a matching algorithm built by political scientists. Picture a graduate student in Santa Clara in late 2026, refreshing a cloud console at midnight because that is when a batch of GPU capacity sometimes frees up, needing that capacity to finish a research result before a conference deadline, and finding that money alone does not seem to be moving the queue the way an economics textbook promised it would. Picture a family in Phoenix checking a lottery number for a housing voucher that closed its waiting list seven years ago and reopened, for one week, in 2024, drawing hundreds of thousands of names for a few thousand slots.

None of these people are shopping in the sense the word usually implies. There is no listed price they can pay to move to the front. There is, instead, a list: a rule, sometimes generous and sometimes cruel, sometimes designed by a Nobel laureate and sometimes improvised by an overworked county clerk, that decides who goes first when there is not enough of something to go around.

The economics profession has a name, if an unglamorous one, for the branch of the field that studies exactly this problem. It is called market design, and it emerged from a discovery that unsettled a discipline built on the premise that prices clear markets: sometimes, for reasons of law, ethics, or plain human revulsion, a price is not allowed to do the job. You cannot buy a spot on the kidney transplant list. You cannot legally buy your way into most public school assignments. You cannot, in most of the world, buy a place at the front of the line for a life-saving vaccine during a pandemic, or a refugee resettlement slot, or, until quite recently, a graduate medical residency. Alvin Roth, an economist who would eventually share a Nobel Prize for this work, gave these forbidden trades an evocative label: repugnant transactions, markets that societies choose not to allow even when a willing buyer and a willing seller can be found for them.

Take the price mechanism away, and something still has to decide. That something is usually a list.

Lists are older than money. A line at a well predates a coin. But the lists this book is interested in are not the simple first-come-first-served queues of folk memory. They are engineered systems, built by economists, computer scientists, hospital administrators, and, increasingly, product managers at technology companies, to solve an old problem with new tools: how do you allocate a scarce, indivisible thing among people who all want it, fairly, efficiently, and in a way that does not collapse into corruption or gaming the moment enough people learn how the algorithm works.

Sometimes that engineering succeeds so well that it becomes invisible, and the reader never learns the name of the economist who fixed it. The National Resident Matching Program, which assigns roughly forty-five thousand new doctors to their first hospital jobs every spring, spent decades collapsing into instability and re-gaming before a redesign, led in part by Roth himself, quietly repaired it in the mid-1990s. Few of the doctors who now match into their residencies each March have any idea that the algorithm sorting their careers was built to solve a problem uncovered by mathematicians studying stable marriages in 1962.

Sometimes the engineering fails in public and expensively, and the whole country watches. Ticketmaster's presale for Taylor Swift's Eras Tour in November 2022 buckled under demand so severe that the company canceled the general public sale entirely, and the resulting outrage helped drag two U.S. Senate committees and the Department of Justice into an examination of how concert tickets are actually allocated in America, which turns out to have far less to do with a fair queue than most fans assume.

And sometimes the engineering is only beginning, built in real time by companies and governments that did not ask permission and are discovering, mid-flight, all the same problems that health economists and market designers spent half a century working through in kidney exchanges and school choice systems. The scramble for graphics processing units that has defined the artificial intelligence buildout of the mid-2020s is, underneath the headlines about billion-dollar chip orders, a waiting-list problem: a fixed, physically limited supply of an input that many well-funded actors want simultaneously, allocated through a shifting mix of long-term contracts, cloud-provider queues, political favor, and outright hoarding, with money mattering enormously in some layers of that system and remarkably little in others.

The chapters that follow move between these worlds deliberately. Kidney exchange chains and cloud-computing shortages do not belong in the same sentence in most people's imagination, and that is precisely the point. The same handful of mechanisms recur everywhere a queue forms: first-come-first-served, which feels fair until you notice who has the time and information to arrive first; lotteries, which feel fair until you notice how much they leave to chance in decisions that arguably deserve judgment; priority rules based on need or seniority, which feel fair until you notice how easily they are gamed by anyone patient or savvy enough to learn the rule; and, increasingly, algorithmic matching markets descended directly from a 1962 mathematics paper that nobody involved in kidney transplantation or school admissions had originally intended to write for that purpose at all.

Money has not disappeared from this picture. It shows up constantly, sometimes as the official mechanism, more often as the black market that grows in the shadow of an official mechanism that refuses to use price. Iran runs the world's only legal, government-regulated market for paid kidney donation from living donors, a fact that most Americans do not know and that most transplant ethicists regard with genuine ambivalence rather than simple horror. Elsewhere, in the absence of any legal market, an illegal one has grown up around exactly the same organ, moving desperate sellers in Bangladesh and the Philippines toward desperate buyers who can pay their way past a legal waiting list they have decided they cannot survive. The line and the black market are not opposites. They are usually two views of the same shortage.

What this book argues, across the chapters that follow, is that the twenty-first century's most consequential allocation decisions are increasingly made not by markets in the textbook sense but by designed queues, and that the quality of that design is one of the least examined forms of power in modern life. A society reveals what it actually values not in its constitution or its advertising but in the fine print of who gets to go first when there is not enough to go around. Sometimes that fine print is the product of decades of careful, evidence-driven economics, tested against real placements and real lives, and it works about as well as a human institution can be made to work. Sometimes it is a spreadsheet built by an intern under deadline pressure, replicating every bias and every loophole that a more careful design would have caught.

The reader who finishes this book will not be able to un-see the lists. They are in the DMV line and in the code behind a ride-hailing app's driver assignment, in the fine print of a vaccine rollout and the terms of service of a dating app, in the way a cloud provider decides whose training job gets the next available server, and in the algorithm, only a few years old, that decides which city a refugee family with no say in the matter will call home. Some of these lists were built by people who understood, with real precision, what fairness requires under scarcity. Others were not built at all; they simply happened, and they are quietly deciding outcomes that a market, or a better-designed queue, would have handled very differently.

The phone is going to ring somewhere tonight. This book is about the machinery, mostly invisible and almost entirely undebated, that decides whose phone it is.
