# Why Money Can't Buy Everything

In 1983, a Virginia physician named H. Barry Jacobs announced a business plan that was almost offensively simple. People in poor countries would sell him kidneys. He would bring them to the United States, or bring buyers and sellers together, and charge a brokerage fee for the transaction. Jacobs was not proposing to steal organs or to take them from unwilling donors. His pitch, as reported at the time and later discussed in the congressional history of organ-transplant law, was a market: consenting adults, a scarce good, a price.

Economics had a familiar answer for that kind of shortage. Raise the price until supply meets demand.

Congress had a different answer.

The National Organ Transplant Act passed in 1984. Among its provisions was a prohibition, now codified at 42 U.S.C. § 274e, making it unlawful to knowingly acquire, receive, or transfer a human organ for “valuable consideration” for use in transplantation when the transfer affects interstate commerce. The statute lists kidneys among the covered organs. It also makes an important distinction that will matter later in this book: reimbursing a donor for expenses such as travel, housing, and lost wages is not the same thing as buying the organ itself.

That distinction sounds technical until you notice what it says about the market Congress was willing to permit. A donor can be made whole for costs. Surgeons can be paid. Hospitals can be paid. Airlines can be paid to move the organ. Laboratories can be paid to type blood and tissue. A recipient’s insurer can pay hundreds of thousands of dollars for the transplant episode. What cannot be priced, at least not as property for sale, is the kidney.

The law did not abolish scarcity. It abolished one way of resolving scarcity.

That is the first important fact about waiting lists. They often begin not because nobody knows how markets work, but because a society has decided that an ordinary market is unacceptable.

Alvin Roth gave this constraint a useful name. In a 2007 essay in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, he wrote about “repugnance” as a force that can shape markets every bit as concretely as technology, incentives, or cost. Some transactions are not merely expensive or inefficient. People recoil from them. Legislatures ban them. Professional bodies refuse to participate in them. The objection can be moral, religious, visceral, political, or some mixture of all four.

Roth’s examples are deliberately unsettling because the boundaries move. Charging interest on loans was once widely treated as morally suspect. Indentured servitude was once legally ordinary in places where it would now be intolerable. California has banned the sale of horse meat for human consumption. France banned dwarf tossing even when an adult performer wanted the work. Slavery is the most obvious case in which the modern moral boundary is not simply a matter of taste but a foundational commitment about what a person cannot consent to sell.

The point is not that all of these things are ethically equivalent. They plainly are not. The point is that markets operate inside social permission. Before price can allocate something, the society around the market has to accept that the thing may legitimately be bought and sold.

Kidneys fail that test in the United States.

Why?

The easy answer is exploitation. A market for kidneys would predictably recruit sellers from people who need money most urgently. A wealthy buyer might describe the exchange as voluntary; a seller facing eviction, debt, or hunger might experience the choice differently. The kidney would move in one direction and the money in the other, but the formal symmetry of the trade would conceal a profound asymmetry in bargaining power.

That concern is not theoretical. Iran has operated the world’s best-known system of compensated living unrelated kidney donation for decades, and it offers unusually useful evidence because both supporters and critics can point to real outcomes rather than imagined ones. The program increased transplant supply. Older reviews reported that it sharply reduced or, for periods, effectively eliminated a renal-transplant waiting list. That is not a trivial achievement. If one starts from the proposition that deaths while waiting are the central moral failure of an organ-allocation system, a policy that produces more kidneys deserves serious attention.

But the Iranian experience does not make the ethical problem disappear. Studies of compensated donors have found financial need to be a major motive. One ethnographic study of paid living kidney donors reported that many participants felt pressure from poverty or family circumstances and that stigma and dissatisfaction remained common after donation. More recent reviews of the Iranian system describe official compensation existing alongside additional negotiated payments from recipient to donor, preserving exactly the bargaining relationship that a tightly regulated system is supposed to tame.

The result is uncomfortable in the useful sense of the word. A compensated system can increase supply and still produce exploitation. A ban can protect people from commodification and still leave patients to die waiting. Neither side gets to own the moral vocabulary.

That is why Roth’s word is better than a simple claim that organ sales are “wrong.” Repugnance describes a constraint without pretending that the constraint ends the argument.

There is a second reason kidney markets make people uneasy: once money enters, the social meaning of the act changes.

A person who gives a kidney to a sibling is usually understood as making a gift. A stranger who gives through a nondirected donation program may be regarded as unusually altruistic. The recipient may feel gratitude, indebtedness, discomfort, or all three, but there is no price that purports to settle the account. Introduce a cash payment and the relationship becomes easier to classify economically and harder to classify socially. Was the donor generous? Employed? Desperate? Exploited? Rational? All of those labels can fit the same person at once.

This is more than squeamishness about money. Some things depend on not having a price because the absence of price is part of what the institution is trying to preserve. Votes are the clearest political example. A person may campaign for your vote, persuade you, flatter you, frighten you, or make policy promises designed to win you over. What the candidate may not ordinarily do is hand you twenty dollars in exchange for checking a box. The prohibition is not based on the belief that money would fail to influence voting. It is based on the opposite belief: money might influence it too effectively, in a way that would destroy the institution’s claim to equal political standing.

The same logic appears whenever we insist that one kind of value should not be translated cleanly into another. A place in a public school is not normally auctioned to the highest bidder because the institution is meant to allocate educational opportunity on grounds other than parental wealth. A criminal sentence is not supposed to be purchasable from the judge. A medal for military valor would mean something different if recipients simply paid the Treasury for one.

Price is powerful precisely because it compresses unlike motives into a common unit. That power is also the reason societies sometimes refuse to use it.

Economists have never been unanimous about the refusal.

In the same 2007 issue of the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* in which Roth examined repugnance, Gary Becker and Julio Jorge Elías made the opposing case directly. They argued that monetary incentives for organ donation could increase supply enough to eliminate large queues and reduce deaths among patients waiting for transplants. Their model treated the donor’s pain, risk, lost income, and other burdens as costs that a sufficiently high payment could compensate. They estimated that doing so would raise the total cost of transplantation only modestly relative to the benefits.

The argument is easy to caricature and therefore worth stating carefully. Becker and Elías were not saying that kidneys are morally identical to televisions. They were asking what follows if the existing ban itself has victims. When patients die because too few kidneys are donated, preserving a no-payment norm has a cost. If compensation can induce additional donors, then refusing to permit compensation is not neutral. It is a policy choice that affects who lives long enough to receive a transplant.

This is the counterargument every serious discussion of repugnant markets has to survive. A prohibition may defend dignity for potential sellers while imposing suffering on potential buyers. A moral rule can create a queue, and a queue can kill.

The usual response is that the market can be redesigned rather than simply unleashed. Instead of allowing rich recipients to bargain directly with poor sellers, a government or nonprofit intermediary could set a fixed compensation, screen donors, provide lifetime medical follow-up, prohibit side payments, and allocate kidneys through the same medical-priority rules used today. The recipient would not purchase “a kidney” from a named person. The system would compensate donation as a public contribution.

That proposal matters because it reveals that the true policy choice is rarely market versus no market. It is mechanism versus mechanism.

The United States already demonstrates this in the details of its organ law. “Valuable consideration” excludes reimbursement for donor expenses. In other words, lawmakers have accepted that money can enter the system when its purpose is to remove a financial penalty from donation. What remains prohibited is payment that turns the organ itself into the object of exchange.

That line is morally intuitive to many people and conceptually unstable under pressure. Suppose one donor loses $4,000 in wages and is reimbursed $4,000. Legal. Suppose another loses no wages but is offered $4,000 as recognition for undergoing the same surgery. Illegal if it constitutes valuable consideration for the organ. The physical act is almost identical. The financial transfer is identical. The legal meaning depends on what the payment is understood to be for.

Markets are full of such boundary work. We permit a lawyer to charge for preparing adoption papers but not to sell the child. We permit a fertility clinic to charge for medical services while states draw different lines around compensated surrogacy. We allow professional athletes to sell performances that would once have been condemned as improper commercialization of amateur sport. We reimburse blood donors in some contexts and insist on unpaid donation in others. The border between gift and sale is neither timeless nor self-enforcing.

Roth’s historical examples make this movement hard to ignore. Repugnance can fade. It can intensify. Institutions that once looked natural can become indefensible, while transactions once condemned can become mundane. This is one reason market designers cannot simply ask what would be efficient and stop there. They have to ask what the participants, lawmakers, and public will accept.

A mechanism that violates a community’s moral boundary will not become successful merely because an economist can prove it produces more total surplus.

That sounds like a concession to irrationality. Sometimes it is. Societies are perfectly capable of sustaining taboos that harm the people they are supposed to protect. But the reverse error is just as serious: assuming that every objection that cannot be expressed in a price is economically meaningless.

Consider the seller’s side of a kidney transaction. A standard model can represent surgical risk, recovery time, lost wages, perhaps even discomfort. It has more trouble representing the possibility that creating a legal market changes who is expected to sell. If kidney sales became ordinary, a creditor might not literally force a debtor onto an operating table, yet the existence of a saleable organ could enter the social arithmetic of desperation. Why have you not sold the asset available inside your own body? A family member might ask. A lender might think it. A welfare bureaucracy might someday be tempted to encode a version of it. What begins as an option can become an expectation.

This is one of the strongest arguments for treating some forms of market participation as more than an individual consumer choice. A right not to sell can be valuable even to someone who would, in a crisis, agree to sell.

That value is easy to miss because it does not appear in the transaction that actually occurs. It lives in the transactions that never have to be proposed.

Imagine two societies with identical wages, debts, and medical technology. In one, kidneys cannot legally be sold. In the other, a healthy adult can sell one through a regulated market for a large payment. The second society has created an asset the first society has not. On a household balance sheet, that looks like added wealth. A person in financial distress has one more option.

But an option can change bargaining even when nobody exercises it.

A relative deciding whether to lend money may know the kidney market exists. A spouse arguing about debt may know it. A creditor cannot legally seize the organ, but the debtor may still hear a version of the question: what have you done to solve this problem? A person declining to sell has to defend not merely the wish to keep both kidneys, but the refusal to convert an available body part into cash.

The market has therefore changed the baseline against which sacrifice is judged.

This does not prove that the market should be prohibited. Many valuable freedoms create pressures as well as opportunities. The ability to work more hours can become an expectation to work more hours. The ability to borrow can become an expectation to borrow. The ability to move for a job can make staying put look less reasonable to an employer. We do not ban every option that changes bargaining power.

The organ case is sharper because the option is irreversible and physically embodied. A person who sells a kidney cannot buy back the original state of having two kidneys later. The sale converts a temporary financial emergency into a permanent anatomical fact. That does not make the choice irrational. It does mean that the ordinary language of consumer options understates what is being exchanged.

A regulated compensation system would have to confront this pressure rather than assume that screening for formal consent has solved it. Independent donor advocates, cooling-off periods, fixed payments, bans on recipient bidding, and long-term follow-up can all reduce some forms of pressure. None can make the surrounding economic conditions disappear. The relevant policy question is not whether the donor said yes. It is what institutional environment made yes and no mean what they did.

That is a harder question than the simple market test of willingness to transact.

It is also a harder question than the simple prohibitionist claim that poverty invalidates consent. Poor people remain adults. They make consequential choices every day. A rule that says their financial circumstances make them categorically incapable of consenting can become paternalism dressed as protection.

The design problem is therefore to protect refusal without pretending choice is meaningless.

That is one reason the distinction between reimbursement and purchase matters beyond legal semantics. Reimbursement tries to remove a penalty for giving without creating a general-purpose asset that can be sold to solve unrelated financial problems. It says donation should not make the donor poorer. Compensation says donation may properly make the donor richer.

Those are not merely two payment levels. They are two theories about what role the body should be allowed to play in a household economy.

The logic is familiar outside transplantation. Bankruptcy law allows people to discharge some debts rather than contract themselves into permanent servitude. Labor law constrains the terms workers may accept even when both parties would sign. We do not permit a person to sell herself into slavery and then defend enforcement on the ground that the original signature was voluntary. The legal system sometimes limits contracts not because consent is irrelevant but because it believes certain forms of consent should not be enforceable.

Still, the kidney shortage remains.

This is where moral philosophy hands the problem back to engineering.

If we reject the auction, we need another allocation rule for the organs that do exist. If we also reject paid donation, we need another way to increase supply. We can encourage deceased donation. We can reimburse living donors’ costs. We can improve procurement. We can allow incompatible donor-recipient pairs to exchange with one another. We can build chains that transform one altruistic donation into several transplants. We can rank recipients by medical urgency, compatibility, waiting time, geography, expected benefit, or some combination.

Every one of those choices contains a value judgment.

A waiting list does not remove morality from allocation. It makes morality operational.

That is the deeper reason the phrase “money can’t buy everything” is both true and incomplete. Money cannot legally buy a kidney in the United States. But once money is excluded, the kidney still has to go to someone. The state has not escaped the act of choosing by refusing to auction the organ. It has accepted responsibility for designing the substitute.

The substitute is often less visible than a price. That makes it easier to mistake for nature.

A patient sees a number on a portal, a status in a registry, perhaps an estimate from a transplant center. Behind that number is a long argument about what should count. Time waiting used to matter differently than it does now. Geography has been repeatedly redesigned because a liver or kidney available on one side of a boundary could once generate very different access than an otherwise similar organ a few miles away. Pediatric patients, highly sensitized patients, people with rare blood types, and people facing particular medical risks may receive different forms of priority. These rules change because there is no neutral queue waiting to be discovered.

Someone writes the queue.

The same pattern will recur throughout this book. Schools do not charge families market-clearing tuition for scarce seats in a public system, so they need zones, lotteries, preferences, or matching algorithms. Governments do not auction vaccine appointments during a pandemic, so they need priority tiers and scheduling rules. Refugee slots are not sold to the families willing to pay the most, so agencies need criteria for vulnerability, admissibility, and placement. A city does not usually auction every subsidized apartment to whoever values it most in cash, because doing so would defeat the purpose of subsidy, so it builds eligibility rules and a lottery instead.

The prohibition on price is never the end of the design. It is the beginning.

This is also why arguments over “markets” and “government” can miss what is actually happening. A kidney-allocation system is not a market in the ordinary retail sense, but it is also not the absence of a market. It is an engineered allocation institution in which law determines what may be exchanged, medicine determines what is feasible, ethics determines what is acceptable, and an algorithm determines who receives what when supply falls short.

Roth’s great practical insight was to take those constraints seriously rather than wishing them away. Repugnance is not a rounding error. If people will not accept organ auctions, then the intellectually interesting problem is not to keep announcing that auctions clear markets. It is to design something that works under the constraint.

That does not mean every repugnance deserves preservation. The history Roth surveyed is a warning against that conclusion. Some taboos were cruel. Some were irrational. Some protected entrenched power. Some restrictions weakened when people discovered that the feared transaction could be regulated without producing the harms they imagined. A society should be capable of revisiting its moral boundaries.

But revisiting is not the same as assuming that price should win by default.

The organ debate remains open because both sides can identify a genuine harm the other side risks understating. Supporters of compensation can point to preventable deaths and years spent on dialysis. Opponents can point to exploitation, commodification, and the concentration of selling among people with the fewest alternatives. Iran supplies evidence for both arguments at once: more transplants, and a persistent set of concerns about how financial desperation structures the donor pool.

A good allocation system has to live inside that tension rather than declaring victory over it.

For the rest of this book, that tension is the starting condition. We will encounter systems in which price is forbidden, systems in which it is merely politically unacceptable, systems in which price exists but fails to clear the market fast enough, and systems in which designers deliberately mix price with queues, lotteries, and priorities. The surface details will change. The underlying problem will not.

There is not enough of something people want.

The simplest economic tool for rationing it has been limited or rejected.

Now somebody has to decide who goes first.

That somebody may be a clerk, a physician, a legislature, a school district, a company, or a few lines of code. The decision may be explicit or inherited. It may be defensible or absurd. But it will exist.

A society can refuse to put a price on a kidney. It cannot refuse to allocate one.
