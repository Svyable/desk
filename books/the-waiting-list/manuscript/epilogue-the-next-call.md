# Epilogue: The Next Call

The phone still rings at two in the morning. No algorithm discussed in this book has changed that, and none was meant to. Scarcity is not a design flaw. It is the condition every mechanism in these chapters was built to survive.

What has changed, over and over, in the stories this book has followed, is who answers the phone, how long they waited for it to ring, and whether the system that made them wait can explain itself when someone asks why. Alvin Roth did not invent a machine that manufactures kidneys. Atila Abdulkadiroğlu and Parag Pathak did not invent new school seats in Boston or New York. Kirk Bansak and his collaborators did not create a country willing to resettle more refugees. Every mechanism in this book operates inside a fixed, often cruelly fixed, supply. What changes is whether the allocation of that supply reflects genuine priorities, openly stated and consistently applied, or reflects instead whoever had the sharpest lawyer, the fastest script, the best information, or simply the good luck to ask at the right moment.

That distinction is not trivial, and it is not, in the end, a technical one. It is a moral argument wearing the clothing of an operations problem.

Consider what a well-built matching system actually promises, stripped of the mathematics. It promises that the rule applied to you is the same rule applied to your neighbor. It promises that telling the truth about what you want will not be used against you, a property market designers call strategy-proofness and most everyone else would simply call honesty being safe. It promises that no two participants, left alone in a room, could improve their situation by secretly swapping places, a property called stability that turns out to be the mathematical signature of an arrangement nobody has an incentive to sabotage. None of that guarantees enough kidneys, enough school seats, enough GPUs, or enough housing vouchers. It guarantees only that the shortage, whatever its size, will be shared according to a rule everyone can inspect rather than a scramble only the fastest or best-connected can win.

That is a smaller promise than it sounds, and a much larger one than most of the systems in this book actually deliver.

The chapters on kidney exchange and the medical residency match describe genuine successes, decades in the making, tested against real placements and real lives, revised when they failed and defended when they worked. The chapters on GPU rationing, refugee resettlement pilots, and algorithmic content and delivery queues describe something earlier and rawer: institutions encountering, in real time and often for the first time, problems that health economists and market designers spent two generations solving in other domains, and discovering that money alone does not reliably solve them. Some of those institutions will eventually build something as considered as the kidney exchange network. Others will simply patch the symptom, quietly, and move on to the next emergency, leaving the underlying allocation as arbitrary as they found it.

The reader who has come this far has one advantage that the designers of the earliest kidney exchanges, the earliest school-choice algorithms, and the earliest vaccine allocation frameworks did not have: a record of what worked, what nearly worked, and what failed in ways that were entirely foreseeable in hindsight. Deferred acceptance was not obvious in 1962; it is well understood in 2027. A single hospital's manual waiting list nearly collapsed the residency match in the early 1990s; a similar failure in a 2026 cloud-compute allocation queue is not a mystery anyone needs to relearn from first principles. The tools exist. What remains scarce, in an odd echo of the book's own subject, is the institutional will to use them before the crisis forces the question, rather than after.

It would be convenient to end here with a formula, a checklist any institution could apply to fix its own waiting list. The honest ending is closer to a caution. Every mechanism in this book was hard-won, argued over, tested against edge cases its designers did not anticipate, and revised more than once after real people were hurt by a version that looked correct on a whiteboard. A hospital administrator who reads the chapter on kidney exchange and concludes that any algorithm beats any queue has learned the wrong lesson. A technology company that reads the chapter on the marriage algorithm and assumes stability alone guarantees fairness has learned the wrong lesson too. The discipline that runs through every success story here is slower and less quotable than that: build the rule in public, test it against the incentive to lie, watch for the participants it quietly fails, and be willing to tear it up when it does.

Somewhere tonight, a different phone will ring. A hospital will call a name near the top of a kidney list built, this time, from decades of accumulated correction rather than improvisation. A school district will mail an assignment letter generated by an algorithm that no longer punishes an honest first choice. A refugee family will learn which city has been matched to their specific mix of language, skill, and family ties, rather than assigned by whichever caseworker had a free hour that week. A researcher will find that the GPU queue she has been refreshing since midnight finally moved, for reasons that a public allocation policy, somewhere, can actually explain.

Not every one of those calls will bring good news. Scarcity does not disappear because the list distributing it grew wiser. But the difference between a defensible no and an arbitrary one is most of what this book has been arguing is worth building, line by line, list by list, before the next shortage arrives and somebody has to decide, on far too little notice, who gets it.
