# School Choice, Rebuilt

In the old New York City high school admissions system, getting several offers could be a problem.

That sounds backwards. An eighth grader with three offers has more options than one with none. But the offers did not arrive inside a single coordinated system. Schools made decisions separately. A student might be admitted by several schools while another student received nothing. Those multiple offers then had to be accepted or declined, vacancies had to be filled, and the whole process lurched through additional rounds.

The congestion was not a metaphor. In the system that New York City wanted to replace, only about fifty thousand students initially received offers, and roughly seventeen thousand of them received more than one. When the process ended, around thirty thousand students had been assigned to a school that was not on their submitted choice list.

The city was trying to place more than ninety thousand entering high school students every year.

A queue had become a traffic jam.

The problem was not merely that there were too few seats at the most desired schools. No algorithm could manufacture more of those. The problem was that the admissions process itself was wasting information and creating incentives that made families behave strategically.

A family deciding how to rank schools was not simply answering the question, Where does my child most want to go?

It also had to ask, Where does my child have a realistic chance?

That second question changed the first.

If a school cared about how highly an applicant ranked it, then putting an ambitious first choice at the top could endanger a safer option below it. A family that understood this might shade its list toward schools it believed were attainable. A family that did not understand it might submit an entirely sincere ranking and be punished for the honesty.

This is one of the stranger forms of inequality an allocation system can create. The scarce thing is a school seat, but the hidden advantage is procedural knowledge.

Two families can want the same schools and have children with the same formal priority. One has spent weeks learning how the mechanism behaves. The other believes that a preference form is asking for preferences.

They are not playing the same game.

The New York City redesign that began in 2003 brought economists Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Parag Pathak, and Alvin Roth into a system with exactly this problem. Roth later described being contacted by the city’s Department of Education about designing a centralized admissions process for the more than ninety thousand students entering ninth grade each year. The economists adapted the logic of deferred acceptance to a school market with capacities, school priorities, student rankings, ties, specialized-school exceptions, and other institutional details that did not appear in Gale and Shapley’s clean classroom model.

The essential change was simple enough to explain to a parent.

Rank schools in the order you actually prefer them.

Under a student-proposing deferred-acceptance system, a student is first considered at the school she ranks highest. If that school has more applicants than seats, it tentatively holds the highest-priority applicants and rejects the rest. Rejected students move to their next choice. A school holding an applicant can later replace that applicant if a higher-priority student arrives in a subsequent round. The process continues until no more rejections are necessary.

The word tentatively does most of the work.

Under a system that awards seats permanently too early, a popular school can fill before the mechanism has had a chance to compare the full set of possibilities. Deferred acceptance delays final commitment long enough to coordinate choices.

It is the same insight that appeared in the medical Match. An offer does not need to become final at the instant it appears.

In New York, the redesign attacked two failures at once.

The first was congestion. Uncoordinated offers meant some students occupied several potential seats during the process while others remained unmatched. The new centralized procedure dramatically reduced the number of students who ended the main process without an assignment to one of their listed schools. Later research comparing the old and new systems found that one-third of applicants had been unassigned after the old main round and subsequently placed administratively. The coordinated mechanism captured most of the welfare gains available from moving away from a purely neighborhood-based assignment toward a system that actually used student preferences.

The second failure was strategic risk.

A strategy-proof mechanism, in the technical sense relevant here, is designed so that a student cannot improve her outcome by misrepresenting her preference ordering, assuming the mechanism’s stated conditions hold. That is not the same as saying nobody has anything strategic to think about. Families still care about where to live, which programs to apply to, what tests to take, whether to attend an audition, how to satisfy eligibility rules, and how many schools to investigate. But the ranking itself becomes safer to answer honestly.

This sounds like a modest administrative improvement until you consider who pays the cost when a mechanism rewards gaming.

A sophisticated parent can hire advice, study prior cutoffs, trade rumors with other families, inspect enrollment numbers, and infer which desired school is too competitive to rank first. Another parent can be working a second shift during the information session.

A mechanism that makes truthful ranking safe cannot equalize those households. It can, however, remove one particular tax on the household with less time and less procedural fluency.

Boston made the same problem easier to see because its old assignment rule became famous enough to lend its name to a family of mechanisms.

Under the old Boston mechanism, first choices were processed first. Students who listed a school first were considered for its seats according to the school’s priority rules. Once those seats were filled, they were gone. Then second choices were processed for whatever seats remained, then third choices, and so on.

This gave the ranking number itself real force.

Suppose Maya prefers Harbor Academy, then Franklin, then Westview. Harbor is extremely competitive. Franklin is also popular.

If Maya lists Harbor first and loses, Franklin may already have filled with students who listed Franklin first. Her sincere ranking can leave her with Westview or nothing.

Another family might understand that and list Franklin first even though Harbor is the child’s true favorite.

The mechanism has now changed the meaning of the application.

First choice no longer means first choice.

It means something closer to first choice after taking account of admissions odds and the penalty for aiming too high.

That can look like savvy behavior when done by a well-informed household. In aggregate it means the school district is collecting distorted data.

The list of reported preferences no longer tells the district what families want. It tells the district what families think they can safely ask for.

Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak, Roth, and Tayfun Sönmez studied years of Boston application data and found evidence of both kinds of behavior. Some families appeared attentive to school capacity and the strategic consequences of their rankings. Others submitted lists that exposed them to avoidable risk. The researchers identified students who went unassigned even though one of their listed schools could have taken them had they arranged the same choices differently.

That finding mattered because it changed the fairness argument.

Economists often discuss manipulation as an efficiency problem: strategic behavior makes the mechanism harder to analyze or leads to poorer assignments. In Boston, manipulation also created a distributional problem. A system that can be gamed gives an advantage to participants who understand the game.

The Boston School Committee voted in July 2005 to replace the old mechanism with deferred acceptance.

It did not do so because deferred acceptance guaranteed every child a coveted seat. It could not.

The reform changed the procedure for allocating a fixed and unequal set of schools.

This distinction is the entire chapter.

School-choice algorithms are unusually vulnerable to being credited with accomplishments they cannot produce and blamed for injustices they did not create.

If a city has a small number of schools that families regard as excellent, those schools will be oversubscribed under almost any assignment mechanism. A lottery will leave disappointed families. A neighborhood rule will capitalize access into housing prices. An exam will make preparation valuable. A preference-based match will still reject students from schools they prefer.

Scarcity survives the redesign.

So does geography.

So does segregation.

So do differences in school quality, transportation, disability access, language programs, selective admissions, sibling priorities, and the ability of families to gather information about what each school actually offers.

A truthful mechanism can tell a parent, You do not need to lie about which school you prefer.

It cannot tell the parent, Every option available to you is equally good.

The temptation to confuse those claims is strong because algorithms arrive with the language of optimization.

There is an input. There is a procedure. There is an output. The output can be measured. The process can be compared with an alternative.

Education is not so tidy.

A school assignment can affect commute time, peer groups, extracurricular opportunities, course offerings, safety, social networks, and a family’s daily schedule. Researchers can estimate effects, but those outcomes do not collapse naturally into one common score. Parents disagree about what they value. Schools change. Children change faster.

The redesign therefore had to be judged first on the failure it was designed to fix.

Did it coordinate offers better?

Did it reduce the need for strategic misrepresentation?

Did it reduce the number of children falling out of the main process and into administrative placement?

On those narrower questions, the case was substantial.

There is a further reason to separate coordination from algorithm choice. Imagine keeping every school’s admissions priorities exactly as they were but forcing all offers into a single clearing process. Even before debating the fine points of deferred acceptance, the city has removed one source of waste: a student can no longer occupy several final offers while other students wait for those seats to be released. The system gains a common clock and a common ledger of who is tentatively holding what.

That sounds bureaucratic because it is bureaucratic. It is also a form of market design.

A surprising amount of institutional failure comes from decisions being locally sensible and globally incompatible. One school protects itself by making an early offer. Another does the same. A family protects itself by holding every offer until it knows what else is available. None of those decisions is absurd in isolation. Together they create congestion.

Coordination changes the incentives before the matching formula does anything clever. A school can wait because other schools are waiting inside the same process. A family can release lower choices because the mechanism carries its higher choices forward. The central system becomes a temporary custodian of uncertainty.

That is why later welfare work on New York is so important to the interpretation of the reform. Much of the measured gain came from coordinated assignment itself, not from discovering one mathematically perfect tie-breaking rule. This is a useful restraint on the usual story of algorithmic improvement. Sometimes the big invention is not a smarter ranking function. It is an institution that makes separately reasonable actors stop stepping on one another.

The distinction also clarifies what the mechanism cannot know. A rank list records an ordering among the options a family investigated and chose to submit. It does not record the schools the family never heard about, could not reach by transit, ruled out because an information session conflicted with work, or considered inaccessible for a child with a particular need. Truthful reporting inside the mechanism does not mean the preference data arrived from a level field.

Nor is administrative placement merely a statistical residue. For a family, falling out of the main match changes the nature of the process. Instead of choosing among ranked options through the same mechanism as everyone else, the family may enter a later stage with fewer seats remaining and less control over the result. Reducing that spillover is therefore more than cleaning up an operations metric. It preserves the meaning of preference for more participants.

The point is not that centralization is always better. A single clearinghouse can make a mistake at scale. Central rules can flatten local knowledge. A mechanism that is difficult to contest can convert many small, visible disputes into one opaque system. Coordination earns its value only if the institution also preserves clear priorities, usable appeals, and enough transparency that families can understand what the common process is doing.

New York and Boston are compelling cases precisely because the old systems supplied a concrete failure to repair. The designers did not begin with the premise that centralization was virtuous. They began with duplicated offers, distorted rankings, avoidable nonassignments, and families being rewarded for guessing how the machinery worked.

The reform could then be judged against those failures.

The New York system also revealed a less visible design choice: tie-breaking.

A school may have clear priorities—perhaps for continuing students, siblings, geographic zones, or program eligibility—but still face many students who are equal within a priority class. If there are fewer seats than equally ranked students, somebody needs to win the tie.

One approach gives each student a single random number used across schools. Another can generate separate lotteries for separate schools. These choices sound procedural, but they can change outcomes and incentives.

Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak, and Roth later analyzed the New York design in detail and showed that the tradeoff was not reducible to one magic property. The city cared about stability, truthful incentives, and efficiency. With ties in school priorities, those goals can conflict. Their analysis favored a common tie-breaker within the student-proposing deferred-acceptance framework because of its incentive properties, while also documenting possible efficiency losses.

This is where the slogan that algorithms remove discretion collapses again.

Someone decides the priority structure.

Someone decides how ties are broken.

Someone decides whether a student can rank five schools or twelve or every school in the city.

Someone decides whether specialized schools sit inside the common system or outside it.

Someone decides how appeals work, how late applicants enter, whether siblings receive priority, and whether geographic preference is attached to residence, school zone, borough, or something else.

Deferred acceptance can make the ranking stage safer. It cannot answer those policy questions.

Nor can strategy-proofness eliminate strategic behavior outside the form.

If access to a school depends partly on an address, families may choose addresses strategically if they can. If admissions depend on test scores, families with resources may purchase preparation. If a program requires an audition, families may invest in coaching. If a school’s reputation rises, application patterns will change. If a district publishes historical demand, families will react to it even when truthful ranking remains optimal.

The algorithm protects one margin of honesty.

That is valuable precisely because it is limited.

It also explains why later debates over school assignment can sound as though participants are talking past one another.

A market designer may say, correctly, that deferred acceptance prevents a family from being punished for ranking a difficult school first.

A parent may reply, also correctly, that the system still sends her child somewhere she does not want.

An equity advocate may point out, again correctly, that truthful preference reporting does not undo residential segregation or unequal access to information about programs.

Those statements do not contradict one another.

They describe different layers of the institution.

The mechanism allocates seats subject to policy.

Policy defines priorities subject to politics.

Politics operates inside a city whose neighborhoods, schools, budgets, and transportation networks were shaped long before the algorithm arrived.

The reason the New York and Boston reforms matter is not that they solved the larger system. It is that they show what happens when one layer is made less perverse.

Before the change, a parent could lose because she misunderstood how to lie.

After the change, disappointment remained, but that particular failure became less central.

There is a moral difference between being rejected from your first-choice school because other students had higher priority and being rejected because the mechanism punished you for telling the truth about what your first choice was.

Both outcomes hurt. Only one is an avoidable design flaw.

The distinction extends far beyond schools.

Whenever an allocation system asks people to report preferences, the designer should ask what the reporting rule teaches them to do. Does it reward honesty? Does it reward tactical understatement? Does it make people hoard options because releasing one is dangerous? Does it favor participants who hire experts to decode the mechanism? Does it collect the information it claims to collect?

A form is not neutral merely because everyone receives the same form.

That is the deeper lesson of the Boston mechanism.

The old system did not explicitly say that affluent, connected, or quantitatively sophisticated parents deserved a better chance. It did not have to. It created a game in which sophistication had value.

Rules distribute advantages even when the rulebook never names the advantaged group.

The market-design response was not to pretend those differences vanished. It was to design a mechanism in which one kind of sophistication mattered less.

The result is easy to understate because nothing about the physical school system changes when the code changes.

No classroom is added.

No teacher is hired.

No school building is renovated.

Yet tens of thousands of decisions can become better coordinated because offers are held rather than prematurely finalized and because a family can rank a dream school without sacrificing a safer school merely for having told the truth.

That is institution design at its most characteristic. It creates value by removing a self-inflicted constraint.

It is also why the reform deserves neither technocratic worship nor cynical dismissal.

The cynical version says the algorithm is irrelevant because inequality remains.

The worshipful version says the algorithm made school choice fair.

Neither survives contact with the mechanism.

The redesign solved a precise problem: strategic manipulation and uncoordinated assignment were causing avoidable mismatches. It did not solve the supply of desirable schools or the politics of admission priorities. Later research on New York found that coordinating offers accounted for most of the gains from the reform; fine-tuning the algorithm mattered less than moving from the fragmented old process to a coordinated one.

That is a useful correction to the way technological reform is often discussed.

The largest gain may not come from a more intelligent algorithm.

It may come from getting everyone into the same room.

New York’s old process behaved as if each school could make admissions decisions independently and the pieces would eventually sort themselves out. But every offer affected every other offer. A student sitting on three acceptances was not merely lucky. During the process, she was also occupying decision space that three other students might need.

Centralization exposed the interdependence.

Deferred acceptance gave the system a disciplined way to handle it.

The distinction will matter in the next group of markets, because the time available for coordination is about to collapse.

A school district can spend weeks processing ranked lists. A residency clearinghouse can run a national Match on a schedule known months in advance. Kidney exchanges can search pools and plan operations.

A ride request appears on a phone and expects an answer in seconds.

The old questions do not disappear when the clock speeds up.

Who gets priority?

Who gets to choose?

What information does the mechanism reveal?

Who benefits from understanding the rules?

The next matching systems would answer those questions continuously, invisibly, and at commercial scale.
