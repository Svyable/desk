# Chapter 20 — How to Build a Better Waiting List

## Purpose

Chapter 20 is a synthesis chapter, not a new empirical case study. Its job is to extract design principles that are defensible across the mechanisms already documented in the book without pretending that one allocation rule is universally optimal.

## Claims supported by the existing research trail

- Allocation rules must be evaluated against explicit objectives; mathematical properties do not choose the institution's ethical priorities.
- Eligibility and priority are distinct design layers. A place in an eligible pool or waitlist is not necessarily an immediate entitlement to the scarce resource.
- First-come-first-served, lotteries, priority rules, matching mechanisms, and exchange mechanisms solve different structural problems. Complexity should be justified by a real failure mode rather than treated as sophistication for its own sake.
- Strategy resistance matters because mechanisms that reward misrepresentation should expect participants to misrepresent. The school-choice material is the cleanest case in the manuscript.
- Allocation reform cannot substitute for supply. Kidney exchange, school assignment, housing lotteries, and cloud reservations can improve distribution without eliminating the underlying shortage.
- Waiting time is a policy variable, not a morally neutral fact; it can reward endurance while also preserving unequal access to information, application timing, and the ability to remain in a queue.
- Discretion and rigid rules both create failure modes. Constrained, logged exceptions and appeals are preferable to either unrecorded discretion or mechanisms incapable of handling outlier cases.
- Audit should examine outcomes and workflows as well as code or formal rules. Chapter 19's NIST, EEOC, NYC, EU, and Colorado material establishes the distinction between compliance evidence and broader effectiveness.
- Allocation systems require periodic re-evaluation because demand, supply, law, participant behavior, and operating environments change.

## Foundational sources already used elsewhere in the book

1. David Gale and Lloyd S. Shapley, “College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage,” *American Mathematical Monthly* 69(1), 1962. Establishes stable matching and the importance of mechanism structure.
2. Alvin E. Roth, “Repugnance as a Constraint on Markets,” *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 21(3), 2007. Supports the book's broader claim that price allocation is institutionally constrained and alternative mechanisms must then do the allocating.
3. Alvin E. Roth and Elliott Peranson, “The Redesign of the Matching Market for American Physicians,” *American Economic Review* 89(4), 1999. Demonstrates that a live allocation mechanism can be redesigned around strategic and operational constraints rather than treated as fixed.
4. Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Parag A. Pathak, and Alvin E. Roth, work on New York City and Boston school choice. Supports the discussion of mechanisms that punish truthful preference reporting and the value of strategy-resistant redesign.
5. Alvin E. Roth, Tayfun Sönmez, and M. Utku Ünver, “Kidney Exchange,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 119(2), 2004. Shows how incompatibility can be converted into feasible exchange without introducing a cash price.
6. NIST, *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)*, 2023. Used in Chapter 19 to support ongoing governance, mapping, measurement, and management rather than a one-time pass/fail audit model.

## Falsification / counterevidence

The chapter should fail if it claims a universal recipe.

Counterexamples preserved in the manuscript show why none exists:

- First-come-first-served can be the right mechanism when stakes are low, participants are similarly situated, and administrative simplicity dominates more elaborate fairness goals.
- A lottery can reduce speed advantages while still ignoring differences in need or compatibility.
- A stable or strategy-resistant match can solve manipulation while leaving unequal supply or upstream priorities untouched.
- Human discretion can incorporate context that formal rules miss; formalization can also make decisions more consistent and auditable.
- Software can create traceability that informal human processes lack, so the relevant comparison is not “algorithm versus human” but mechanism versus mechanism.
- Better allocation can become political cover for failing to expand supply.

## Guardrails for prose

- Do not turn the chapter into a numbered management framework in the manuscript, even though the argument contains recurring design principles.
- Keep the distinctions concrete and tied to cases already earned earlier in the book.
- Avoid claiming that mechanism design removes politics; the stronger claim is that it makes otherwise hidden policy choices more explicit and testable.
- End on institutional humility rather than a promise of optimization: scarcity guarantees disappointment, but a better rule can make disappointment less arbitrary, more explainable, and more revisable.
