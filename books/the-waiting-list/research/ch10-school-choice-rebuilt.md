# Chapter 10 research note — School Choice, Rebuilt

Checked September 2, 2026.

## Claim under test

Chapter 10 argues that coordinated school-assignment mechanisms can reduce strategic ranking, duplicated offers, and congestion relative to fragmented application systems, while still leaving the underlying priority rules politically contestable.

The claim is supported by the New York City and Boston redesign literature. The main discipline is to separate gains from coordination and strategy-proofness from claims about broader educational equity.

## Primary research anchors

Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak, and Roth's account of the New York City high-school match documents the pre-reform process, including congestion caused by multiple offers and a large number of students assigned outside their stated choices, and explains the move to a coordinated matching mechanism.

Source: Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Parag A. Pathak, and Alvin E. Roth, “The New York City High School Match,” *American Economic Review* Papers and Proceedings, 2005: https://doi.org/10.1257/000282805774670167

The Boston mechanism literature shows why manipulable school-choice rules create unequal burdens: families who understand the strategic incentives can rank differently from families who simply state sincere preferences. Boston adopted a deferred-acceptance-style mechanism in 2005 partly to reduce that strategic disadvantage.

Source: Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Parag A. Pathak, Alvin E. Roth, and Tayfun Sönmez, “Changing the Boston School Choice Mechanism,” NBER Working Paper 11965, 2006: https://www.nber.org/papers/w11965

Later work on the NYC mechanism makes the tradeoff explicit: strategy-proofness, stability, tie-breaking, and efficiency do not collapse into one objective. Improving one property can alter another.

Source: Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Parag A. Pathak, and Alvin E. Roth, “Strategy-Proofness versus Efficiency in Matching with Indifferences: Redesigning the NYC High School Match,” *American Economic Review* 99(5), 2009: https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.99.5.1954

Follow-up welfare analysis attributes substantial gains to coordinating offers rather than to a magical property of one algorithm alone. This distinction matters because an institution can benefit simply by replacing fragmented offers with one coherent assignment process.

Source: Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Nikhil Agarwal, and Parag A. Pathak, “The Welfare Effects of Coordinated Assignment: Evidence from the NYC HS Match,” NBER Working Paper 21046, 2017: https://www.nber.org/papers/w21046

## Counterevidence and falsification

A strategy-proof assignment rule cannot make school quality equal, create additional seats at oversubscribed schools, or determine which priorities deserve moral weight. Neighborhood preference, sibling preference, screened admissions, set-asides, and tie-breaking rules can all produce distributional consequences upstream of the matching algorithm.

The chapter would be overstated if it claimed that deferred acceptance by itself improved educational outcomes. The strongest evidence concerns allocation process, strategic incentives, coordination, and assignment welfare—not a universal causal claim about later student achievement.

The mechanism also depends on families being able to submit usable preferences and understand the process. Strategy-proofness reduces the cost of sophisticated gaming but does not eliminate unequal information, counseling, or access to attractive schools.

## Prose boundaries

- Distinguish coordination gains from gains caused by any one algorithmic property.
- Do not equate strategy-proofness with overall fairness.
- Keep seat scarcity and school-quality inequality separate from assignment design.
- Preserve the countercase that local priorities can be legitimate even when they reduce a system's formal symmetry.

## Readiness judgment

The chapter's core institutional claims are well supported by peer-reviewed and working-paper evidence from the actual redesigns. No factual correction is required from this check; the main readiness boundary is to keep mechanism improvement distinct from claims about educational equality.
