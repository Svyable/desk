# Chapter 5 — Ticketing evidence note

Research checked 2026-09-03 for “Scalpers, Bots, and the Ticketmaster Problem.” This note separates facts that can support the chapter from allegations that should remain attributed.

## Eras Tour presale

Ticketmaster’s November 19, 2022 postmortem says more than 3.5 million people registered for the Taylor Swift Verified Fan presale, about 1.5 million received codes, and roughly 2 million were wait-listed. Ticketmaster attributed the failure to unprecedented demand, including bot attacks and traffic from people without codes, and reported 3.5 billion system requests—four times its previous peak. These are company-reported figures, useful for describing scale but not independent proof of the causes Ticketmaster assigned.

The Senate Judiciary Committee’s January 24, 2023 hearing treated the presale failure as a prompt for a broader competition inquiry. Chairman Dick Durbin’s opening statement explicitly connected the hours-long virtual queues to concerns about concentration in live ticketing. Hearing exchanges also preserved disagreement over causation: Live Nation described a “battle of the bots,” while competing ticketing executive Jerry Mickelson argued that bot mitigation was itself part of a ticketing platform’s responsibility. The chapter should present that dispute rather than convert either side’s testimony into settled fact.

## Bots and enforcement

The Better Online Ticket Sales Act of 2016 prohibits circumvention of online ticket-purchase security measures and purchase limits for covered events and gives the Federal Trade Commission enforcement authority. In January 2021 the FTC announced its first cases under the Act. The agency alleged that three brokers used automated ticket-buying software, IP-concealment tools, hundreds of fictitious Ticketmaster accounts, and multiple credit cards to evade limits, acquiring more than 150,000 tickets. Proposed judgments exceeded $31 million, with payments reduced to about $3.7 million because of inability to pay.

That enforcement record supports a narrow claim: sophisticated circumvention was real and commercially meaningful even after Congress outlawed it. It does not establish that bots explain every high-demand sellout, nor that technical filtering alone can solve scarcity when legitimate demand exceeds supply.

## Competition and the primary/secondary market

On May 23, 2024 the U.S. Department of Justice and state attorneys general filed a civil antitrust action against Live Nation Entertainment and Ticketmaster. The complaint alleged monopolization and exclusionary conduct across concert promotion, large venues, primary ticketing, and related markets. Those allegations should remain attributed as allegations rather than rewritten as adjudicated findings.

The procedural posture changed in 2026. DOJ’s case page records a notice of settlement, followed by a June 12, 2026 stipulation and proposed final judgment and a June 29, 2026 competitive impact statement. The proposed judgment remains subject to the Antitrust Procedures and Penalties Act process; the existence of a settlement proposal is not the same thing as a court finding that every allegation in the 2024 complaint was proven.

The case is relevant because Chapter 5 is not only about a website queue. Ticket allocation sits inside a vertically connected industry in which venue contracts, promotion, primary ticketing, fees, resale, and artist choices can affect what inventory reaches a queue and on what terms. A technically perfect queue cannot answer the upstream question of how many seats are offered, at what initial price, with what holds, and through which channels.

## Counterevidence and falsification

The strongest counterargument is that resale prices reveal demand suppressed by an underpriced primary sale. If a face-value ticket is deliberately priced below what many buyers will pay, scarcity rents do not disappear; they can accrue to lucky early buyers, brokers, platforms, or scalpers. Dynamic pricing and authorized resale can therefore be defended as ways to move those rents toward artists and rights-holders while reducing arbitrage opportunities.

That defense does not settle the policy question. Price discrimination can reduce some forms of queue gaming while creating affordability and legitimacy problems of its own. Nor should the chapter imply that every reseller is a bot operator: lawful secondary markets also include ordinary buyers reselling tickets they can no longer use.

A useful falsification test for the chapter’s mechanism-design argument is whether stronger identity verification and bot enforcement materially improve access when the underlying event remains heavily underpriced and oversubscribed. If resale premiums and access disparities remain large even with effective bot suppression, the bottleneck is scarcity and pricing design, not merely queue security.
