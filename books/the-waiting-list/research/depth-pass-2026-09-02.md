# Depth pass — shorter-chapter expansion plan

Checked September 2, 2026 after the full-book readiness audit.

## Purpose

The full-book review identified exact chapter-length verification as the remaining substantive completion gate. Repository metadata show several numbered chapters with materially smaller manuscript files than the book's longer chapters. File size is not a word count, so this note does not claim that any chapter fails the 3,000-word floor. It records the best evidence-led expansion work to do if local counting confirms a shortfall, and it prevents a future pass from padding merely to clear a number.

## Chapter 8 — Kidneys Without Cash

The existing chapter already explains two-way exchange, simultaneity, nondirected-donor chains, bridge donors, chain endings, and the unequal endowment created by arriving with a willing donor. If more depth is required, the strongest addition is operational rather than rhetorical: show how a mathematically feasible exchange becomes a logistical object involving cross-center testing, surgery timing, kidney or donor travel, and the possibility that a chain fails without invalidating the design. The existing Rees et al. chain evidence and National Kidney Registry material are sufficient anchors.

Do not add another general explanation of compatibility or another restatement that exchange does not create kidneys. The chapter already makes both points repeatedly enough.

## Chapter 9 — A Nobel for a Marriage That Never Happened

If local counting shows a shortfall, deepen the theory-to-practice distinction behind the 2012 prize. The useful addition is not more Nobel ceremony. It is the institutional path by which Gale and Shapley's abstract stability result became a design language for markets that do not use prices, while preserving the credit boundary recorded in the dedicated Chapter 9 research note: the prize recognized Shapley and Roth, not every contributor to matching theory or every later implementer.

A second useful lane is to make the limits of stability concrete. Stable does not mean equal, welfare-maximizing, strategy-proof for both sides, or uniquely determined. That material would strengthen the book's recurring warning that a mathematical property is not a moral verdict.

## Chapter 11 — The Uber Problem

The existing chapter is strongest when it distinguishes visible price from invisible dispatch. If more depth is required, add one sustained comparison between a legible physical taxi queue and a dynamic ride-hailing allocation system. The point should be a real tradeoff: a crude queue can expose its ordering rule to participants, while a dynamic system can use geography and predicted demand more efficiently but make individual outcomes harder to audit.

The dedicated Chapter 11 evidence note already supports the relevant boundaries. Uber's public marketplace pages can establish the company's stated pricing concepts, while Yan et al. supports the independent claim that pricing and matching are coupled design levers. Do not infer proprietary production weights, claim that surge always clears shortages, or turn a modeled objective function into a description of Uber's exact dispatch code.

The chapter can also earn depth by following one allocation decision through its downstream consequences: assigning a driver changes not just the current pickup but the location of future supply. That is more useful than adding another list of possible platform objectives.

## Chapter 13 — The Vaccine Line

If this chapter is short, deepen the distinction between priority categories on paper and throughput in practice. A priority rule can be ethically defensible yet fail operationally when appointment access, eligibility proof, geography, supply cadence, or expiring doses create a second allocation layer. The chapter should preserve the countercase that simple age thresholds and broad categories can be administratively valuable precisely because finer-grained need scores are difficult to verify quickly at population scale.

Use only the chapter's existing primary public-health guidance and documented rollout evidence unless a fresh factual claim requires a new source. Avoid hindsight that treats early-pandemic information as if policymakers possessed later knowledge.

## Order of work

1. Run the repository's actual local word-count/catalog tooling when a shell is available.
2. Expand only chapters that are below the repository floor or materially underdeveloped despite clearing it.
3. Prefer Chapters 11 and 9 first if both are short: each has an underused conceptual boundary already supported by dedicated evidence notes.
4. Recount after each coherent revision batch.
5. Promote the book only after every numbered chapter and the qualifying manuscript total satisfy the current standard.

## Falsification rule

If exact local counts show that these chapters already clear the floor and the total manuscript clears 65,000 words, this plan is not a mandate to lengthen them. The minimum is a gate, not a target for padding. In that case, close the readiness record with the measured counts and proceed to the remaining mechanical finalization checks.
