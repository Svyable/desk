# Chapter 18 research — The Line for College

## Core claim

Selective college admissions is not a single merit ranking. It is an allocation mechanism operating under scarcity, institutional goals, legal constraints, applicant strategy, and a growing volume of applications. The most useful chapter should resist the metaphor of one national line and instead show how colleges construct classes from many overlapping queues and preference systems.

The mechanism-design question is not whether admissions can become perfectly objective. It is which criteria are allowed to matter, how visible those criteria are, and whether the institution can explain why two highly qualified applicants receive different outcomes when there are far more plausible candidates than seats.

## Current verified evidence — September 2026

- **Application volume continues to rise faster than the number of applicants.** Common App reported that through February 1, 2026, 1,401,214 first-year applicants had submitted 9,188,630 applications, up 2% and 5% respectively from the same point a year earlier. Applicants submitted an average of 6.56 applications, compared with 6.37 a year earlier. Source: Common App, February 16, 2026, https://www.commonapp.org/about/reports-and-insights/february-2026-deadline-update/
- **The same pattern was visible earlier in the cycle.** Through November 1, 2025, 962,284 first-year applicants had submitted 4,716,352 applications, a 5% increase in applicants and a 10% increase in applications. Average applications per applicant rose from 4.68 to 4.90. Source: Common App, November 13, 2025, https://www.commonapp.org/about/reports-and-insights/november-2025-deadline-update/
- **The prior full cycle exceeded ten million applications.** Common App’s 2024–2025 end-of-season report counted 1,498,199 distinct first-year applicants submitting more than 10 million applications, with applicants applying to an average of 6.80 institutions. Source: Common App, August 13, 2025, https://www.commonapp.org/about/reports-and-insights/end-season-report-2024-2025-first-year-application-trends
- **Applicant growth is not confined to high-income or continuing-generation students.** Common App’s January 1, 2026 update reported faster growth among fee-waiver-eligible applicants, applicants from below-median-income ZIP codes, first-generation applicants, rural applicants, Black applicants, and applicants identifying with two or more races. These figures describe the Common App platform, not all U.S. higher education. Source: Common App, January 15, 2026 deadline update, https://www.commonapp.org/files/DAR/deadline-updates/2025-26/Common-App-Deadline-Update-2026.01.15.pdf
- **The legal boundary around race-conscious admissions changed sharply in 2023.** In Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard and the companion UNC case, decided June 29, 2023, the Supreme Court held that the admissions programs before it violated the Equal Protection Clause/Title VI framework governing race-conscious admissions. The majority criticized the programs for lacking sufficiently measurable objectives and for using race in ways inconsistent with constitutional limits. Source: Supreme Court opinion, 600 U.S. 181 (2023); accessible text at https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-1199 and official slip opinion at https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_l6gn.pdf
- **The Court did not say an applicant’s experiences involving race must be ignored.** The majority opinion expressly distinguished impermissible use of race as a category from consideration of an applicant’s discussion of how race affected that applicant’s life, character, or achievements, while warning universities not to use essays as a proxy for the prohibited regime. The chapter should preserve this legal nuance rather than saying “race can no longer be discussed in admissions.” Source: same Supreme Court opinion.
- **Federal enforcement and reporting expectations have continued to change after the decision.** In March 2025 the U.S. Department of Education issued FAQs tied to a Dear Colleague Letter addressing race preferences and essay prompts, and in August 2025 the Department announced expanded admissions-data collection disaggregated by race and sex, including academic measures for applicants, admits, and enrolled cohorts. These are executive-branch actions and should be described as current federal policy/enforcement posture, not as additional Supreme Court holdings. Sources: U.S. Department of Education, March 1, 2025, https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-releases-frequently-asked-questions-dear-colleague-letter-about-racial-preferencing ; August 7, 2025, https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-secretary-of-education-linda-mcmahon-directs-national-center-education-statistics-collect-universities-data-race-discrimination-admissions
- **Legacy and donor preferences have become an explicit policy target.** California enacted AB 1780 in September 2024, prohibiting private nonprofit colleges in the state from considering legacy and donor preferences in admissions and requiring annual compliance reporting. California public universities had already excluded legacy preferences. Source: Governor of California, September 30, 2024, https://www.gov.ca.gov/2024/09/30/california-bans-legacy-and-donor-preferences-in-admissions-at-private-nonprofit-universities/

All current sources above were rechecked in September 2026.

## Mechanism-design distinctions for the chapter

- **More applications do not necessarily mean proportionally more people are seeking college.** A rising applications-per-applicant rate creates congestion for admissions offices and makes admit rates mechanically lower at institutions whose seat counts do not expand correspondingly. This can make selectivity appear to intensify even when the number of distinct applicants grows much more slowly.
- **An admit rate is not a pure measure of demand.** It is a ratio produced by applicant behavior, application friction, institutional marketing, class size, yield expectations, early-decision strategy, and duplicate applications across schools. Do not treat a falling admit rate as proof that a college became academically “better.”
- **Selective admissions builds a class rather than sorting one universal score.** Grades, course rigor, test scores where used, essays, recommendations, geography, intended field, athletics, institutional priorities, socioeconomic context, and other permissible considerations can interact. The absence of one master ranking is not necessarily evidence of arbitrariness; it is often a consequence of multiple institutional objectives.
- **Preferences are policy choices, not natural facts.** Legacy preferences, donor preferences, recruited-athlete priorities, geographic preferences, first-generation goals, socioeconomic context, and program-specific needs all alter who is effectively competing with whom. Some are defended as mission-related; others are increasingly restricted or abandoned.
- **Legal constraints change the feasible mechanism.** The 2023 Supreme Court decision did not remove scarcity or institutional interest in assembling diverse classes. It changed which tools can legally be used and increased pressure on institutions to justify or redesign the remaining criteria.
- **The applicant also strategizes.** Early decision, early action, application breadth, school-list construction, test submission where optional, and financial-aid considerations change both applicant choices and institutional yield management. Admissions is therefore not a one-sided ranking problem.
- **Opacity can be functional and still costly.** Holistic review allows institutions to consider context that a simple score misses, but it also makes it harder for applicants to predict outcomes or audit whether stated values match actual practice. That tension should be treated as real rather than solved rhetorically.

## Strong counterevidence

- **Holistic review is not inherently a euphemism for favoritism.** A purely numerical system can reward unequal access to advanced coursework, test preparation, counseling, extracurricular opportunities, and stable schooling. Context-sensitive review can correct information loss that occurs when applicants are reduced to scores.
- **More transparent rules can create more gaming.** If institutions publish precise point weights for every criterion, affluent applicants and advising markets can optimize directly against them. Opacity may sometimes protect institutional flexibility, though it also weakens auditability.
- **Legacy bans do not make an admissions system egalitarian.** Removing one inherited advantage leaves many other unequal inputs untouched, including school quality, tutoring, neighborhood, family wealth, unpaid extracurricular time, and access to application guidance.
- **Application growth can reflect improved access as well as anxiety or strategic over-application.** Common App reports substantial recent growth among first-generation and lower-income applicants. A chapter that treats rising application counts only as arms-race behavior would miss a real expansion in participation.
- **The Supreme Court decision did not create one uniform admissions regime.** Public and private institutions face different legal structures; state laws differ; institutional missions differ; and federal policy can change. Avoid implying that every U.S. college now uses the same post-2023 method.

## Falsification notes

Do not say the Supreme Court banned colleges from reading essays that discuss race. The Court allowed consideration of an applicant’s individual experiences and character while barring use of race itself as the prior categorical admissions factor challenged in the cases.

Do not describe executive-branch guidance from 2025 as if it were part of the 2023 Supreme Court holding. Keep court doctrine, agency enforcement posture, state legislation, and institutional policy distinct.

Do not use Common App application counts as national totals for all college applications. They cover participating institutions and applicants using that platform.

Do not infer that a lower admit rate automatically means a school became more selective in a meaningful academic sense. Duplicate applications and changes in applicant behavior affect the denominator.

Do not imply that all selective colleges use legacy preferences. Policies vary, and California’s 2024 law illustrates that some jurisdictions now prohibit them for covered institutions.

Do not reduce admissions to “best grades get in.” Selective institutions routinely face more academically qualified applicants than available places and use multiple criteria and constraints to shape a class.

## Strong chapter tension

Housing and college admissions share a structural problem: there are more plausible claimants than scarce slots, and formal eligibility does not determine the final allocation.

Housing often makes the lottery visible and the value judgments administrative.

Selective college admissions often does the reverse. The institution may avoid literal randomization while making many policy choices about which attributes should break ties among thousands of qualified candidates.

That creates a useful question for the chapter: when two applicants are both capable of succeeding, what does “merit” actually mean as an allocation rule?

A test score is measurable but incomplete.

A holistic file is richer but harder to audit.

A preference can advance an institutional mission or entrench an inherited advantage.

A legal rule can remove one criterion without resolving the scarcity underneath it.

And rising application volume can make every decision appear more extraordinary even when much of the change comes from applicants sending more applications per person.

The chapter should end by moving from the applicant’s frustration to the institutional problem that remains after every criterion is chosen: who verifies that the algorithm, rubric, reader, or committee is doing what the institution says it is doing? That is the handoff to Chapter 19, “Who Audits the Algorithm.”
