# Chapter 4 — The Black Market Down the Hall

## Research purpose

Prepare the evidence base for a chapter comparing three distinct things that are too often collapsed into one another: (1) a legally regulated compensated living-kidney system such as Iran's, (2) prohibited organ trafficking and trafficking in persons for organ removal, and (3) ordinary travel for transplantation. The chapter should not use the existence of one as proof about the others.

## Iran: evidence and limits

Ahad Ghods's 2006 ethical review describes Iran's compensated living-unrelated kidney program, in operation since the late 1990s, as having increased the supply of kidneys available for transplantation. The same review also identifies a central ethical weakness: insufficient safeguards against a direct monetary relationship between donor and recipient. This makes it a useful source precisely because it does not fit a simple pro-market or anti-market story.

A later ethnographic study by Fry-Revere and colleagues interviewed compensated living kidney donors in Iran. The published abstract reports no evidence of coercion in the study's sample, while also reporting that 68% felt pressure to donate because of extreme poverty or family pressure, 74% had complaints about the donation process or its results, and 84% feared social stigma. Those findings are important counterevidence against any claim that formal legality by itself eliminates vulnerability or exploitation concerns.

A 2025 review already recorded in the book's ledger should be used for contemporary institutional context, but the prose should distinguish what is verified about the formal program from what is inferred about bargaining power and welfare.

## Organ trafficking and transplant tourism: definitions

The 2018 edition of the Declaration of Istanbul provides a useful definitional boundary. It defines organ trafficking broadly to include removal without valid consent or authorization or in exchange for financial gain or comparable advantage, as well as specified forms of facilitation, recruitment, and solicitation. It separately defines trafficking in persons for organ removal using coercion, fraud, deception, abuse of power or vulnerability, and related means.

The Declaration also distinguishes travel for transplantation from transplant tourism. Cross-border transplantation is not automatically unethical; travel becomes transplant tourism under the Declaration when it involves organ trafficking or trafficking in persons for organ removal, or when resources devoted to non-resident transplant patients undermine the host country's ability to serve its own population.

This distinction should control the chapter's vocabulary. Do not use “transplant tourism” as shorthand for any patient who receives a transplant abroad.

## What the chapter may safely argue

- Scarcity creates incentives for side payments and intermediary markets when formal allocation systems cannot meet demand.
- A regulated compensated system and an illegal trafficking network can share an economic pressure — scarce kidneys and willing recipients — while differing materially in law, consent, oversight, bargaining structure, and clinical safeguards.
- The Iranian evidence complicates both poles of the debate. Compensation can increase supply, but documented poverty pressure and dissatisfaction mean increased supply is not equivalent to demonstrated distributive justice.
- Professional transplant ethics does not treat every international transplant as trafficking. The Declaration of Istanbul's definitions are narrower and more precise.
- The strongest case for regulated compensation is not that exploitation concerns are imaginary; it is that a legal, supervised system might reduce deaths and perhaps displace more dangerous clandestine markets. That empirical claim must be presented as an argument to test, not as an established result.

## Falsification and counterevidence

Revise the eventual chapter if stronger evidence shows that:

- claims about Iran's waiting-list effects depend on definitions or periods that are not comparable with U.S. or global waiting-list statistics;
- the ethnographic donor sample is too narrow to support general population claims; use its percentages only as findings within that study, not as national prevalence estimates;
- a supposed trafficking example cannot distinguish payment from coercion, or relies on advocacy claims without documentary or peer-reviewed support;
- legal compensation is portrayed as equivalent to trafficking despite the Declaration of Istanbul's more specific definitions;
- the chapter implies that bans themselves cause trafficking without evidence sufficient to establish that causal relationship.

## Narrative guardrails

Do not invent donor stories, prices, brokers, operating rooms, dialogue, or sensory detail. If a named trafficking case is used, source the person's circumstances to reliable investigative or court records and keep uncertainty where the record is incomplete. The chapter is about scarcity and mechanism design, not shock value.

Avoid the easy moral arc in which a black market merely proves that prohibition failed. A market can emerge because scarcity remains, while still creating harms serious enough that prohibition or strict regulation has independent justification. The useful question is which institutional design reduces both the shortage and the exploitation risk, and what evidence would distinguish success from displacement.
