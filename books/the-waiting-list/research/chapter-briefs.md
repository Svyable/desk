# Chapter Briefs

These are research and argument briefs, not prose outlines. Each chapter should still discover its own narrative shape from the evidence available when it is written. Target length is 3,500–4,500 words per chapter. No invented quotations, statistics, or named individuals; use real, checkable sources and preserve genuine counter-evidence.

## Part I — The Myth of the Free Market

### Chapter 1 — Why Money Can't Buy Everything

**Job:** Introduce the concept of a repugnant transaction and establish that many of the allocation decisions that matter most to people are, by law or by custom, walled off from ordinary price competition.

**Questions to answer:** What does economist Alvin Roth mean by "repugnance"? Which transactions does U.S. law explicitly forbid selling (kidneys under the National Organ Transplant Act of 1984, votes, babies, sex in most jurisdictions) and why did lawmakers draw the line where they did? How has the boundary of repugnance moved over time (life insurance was once considered repugnant; commercial surrogacy remains contested)?

**Evidence to seek:** Roth's own writing on repugnant markets (his 2007 Journal of Economic Perspectives paper "Repugnance as a Constraint on Markets" is a strong anchor); the text and legislative history of the National Organ Transplant Act of 1984; historical accounts of repugnance shifting over time.

**Counterevidence:** Some economists argue repugnance is simply inefficiency dressed up as ethics, and that regulated markets (Iran's kidney system, life settlements) can outperform bans. Represent this view fairly even though the book does not adopt it uncritically.

**Avoid:** A dry definitional chapter. Ground the concept in a real, well-documented case before naming the theory.

**Handoff:** If price is off the table, something else has to decide. That something is the waiting list.

### Chapter 2 — A Taxonomy of Lines

**Job:** Show the reader how many different kinds of consequential lines already structure ordinary life, most of them unnoticed, and sort them into a working typology: pure first-come-first-served, lottery, priority-by-need, priority-by-tenure, and matched/algorithmic allocation.

**Questions to answer:** What do a kidney waitlist, a public-housing waitlist, an IPO share allocation, a NICU bed, a Space-A military standby flight, and a parking-permit list have in common structurally? Where did each rule come from and who chose it?

**Evidence to seek:** Concrete examples with real institutional sources (OPTN kidney allocation policy; HUD public-housing waitlist rules; SEC/FINRA IPO allocation practices; U.S. military Space-Available travel regulations).

**Counterevidence:** Some domains deliberately mix mechanisms (a lottery among the eligible, rather than a pure lottery or pure queue) precisely because pure versions of any single rule fail in practice; note this rather than flattening the taxonomy.

**Avoid:** A dry list-of-lists. Use two or three lines in detail rather than cataloguing dozens shallowly.

**Handoff:** Most of these lines were never designed by anyone in particular. The rest of the book studies what happens when someone actually designs one.

## Part II — When Queues and Cash Both Fail

### Chapter 3 — First Come, First Served, and Why It Isn't Fair

**Job:** Take apart the intuition that a simple queue is the fairest possible mechanism, using queueing theory and real institutional failures.

**Questions to answer:** What does classical queueing theory say about how FCFS interacts with arrival-time advantage (who can afford to arrive first, camp out, or hire a line-sitter)? How do hospital emergency departments deviate from FCFS on purpose, and why does that deviation (triage) feel more fair rather than less? What happened when Disney moved from a first-come standby system to paid FastPass and then Genie+ tiers, and how did guests' sense of fairness change?

**Evidence to seek:** Emergency-department triage protocol documentation (e.g., the Emergency Severity Index); reporting and company statements on Disney's FastPass/Genie+ evolution; queueing-theory literature on server utilization and fairness (Little's Law can be introduced briefly and explained in plain language, not as a formula-heavy digression).

**Counterevidence:** FCFS remains the default for good reasons in some domains: it is simple to audit, hard to manipulate administratively, and doesn't require a central authority to define "need."

**Avoid:** Turning this into a theme-park trivia chapter. Keep queueing theory in service of the fairness argument.

**Handoff:** When a queue's flaws become severe enough, a market grows up beside it — often an illegal one.

### Chapter 4 — The Black Market Down the Hall

**Job:** Examine what happens when the queue for kidneys is genuinely felt to be a death sentence: Iran's regulated paid-donation system on one side, and global organ trafficking on the other.

**Questions to answer:** How does Iran's living-donor compensation system actually work, and what do transplant researchers say about its outcomes and its problems? What has investigative reporting and academic research (Nancy Scheper-Hughes's "Organs Watch" project, journalistic investigations into kidney trafficking networks in South Asia and the Philippines) documented about illegal organ markets? Why do most transplant ethicists remain uneasy about both systems?

**Evidence to seek:** Peer-reviewed transplant-medicine and health-policy literature on Iran's model; Scheper-Hughes's published research and interviews; investigative journalism on trafficking networks, with careful sourcing and no invented victim details beyond what reporting has documented.

**Counterevidence:** Some bioethicists and economists (including some transplant surgeons) argue a regulated compensation system in wealthy countries would reduce trafficking and deaths, not increase exploitation; represent this position with its actual proponents.

**Avoid:** Sensationalizing trafficking without engaging the underlying economics — this is a chapter about scarcity and mechanism design, not true crime.

**Handoff:** The bans and the black markets both fail for related reasons; the next chapters show economists who found a way to satisfy the same demand for kidneys without any money changing hands.

### Chapter 5 — Scalpers, Bots, and the Ticketmaster Problem

**Job:** Use the 2022 Ticketmaster/Eras Tour presale collapse and the broader bot-driven resale economy to show how an under-designed digital queue creates its own black market in real time, in a domain far lower-stakes than organs but structurally identical.

**Questions to answer:** What technically happened during the November 2022 Eras Tour Verified Fan presale? What did the resulting Senate Judiciary Committee hearing (January 2023) and subsequent Department of Justice/state antitrust suit against Live Nation-Ticketmaster reveal about how the primary and secondary ticket markets actually interact? What did the 2016 BOTS Act attempt to fix, and why has bot-driven scalping persisted anyway (extending the pattern to sneaker drops and GPU/console resale)?

**Evidence to seek:** Congressional hearing records and testimony; DOJ/state civil antitrust complaint against Live Nation Entertainment (filed May 2024) and Ticketmaster's own public statements; reporting on bot mitigation technology and its limits.

**Counterevidence:** Dynamic and resale pricing can be defended as a legitimate way to let a market clear when a queue systematically underprices true demand; represent the "let the market work" counterargument fairly.

**Avoid:** Retelling the Eras Tour story as celebrity gossip; keep the focus on mechanism design and enforcement.

**Handoff:** All of these failures share a structural fix that a different set of economists had already worked out, decades earlier, for a much higher-stakes problem: how to match people stably without letting anyone game the rules.

## Part III — The Economists Who Redesigned Fairness

### Chapter 6 — The Marriage Algorithm

**Job:** Explain David Gale and Lloyd Shapley's 1962 paper "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage" clearly enough that a general reader understands deferred acceptance and why it produces a stable matching, without turning the chapter into a math lecture.

**Questions to answer:** What problem were Gale and Shapley actually solving, and why did they frame it as a stable-marriage problem? What is a "blocking pair," and why does its absence matter so much in practice? Who proposes matters — how does the classic result that the proposing side gets weakly better outcomes than the side receiving proposals show up in real systems?

**Evidence to seek:** The original 1962 American Mathematical Monthly paper; secondary explanations from Roth's own retrospective writing and Nobel Prize documentation; a concrete, small, fully worked example the reader can follow by hand.

**Counterevidence:** Stable matching is not unique — multiple stable matchings can exist for the same preferences, and which side proposes changes who benefits, which is itself a policy choice with distributional consequences.

**Avoid:** Presenting the algorithm as pure mathematics detached from institutions; keep tying it back to the real systems that would later use it.

**Handoff:** The theory sat mostly unused for over twenty years until a crisis in how American hospitals hired their doctors made it suddenly indispensable.

### Chapter 7 — The Man Who Fixed the Medical Match

**Job:** Tell the history of the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP): its origins in the chaotic, exploitative pre-match era of the 1940s and 1950s, its original 1952 algorithm (later understood to favor hospitals over applicants), the mid-1990s crisis over married couples and specialty pairs that the old algorithm handled badly, and the redesign led substantially by Alvin Roth and Elliott Peranson.

**Questions to answer:** What was medical residency hiring like before a centralized match (exploding offer deadlines, exploding pressure, and unraveling)? What was structurally wrong with the original algorithm once it was studied with modern game theory? What specifically changed in the 1990s redesign, and how was it validated before deployment?

**Evidence to seek:** NRMP's own official history; Roth's academic papers analyzing the pre-1998 algorithm ("The Redesign of the Matching Market for American Physicians," with Elliott Peranson, published in the American Economic Review in 1999, is a strong anchor); contemporaneous reporting on the couples-matching controversy.

**Counterevidence:** The match still draws criticism today, particularly around the binding nature of the outcome and its effect on bargaining power between applicants and hospitals; include current critique, not only the historical success story.

**Avoid:** A hagiography of one economist; credit the full team and the institutional stakeholders who had to agree to change a live, high-stakes system.

**Handoff:** If a stable algorithm could fix how doctors get jobs, the same team wondered whether it could fix something with even higher stakes: who gets a kidney.

### Chapter 8 — Kidneys Without Cash

**Job:** Tell the story of kidney exchange: the theoretical work by Roth, Tayfun Sönmez, and Utku Ünver showing incompatible donor-patient pairs could "swap," the first real paired exchanges in the early 2000s, the leap to longer chains (including altruistic-donor-triggered nonsimultaneous chains), and the growth of registries such as the National Kidney Registry and the Alliance for Paired Donation.

**Questions to answer:** Why can't a willing, loving donor always give to the specific person they want to help (blood type and tissue incompatibility)? How does a paired exchange solve that without money? How did chains extend the idea, and what is the largest, best-documented chain in the public record? What do published outcome studies (graft survival, number of transplants enabled) show?

**Evidence to seek:** Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver's founding papers (their 2004 Quarterly Journal of Economics paper "Kidney Exchange" is a strong anchor); National Kidney Registry public data and reporting; well-documented chain reporting such as Kevin Sack's 2012 New York Times series on a multi-transplant chain, cited as journalism, not as a source of invented dialogue.

**Counterevidence:** Kidney exchange has real limits — it still depends entirely on the supply of willing living donors, doesn't help patients with no willing donor at all, and raises its own fairness questions about how chains are prioritized and who benefits from being "used" early versus late in a long chain.

**Avoid:** Overstating kidney exchange as having solved the organ shortage; it has enabled thousands of additional transplants, not eliminated the underlying scarcity documented in the prologue.

**Handoff:** In 2012, this body of work won the Nobel Prize — an unusually direct line from a 1962 math paper to a working transplant network.

### Chapter 9 — A Nobel for a Marriage That Never Happened

**Job:** Cover the 2012 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, awarded jointly to Lloyd Shapley and Alvin Roth "for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design," and use it to explain the birth of market design as a recognized subfield of economics that treats institutions as engineered objects.

**Questions to answer:** What did the Nobel committee's own citation and public materials say the two men's contributions were, and how did the prize explicitly bridge pure theory (Shapley, working largely alone decades earlier) and applied design (Roth's later work with hospitals, schools, and kidney registries)? What is "market design" as a subfield, and how does it differ from traditional market economics, which mostly assumes prices already exist and clear?

**Evidence to seek:** The Nobel Foundation's official press release and Roth's Nobel lecture; Roth's own popular writing on market design as an engineering discipline (his book *Who Gets What — and Why* and his public essays are legitimate secondary sources to draw on for framing, cited as his own writing rather than paraphrased as neutral fact).

**Counterevidence:** Some economists critique market design as underselling how much of its success depends on cooperating institutions and enforceable rules that a purely mathematical design cannot guarantee; note this limitation.

**Avoid:** A dry prize-ceremony recap; use the Nobel as a hinge point to describe the field's expansion into the domains the rest of the book covers.

**Handoff:** With the tools proven in medicine, the same researchers turned to a problem just as emotionally fraught and far more numerous in participants every year: which school your child attends.

### Chapter 10 — School Choice, Rebuilt

**Job:** Tell the story of the redesign of school-choice assignment systems in Boston (2005) and New York City (2003–2004), led by Atila Abdulkadiroğlu, Parag Pathak, Alvin Roth, and Tayfun Sönmez, and explain why the older "Boston mechanism" punished honest applicants.

**Questions to answer:** What was wrong, mechanically, with the pre-reform systems (families needed to strategically misrepresent their true first choice to avoid being shut out, and low-information families were disproportionately hurt)? How does a deferred-acceptance-based system remove that incentive to lie? What measurable effects did researchers document after the switch?

**Evidence to seek:** Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak, and Roth's papers on the NYC high school match ("The New York City High School Match," American Economic Review Papers and Proceedings, 2005, is a strong anchor) and the Boston mechanism critique; Boston Public Schools' own record of adopting the new algorithm in 2005; follow-up research assessing outcomes.

**Counterevidence:** Algorithmic school choice does not by itself desegregate schools or equalize school quality; critics argue it can launder unequal access to information about how to rank preferences strategically, and some cities have since revisited or modified their systems.

**Avoid:** Implying the algorithm alone solved educational inequality; it solved a specific, real problem of strategic manipulation, which is a narrower and still important claim.

**Handoff:** These systems all still require a human match between two static lists. The next wave of matching would have to work at the speed of a smartphone.

## Part IV — Matching at the Speed of a Phone

### Chapter 11 — The Uber Problem

**Job:** Treat ride-hailing dispatch and surge pricing as a real-time, two-sided matching-market design problem and connect it back to the market-design vocabulary established earlier in the book.

**Questions to answer:** How do ride-hailing platforms actually decide which driver gets matched to which rider request, and how does that differ from a pure price-clears-everything model? What does surge/dynamic pricing actually do mechanically (drawing supply toward high-demand areas) versus what riders perceive it as doing (punishing them)? What have economists who study these platforms (including some who worked directly with ride-hailing companies) found about the tradeoffs?

**Evidence to seek:** Published research on ride-hailing matching and dynamic pricing (work by economists such as Jonathan Hall or academic partnerships published via NBER or peer-reviewed journals); company statements and disclosures on dispatch design; reporting on driver-side critiques of matching algorithms.

**Counterevidence:** Drivers and riders both have documented, legitimate complaints that algorithmic matching in this domain is opaque compared with the academic mechanisms in earlier chapters, and that the company's incentives (extracting a take rate) are not neutral in the way an academic market designer's incentives are meant to be.

**Avoid:** A press-release retelling of "how Uber works"; keep interrogating the gap between this commercial matching system and the transparency standards market design set in kidney exchange and school choice.

**Handoff:** If a stranger's ride can be matched by algorithm in seconds, it was only a matter of time before a stranger's romantic compatibility would be too.

### Chapter 12 — Dating by Algorithm

**Job:** Examine how dating apps use matching concepts, where they genuinely borrow from stable-matching theory and where "matching" is really just a recommendation-and-swipe system with a different name.

**Questions to answer:** What is actually known, from company statements, patents, and independent research, about how major dating apps rank and surface potential matches? Where does real economic research on matching markets (including work using OkCupid- and dating-platform-adjacent data) intersect with these products? Why does the classic Gale-Shapley result about "who proposes benefits" map imperfectly onto a two-sided swiping system with no formal proposal round?

**Evidence to seek:** Published research on online dating markets (economists and sociologists who have studied platform data, such as work referencing OkCupid or Hinge datasets with appropriate academic sourcing); company patent filings and public product explanations; critical reporting on dating-app algorithm opacity and "pay-to-play" visibility features.

**Counterevidence:** Dating markets are not truly two-sided in the clean academic sense (preferences are unstable, strategic self-presentation is rampant, and platforms profit from prolonging rather than resolving the search), which limits how directly market-design theory applies.

**Avoid:** Overclaiming that dating apps are literal deferred-acceptance systems; be precise about where the analogy holds and where it breaks.

**Handoff:** Not every matching problem is voluntary or low-stakes. The next chapter turns to a matching problem millions of people were forced into during a global emergency: who got a vaccine first.

### Chapter 13 — The Vaccine Line

**Job:** Reconstruct how COVID-19 vaccine allocation actually worked in the United States: the CDC Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices' phased prioritization framework, the wide state-by-state variation in implementation, and the resulting equity gaps and "vaccine hunter" phenomenon.

**Questions to answer:** What did ACIP's official phased framework (1a, 1b, 1c, and beyond) actually specify, and why? How much did individual states deviate from that federal framework, and with what documented consequences for equity and speed? What role did informal information networks (family members monitoring pharmacy websites, volunteer "vaccine hunter" groups) play in who actually got vaccinated early, and what does that reveal about queues that depend on unequally distributed information and time?

**Evidence to seek:** CDC/ACIP official prioritization documentation; peer-reviewed and government analyses of state-level rollout variation and equity outcomes; contemporaneous reporting on vaccine-hunter volunteer networks.

**Counterevidence:** Some public-health researchers argue that simpler, broader eligibility (rather than granular prioritization) actually moved doses into arms faster and reduced net harm, even if it looked less "fair" on paper; represent this efficiency-versus-equity tension honestly.

**Avoid:** Relitigating pandemic politics broadly; keep the focus tightly on the allocation-mechanism question.

**Handoff:** A vaccine queue, however imperfect, was still built around lives inside a single country. An even harder version of the same problem asks which country, and which city, a displaced family should be matched to at all.

### Chapter 14 — Matching Refugees to Cities

**Job:** Cover the emerging research and pilot programs applying algorithmic matching to refugee resettlement, associated most prominently with political scientists Kirk Bansak, Jeremy Weinstein, and Justin Grimmer and their collaborators, including real-world pilots in Switzerland and the United States.

**Questions to answer:** What is the core insight of "geographic matching" or algorithmic resettlement — that outcomes (such as employment) vary enormously depending on which specific locality a refugee family is placed in, and that this variation can be predicted well enough to inform placement? What did the published pilot studies actually find about improved outcomes? Who currently makes resettlement-location decisions in the absence of such a system, and on what basis?

**Evidence to seek:** Bansak, Grimmer, Weinstein, and coauthors' peer-reviewed papers (their 2018 Science paper "Improving refugee integration through data-driven algorithmic assignment" is a strong anchor) and later pilot-program reporting; UNHCR and national resettlement agency materials describing current, non-algorithmic placement practice.

**Counterevidence:** Algorithmic placement raises real concerns about reducing deeply personal decisions (where to live, near which relatives or community) to a predicted-employment-outcome optimization; represent advocate and refugee-community critiques of the approach.

**Avoid:** Presenting the pilots as already fully deployed policy; be precise about what is proven, what is piloted, and what remains proposed.

**Handoff:** These are still fundamentally human-scale matching problems. The final scarcities in this book are the ones defining the current technological moment — where the "people" waiting in line are sometimes companies, and the good being rationed can be measured in gigawatts.

## Part V — The New Scarcities of 2027

### Chapter 15 — The GPU Waiting List

**Job:** Treat the 2023–2026 AI-accelerator shortage as a waiting-list problem: who actually gets access to scarce, high-end GPU capacity, and through what mixture of long-term contracts, cloud-provider queues, and outright allocation decisions by chip and cloud suppliers.

**Questions to answer:** How does Nvidia and major cloud providers' capacity actually get allocated among customers when demand exceeds supply (multi-year committed contracts, priority tiers, direct allocation decisions)? What reporting exists on smaller AI labs, startups, and researchers struggling to access compute even with money in hand, and why does money not always clear this particular market the way basic economics would predict? What has been proposed or implemented under the banner of "compute governance"?

**Evidence to seek:** Company earnings disclosures and public statements on capacity allocation (Nvidia, major cloud providers); reporting on AI compute scarcity and its effects on smaller labs and researchers; policy and academic writing on compute governance proposals.

**Counterevidence:** Some economists argue this is simply an ordinary, temporary supply-demand imbalance that will resolve as manufacturing capacity catches up, not a case requiring special non-price allocation mechanisms; represent this view.

**Avoid:** Speculative claims about specific future chip generations; stick to documented allocation practice and reporting.

**Handoff:** Compute is a scarcity built by humans, in principle solvable by building more factories. Water is a scarcity nature imposes, and the rules for sharing it are far older and far harder to change.

### Chapter 16 — Whose Water Is It

**Job:** Explain the Colorado River Compact's prior-appropriation seniority system ("first in time, first in right"), the 2022–2023 shortage declarations, and the multi-state renegotiation process, as a century-old, high-stakes waiting-list problem with no algorithmic fix in sight.

**Questions to answer:** How does prior appropriation actually rank water rights, and why does that ranking sometimes look nothing like present-day need or population? What triggered the federal shortage declarations of the early 2020s, and how did the seven basin states respond? What tensions exist between this legally entrenched seniority queue and calls for a more flexible, needs-based or market-based reallocation?

**Evidence to seek:** Bureau of Reclamation official shortage declarations and Colorado River management documents; reporting and analysis on the interstate renegotiation process; academic work on prior-appropriation water law and proposed water markets as an alternative.

**Counterevidence:** Water markets (voluntary transfers and leasing among rights-holders) already exist in parts of the western U.S. and offer a market-based counterexample within the same domain; include this rather than presenting seniority as the only mechanism in play.

**Avoid:** A comprehensive history of Western water law; keep the focus on the allocation-mechanism argument.

**Handoff:** If water rights show how hard it is to redesign a queue once it is a century old, housing shows how hard it is to build a fair one even when the mechanism is barely a decade old.

### Chapter 17 — The Housing Lottery

**Job:** Cover U.S. affordable-housing allocation: Section 8 housing-choice voucher waitlists that in many cities have been closed for years, and the rise of lottery-based systems such as NYC Housing Connect, using them to compare lottery and waitlist mechanisms directly.

**Questions to answer:** How long are typical Section 8 waitlists in major cities, and what happens when a waitlist reopens after years closed (documented application surges)? How does a lottery system like Housing Connect work, and what tradeoffs does a lottery make relative to a tenure-based waitlist (a lottery treats a family that has waited a decade the same as one that just applied)? What have researchers found about who actually navigates these systems successfully?

**Evidence to seek:** HUD data and reports on voucher waitlist status nationally; NYC Housing Connect's own published mechanics and lottery-odds data; academic or investigative reporting on waitlist reopenings and their documented demand surges.

**Counterevidence:** Straightforward tenure-based waitlists reward patience and commitment in ways some housing advocates prefer over a lottery's pure chance; represent this preference fairly.

**Avoid:** Reducing this to a single city's anecdote; use documented, comparable data across the mechanisms.

**Handoff:** Housing lotteries decide who gets a home. A closely related, higher-visibility line decides who gets a diploma from a particular gate — and it has just been forced to redesign itself by the courts.

### Chapter 18 — The Line for College

**Job:** Cover elite college admissions after the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling in *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard*, which ended race-conscious affirmative action, alongside the persistent, less-discussed queue advantage of legacy admissions, to show how an allocation system can be legally redesigned in one dimension while leaving another largely untouched.

**Questions to answer:** What did the 2023 ruling actually require and prohibit, and how have selective universities changed their stated admissions criteria in response? What does the data show about legacy and donor-connected admissions preferences at elite institutions, and how has scrutiny of that practice changed (including state-level bans and institutional policy changes)? How does "holistic review," as an allocation mechanism, compare in transparency to the algorithmic systems described earlier in the book?

**Evidence to seek:** The Supreme Court opinion itself and contemporaneous legal analysis; university public statements on post-ruling admissions policy; research and reporting on legacy-admissions rates and their effects (including institutional disclosures where states have required them).

**Counterevidence:** Holistic review's defenders argue that its opacity is a feature, allowing judgment that a formal algorithm cannot replicate; represent this argument rather than assuming transparency is always superior.

**Avoid:** Re-litigating the constitutional merits of the ruling; keep the focus on admissions as an allocation mechanism and its comparative transparency.

**Handoff:** Every mechanism examined so far, however good or bad, was at least built by someone accountable enough to name. The book's final section asks who is accountable when nobody is.

## Part VI — Designing Better Lines

### Chapter 19 — Who Audits the Algorithm

**Job:** Examine the accountability gap in modern allocation systems: who has the standing, access, and technical ability to audit a consequential algorithm, and what happens when no one does.

**Questions to answer:** What does the algorithmic-fairness and auditability research literature say about auditing allocation systems used in criminal justice, benefits administration, and hiring, and how does that literature's findings extend to the waiting-list systems covered earlier in the book? What real cases exist of an allocation algorithm being successfully challenged, revised, or made more transparent after outside scrutiny? What structural reasons make public-sector and platform allocation algorithms harder to audit than an academic market-design mechanism published in a peer-reviewed journal?

**Evidence to seek:** Academic literature on algorithmic auditing and fairness (including well-documented case studies such as scrutiny of state benefits-eligibility algorithms or hiring-screening tools); reporting on successful transparency or reform campaigns targeting a specific public allocation algorithm.

**Counterevidence:** Full transparency about an allocation algorithm can itself enable more sophisticated gaming by sophisticated actors, creating a real tension between openness and manipulation-resistance; represent this tradeoff rather than treating maximal transparency as costless.

**Avoid:** A generic AI-ethics survey chapter disconnected from the book's specific case studies; tie the argument back to kidneys, schools, GPUs, water, and housing.

**Handoff:** The book closes not with a warning but with a synthesis: what the successes documented throughout actually have in common, and what that implies for the queues still being built.

### Chapter 20 — How to Build a Better Waiting List

**Job:** Synthesize the book's argument into a closing chapter that distills what separates the genuine market-design successes (kidney exchange, the medical match, reformed school choice) from the improvised or poorly audited systems (early ticket presales, opaque platform matching, some 2020s compute and housing rationing), and what that implies for institutions building new allocation systems now.

**Questions to answer:** Across every case in the book, what recurring properties (strategy-proofness, stability, transparency, a real feedback loop for revision) distinguished the systems that earned public trust from the ones that didn't? What is realistically achievable for an institution that cannot hire a team of market-design economists, and what is the minimum viable version of "designing the queue on purpose" rather than letting one happen by default? Circle back explicitly to the kidney-transplant waitlist from the prologue and note what, if anything, has changed or is changing about it as of 2026–2027.

**Evidence to seek:** Synthesis of sources already gathered across the manuscript; any recent (2025–2027) reporting on organ-allocation policy reform efforts, since this chapter should close the kidney thread opened in the prologue with current, real information rather than a static conclusion.

**Counterevidence:** Acknowledge directly that better mechanism design cannot manufacture more kidneys, more affordable housing units, or more GPUs — it can only make the sharing of a fixed scarcity more defensible, and the chapter should not overclaim that good design solves scarcity itself.

**Avoid:** A bullet-point "principles for success" list; let the synthesis emerge as argument and narrative, consistent with the rest of the book's voice.

**Handoff:** Into the epilogue, which returns to the 2 a.m. phone call from the prologue.
