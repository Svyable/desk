# Chapter 8 — Kidneys Without Cash

## Claims supported

- Kidney paired donation converts biological incompatibility between willing donor-recipient pairs into a matching problem rather than a monetary transaction.
- Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver’s 2004 QJE paper formalized larger-scale kidney exchange and reported simulations suggesting gains in feasible live-donor transplants and match quality.
- Pairwise exchanges were attractive operationally because longer cycles required more simultaneous surgeries and created coordination risk.
- Nondirected donors permit open chains rather than closed cycles.
- Rees et al. (NEJM, 2009) documented a 10-transplant nonsimultaneous altruistic-donor chain spanning eight months, six transplant centers, and five states, with bridge donors extending the chain.
- Chain structures change the consequence of reneging: a break is harmful but need not leave the next pair without the willing donor it brought into the exchange.
- Chain-ending kidneys can reach patients on the ordinary waitlist, partially answering—but not eliminating—the fairness concern that paired exchange initially privileges patients who arrive with willing living donors.

## Anchors

1. Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver, “Kidney Exchange,” Quarterly Journal of Economics 119(2), 2004, 457–488.
2. Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver, “Pairwise Kidney Exchange,” Journal of Economic Theory 125(2), 2005, 151–188.
3. Rees et al., “A Nonsimultaneous, Extended, Altruistic-Donor Chain,” New England Journal of Medicine 360, 2009, 1096–1101.
4. Osbun et al., “The benefit to waitlist patients in a national paired kidney exchange program,” American Journal of Transplantation, 2021/2022 publication cycle; study population February 2008–September 2020.
5. National Kidney Registry current donor and paired-donation materials for present-day operating context. Treat its performance claims as institutional claims, not neutral evaluation.

## Counterevidence and boundaries

- Do not say kidney exchange solved the kidney shortage. It only converts some otherwise unusable living-donor opportunities into transplants.
- Patients without willing donors generally cannot enter an ordinary paired exchange, although nondirected-donor chains can terminate in the broader waitlist.
- More transplants is not the only plausible objective. Highly sensitized candidates and other hard-to-match patients create distributional tradeoffs.
- Living donors remain autonomous human participants. A computationally feasible chain is not a binding command to undergo surgery.
- Long chains reduce simultaneity constraints but create bridge-donor, scheduling, transport, and institutional coordination risks.

## Falsification note

The argument would need revision if paired exchange did not materially increase feasible living-donor transplantation, if nondirected chains did not alter simultaneity/reneging risk as described by Rees et al., or if evidence showed that chain-end donation never benefited ordinary waitlist patients. The cited literature supports all three mechanisms while leaving the overall organ shortage plainly unresolved.
