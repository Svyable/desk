# Chapter 4 — The Black Market Down the Hall

## Evidence anchors

- Ahad J. Ghods, “Compensated kidney donation: an ethical review of the Iranian model” (2006): use for the historical case that regulated compensated living-unrelated donation increased kidney supply in Iran. Do not use this source alone for current payment amounts or present-day program structure.
- Sigrid Fry-Revere et al., “Coercion, dissatisfaction, and social stigma: an ethnographic study of compensated living kidney donation in Iran” (2018): use as donor-level counterevidence on financial pressure, dissatisfaction, complaints, and stigma. Treat findings as study-sample evidence, not a census of all Iranian donors.
- Iranian transplant researchers, “20 Years Iranian Experience of Organ Donation: Shifting From Compensated Living Unrelated Kidney Transplantation” (2025): use for current context. The review reports that transplant hospitalization costs are government-covered; living donors receive an official government payment; a second, unofficial recipient-to-donor payment is negotiated; and policy/infrastructure has shifted toward greater deceased donation. It explicitly retains exploitation concerns.
- Declaration of Istanbul Custodian Group, 2018 edition: use its exact conceptual distinctions. “Travel for transplantation” is not automatically “transplant tourism”; the latter applies when travel involves organ trafficking / trafficking in persons for organ removal or undermines the source country’s transplant resources. Preserve the distinction between organ trafficking and trafficking in persons for organ removal.

## Factual discipline

The earlier Chapter 4 draft contained highly specific claims about individual trafficking cases, village prevalence, broker margins, criminal sentences, historical Iranian waiting-list dates, and recent behavioral-economics studies that were not represented in the book’s source ledger. Those details were removed rather than carried forward without a checkable source trail.

Do not state that Iran’s system proves compensation “eliminated” organ scarcity in a durable or generalizable way. The defensible claim is narrower: compensation increased living-unrelated kidney supply, while later research documents continued ethical concerns and a policy shift toward deceased donation.

Do not equate poverty with incapacity to consent. The chapter should preserve both donor agency and the evidence that financial vulnerability can narrow choices and complicate what a signed consent form tells us.

## Counterevidence to preserve

A compensation regime could plausibly increase supply and reduce harms associated with shortage. The chapter should not argue that payment is self-evidently disqualifying. Its stronger claim is institutional: once compensation is permitted, the quality of donor protection, bargaining rules, follow-up care, payment structure, transparency, and oversight become central design questions.

Likewise, prohibition is not costless. A ban can reduce commodification and exploitation risk while leaving patients exposed to prolonged shortage and creating incentives for evasion. The chapter should compare whole systems rather than treating either prohibition or compensation as morally free.

## Handoff

The chapter should end by widening the design space beyond “queue versus cash.” Kidney exchange becomes the key next move: many patients already have a willing donor, but biological incompatibility prevents the direct transplant. Matching can unlock supply without permitting an ordinary organ market.
