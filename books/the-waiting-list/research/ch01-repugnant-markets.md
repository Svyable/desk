# Chapter 1 research note — Why Money Can't Buy Everything

Checked September 2, 2026.

## Claim under test

Chapter 1 argues that scarcity does not disappear when a society refuses to allocate a good by price. Instead, law and institutions substitute another mechanism. Kidney transplantation is the chapter's anchor because U.S. law permits reimbursement of donation-related costs while prohibiting acquisition, receipt, or transfer of a human organ for valuable consideration.

The claim survives, but it needs four boundaries kept explicit: the legal ban is on valuable consideration for the organ, not on every monetary transfer around transplantation; repugnance is an institutional constraint rather than proof that a taboo is morally correct; compensated donation can increase supply; and the strongest case for compensation is a regulated intermediary model rather than an unconstrained bilateral market.

## Primary and authoritative anchors

### 1. Federal organ-sale prohibition

42 U.S.C. § 274e makes it unlawful to knowingly acquire, receive, or otherwise transfer a human organ for valuable consideration for use in human transplantation when the transfer affects interstate commerce. The definition of valuable consideration excludes reasonable payments associated with removal, transportation, implantation, processing, preservation, quality control, storage, and donor expenses including travel, housing, and lost wages.

This supports the manuscript's distinction between reimbursing costs and purchasing the kidney itself. The chapter should not imply that transplantation is a money-free institution; many surrounding services are priced.

Source: U.S. Code, 42 U.S.C. § 274e, current text: https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title42-section274e

### 2. Repugnance as a market constraint

Alvin E. Roth's 2007 *Journal of Economic Perspectives* article treats repugnance as a real constraint on what markets can be designed and sustained, while emphasizing that the boundary changes across time and jurisdictions. The chapter uses the concept correctly when it treats repugnance as a condition designers must work within, not as a theorem that every prohibited transaction should remain prohibited.

Source: Alvin E. Roth, “Repugnance as a Constraint on Markets,” *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 21(3), 2007: https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.21.3.37

### 3. Countercase for compensation

Gary S. Becker and Julio Jorge Elías argue that monetary incentives for live and cadaveric organ donation could materially increase supply and reduce waiting-list deaths at a cost small relative to the value of additional transplants. Their case matters because a no-payment rule is not costless: scarcity remains and patients bear part of that cost through dialysis, illness, and death while waiting.

Source: Gary S. Becker and Julio Jorge Elías, “Introducing Incentives in the Market for Live and Cadaveric Organ Donations,” *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 21(3), 2007: https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.21.3.3

### 4. Iran as evidence in both directions

Iran remains the main empirical counterexample to the claim that compensation necessarily collapses organ allocation into an unregulated spot market. The literature describes a regulated compensated living-unrelated donor system that increased kidney supply, while donor-level and review literature also documents financial pressure, dissatisfaction, stigma, and recipient-to-donor side payments.

The chapter should therefore avoid two opposite overclaims: that Iran proves compensation solves the ethical problem, or that the existence of exploitation proves compensation cannot increase supply. The useful lesson is that supply and bargaining-power effects can move in opposite moral directions at once.

Sources:
- Ahad J. Ghods, “Compensated kidney donation: an ethical review of the Iranian model,” 2006: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17091561/
- Sigrid Fry-Revere et al., “Coercion, dissatisfaction, and social stigma: an ethnographic study of compensated living kidney donation in Iran,” 2018: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29480443/
- Recent review of the Iranian model and its evolution, 2025: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034887/

## Falsification and counterevidence

The chapter's thesis would be weakened if a prohibited price mechanism could be removed without any substitute allocation rule because supply reliably exceeded demand. Kidney transplantation does not satisfy that condition. More generally, the argument should not be generalized from kidneys to every good that is difficult or controversial to sell.

A stronger challenge is normative rather than empirical: if a tightly regulated compensation system could increase organ supply while materially reducing coercion and side payments, the case for preserving the current U.S. prohibition would become weaker. The chapter already leaves that possibility open and should continue to do so.

## Prose boundaries for later revision

- Do not say Congress “abolished the market” in transplantation; it prohibited valuable consideration for the organ while leaving many surrounding services priced.
- Do not describe repugnance as irrationality by definition.
- Do not describe Iran as having permanently eliminated every kidney waiting list or as proving a universal result across periods and provinces.
- Do not imply that every compensated donor is coerced; the evidence supports financial vulnerability and pressure as important concerns, not a universal donor biography.
- Keep the strongest pro-compensation argument in the chapter: refusing payment has victims too.

## Readiness judgment

Chapter 1's central factual architecture is supported by statute, peer-reviewed economics, and transplant literature. Its strongest counterargument is already present in the manuscript. No factual correction to the manuscript is required from this check; the main readiness gain is a dedicated falsification record that makes the legal and empirical boundaries explicit.
