# The Price of Looking

In the cleanest version of economics, choice begins when the options are already on the table.

There is a price. There is a product. There is a buyer. Preferences exist somewhere inside the buyer, and the work of decision is to match those preferences against what the market offers. The picture is useful partly because it removes a messier problem from view: how did the buyer learn what was available, what it cost elsewhere, whether the seller could be trusted, whether a substitute existed, or whether the purchase was worth thinking about at all?

Looking has a price.

That price can be measured in money, but more often it is paid in minutes, inconvenience, cognitive effort, embarrassment, uncertainty, and the risk that further search will make the decision harder rather than easier. A person who spends an hour comparing two nearly identical cables has not escaped cost by saving three dollars. The cost has moved from the receipt into the afternoon.

This sounds obvious until one notices how much economic reasoning depends on pretending it is not.

In 1961, George Stigler published a short paper called “The Economics of Information.” Its opening move was almost mischievously simple: information is not free, so people will not acquire it without limit. A buyer who wants to learn the prices charged by different sellers has to search. The search takes time or money. The expected benefit from another inquiry eventually falls below the expected cost. At that point, stopping is not laziness. It is part of the optimization problem.

The idea helped make an awkward fact respectable. Prices for the same good can differ even in competitive markets because buyers do not inspect every seller. Dispersion can survive not only because markets are broken, but because complete comparison is expensive.

There is a world of difference between saying that information matters and saying that information has a production cost.

The first statement leaves the classical picture largely intact. The second changes the geometry of choice. If learning about an option costs something, then an option can be economically real and behaviorally absent. It may exist in the city, on the shelf, in the job market, or on a website, yet remain outside the practical choice set because discovering it costs more than the chooser expects to gain.

That is why search is not an ornament around markets. It is one of the things markets are made of.

Herbert Simon had approached the problem from another direction several years earlier. In his work on rational choice, he argued that the ideal of exhaustive optimization often had little to do with the actual conditions faced by people and organizations. Real decision-makers operate with limited knowledge, limited computational capacity, and limited time. They do not usually compare every possible action against every possible consequence. They search until they find an alternative that is good enough relative to some aspiration level.

Simon called the behavior satisficing.

The word is sometimes treated as a polite synonym for settling. That misses the point. Satisficing is not merely what happens when people are too tired to optimize. It is a response to a world in which the option set itself must be discovered and the cost of discovering more options can exceed their expected value.

If you are hiring for an important role, there is no natural final applicant. You can always wait another week. You can always widen the geography, call another recruiter, ask another colleague, post in another network, lower one credential and raise another. A better candidate may exist. The possibility cannot be disproved. The real question is whether the expected improvement from further search is worth the delay, vacancy cost, management attention, and chance that the best current candidate accepts another offer.

That is a search problem before it is a ranking problem.

The same structure appears in ordinary life. Consider a person buying a used car. The theoretical market is enormous. The practical market is the handful of cars that survive several filters: distance, time, budget, familiarity with makes, comfort with private sellers, willingness to inspect maintenance records, tolerance for negotiating, and confidence in judging mechanical risk. The buyer may tell a friend that they considered “everything under twenty thousand dollars,” but they did not. They considered what they could find, understand, and bear to investigate.

Economists can call these search costs. Anthropologists might notice something else: search costs are not evenly distributed across people.

A mechanic and a first-time buyer do not face the same used-car market even if they have the same bank balance. The mechanic can inspect more cheaply because knowledge reduces the cost of evaluating what is found. A person fluent in the local language can call sellers with less friction. Someone with flexible work can travel farther. A buyer with a trusted network may learn which dealerships to avoid without paying for that information personally. A person with poor credit may not only face a higher financing cost but a narrower search space because some options are effectively inaccessible.

Markets are filtered through capabilities.

This matters because the usual language of choice can quietly assign responsibility to the chooser for options they never had a realistic way to evaluate. “Why did they not shop around?” is sometimes a reasonable question. Sometimes it is a confession that the observer has mistaken nominal availability for practical reach.

Search cost is one reason poor people often face higher effective prices. A household under financial and time pressure may have less ability to wait for sales, travel to cheaper stores, compare complicated tariffs, or absorb the risk of switching providers. The cheapest option on paper can require cash upfront, a vehicle, storage space, a credit card, a flexible schedule, or confidence with paperwork. The market rewards search, but the capacity to search is itself unequally distributed.

The pattern is visible in finance. A sophisticated investor can compare fee structures, tax implications, liquidity, counterparty exposure, and tracking error. A novice may choose the first product whose language feels legible. The novice is not necessarily less intelligent. The cost of translating the product into a meaningful comparison is simply higher.

Legibility is an economic advantage because it lowers the price of looking.

Brands understand this. A familiar brand reduces the need to investigate. So does a standard, a certification, a rating, a guarantee, a return policy, a trusted intermediary, or a recommendation from someone whose judgment already has a place in your head. These devices can improve markets by compressing information costs. They can also become tollbooths.

A platform that becomes the default place to search for restaurants, hotels, jobs, products, music, or software does more than aggregate supply. It begins to shape which supply is visible at reasonable search cost. What appears on the first screen exists in a different economic sense from what appears on the fifteenth.

Search ranking is therefore market design.

The power is easy to underestimate because ranking can look like clerical work. The platform is merely organizing what already exists. Yet organization determines exposure, and exposure affects demand, reviews, seller investment, inventory, and future ranking. The directory can become part of the market’s causal machinery.

A similar dynamic operates inside firms.

Senior executives often say they want the best ideas to rise. But ideas do not rise through vacuum. They move through reporting lines, budget cycles, presentation formats, political relationships, metrics, and the limited attention of people who have already attended six meetings that day. An idea that requires three departments to understand it has a higher search cost than an idea that maps neatly onto an existing budget category. A proposal with a familiar analogy is cheaper to process than one requiring the committee to learn a new technical language.

This is one reason organizations can be full of intelligent people and still miss nearby possibilities. The problem is not always a lack of ideas. It can be the cost of making an unfamiliar idea legible enough to survive internal search.

A company can lower that cost deliberately. It can create small discretionary budgets, rotating review panels, technical fellow tracks, internal demo days, lightweight experiment processes, or channels that allow ideas to cross the hierarchy without immediately carrying a full business case. None of these guarantees good innovation. They simply alter the economics of looking.

The same principle helps explain why some bureaucracies feel slow even when every participant is behaving sensibly.

Suppose a public agency must verify legal compliance, budget authority, procurement rules, security, accessibility, data handling, and political exposure before trying a new service. Each check may exist for a reason. Together they raise the cost of searching over policy alternatives. The result may be fewer experiments, larger bets, and stronger preference for familiar vendors or methods because familiarity lowers review cost.

Rules can reduce one kind of risk by shrinking search.

That trade can be desirable. A hospital should not treat every speculative intervention as equally testable. A nuclear plant should not optimize for improvisational freedom. The mistake is to discuss rules only in terms of what they prohibit. They also determine the width of the search frontier.

A high-reliability environment often pays deliberately to make certain searches expensive.

This brings us to a distinction that matters throughout the book: search cost and experiment cost are not the same thing.

It can be cheap to discover an option and expensive to test it. Reading about a new factory process costs little. Reconfiguring the plant may cost millions. It can also be expensive to discover an option but cheap to test it once discovered. A rare technical insight might require years of research, while the confirming experiment takes an afternoon.

Good search systems keep these costs separate.

If generating ideas is cheap but testing is expensive, the bottleneck is evaluation and selection. If testing is cheap but ideas are scarce, the system should widen generation. If both are expensive, portfolio design becomes critical because each branch consumes a meaningful share of the available future.

The consumer internet lowered some search costs dramatically. A traveler once called hotels, consulted a guidebook, or trusted a travel agent. A shopper drove between stores. A job seeker read classifieds or worked through personal networks. Digital marketplaces made comparison easier across enormous inventories.

But lower search cost in one dimension can create higher search cost in another.

A hundred options are easier to access than five were before, yet harder to evaluate. Reviews reduce uncertainty until they become numerous enough to require their own interpretation. Filters save time until the user must decide which filters matter. Recommendation systems narrow abundance but make the logic of narrowing less visible. Search becomes a layered process: first find the platform, then trust the ranking, then interpret the signals inside the ranking, then decide when to stop.

The friction did not vanish. It changed form.

This is a recurring pattern in technological progress. A system solves the old bottleneck and exposes the next one.

Printing reduced the cost of reproducing text. Catalogs and indexes became more valuable. The internet reduced the cost of publishing and distributing information. Search engines became more valuable. Social media reduced the cost of broadcasting. Feeds, recommendation systems, moderation, reputation, and attention became more valuable. Generative systems reduce the cost of producing candidate text, code, images, plans, and explanations. Evaluation becomes more valuable.

Every abundance manufactures its own scarcity.

The shift matters for strategy because companies often keep optimizing the cost that has already collapsed. They celebrate the ability to generate a hundred concepts when the organization can only test three. They install dashboards that produce more metrics when executives already lack the time to interpret the ones they have. They expand a product catalog while customers become less willing to browse it.

The rational response to cheaper search is not always more search.

Sometimes it is better stopping.

A classic search problem makes this visible. Imagine sampling prices from sellers one at a time. Each additional quote may reveal a lower price, but obtaining it costs something. The first few searches are valuable because they teach you a great deal. Later searches tend to produce smaller expected improvements. At some point the expected saving from one more quote falls below the cost of asking.

The exact stopping rule depends on assumptions, but the practical intuition is robust: the value of another look is usually highest when ignorance is greatest and falls as the search accumulates useful information.

People often violate this in both directions.

We stop too early when the first plausible answer relieves uncertainty. We continue too long when stopping would force commitment.

Those errors feel psychologically different even though they belong to the same structure. Premature stopping often masquerades as decisiveness. Excessive search often masquerades as rigor.

The second error is especially common among educated professionals because additional analysis is socially defensible. Nobody gets mocked for requesting one more scenario model after a bad outcome. The spreadsheet offers protection. Yet there are decisions for which the tenth analysis adds less value than making the decision today.

Search can become avoidance with excellent formatting.

This is where behavior complicates the clean economics. The cost of looking is not only external. Search changes the searcher.

More options can increase regret because rejected alternatives remain mentally available. More information can create an obligation to explain the decision. More comparison can move attention from whether an option is good to whether another option might be marginally better. A person who would have been satisfied with a strong choice can become dissatisfied after learning that a theoretically superior alternative existed three states away.

There is no universal law that more information makes people happier with what they choose.

That does not mean ignorance is bliss. It means information has consequences beyond prediction accuracy.

The consumer in an aisle is not a disembodied utility calculator. They may be tired, hungry, hurried, watched by children, worried about money, loyal to a family habit, skeptical of unfamiliar claims, or embarrassed to ask a question. Search cost enters through the body before it enters the equation.

This is why ethnography and economics belong in the same chapter.

The equation can tell us that a person should stop when the expected marginal benefit of another search falls below its marginal cost. Observation can tell us what those costs actually are.

A field researcher watching people choose health insurance might see that one plan is ignored because its deductible language is difficult to interpret. A researcher watching grocery shopping might notice that shoppers use package shape as a shortcut for category membership. A researcher shadowing a procurement team might discover that the cheapest vendor is avoided because resolving an invoice problem with that vendor has historically consumed hours of administrative labor.

The spreadsheet can miss these costs because they are not always priced.

Organizations miss them too.

A manager may ask why employees do not use an internal knowledge base containing the answer to a recurring problem. The answer may technically be present. But if finding it requires remembering a taxonomy, opening a slow system, trying three search terms, and determining which document is current, the information is not functionally cheap. A colleague two desks away may be the lower-cost search engine.

This is one reason informal networks persist inside highly digitized organizations. People route around expensive information systems.

The same phenomenon shapes science.

A literature may contain a relevant result without that result being practically discoverable to the researcher who needs it. Terminology differs across fields. Negative findings are less likely to be published. Old results sit behind unfamiliar notation. Data may exist without metadata that makes reuse cheap. Search tools expand access but cannot fully solve the problem of knowing what language another community used for the thing you are trying to find.

Knowledge can exist and still be expensive to locate.

Civilization pays this tax constantly.

Two laboratories solve similar problems independently. Two companies build the same internal tool. Two agencies commission reports whose conclusions already exist elsewhere. The duplication is not always waste. Independent rediscovery can validate a result. Contexts differ. Proprietary constraints matter. But some duplication happens because the cost of finding prior work exceeds the perceived cost of starting again.

This is the dark mirror of institutional memory. Too little memory repeats mistakes; too much badly organized memory makes retrieval expensive enough that people ignore it.

The design problem is not merely storage. It is lowering the cost of the next useful look.

That principle explains the value of indexes, standards, APIs, common schemas, scientific abstracts, interoperable formats, citations, trusted curators, and clear naming. Their economic role is not glamorous. They make possibilities cheaper to find and compare.

A good standard can expand search by reducing the cost of trying alternatives. A universal connector lets multiple components compete because each no longer requires a custom interface. A common data format lets tools enter the candidate set because adoption does not demand rebuilding everything around them. Compatibility lowers the price of looking.

The reverse is also true. Proprietary lock-in can raise search cost even before it raises switching cost. A buyer may not investigate alternatives if evaluating them requires migrating data, retraining staff, rewriting integrations, or renegotiating a web of contracts. The incumbent does not need to be best in every dimension. It only needs the alternatives to be expensive enough to inspect seriously.

Search costs create moats.

They also create professions.

Brokers, agents, librarians, recruiters, analysts, consultants, scouts, editors, reviewers, matchmakers, and curators all exist partly because specialized search can be cheaper than repeated amateur search. The intermediary learns the map once and sells navigation many times.

This can produce extraordinary value. It can also create conflicts because the person who controls the map can steer traffic.

A recruiter may favor roles with better fees. A broker may privilege inventory that pays a commission. A platform may rank sellers who buy advertising. A consultant may recommend problems that fit the consultant’s toolkit. Search assistance is rarely neutral because the intermediary has its own incentives.

The searcher therefore faces a recursive problem: how much should I search for the best searcher?

The joke is serious. Delegation does not remove search; it moves it up one level.

A firm hires a consultant to evaluate vendors. An investor hires a fund manager to evaluate companies. A patient relies on a physician to evaluate treatments. A consumer relies on a marketplace to evaluate products. At each layer, the cost of direct search is exchanged for the cost of trust.

Trust is a compression algorithm for search.

When trust works, it saves enormous effort. We do not personally inspect the kitchen of every restaurant, audit the code in every payment system, test every drug batch, or re-derive every scientific claim we use. Institutions allow us to inherit other people’s search and verification.

When trust fails, the hidden search cost returns all at once.

A scandal in a certification body forces buyers to investigate what the label used to settle. A failure in a ratings agency sends investors back into underlying documents. A compromised recommendation system makes users question every ranking. The institution had been carrying search on their behalf. Once credibility breaks, the burden falls back onto the individual.

This is why institutional trust has economic value even when no one sells it by the unit.

It reduces the amount of world each person must inspect personally.

The coming abundance of machine-generated possibilities makes this chapter less historical than it may appear.

If software can generate hundreds of candidate designs, legal arguments, marketing concepts, code paths, scientific hypotheses, investment theses, or strategic scenarios at low marginal cost, the old dream of eliminating scarcity has been aimed at the wrong layer. Possibilities become abundant. Attention does not.

The price of looking may rise precisely because generation gets cheaper.

A manager handed three proposals can read all three. A manager handed three hundred needs a filter. If the filter is weak, the additional generation creates noise. If the filter is strong but opaque, control moves toward whoever designed it. If the filter learns from prior choices, it can reinforce the organization’s existing search habits while appearing to widen them.

The new scarcity is not ideas. It is discriminating contact with reality.

That contact is often stubbornly physical and slow. A customer has to use the product. A bridge component has to bear load. A drug has to pass trials. A chip has to be fabricated. A policy has to encounter institutions and citizens. A strategy has to survive competitors who respond.

The machine can make the candidate set larger faster than the world can answer.

The economics of search therefore becomes the economics of civilization’s attention budget.

What deserves another experiment? What can be rejected cheaply? Which uncertain branches justify patient capital? Which decisions should use trusted shortcuts, and which are too consequential to delegate? When does a standard expand the search space, and when does it freeze it? When does a recommendation system reduce friction, and when does it quietly decide what is thinkable?

These questions are not separate from strategy. They are strategy before commitment.

The point is not to search forever. It is to understand what another look costs, what it might teach, who can afford it, and what happens to the possibilities that never become cheap enough to see.

The future contains more options than any person or institution can inspect.

The shape of that future will depend, in part, on who can afford to keep looking.
