# The Local Maximum

A climber can be going uphill and still be going to the wrong summit.

That is the uncomfortable fact hidden inside a great deal of competent work. The next step is better than the last one. The numbers improve. Customers complain less. The process gets faster. Costs fall. The team learns. Nothing in the immediate evidence says stop. Then, from a distance that the climber cannot see, the shape of the landscape becomes obvious: the path has led to a respectable hill with a much higher mountain beyond a valley.

To reach the higher ground, the climber would first have to descend.

Search problems become difficult when improvement is not a smooth road toward a single best answer. In a smooth landscape, local progress and global progress point in roughly the same direction. Make the product cheaper, the engine more efficient, the forecast more accurate, and each improvement carries you toward the best available design. But many real systems are made of interacting choices. Change one part and the value of another part changes with it. A manufacturing method that is excellent for one material may be terrible for its replacement. A sales organization built around one pricing model may make a technically superior product harder to sell. A political institution that works under one coalition can behave differently under another.

The landscape becomes rugged.

Biologists and theorists have used fitness landscapes to reason about this kind of adaptation for decades. In the simplest picture, each possible configuration occupies a location and its performance gives it a height. Nearby configurations differ by a small change. An adaptive walk moves toward fitter neighbors. Stuart Kauffman and Simon Levin examined such walks formally in 1987, including the conditions under which a sequence of locally improving moves ends at an optimum that is better than its neighbors but not necessarily best in the whole space.

The metaphor is useful because it gives shape to a common strategic failure. A local maximum is not a bad place. That is precisely why it is dangerous.

Bad strategies often die quickly. A restaurant nobody likes closes. A machine that constantly breaks gets redesigned. A software feature that no one uses is easier to remove than one that serves a profitable but shrinking customer segment. Failure generates permission to move. Moderate success can remove it.

The organization climbs until every nearby step looks worse.

Then improvement becomes a trap.

Daniel Levinthal brought rugged landscapes into organizational analysis in a particularly direct way. In his 1997 model, the attributes of an organization interact in their effects on performance. Because those choices are interdependent, adaptation can lead different organizations toward different peaks, and an organization's starting position can influence which peak it discovers. The point is not that companies literally occupy coordinates on a mathematical mountain. The point is that when choices reinforce one another, history matters.

Consider a firm whose product, distribution system, incentives, customer base, manufacturing process, accounting categories, and employee skills have grown together for twenty years. None of these is independent. The product is designed for the factory. The factory is justified by the volume supplied through the distribution system. Salespeople are paid to move what the factory makes. Customers learn to ask for what the salespeople know how to explain. Managers are promoted for improving the economics that the accounting system can see.

Now introduce an alternative that is superior only if several of those elements change together.

On a spreadsheet that changes one variable at a time, the alternative can look worse at every step.

A new product architecture may be more expensive until the factory changes. A new factory may be unjustifiable until volume shifts. Volume may not shift until the sales model changes. The sales model may not change until customers understand the new product. Each individual move descends from the current peak. The better configuration exists across a valley of coordinated inconvenience.

This is why exhortations to innovate are often weaker than they sound. They ask people to move while leaving the evaluation landscape intact.

If every proposed step must beat the current business on the current business's metrics, at the current business's scale, using the current business's capabilities, the search has been rigged in favor of the incumbent configuration.

The institution is allowed to explore only uphill.

That is not exploration. It is refinement.

Refinement is powerful. Much of civilization depends on it. Aircraft became safer through thousands of improvements that did not require reinventing flight each Tuesday. Semiconductor fabrication advances through relentless process control. A hospital should not abandon a proven sterile procedure merely because a different peak might exist. Local search is often exactly what competence requires.

The trouble begins when the environment, technology, or objective changes faster than the peak can move.

A local maximum is defined relative to a landscape. Change the landscape and yesterday's altitude can become today's slope.

This happens in markets when a new technology alters the cost structure, when regulation changes what is permitted, when consumer habits shift, when a complementary technology becomes cheap, or when a new distribution channel removes an old bottleneck. It happens in careers when a skill that once commanded scarcity becomes automated. It happens in geopolitics when a defensive doctrine optimized for one class of threat encounters another. It happens in science when an instrument makes previously inaccessible observations routine.

The unnerving part is that the old system may continue improving during the transition.

A mature technology does not necessarily stop getting better when a rival appears. Its engineers keep working. Its supply chain keeps learning. Its customers keep requesting improvements. Its economics may remain attractive for years. The old peak can rise while another mountain rises faster.

This creates a perceptual problem. People inside the incumbent system see genuine progress and infer strategic safety.

They are not imagining the progress. They are misreading its meaning.

The difference between local and global improvement is easiest to see in optimization, but it appears just as clearly in ordinary life. Suppose a person chooses jobs by asking whether each one offers a modest salary increase and greater responsibility. This rule can produce a strong career. It can also move the person deeper into a specialty that becomes increasingly expensive to leave. Every promotion increases income, reputation, and domain-specific capital. A move into a different field may require a lower title or temporary pay cut.

The valley gets deeper as the hill gets higher.

Success increases switching cost.

That is one reason people sometimes change direction after a shock. Layoffs, company failures, illness, relocation, or technological disruption can force a descent that voluntary decision-making resisted. The shock destroys some of the accumulated advantage of staying put. Once the old peak is no longer available, alternatives that previously looked irrational become thinkable.

This does not make catastrophe desirable. It reveals the option value of voluntary descent before catastrophe makes it mandatory.

The same logic explains why small experiments can matter even when they underperform the core business. Their purpose may be to map the valley.

A pilot program can answer questions that the incumbent operation cannot. How steep is the initial loss? Which capabilities transfer? Which assumptions fail? How quickly does performance improve with experience? Can two changes be bundled so that the system crosses the low ground faster? A pilot that looks mediocre as a miniature business can be valuable as reconnaissance.

This distinction is easy to lose once managers demand that every experiment justify itself as a business line.

A scout is not a small army.

Its job is to return with information.

Rugged landscapes also explain why copying a successful organization is harder than copying its visible practices. If performance comes from interactions among choices, a practice that works in one configuration may fail in another. A company sees a rival's open office, compensation system, supplier policy, product ritual, or management method and imports it as though the practice carries its value independently.

But the value may live in the combination.

A decentralized decision rule works differently when employees have deep shared training than when they do not. A just-in-time supply system behaves differently with stable suppliers than with fragile ones. A high-autonomy research culture depends on selection, norms, time horizons, and evaluation methods that may not survive transplantation. The visible practice is one coordinate. The performance comes from the neighborhood.

This is why business fashion is so often disappointing.

Observers copy the peak's landmarks without reproducing the geology.

The landscape metaphor has limits, and they matter. Real organizations do not know the full set of possible configurations. They do not receive a clean fitness score. Their objectives are multiple and contested. Competitors move. Employees learn. Customers react. A firm's actions can alter the landscape itself. There may be no fixed mountain waiting to be discovered.

Economic opportunity is not merely a static terrain over which bounded searchers crawl.

Entrepreneurs can invent new dimensions of competition. Scientists can create instruments that make new questions possible. Regulators can redraw constraints. A platform can attract complements that increase its own value. Search sometimes changes the space being searched.

So the local-maximum metaphor should not be mistaken for a complete theory of innovation.

It remains useful because it isolates one mechanism: local feedback can be truthful and still be strategically misleading.

That is a profound problem for evidence-based management. We often speak as if more feedback automatically improves decisions. Usually it helps. But feedback is sampled from the region the organization already occupies. A company tests price changes among current customers, product changes within the current architecture, advertising messages through current channels, and hires from networks its employees already know.

The evidence becomes increasingly precise about the neighborhood.

Precision is not coverage.

A map can become exquisitely accurate and remain small.

This is where local maxima meet cognition. People do not search abstract spaces uniformly. We generate possibilities from memory, analogy, language, social networks, professional training, and available tools. The alternatives that feel adjacent are partly psychological. A chemist and a financier looking at the same industrial problem may inhabit different neighborhoods because different moves occur to them.

Expertise changes the topology of search.

It makes some distant possibilities feel near. A master technician sees adjustments a novice would never consider. A lawyer recognizes doctrinal routes that are invisible to a layperson. Expertise therefore steepens useful paths within a domain.

It can also make other routes harder to imagine.

The expert knows why many naive ideas fail. This saves enormous time. Yet the reasons an idea failed ten years ago may depend on a constraint that has since disappeared. Component prices fall. computing becomes cheap. regulations change. infrastructure spreads. consumer expectations move. The old impossibility remains encoded in professional intuition after the landscape has shifted.

Knowledge can preserve obsolete valleys.

This is one reason outsiders occasionally find opportunities insiders dismissed. The outsider is missing useful knowledge, but also missing some inherited prohibitions. Most outsider ideas fail for reasons insiders understand. A few survive because the outsider unknowingly retests a boundary condition that changed.

The romantic version says ignorance is creative.

Usually ignorance is just ignorance.

The more interesting lesson is that search systems need ways to revisit old impossibilities without forgetting why they were once impossible.

That requires memory with timestamps.

An organization should not merely record that an approach failed. It should record the conditions under which it failed: material cost, computing limit, customer behavior, legal restriction, technical dependency, scale requirement. Then a change in one of those conditions can trigger a new search rather than relying on someone to rediscover the entire argument from scratch.

Failure becomes more useful when its boundary conditions are preserved.

The alternative is institutional folklore. “We tried that.” Three words close a door. Nobody remembers whether “that” means the same technology, the same customer, the same decade, or simply the same noun.

A good search culture asks a more irritating question: what would have to be different for the old failure to become worth trying again?

That question does not commit the organization to descent. It measures the valley.

There are several ways to escape a local maximum, and none is free.

One is random variation: make moves that are not justified by immediate improvement and see what happens. Biological evolution can generate variation without foresight. Organizations can approximate this through discretionary projects, rotation, independent teams, research budgets, acquisitions, or deliberately diverse portfolios. The cost is obvious. Most deviations from a well-adapted configuration are worse.

Another is recombination. Instead of inventing from nothing, combine elements that evolved in different neighborhoods. A manufacturing technique from one industry meets a material from another. A financial mechanism migrates into software. A scientific method developed for one organism becomes useful in another. Recombination can cross valleys because the imported component arrives with some evidence from elsewhere.

A third is representation change.

Sometimes the landscape looks rugged because the problem has been described badly. Change the unit of analysis and a route appears. A company that asks how to sell more drills searches differently from one that asks how customers make holes. A city that asks how to move more cars searches differently from one that asks how to move more people. The new description does not guarantee a better answer, but it changes which moves count as adjacent.

Representation is a search technology.

This is why metaphors matter in technical and strategic work. They are not merely decorative language. A good analogy imports a neighborhood structure from another problem. It says: perhaps these things that look far apart are actually neighbors if we describe them differently.

The risk, of course, is that the analogy is wrong.

Crossing valleys requires tolerance for temporary deterioration, but temporary deterioration is also what genuine decline looks like.

That is the governance problem.

How does an institution distinguish a necessary descent toward a higher peak from an expensive march into a swamp?

There is no universal answer. The distinction has to be made with staged commitments, explicit hypotheses, milestones that test the logic of the new configuration, and limits on how much can be lost before evidence improves. The point of a protected experiment is not to suspend judgment. It is to use a different judgment schedule.

A mature business can be evaluated on current output because its model is known. A valley-crossing project must be evaluated partly on whether the assumptions needed for the far side are becoming more plausible.

This changes the question from “Is it profitable yet?” to “What did we need to learn by now, and did we learn it?”

The second question is not softer.

Done properly, it is harsher. It denies weak projects the excuse that all innovation takes time. A project that cannot produce the information its own thesis requires is not heroically crossing a valley. It may simply be lost.

The discipline is to tolerate the right kind of bad news.

An experiment may show low revenue while proving that customers use the product repeatedly. A prototype may be expensive while demonstrating a technical capability that was previously uncertain. A new distribution model may have poor margins while revealing dramatically lower acquisition cost. These are not victories, but they can be evidence that the shape of the distant peak is becoming clearer.

Conversely, a project can hit an early revenue target by selling to friendly customers while failing to validate the mechanism that would allow it to scale. Local success can occur inside an exploratory project too.

Local maxima nest.

That is why search needs recursive skepticism. At every level, the system should ask whether it is improving the objective or merely improving the nearest proxy for the objective.

Markets sometimes help by running many searches in parallel. Different firms begin from different positions, hold different beliefs, recruit different people, and die at different peaks. One firm's valley is another firm's starting point. Population-level variation can therefore discover configurations that no single incumbent would rationally reach through local adaptation.

This is one of competition's epistemic functions.

It does not make markets omniscient. Capital can herd. Narratives synchronize. Regulation can favor incumbents. Network effects can lock populations onto a mediocre standard. Investors can punish valley-crossing before evidence arrives. But decentralized entry creates multiple starting points, and multiple starting points matter when landscapes contain multiple peaks.

The same principle applies inside large organizations. Independent teams are valuable not only because they work in parallel but because they can begin from different assumptions. If every team shares the same architecture, metrics, customer definition, and executive sponsor, ten teams may be ten climbers tied to the same rope.

Diversity in search is diversity of starting points and representations, not merely headcount.

There is a political consequence here.

People who prosper on the current peak often control the resources required to cross the valley.

This is not necessarily corruption. Their judgment was validated by success. They know the current system. They bear responsibility for its output. Asking them to fund a configuration that initially makes their assets less valuable can be asking them to act against both evidence and identity.

Institutions therefore need mechanisms that do not require the current peak to vote itself out of existence.

Venture funds, autonomous research groups, constitutional constraints, competing jurisdictions, academic tenure, independent grant programs, internal capital markets, spinouts, and protected pilot programs all solve different problems, imperfectly, but they share a structural purpose: they create pockets in which search can continue without winning every comparison against the incumbent on day one.

The design challenge is to grant enough independence for a different peak to be reachable without granting so much insulation that bad ideas become immortal.

That balance cannot be solved by culture alone.

It needs budgets, deadlines, decision rights, evidence standards, and an exit path.

The local maximum is therefore not just an optimization concept. It is a theory of why rational improvement can produce strategic blindness.

We are drawn to stories in which failure causes collapse. More often, the interesting systems fail after long periods of competence. Their processes work. Their people are skilled. Their metrics improve. Their customers are real. They continue climbing.

The tragedy is geometric.

They reach the top of the hill they know how to climb.

From there every nearby move is down.

The next search problem is not whether to work harder. It is whether the system can recognize that descent may be information, that temporary loss may purchase access to a different neighborhood, and that the evidence available on one summit cannot tell you the height of a mountain you have never visited.

That requires a peculiar kind of confidence: enough confidence to exploit what works, and enough doubt to leave altitude on the table.

The consumer standing in an aisle faces a smaller version of the same problem. Familiar brands, shelf placement, habit, memory, and the cost of comparison turn a supermarket into a search landscape of its own. The mountains there are not factories or technologies. They are attention, trust, and choice.
