# The Politics of What Gets Tried

A civilization never searches everywhere at once.

It says that it wants cures, cleaner energy, safer streets, better schools, stronger companies, more productive workers, more reliable institutions, and new technologies. But aspiration is not a search strategy. Someone still decides which laboratories receive grants, which diseases get trials, which neighborhoods get pilots, which entrepreneurs get meetings, which prototypes get procurement contracts, and which ideas remain one page in a notebook.

Those decisions are political even when no politician is in the room.

Politics, in this sense, is not merely elections or parties. It is the allocation of scarce permission. A research council decides that one proposal deserves five years of protected time while another does not. A hospital ethics board decides which experiment may involve patients. A venture partnership decides which founder gets another eighteen months of runway. A standards body decides which technical path becomes legible to an industry. A ministry writes a procurement rule that determines which firms can plausibly bid. A university chooses which field receives a faculty line. A company gives one team access to production traffic and another team a sandbox nobody uses.

The search frontier is built out of these permissions.

Chapter 17 argued that cheap generation makes verification more valuable. But before verification comes selection. A system can generate a thousand possibilities and still test only ten. The politics of search begins in the gap between those two numbers.

That gap cannot be eliminated by saying “pick the best.” Before an experiment runs, “best” is partly a forecast. The evidence that would settle the argument does not yet exist. Selection therefore depends on beliefs about importance, feasibility, novelty, legitimacy, risk, constituency, and timing. Different institutions weight those dimensions differently, and the weights change what kinds of futures can even gather evidence.

Consider the National Science Foundation.

Its current merit-review system asks reviewers to judge proposals on intellectual merit and broader impacts. NSF also says that, when it builds a portfolio, it may consider factors such as different approaches to important questions, potential for high-risk transformational advances, capacity building in promising areas, and special program objectives. That description is useful because it makes an often-hidden fact explicit: a funding agency is not only ranking isolated papers. It is constructing a portfolio of searches.

A portfolio implies choice among unlike things.

One project may be technically safer but incremental. Another may be fragile but capable of changing a field. A third may be scientifically strong and also build an institution in a region with little research infrastructure. A fourth may be excellent by disciplinary standards but redundant with work already funded. These are not all comparisons on a single ruler.

The organization has to decide what kind of search system it wants to be.

The National Institutes of Health makes the same issue visible from another angle. For many research project grants with application due dates on or after January 25, 2025, NIH reorganized peer review around three factors: importance of the research, rigor and feasibility, and whether the investigators and environment have the needed expertise and resources. NIH says part of the rationale was to reduce the chance that general scientific reputation would exert undue influence on review.

That reform matters because reputation is itself a search prior.

A famous investigator from a famous institution may be a good bet. Experience, equipment, collaborators, and an established record are real information. But if reputation becomes too strong a proxy for future value, the system can repeatedly search near places where it has already found success. The prior becomes self-reinforcing. Prestigious groups win resources, which create results, which strengthen the case for more resources.

None of this requires corruption.

The loop can emerge from sensible decisions made one proposal at a time.

This is a recurring pattern in search systems. Local rationality can produce global narrowness.

A venture investor who prefers founders with prior exits is not irrational. A journal editor who sends a paper to reviewers already known in the field is not irrational. A procurement officer who favors vendors with a record of delivery is not irrational. A manager who gives the critical project to the engineer who has succeeded before is not irrational.

But if every gatekeeper uses the same shortcut, the system may keep asking the future to resemble the past.

The difficulty is that the alternative can also fail.

Novelty is not automatically value. Outsiders are not automatically right. High variance includes bad outcomes by definition. An institution that funds only unusual ideas will not become visionary; it may simply become careless. The politics of what gets tried is therefore not a morality play in which brave rebels face timid bureaucrats. Bureaucracies often exist because previous experiments hurt people, wasted money, or produced claims that could not survive scrutiny.

Search has externalities.

A failed bench experiment may cost little beyond time and reagents. A failed bridge design can kill. A failed financial experiment can transmit losses to people who never consented to participate. A failed medical intervention can injure the patient who made the uncertainty real. A failed labor policy can alter livelihoods. The person selecting experiments is not always choosing only how to spend the experimenter's budget.

That is why institutions build gates.

The interesting question is what the gates reward.

DARPA provides one unusually clear statement of a selection philosophy. The agency uses the Heilmeier Catechism, a set of questions associated with former director George Heilmeier. The questions ask, among other things, what a program is trying to do, how the problem is handled today, what is new, who cares, what risks exist, what the effort will cost, how long it will take, and what mid-course and final tests will establish success.

The brilliance of those questions is not that they identify winners. Nothing can do that reliably in advance. Their value is that they force a proposal to expose its theory of search.

What is the current local maximum? Why might this route escape it? What evidence would tell us that the route is failing? What would success actually change? How much uncertainty are we buying, and by when should reality begin paying us back?

A strong selection system does not merely ask whether an idea sounds exciting. It asks whether the experiment is designed so that excitement can become knowledge.

This distinction separates speculation from exploration.

Speculation is a story about what might be true. Exploration pays for contact with reality.

Governments, firms, and foundations often blur the two because proposals are necessarily written before results exist. The applicant must describe a future persuasively enough to win resources. That creates an odd literary problem inside technical institutions: uncertainty has to be packaged as confidence.

A grant proposal cannot simply say, “We have no idea whether this will work, which is why it is worth finding out.” Reviewers need a rationale, methods, competence, milestones, and a reason to believe the work is not random. Yet if the proposal describes the outcome too confidently, it begins to sound as though the answer is already known.

Every serious research funder lives inside that contradiction.

The safest proposal often has enough preliminary evidence to look likely to succeed. But the more certain the result, the less informational value the experiment may contain. A system that rewards only feasibility can drift toward demonstrations of what was already probable.

Institutions know this, which is why they periodically create explicit exceptions.

NIH's High-Risk, High-Reward Research program says it supports unusually creative work with potential for broad impact, including ideas that may be too risky or too early to fare well in traditional peer review. Some of its awards do not require preliminary data. The existence of such programs is revealing. They are not simply additional funding categories. They are patches applied to a known tendency of mature selection systems: conventional evaluation can underprice work that lacks the signals conventional evaluation is built to read.

This is not unique to science.

A bank can evaluate a borrower more easily when there is a credit history. An employer can evaluate a worker more easily when there is directly relevant experience. An investor can evaluate a business more easily when there is revenue. A publisher can evaluate an author more easily when there are prior sales. A government buyer can evaluate a contractor more easily when the contractor has already performed government work.

Evidence reduces uncertainty, but requiring evidence can make first evidence hard to obtain.

Every search system has some version of this cold-start problem.

Who gets to try before they have proof that trying them is safe?

That question determines more than fairness. It determines variety.

If only already-legible actors can gain access to experiments, the system narrows its sources of hypotheses. People from different industries, institutions, classes, regions, and intellectual traditions often notice different anomalies because they occupy different parts of the world. Excluding them can remove not only participants but questions.

The lost question is harder to see than the rejected proposal.

A funding database can show what was declined. It cannot easily show the experiments nobody bothered to propose because the applicant correctly anticipated that the idea would not fit the call, the category, the budget, the credential expectations, or the political moment.

Search systems therefore shape themselves upstream of formal selection.

Language matters. Categories matter. Deadlines matter. Eligibility rules matter. The size of an award matters. Whether preliminary data are required matters. Whether an application is judged by disciplinary peers or a cross-functional panel matters. Whether failure will damage a career matters.

These details sound administrative because administration is where a surprising amount of politics hides.

Suppose two agencies both claim to support innovation.

The first offers small, rapid grants, permits uncertain methods, and evaluates progress after six months. The second offers large five-year awards but requires extensive preliminary evidence, institutional cost sharing, and a detailed implementation plan. Both may fund excellent work. But they will attract different searches.

The first can support cheap probes. The second favors searches that are mature enough to be described as programs.

Neither is universally better.

Some problems need continuity, large teams, specialized infrastructure, and years of patient work. Particle physics cannot be financed as a weekend hackathon. Clinical trials cannot be reduced to a thousand tiny grants. Large telescopes, semiconductor fabs, energy systems, and longitudinal studies require commitments that would be impossible if every uncertainty triggered a funding reset.

Long commitments let a search travel through valleys.

That matters because Chapter 4's local maximum problem has a political version. If funders demand visible improvement every quarter, projects may be trapped on slopes that provide immediate feedback. Some important searches become worse before they become better. Instruments have to be built. Datasets have to be assembled. teams have to learn to work together. Negative early results may reveal that the original path was wrong without revealing the replacement yet.

A patient funder can buy distance from the nearest hill.

But patience creates agency problems. A project protected from short-term judgment can also become insulated from reality. Its leaders can reinterpret every disappointment as evidence that more funding is needed. Institutions can become constituencies for their own continuation.

The same protection that enables deep search can shelter dead search.

This is why selection cannot be separated from stopping.

Chapter 19 will deal with stopping directly, but the politics begins earlier. A project that acquires employees, suppliers, facilities, congressional districts, prestige, and an internal mythology is no longer merely a hypothesis. It becomes an organization. Ending it then imposes costs on identifiable people, while the benefits of reallocating resources are diffuse and hypothetical.

The future has weak lobbyists.

Existing searches do not.

This asymmetry helps explain why search portfolios can become sticky. It is psychologically difficult to take money from a known team pursuing a known problem and give it to an uncertain team pursuing an uncertain possibility. The existing project can show milestones, buildings, publications, and relationships. The alternative can show a proposal.

One side arrives with receipts. The other arrives with imagination.

Markets are often praised as a way around this problem because no single authority decides what gets tried. Chapter 9 described that virtue: distributed actors can place different bets with local information. But markets do not abolish the politics of selection. They move it.

Capital markets decide which firms can finance losses. Customers decide which experiments produce revenue soon enough to survive. Platforms decide who gets distribution. Insurers decide which risks are coverable. Regulators decide what can be sold. Labor markets determine which teams can assemble. Property rights determine who can exclude competitors from returns.

A market is a search institution with many selectors.

That pluralism is valuable, but it is not neutral.

Searches with clear property rights and near-term paying customers often attract capital more easily than searches whose benefits are diffuse, delayed, or difficult to appropriate. Basic knowledge is the classic case: an insight can create enormous social value while being difficult for its discoverer to capture. Pollution reduction may benefit people who are not the buyer. Pandemic preparedness may look expensive precisely when it is working. Public infrastructure often produces value through networks rather than a single transaction.

This is one reason public funding exists: some socially valuable searches do not generate a private revenue stream at the moment the experiment must be financed.

Public funding introduces a different distortion. The selector now answers, directly or indirectly, to law, budgets, public legitimacy, and political leadership. Priorities can change with administrations, emergencies, geopolitical competition, or public outrage. Research institutions may experience this volatility as interference. Voters may experience insulation from it as unaccountable technocracy.

Both views contain something true.

A society has a legitimate interest in how public resources are used. It also has an interest in allowing experts enough independence to pursue work whose value is not obvious on an election timetable.

The search frontier sits between those clocks.

Some discoveries arrive on political time. A crash program can mobilize resources rapidly when a threat is clear. Others require long stretches in which the public case is weak because the result does not exist yet.

An institution capable of only the first kind of search will be reactive. An institution capable of only the second may become detached from public purpose.

The design problem is portfolio governance.

How much resource goes to exploitation of established lines? How much to adjacent exploration? How much to strange bets that may fail completely? How many selectors are allowed to disagree? How reversible are their decisions? What evidence is required before continuation? How is failure recorded? Can a project that loses one competition return in a different form, or does rejection become a permanent label?

These questions look procedural. Together they define a civilization's appetite for surprise.

A resilient search system usually needs more than one gate.

If a single committee, metric, market, model, or ideology controls access to experimentation, its blind spots become the frontier's blind spots. Multiple selectors can be wasteful. They may duplicate projects, fund competing standards, or support ideas another institution has already rejected. That apparent inefficiency can be the price of epistemic diversity.

Redundancy lets one gatekeeper be wrong.

Science benefits when governments, universities, philanthropies, companies, and researchers can sometimes pursue overlapping or contradictory programs. Entrepreneurship benefits when more than one kind of investor exists. Art benefits when creators can reach audiences through more than one distributor. Public policy benefits when cities and states can sometimes test approaches before national adoption.

The point is not that decentralization always wins. Fragmentation can prevent coordination, starve infrastructure, and leave important collective problems undersupplied. The point is narrower: a search system should hesitate before making one selector universal.

Monocultures are efficient until the criterion is wrong.

This is also true inside organizations.

A chief executive who personally approves every experiment may initially improve discipline. Soon teams learn the chief executive's taste. Proposals begin to resemble previous approvals. People pre-filter themselves. The formal decision remains centralized, but the larger loss happens before the meeting: ideas that do not look legible to the selector stop being developed.

A company can become intellectually homogeneous without ever issuing an order to conform.

The cure is not a suggestion box.

Search diversity requires budgets, authority, and protected routes to reality. An employee who can submit an idea but cannot get data, customers, compute, equipment, legal review, or a small experimental budget has been given expression without search capacity.

Permission has to reach the experiment.

This is why small discretionary budgets can matter disproportionately. They lower the political cost of being wrong. A manager who must defend a million-dollar bet will favor projects with a strong narrative and visible constituency. A ten-thousand-dollar probe can be approved with less theater. Cheap experiments allow institutions to postpone political commitment until after some evidence exists.

The best response to disagreement is often not a bigger argument. It is a cheaper test.

This principle has limits. Some disputes cannot be tested cheaply. Some experiments are unethical. Some outcomes take decades. Some effects spill across borders. Some systems change behavior merely because they are being tested. But where a reversible probe exists, reducing its cost can lower the stakes of selection.

That changes politics.

When experiments are expensive, gatekeepers must predict. When experiments are cheap, they can measure sooner.

The shift from prediction to evidence is one of the deepest benefits of lowering search cost.

Yet cheap generation alone does not accomplish it. A model may create policy ideas for pennies while the pilot still requires a city, staff, procurement, legal authority, and public trust. A machine may propose molecules cheaply while experimental validation remains constrained by laboratory capacity. A software system may generate product concepts while access to customers remains controlled by a sales organization.

The scarce resource moves.

Politics follows it.

This is the broader lesson of the search frontier. Power accumulates around whichever stage cannot be cheaply replicated.

When ideas are scarce, idea generators matter. When capital is scarce, financiers matter. When distribution is scarce, platforms matter. When verification is scarce, evaluators matter. When permission to run consequential experiments is scarce, gatekeepers matter.

Technological progress can therefore change politics without changing any constitution. It changes which bottleneck allocates the future.

As generation becomes abundant, the most consequential institutions may be those that decide what reaches reality.

That makes their design a first-order problem.

We should want selectors that can explain their criteria without pretending the criteria are complete. We should want portfolios that reserve space for both dependable progress and genuine surprise. We should want failed experiments to remain informative rather than career-ending. We should want more than one path to permission where duplication is tolerable, and stronger coordination where fragmented action creates danger. We should want evidence standards proportional to consequence, and small probes wherever small probes can replace large arguments.

Most of all, we should notice that “what should we try?” is never answered only by knowledge.

It is answered by institutions deciding whose uncertainty is worth financing.

Every unopened grant application, uncalled founder, rejected pilot, delayed trial, unfunded prototype, and protected incumbent changes the map a little. Some of those decisions are wise. Some prevent harm. Some conserve scarce resources for better uses. Some quietly remove futures that nobody will ever know were available.

That last category is the hardest to govern because it leaves no wreckage.

A failed experiment produces evidence. An experiment never permitted produces absence.

Civilizations become path dependent not only through the things they choose, but through the things they repeatedly decline to test.

The politics of search is the politics of that invisible remainder.
