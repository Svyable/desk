# Science and the Cost of a Negative Result

A laboratory can spend three years learning that something does not work.

This sounds, at first, like the bleakest possible description of scientific progress. A grant is awarded. Equipment is ordered. Postdocs are hired. Samples are prepared. The experiment is calibrated, run, rerun, repaired, argued over, and finally understood well enough for the team to say that the effect they hoped to see is not there—or at least not there at the scale their apparatus could detect.

Nothing discovered. Nothing invented. No triumphant graph bending away from the expected line.

Except that a portion of the possibility space has just become smaller.

The distinction is easy to miss because science has two economies operating at once. There is the economy of knowledge, in which a well-designed negative result can be genuinely valuable, and the economy of careers, journals, grants, prestige, and attention, in which the same result may be awkward merchandise. The first economy wants uncertainty reduced. The second often rewards surprise.

A search system becomes strange when the information it most needs is expensive to produce and unrewarding to report.

Consider what a good null result actually contains. It is not merely the sentence “we tried and failed.” The apparatus has to be capable of seeing the effect if the effect exists at the relevant scale. The controls have to behave. The measurement has to be sensitive enough. The sample has to represent what the claim is about. The analysis has to distinguish absence of evidence from evidence that meaningfully constrains the hypothesis.

That last distinction is where casual talk about negative results goes wrong. A non-significant result can mean that an effect is absent. It can also mean that the experiment was too small, too noisy, badly aimed, poorly measured, or unlucky. Science cannot treat every failure to reject a null hypothesis as a useful demolition of the alternative.

A negative result earns its value.

In particle physics this logic is unusually visible because searches are often designed to end in exclusion limits. A detector looks for a particle, decay, or interaction and does not simply announce that nothing happened. It says, more carefully, that under specified assumptions and within specified regions of parameter space, an effect above a certain strength would probably have appeared. The absence of that appearance cuts away territory.

An old paper in *Physical Review D* made the structure explicit in its title: “Lack of low-density cosmic-ray tracks.” The authors had searched cloud-chamber photographs for fractionally charged particles. They did not find them. Their result was not an empty page. It was a set of upper bounds on the flux such particles could have had while remaining consistent with what the apparatus observed.

The search returned a fence.

This is one of the deepest functions of science. A successful experiment does not always reveal the thing you were looking for. Sometimes it makes the world less permissive.

The effect compounds. A theory may begin with a broad range of possible parameter values. One experiment excludes a corner. Another rules out a band. A third finds that a proposed mechanism would have produced a signal already absent from existing data. Researchers redesign the next experiment around the surviving region. Instruments become more sensitive. The search moves.

No individual negative result has to be glamorous for the frontier to change.

This is why the word “failure” needs care. An experiment can fail technically: the detector breaks, the sample is contaminated, the intervention is not delivered, the data cannot answer the question. Or an experiment can work perfectly and fail to confirm the hypothesis. Those are nearly opposite outcomes from the standpoint of search.

A technical failure consumes resources without reliably shrinking uncertainty. A clean negative result consumes resources and buys information.

The trouble is that our institutions do not always price the difference correctly.

Publication bias has been documented for decades. Kay Dickersin described it in 1990 as a tendency for investigators, reviewers, and editors to submit or accept manuscripts partly according to the direction or strength of their findings. The concern was not aesthetic. If positive studies are more likely to enter the literature, then anyone reading the literature sees a distorted sample of the experiments that were actually run.

The missing experiments do not vanish from the history of science. They vanish from the map available to the next searcher.

Imagine ten teams test variants of the same idea. Eight obtain results that do not support it and move on. Two obtain positive results, perhaps because the effect is real under particular conditions, perhaps because of noise, flexible analysis, measurement error, or chance. If only the two positive studies become visible, a new team may rationally conclude that the idea looks unusually promising. It spends money repeating territory that the scientific community has, in some distributed sense, already explored.

The tragedy is not only false belief. It is duplicate search.

The file drawer is a memory failure.

Scientific progress depends on forgetting enormous amounts of detail. No researcher can read every lab notebook, abandoned analysis, failed synthesis, unusable reagent, or discarded prototype. Most of that information should disappear. Search requires compression. The question is what deserves to survive.

A result deserves to survive when another competent searcher would make a different decision if they knew it.

That criterion is broader than publishability and narrower than total archival preservation. It asks whether the result changes the expected value of a future experiment. A carefully controlled failure to reproduce an influential effect may qualify. So may an experiment showing that a promising mechanism does not operate under a specific boundary condition. A dozen half-finished attempts with broken equipment probably do not.

This sounds obvious until incentives enter the room.

Researchers face finite time. Writing a paper is itself a search cost. A negative study may require more explanation because readers want assurance that the missing effect was not simply missed. Journals may expect novelty. Hiring and promotion committees count visible outputs. Grant applications are narratives about promising futures, not usually catalogs of hypotheses the applicant has killed. Students need papers before their contracts end.

The result is not necessarily misconduct. Often nobody decides to hide anything. The experiment simply becomes the project that never quite gets written.

A spreadsheet survives on an old drive. A postdoc leaves. The principal investigator remembers the gist but not the calibration. Five years later another laboratory repeats the work from scratch.

Civilization pays twice for the same dead end.

This is where the economics of search becomes useful. We usually discuss the cost of science in terms of salaries, instruments, reagents, compute, facilities, and time. But the cost of an experiment includes what happens to the information after the experiment ends. A result that cannot be found is, for future searchers, close to a result that never existed.

The effective cost of learning therefore depends on memory.

If an experiment costs one million dollars and its useful negative result is preserved in a form that prevents five other teams from spending one million dollars each on the same question, the social return can be large even though the original hypothesis died. If the result disappears, the scientific system repeatedly repurchases the same information.

This does not mean every null result deserves a journal article. Journals are scarce attention systems too. Filling them with every inconclusive test would create another failure: the cost of finding useful information would rise under a flood of weak negatives.

The problem is not simply “publish more.” It is “make consequential search history legible.”

Registries are one answer.

ClinicalTrials.gov and related systems attack a specific form of disappearance by registering trials and, for covered studies, creating obligations or expectations around results reporting. The important architectural move is that the existence of the search becomes visible before its outcome is known. Once the experiment has an identifier and a declared protocol, silence itself can be noticed.

That changes the information environment.

In 2015, Monique Anderson and colleagues examined results reporting for trials likely subject to the Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act. The law had created requirements for timely reporting of certain applicable clinical trials. Compliance was far from complete. The point for our purposes is not the exact percentage in one historical sample. It is that the registry makes a new question possible: where are the results from the experiments we know occurred?

Before registration, the denominator was partly invisible.

This denominator problem sits at the center of publication bias. If you see six positive papers, you need to know whether they emerged from six experiments or six hundred. Without the missing searches, the visible evidence can look stronger than it is.

Registration does not solve the entire problem. Protocols change for legitimate reasons. Trials can be exempt from particular rules. Results can be posted late or incompletely. A registry can become bureaucratic theater if nobody uses it. But it establishes a valuable principle: some searches should leave a trace before we know whether their outcome will be exciting.

Registered Reports push the principle further in scholarly publishing. In that model, a journal can evaluate the importance of a question and the quality of a proposed method before the results are known, then commit in principle to publication if the authors execute the approved study adequately. The outcome is separated, at least partly, from the decision that the work deserves an audience.

This is a clever institutional hack because it changes what is being selected.

Ordinary publication often selects a bundle: question, method, result, narrative. Registered Reports try to select the question and method first. A boring answer to a good question can still be useful.

The distinction matters more as scientific tools become capable of running more experiments.

Cheap sequencing, automated laboratories, high-throughput screening, simulation, remote sensing, large administrative datasets, and machine learning can all expand the number of hypotheses that can be tested. This sounds like an uncomplicated increase in scientific power. But generating tests faster also generates failures faster.

If the system cannot absorb negative information, cheaper experimentation can produce more repeated waste.

Imagine an automated chemistry platform that can test ten thousand reaction conditions in a week. The exciting output may be the handful that work. Yet the failed conditions contain much of the information needed to predict where the next search should go. Throwing them away because they are not publishable would be absurd. The machine itself needs the negatives to learn the shape of the landscape.

What looks like “failed data” to a human prestige system can be training data to a search system.

This is already familiar in engineering. A crash test that reveals no failure under a specified load is not embarrassing. A software test that confirms a bug is absent under a reproducible condition is not worthless. Reliability engineering accumulates information about where systems break and where they do not. The desired output is not a stream of surprises. It is a trustworthy boundary around behavior.

Science often has the same need but a different culture of reward.

Part of the difference comes from the structure of claims. Engineering frequently begins with a concrete object and a required performance envelope. Does the bridge survive this load? Does the chip meet this timing margin? Does the drug reduce this endpoint enough to justify its harms? A negative answer can force a decision.

Exploratory science often faces a more open landscape. The hypothesis can be reformulated. The assay can be questioned. The population can be narrowed. A surprising failure may suggest a new mechanism rather than terminate the old one. This flexibility is scientifically valuable, but it makes stopping difficult.

A theory rarely dies from one clean blow.

That is another reason negative results are costly: they require interpretation.

Suppose a predicted effect does not appear. The theory may be wrong. Or an auxiliary assumption may be wrong. The instrument may not access the relevant regime. The sample may differ from the one in which the theory applies. A hidden confounder may cancel the effect. The model may predict a smaller signal than the study could detect.

The searcher now has a choice. Abandon the hypothesis, modify it, or design another test.

This is not a defect unique to science. It is the general problem of learning from feedback when the world is only partially observed. A startup can blame weak sales on distribution rather than product. A general can blame a failed maneuver on weather rather than doctrine. A scientist can blame a null result on measurement rather than theory. Sometimes each is right.

The quality of a search system depends on how cheaply it can distinguish repairable failure from terminal failure.

Good experimental design is one answer. Strong controls, adequate power, preregistered outcomes, blinded analysis where appropriate, independent replication, sensitivity checks, and explicit falsification conditions make the meaning of failure sharper. They raise the chance that a negative result changes belief rather than merely provoking another excuse.

But rigor itself costs money.

A cheap experiment may produce an ambiguous negative. A decisive experiment may require a larger sample, better detector, longer follow-up, more independent laboratories, or a more expensive control condition. The scientific searcher therefore faces a version of the same stopping problem we encountered earlier: how much should we spend to learn whether the hypothesis is truly dead?

The answer depends on the stakes and the remaining uncertainty.

If a proposed treatment could affect millions of patients, an expensive definitive trial may be worth running even after several suggestive failures. If a speculative mechanism has weak prior support and repeated high-quality experiments fail to detect it, continuing may become an indulgence. If a new detector will test many hypotheses at once, its value cannot be assigned to one question alone.

Search budgets should follow information value, not emotional attachment.

That is easy to write and hard to practice because scientists are not detached Bayesian machines. A research program can become an identity, a community, a source of grants, a set of specialized skills, a laboratory full of equipment, a graduate student's dissertation, and a network of friendships. Abandoning a hypothesis can strand capital—human as well as physical.

The sunk costs are not merely psychological. They are institutional.

A laboratory built to study one phenomenon cannot instantly become a laboratory for another. A scientist whose expertise took fifteen years to acquire cannot costlessly switch fields. A funding agency that created a program has staff, advisory committees, political constituencies, and public promises. Scientific search therefore has inertia for many of the same reasons firms do.

This makes independent negative results especially important.

An outsider has different sunk costs.

Replication is often described as checking whether a result is true. It is also a way of diversifying the searcher's incentives. A laboratory that did not produce the original claim may have less reputational need to rescue it. A team using a different method can reveal whether the result depends on one apparatus or analytic convention. Independence is not a guarantee of objectivity, but it reduces correlated error.

The scientific system becomes more trustworthy when important claims can survive searchers who are not invested in their survival.

The same logic explains why adversarial collaborations can be valuable. Researchers who disagree about a claim can jointly specify an experiment whose outcomes they agree would matter. The goal is not to eliminate disagreement before the test. It is to reduce the space of post-hoc reinterpretation afterward.

A well-designed negative result is, in this sense, an agreement about what absence would mean.

There is a temptation to turn this into a morality play: heroic skeptics versus flashy positive-result hunters. That would be too simple. Positive findings are selected partly because they often contain more information. If an intervention truly changes an outcome, a new effect appears where none was expected. A weak null may leave an enormous range of possible effects intact. Journals and readers are not irrational to care about signal.

The real problem is asymmetric forgetting.

When positive outcomes are preserved with much higher probability than informative negative outcomes, the literature ceases to represent the search process that generated it. We see the survivors without seeing the trials.

This is selection bias applied to ideas.

The effect can become self-reinforcing. A published positive result attracts citations, replications, grants, and students. Those investments generate more chances for another positive result. An unpublished negative attracts almost nothing. The visible branch of the search tree grows; the invisible branches disappear.

Soon the field's history looks more inevitable than it was.

This matters for how science allocates its next dollar. Funding decisions are made from visible evidence. If the map hides dead ends, the expected return to familiar hypotheses is overstated. Novel approaches may look unnecessarily risky compared with established lines whose failures have been selectively forgotten.

Publication bias can therefore narrow the search frontier as well as distort individual claims.

The remedy is not to fetishize nulls. A scientific culture that celebrates every failed hypothesis would create its own perverse incentives. Researchers could generate endless low-quality tests of implausible ideas and demand credit for not finding anything. The useful unit is not negativity. It is information gained per unit of scarce search resource.

Some negative results are extraordinarily informative. Others barely move belief.

A null result from a tiny underpowered study may say little. A precisely estimated result that rules out any effect large enough to matter can close a practical question even if a microscopic effect remains possible. A failed replication using a different method can matter more than another successful replication using the original laboratory's exact procedure. An exclusion across a broad parameter range can redirect an entire field.

The scientific search system needs ways to distinguish these cases.

One useful question is counterfactual: if this result had been known before the experiment, would we still have run the experiment?

If the answer is no, the result has likely changed the map.

Another is whether the result changes the next experiment. Does it alter the sample, instrument, parameter range, mechanism, dose, population, model, or hypothesis worth testing? If nothing about future search changes, the informational value may be small.

A third is whether the result can be trusted outside the laboratory that produced it. Are the methods and data described well enough for someone else to understand the boundary being claimed? A negative result with an opaque protocol is difficult to reuse because future researchers cannot tell whether their proposed experiment is actually the same search.

This is where mundane research infrastructure becomes part of discovery.

Identifiers, registries, repositories, versioned code, machine-readable data, laboratory metadata, persistent links, and standardized reporting can sound like clerical concerns. They are search technology. They reduce the cost of determining what has already been tried.

A civilization with brilliant experiments and terrible memory will keep rediscovering its own ignorance.

There is a broader lesson here about automation and artificial intelligence. Systems that can generate hypotheses, code analyses, design molecules, propose experiments, and scan literatures will make the front end of scientific search cheaper. They can widen the tree of possible moves dramatically.

The obvious danger is false positives at scale. The less obvious danger is forgotten negatives at scale.

If a machine can propose a thousand plausible hypotheses before lunch, the value of knowing which nearby hypotheses have already failed rises. Otherwise the machine may simply regenerate the file drawer. Faster generation without durable negative memory creates an expensive loop disguised as acceleration.

Future scientific infrastructure may therefore need to treat failed searches as first-class objects—not because failure is noble, but because search history is data.

This will require judgment. Failed attempts can be misleading when methods were poor. Preliminary negatives can unfairly poison promising ideas. Proprietary or sensitive research cannot always be disclosed. Raw laboratory records can contain errors, private information, or uninterpretable noise. More memory is not automatically better memory.

The design problem is selective preservation.

What was tried? Under what conditions? With what sensitivity? What happened? What does the result actually rule out? How confident should another searcher be? What would need to change before trying again?

Those questions turn a dead end into a road sign.

Science has always advanced partly by learning where not to look. Michelson and Morley became famous for an experiment that did not detect the expected motion through a luminiferous ether, though the historical path from that result to later physics was far less tidy than textbook retellings suggest. Modern particle searches routinely publish constraints rather than discoveries. Clinical trials can rule out treatments that looked plausible. Drug screens eliminate compounds. Replications identify effects that do not travel well across settings. Engineers destroy prototypes so later prototypes survive.

The negative result is not the opposite of discovery.

It is one of discovery's prices.

The real waste begins when we pay that price and throw away the receipt.

A mature search institution does not demand that every experiment succeed. It demands that consequential failure leave the possibility space different from how it found it. The frontier moves when a new path opens, but also when a path closes with enough confidence that the next traveler can spend their time somewhere else.
