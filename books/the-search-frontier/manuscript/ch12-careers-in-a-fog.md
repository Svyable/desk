# Careers in a Fog

A career is one of the longest searches most people conduct, and one of the strangest, because the object of the search changes while the search is underway.

At eighteen, twenty-two, thirty, forty-five, a person is not choosing among fixed occupations with known payoffs. The person is choosing among partial descriptions of work, institutions seen mostly from the outside, skills not yet developed, colleagues not yet met, cities not yet lived in, and versions of the self that do not yet exist.

The map is blurry. The traveler is changing. The roads move.

Yet career advice is often presented as if the central problem were preference revelation: look inward, discover what you really want, then choose the matching path. That model is comforting because it suggests the answer already exists somewhere inside the chooser. Search becomes introspection.

Sometimes that is useful. A person who hates selling may save years by admitting it. A person drawn repeatedly toward mechanical problems should probably notice the pattern. But the strongest career preferences are often impossible to know in advance because they depend on experience. You may not know whether you enjoy managing until people depend on your decisions. You may not know whether research suits you until a project remains unsolved for six months. You may not know whether you like a field until you meet its actual practitioners rather than its public image.

Careers contain hidden variables.

The economist Boyan Jovanovic formalized one part of this in a 1979 model of job matching. A worker's productivity in a particular job, in the model, is not fully known before the match begins. It becomes known more precisely with tenure. Turnover follows partly because some matches reveal themselves to be poor.

That is a more realistic starting point than the fantasy of perfect foresight.

The employee does not arrive with complete knowledge of the employer. The employer does not arrive with complete knowledge of the employee. A job interview compresses months of future interaction into a few staged conversations. Both sides infer from credentials, stories, references, small tests, and impressions. Then the actual work starts and reality produces a much larger sample.

Can this person do the work at the required speed? Does the manager teach or hoard information? Does the company reward initiative or punish deviation? Is the work boring in a pleasant way or boring in a soul-eroding way? Does the employee become better at the job? Does the job become more interesting with competence? Does the commute matter more than expected? Does the prestige matter less?

The match generates data.

This means a first job is not only income. It is an experiment.

The same is true of a major, an internship, a freelance project, a volunteer role, a move to another city, a collaboration, or a side business. Each samples a region of the career landscape that cannot be inspected fully from brochures and salary tables.

Some experiments are cheap. A weekend project can reveal whether writing software is tolerable. Shadowing someone for a day can puncture a glamorous occupational image. A six-week contract can expose whether a field's everyday texture fits the stories told about it.

Other experiments are expensive. Medical training, academic specialization, military service, professional licensing, immigration, and entrepreneurial bets can consume years before enough evidence arrives to judge the match well.

This difference in experiment cost should shape career search.

When trial is cheap and uncertainty is high, search can be broad. When trial is expensive, information collected before commitment becomes more valuable. The principle sounds obvious, yet many institutions reverse it. Young people are often asked to make highly consequential educational choices before they have sampled the work those choices point toward.

The fog is thickest near the beginning, precisely when maps are often treated as destiny.

Robert Topel and Michael Ward studied job mobility and wage growth among young men and found a pattern that fits the search interpretation. In their longitudinal data, early careers involved substantial job changing, then tended to stabilize. In the published 1992 paper, the authors reported that a typical worker in their sample held seven jobs during the first ten years of labor-market experience, and that wage gains associated with job changes accounted for at least a third of early-career wage growth.

The number should not be turned into advice to hold seven jobs. The sample is historically and demographically specific, and a labor market is not a universal template for a life. What matters is the mechanism: apparently disorderly movement can be part of a process by which workers and employers discover better matches.

From the outside, exploration often looks like indecision.

That creates a social problem. Careers are judged retrospectively. Once a path works, earlier moves are edited into a coherent story. The person who moved from journalism to software to product management says each step taught the skill needed for the next. Perhaps it did. But during the first move, the third job did not yet exist as a visible destination.

Hindsight makes search look like planning.

This is why biographies can be hazardous career manuals. A successful person's path acquires narrative gravity. The accidental meeting becomes pivotal. The rejected application becomes necessary redirection. The dead-end job becomes preparation. The story may be true as memory, but it hides the branching structure that existed at the time.

For every path later called inevitable, there were adjacent paths that would have produced different identities.

The useful question is not, “What is my one true career?” It is closer to, “What experiments will reveal useful information without closing too many future doors?”

That formulation sounds less romantic. It is also kinder to reality.

A career experiment produces several kinds of information at once. It can reveal facts about the occupation, facts about a particular organization, facts about the market, and facts about the person doing the work. These should not be confused.

A miserable first job in law may mean the profession is a poor fit. It may mean the firm is badly managed. It may mean the person dislikes litigation but would enjoy contracts. It may mean the first year of a difficult craft is unpleasant before competence makes it rewarding. Search is hard because a bad observation does not identify the cause.

People frequently overgeneralize from one sample.

They work for one startup and decide startups are chaotic. They work for one bureaucracy and decide large organizations are deadening. They try one sales role and conclude they cannot sell. They encounter one toxic laboratory and leave science.

Sometimes they are right. Sometimes the local maximum is being mistaken for the landscape.

A good career search therefore needs variation. If the first sample is ambiguous and another trial is affordable, change one variable. Try the same function in a different organization. Try a different function in the same industry. Work with a better manager. Move from front-line execution to analysis. Shift from a mature company to a young one. The aim is not restless novelty. It is identification.

Scientists do not learn much from changing five variables at once. Careers are messier, but the same logic helps.

Networks matter because they determine which experiments become visible.

Mark Granovetter's 1973 paper on weak ties made this point famous in a broader sociological frame. Strong ties often connect people embedded in overlapping circles. Weak ties can bridge into other groups and carry different information. Granovetter explicitly connected this structure to mobility opportunities.

For career search, the implication is simple and easy to underestimate: close friends are often excellent sources of support and mediocre sources of novelty.

They know you well, but they may know roughly the same labor market you know. The former colleague you speak to twice a year, the neighbor who works in another industry, the person met at a conference, the alumni contact, the collaborator from a short project—these people may expose branches your immediate circle does not contain.

A career opportunity is partly an information event.

This is one reason broad social exposure can matter so much early on. Not networking in the transactional sense of collecting names, but increasing the number of occupational worlds one can actually inspect. Many careers remain invisible because nobody nearby can explain what the work consists of, how people enter, what beginners earn, which credentials matter, or what the hidden costs are.

The cultural search radius from the previous chapter becomes personal here.

A child of physicians knows more than the formal admissions requirements of medicine. The child has observed schedules, frustrations, status, vocabulary, training pathways, and professional networks for years. A first-generation applicant may confront the same official choice with a much thinner model.

The opportunity is nominally identical. The search cost is not.

Mentors can reduce that cost, but even mentoring is often described too narrowly. The best mentor does not simply tell a younger person what to choose. The mentor makes the landscape legible. Which jobs that sound similar are actually different? Which prestigious roles are mostly signaling? Which skills travel across industries? Which mistakes are recoverable? Which commitments are hard to reverse? Which doors appear closed but are not?

Good advice expands the map. Bad advice substitutes somebody else's utility function.

This distinction matters because older advisers are looking backward from a path that has already stabilized. They may recommend exploiting the assets that worked for them when the younger person's problem is still exploration.

The twenty-five-year-old and the fifty-five-year-old often face different search problems.

Early in a career, option value can be unusually important. A role that teaches a portable skill may be worth more than its immediate salary suggests. A city with dense labor-market connections may create more future branches than a higher-paying but isolated job. Working with strong colleagues can widen the set of later projects one is capable of attempting.

But option value can become an excuse for never committing.

A person can spend a decade choosing jobs for what they might lead to and never become unusually good at anything. Search has a cost not only in time but in foregone compounding.

This is where the other side of the labor literature matters. Robert Topel's work on job seniority and wages found substantial value associated with firm-specific capital. Staying can create knowledge, trust, reputation, and coordination advantages that do not travel cleanly to another employer.

A career is not improved by maximizing mobility.

Commitment creates assets that exploration cannot.

The engineer who remains on a difficult system for five years learns failure modes invisible to a newcomer. The salesperson who stays in a market builds relationships that make later conversations possible. The scholar who lives with a problem long enough learns which obvious approaches have already failed. The manager who earns trust can make changes that would be impossible during the first six months.

Depth is a search technology too.

The challenge is knowing when persistence is producing information and capability, and when it is merely increasing sunk cost.

This is hard because the signals are noisy. A difficult year can mean a path is wrong. It can also be the year before competence arrives. A promotion can mean the match is improving. It can also make leaving harder by raising the opportunity cost of experimentation.

There is no universal stopping rule.

Still, some questions are better than intuition alone.

Is the work teaching something that will matter outside this exact setting? Is responsibility expanding? Are better people willing to invest in you? Are you seeing more of the underlying system than you saw a year ago? Does growing competence make the work more interesting or merely easier? Are attractive adjacent opportunities appearing because of what you are learning? If the answer to all of these is no, tenure may be accumulating without much informational return.

Another useful question is more brutal: what evidence would make you leave?

If there is no answer, the position may have stopped being a choice.

Careers drift because default continuation is psychologically cheap. Leaving requires explanation, uncertainty, applications, negotiation, and the risk of regret. Staying often requires nothing today. A person can remain in a poor match not because the expected future is attractive but because each individual day fails to produce enough pain to trigger a search.

Search has startup costs.

The same friction that prevents wasteful job hopping can also trap people in stale matches.

Institutions understand this asymmetry when they design retention systems. Vesting schedules, deferred compensation, noncompete clauses where legal, promotion tracks, pensions, and seniority privileges all change the cost of leaving. Some of these arrangements solve real coordination problems. Employers invest more readily when relationships are durable. Workers benefit from predictable progression. But the arrangements also shape the individual's search frontier.

A golden handcuff is a search constraint with good upholstery.

This is not an argument against loyalty. Loyalty can support long projects, mutual investment, and forms of trust impossible in purely transactional markets. The question is whether loyalty remains responsive to evidence.

A healthy commitment is not a refusal to search. It is a decision to exploit a promising region while preserving enough external information to notice if the landscape changes.

That suggests a practical architecture for careers: broad peripheral awareness, selective experiments, and deep commitment where evidence accumulates.

The peripheral awareness matters even for satisfied people. Industries change. Technologies alter tasks. Organizations decline. Managers leave. Geography shifts. Health changes. A stable career can become unstable without the worker doing anything wrong.

Maintaining weak ties, reading outside one's specialty, occasionally interviewing, teaching, advising, attending gatherings, or doing small projects can function as low-cost sensing. These activities need not imply disloyalty. They are how a person keeps the map from going stale.

Search can be continuous even when switching is rare.

This is an important distinction. The choice is not between permanent wandering and permanent settlement. One can explore informationally while exploiting behaviorally.

The venture investor does something similar by watching many companies while funding few. The scientist reads broadly while running a narrow set of experiments. The firm scouts technologies while operating a stable core business.

A mature career can use the same architecture.

Chance complicates all of this because many important opportunities arrive unplanned.

Kathleen Mitchell, Al Levin, and John Krumboltz argued in 1999 for what they called planned happenstance: not the belief that careers should be left to luck, but the idea that exploratory activity can increase exposure to unexpected opportunities and make people more capable of using them.

The phrase risks sounding magical if stripped from its mechanism. Chance cannot be scheduled. Exposure can.

A person who works only with the same colleagues on the same tasks in the same institution is sampling from a narrower event distribution than a person who occasionally enters new rooms, learns an adjacent skill, helps on unfamiliar projects, or talks seriously with people outside the immediate profession.

More exposure does not guarantee better outcomes. It increases the number of opportunities for surprise.

This book began with the idea that systems differ not only in what they know but in what they search. Careers make that painfully concrete. Two equally capable people can live in different opportunity distributions because their networks, savings, passports, caregiving duties, health, cities, employers, and social expectations determine which experiments are affordable.

This is why career advice that celebrates boldness can become insulting.

Not everyone can quit to explore. The worker supporting children, parents, or medical costs faces a different downside from the worker with family wealth and a spare bedroom waiting at home. An unpaid internship is a cheap experiment only for someone who can afford unpaid time. Moving to a more promising city is not a reversible move for everyone.

Search budgets are unequal.

That inequality affects more than individual happiness. It affects which talents a society discovers.

If exploration is expensive, people with less financial slack will rationally choose narrower, safer searches. Some of those choices are wise. But at the population level, valuable matches remain untested. A person who might become an excellent engineer never samples engineering. A capable founder never leaves salaried work long enough to test a business. A gifted teacher avoids training because the income path is too fragile.

Civilization loses options before it knows they existed.

Institutions can lower these costs. Paid internships make occupational experiments available to more people. Apprenticeships generate information while producing income. Community colleges and short credentials can reduce the cost of sampling fields. Portable health insurance and benefits reduce the penalty for changing employers. Fellowships can buy exploration time. Returnships can reduce the cost of reentry after caregiving. Clear occupational information can make hidden paths legible.

These policies are often discussed in terms of fairness or labor supply. They are also search infrastructure.

They alter who gets to run experiments.

The same logic applies inside firms. Rotational programs let employees test different functions without leaving the organization. Internal talent marketplaces expose projects across departments. Temporary assignments reduce the cost of sampling a new role. A company that makes internal movement easy can keep talent that would otherwise have to exit in order to explore.

But internal mobility can also become fake exploration if every role shares the same culture, incentives, and promotion logic. The experiment changes the title while holding the important variables constant.

Real search needs informative variation.

There is another danger in treating careers as experiments: people are not laboratory mice, including when the subject is oneself. Work has relationships, duties, identity, and moral consequence. A colleague is not merely a node in a network. An employer is not merely an option. Constantly evaluating every relationship for its future value can corrode the very trust that makes long commitments productive.

Search language can become pathological if it turns life into portfolio management.

The point is not to live at arm's length from every commitment.

It is to recognize uncertainty honestly before commitment and evidence honestly after it.

A career deserves periods when search recedes into the background. Deep work requires forgetting alternatives for a while. So does raising a family, finishing a difficult apprenticeship, building a company, mastering a craft, or seeing a long institutional project through.

The mature searcher is not the person always looking for an exit.

It is the person who can commit without pretending the choice was ordained.

That attitude protects against two symmetrical errors. One is premature closure: deciding at twenty-two that an identity must be defended forever because years have already been invested. The other is compulsive reopening: treating every difficult season as proof that a better option must be elsewhere.

Both mistakes confuse uncertainty with information.

Sometimes the fog clears only by staying.

Sometimes it clears only by moving.

The craft is to design a career that keeps producing evidence.

This may be why the most useful career plans often resemble hypotheses rather than itineraries. They say: I think I may be good at this kind of problem. I think this environment may teach me something. I think this role gives me a useful combination of responsibility and reversibility. I will stay long enough for the test to mean something. Then I will update.

That is less satisfying than a calling.

It is also closer to how many good careers actually unfold.

The fog never fully disappears. By the time a person understands one part of the landscape, the person has changed enough that new questions matter. Compensation that once dominated a choice may become less important than autonomy. Prestige may fade. Geography may become central. A skill acquired for one reason may open a field that did not exist when training began.

The search objective moves because the searcher moves.

That makes a career unlike chess. There is no fixed board, no agreed evaluation function, no final position at which everyone can say the game was played correctly.

There is only a sequence of bounded commitments made under partial information.

The best we can do is widen the map enough to see meaningful alternatives, choose experiments whose downside we can bear, learn from the matches we actually enter, preserve relationships that carry different information, and remain long enough in good regions for depth to compound.

A career is not found.

It is searched, sampled, revised, and, for stretches that matter, inhabited.
