# Chapter 15 research note — Search Under Constraint

## Sources used

- Wayne Chi, Steve Chien, and Jagriti Agrawal, “Scheduling with Complex Consumptive Resources for a Planetary Rover,” *Proceedings of the International Conference on Automated Planning and Scheduling* 30(1), 2020, pp. 348–356. DOI: 10.1609/icaps.v30i1.6680. Used for the concrete claim that rover activity scheduling must account for consumptive resources and time-dependent resource interactions; the paper applies its scheduling methods to planetary-rover awake/asleep episodes and discusses the Mars 2020 onboard scheduler.
- Daniel Gaines, Heather Justice, Vincent Wong, Joseph Russino, and Steve Schaffer, “Integrated Planning and Execution for a Self-Reliant Mars Rover,” Jet Propulsion Laboratory / NASA Technical Reports Server, 2018, NTRS 20210008414. Used for the chapter’s account of rover autonomy responding to opportunity and disruption through behavioral adaptation, plan flexibility, repair, and more comprehensive replanning.
- Anuj K. Shah, Sendhil Mullainathan, and Eldar Shafir, “Some Consequences of Having Too Little,” *Science* 338(6107), 2012, pp. 682–685. DOI: 10.1126/science.1222426. Used for the experimentally supported claim that scarcity can shift attention toward pressing problems while causing neglect elsewhere.
- Anandi Mani, Sendhil Mullainathan, Eldar Shafir, and Jiaying Zhao, “Poverty Impedes Cognitive Function,” *Science* 341(6149), 2013, pp. 976–980. DOI: 10.1126/science.1238041. Used cautiously for evidence that financial concerns can consume cognitive resources; the chapter describes the mall and sugarcane-farmer study designs without converting the finding into a claim about inherent intelligence.

## Falsification note

The chapter could easily overstate a simple story in which scarcity always worsens decisions and slack always improves them. The Shah et al. experiments instead support a more conditional mechanism: scarcity can intensify attention to the immediate problem even as other concerns are neglected. That focus can sometimes be useful. The manuscript therefore treats scarcity as changing the geometry and allocation of search rather than as a universal deficit.

Likewise, the chapter does not claim that organizational slack is automatically productive. Chapter 6 already preserves counterevidence that slack can dampen problemistic search. Here the narrower claim is that resources not already irreversibly committed can have option value when new information arrives.

The rover material is used as a concrete engineering case in which resource constraints must be represented explicitly. It is not offered as evidence that human decision-making is computationally equivalent to rover scheduling.
