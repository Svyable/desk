# Chapter 20 research — A Civilization That Can Change Its Mind

## Research spine

- National Archives and Records Administration, “Article V, U.S. Constitution” and “Constitutional Amendment Process.” Authoritative constitutional text and process description. Used for the narrow institutional-design point that the U.S. Constitution contains a deliberately demanding but real mechanism for amending its own governing text: proposal through specified supermajoritarian routes and ratification by three-fourths of the states. Re-verified on 2026-08-30.
- U.S. Geological Survey, “Structured Decision Making.” Authoritative agency overview. Used for the distinction between one-time structured decisions and adaptive management for repeated decisions in which monitoring and early actions can produce learning that improves later choices. Re-verified on 2026-08-30.
- Williams, Szaro, and Shapiro, *Adaptive Management: The U.S. Department of the Interior Technical Guide* (2009), U.S. Department of the Interior / USGS. Authoritative technical guide. Used for the bounded characterization of adaptive management as structured decision making under uncertainty in which learning is a means to improved management, not an end by itself.
- Allen et al., “Adaptive management of rangeland systems” (2017), USGS Publications Warehouse record, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-46709-2_11. Used for the claim that adaptive management combines monitoring, evaluation, adjustment, and structured learning while also facing legal and governance constraints.
- Internet Engineering Task Force, “Running Code.” Official IETF material. Used for the IETF's unofficial “rough consensus and running code” mantra and for the bounded claim that IETF hackathons use implementation work to gain practical experience with existing and proposed standards and interoperability. Re-verified on 2026-08-30.

## Claims kept deliberately narrow

The constitutional example is used as an illustration of layered change costs, not as an argument that the U.S. amendment process is optimally calibrated or that constitutional rigidity always improves governance.

Adaptive management is not presented as universal managerial doctrine. The chapter uses it where decisions recur, uncertainty can be reduced through monitoring, and adjustment is institutionally possible; the cited USGS material itself notes constraints and limits.

The IETF example does not claim that “running code” settles every standards dispute or that implementation success proves security, scalability, social value, or good governance. It is used to show how implementation can provide a feedback channel distinct from rhetorical consensus.

Claims about organizational memory, slack, transition paths, reputational incentives, and migration costs are synthesis and analysis rather than statistics attributed to the cited institutions.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The strongest counterargument is that making revision easier can destroy the stability required for coordination, investment, trust, and learning. The chapter therefore rejects constant flexibility and argues for layers with different change costs: some decisions should be cheap to revise, foundational commitments deliberately harder.

A second counterargument is that institutional memory can itself block innovation through precedent and “we tried that before” reasoning. The chapter preserves this by distinguishing preservation of the conditions and failure modes of an old attempt from preservation of its verdict for all future conditions.

A third counterargument is that slack can shelter waste rather than adaptation. The chapter does not treat spare capacity as automatically productive; it argues that slack is adaptive only when it is connected to real search, testing, migration, or resilience capacity.

A fourth counterargument is that reversals can weaken legitimacy. The chapter therefore separates corrigibility from improvisation: credible revision is easier when assumptions, monitoring, evidence thresholds, and update mechanisms exist before the reversal becomes politically necessary.
