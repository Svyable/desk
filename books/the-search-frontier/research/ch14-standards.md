# Chapter 14 research note — Standards Shrink the Search Space

## Sources used

- Joseph Farrell and Garth Saloner, “Standardization, Compatibility, and Innovation,” *RAND Journal of Economics* 16(1), 1985, pp. 70–83. DOI: 10.2307/2555589. Used for the chapter’s treatment of compatibility benefits, interdependent adoption decisions, coordination problems, and the possibility that standardization can alter innovation incentives.
- Paul A. David, “Clio and the Economics of QWERTY,” *American Economic Review* 75(2), 1985, pp. 332–337. Used for the classic path-dependence account of QWERTY and the broader idea that historical sequence and increasing returns can influence durable standards.
- S. J. Liebowitz and Stephen E. Margolis, “The Fable of the Keys,” *Journal of Law & Economics* 33(1), 1990, pp. 1–25. DOI: 10.1086/467198. Used as direct counterevidence to a simplistic QWERTY lock-in story; the chapter preserves their challenge to claims that Dvorak was clearly superior or that persistence alone demonstrates inefficient lock-in.

## Falsification note

The chapter’s strongest tempting claim is that durable standards are often inefficient accidents. The QWERTY dispute is kept specifically because the evidence does not support treating persistence as proof of inferiority. A standard may persist because transition costs and compatibility benefits are real, because the challenger is only modestly better, or because the historical claim of technical superiority is weak. The chapter therefore treats standards as transition and coordination problems rather than assuming either that incumbency proves merit or that path dependence proves failure.

The container opening is used as an illustrative mechanism, not as a claim that containerization’s history can be reduced to dimensional agreement alone. The argument is limited to the search effect of standardized interfaces: repeated compatibility decisions can be removed from downstream actors, while the broader historical causes and distributional effects of containerization are outside this chapter’s evidentiary burden.
