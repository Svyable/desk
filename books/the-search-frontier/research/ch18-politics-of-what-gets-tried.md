# Chapter 18 research — The Politics of What Gets Tried

## Research spine

- U.S. National Science Foundation, “How We Make Funding Decisions — Funding at NSF.” Authoritative current description of NSF merit review. Used for the two core review criteria, intellectual merit and broader impacts, and for the bounded claim that portfolio construction may also consider different approaches, high-risk transformational potential, capacity building, and program objectives. Re-verified on 2026-08-30.
- U.S. National Science Foundation, “Overview of the NSF Proposal and Award Process.” Authoritative current process description. Used for the claim that NSF explicitly encourages high-risk, high-payoff science and engineering and that expert review feeds, but does not mechanically determine, funding decisions. Re-verified on 2026-08-30.
- National Institutes of Health, “Simplified Peer Review Framework.” Authoritative NIH grants policy. Used for the current review structure applying to most research project grant applications with due dates on or after January 25, 2025: Importance of the Research; Rigor and Feasibility; Expertise and Resources. NIH states that the restructuring is intended in part to reduce the potential for general scientific reputation to exert undue influence on review. Re-verified on 2026-08-30.
- NIH Common Fund, “High-Risk, High-Reward Research.” Authoritative program description. Used for the narrow claim that NIH maintains dedicated programs for unusually innovative work that may be too risky or too early to fare well in traditional peer review, and that preliminary data are not required for the umbrella program's opportunities as described by NIH. Re-verified on 2026-08-30.
- Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, “The Heilmeier Catechism.” Authoritative DARPA description. Used for the eight-question program-assessment framework concerning objectives, current practice, novelty, relevance, risk, cost, duration, and tests of success. Re-verified on 2026-08-30.

## Claims kept deliberately narrow

The chapter does not claim that peer review is generally conservative, biased, or ineffective as a universal empirical proposition. Instead it shows that mature institutions themselves design explicit mechanisms to balance feasibility, importance, novelty, portfolio diversity, and high-risk work.

The discussion of NIH reputation effects follows NIH's stated rationale for its simplified framework. It does not assert that the reform has already eliminated reputational influence or quantify the size of such influence.

DARPA is used as an example of an institution making its theory of program selection unusually explicit. The chapter does not claim that use of the Heilmeier Catechism guarantees successful innovation or that DARPA's model transfers unchanged to medicine, basic science, venture capital, or public policy.

The market-funding discussion is economic analysis rather than a claim that private markets systematically underfund every public-good problem. The relevant point is narrower: searches whose benefits are diffuse, delayed, or difficult to appropriate can be harder to finance through direct private returns alone.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The strongest counterargument is that stricter selection and reliance on track records can be rational rather than merely exclusionary. Equipment, prior execution, institutional support, and demonstrated competence contain real information about whether a difficult project can be completed. The chapter incorporates this point and rejects the simple outsider-good / bureaucracy-bad story.

A second counterargument is that decentralized selection creates duplication, fragmentation, incompatible standards, and underinvestment in shared infrastructure. The chapter therefore treats multiple gatekeepers as valuable mainly where duplication and disagreement are tolerable, while preserving the case for coordination in high-cost or high-externality domains.

A third counterargument is that long-horizon funding intended to protect exploratory work can shelter failing programs. The chapter explicitly links selection to stopping: protection from short-term metrics can enable deep search, but it can also create constituencies that make dead searches politically difficult to end.
