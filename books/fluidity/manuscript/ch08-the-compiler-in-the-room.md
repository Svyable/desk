# The Compiler in the Room

Mathematicians do not usually describe their colleagues as compilers.

They should try it once.

Every research group has, if it is lucky, a person who listens to an argument and refuses to let the speaker cross a gap by changing tone. The hand goes up. Which topology? Is that convergence strong or weak? Where did the boundary term go? Does the constant depend on the approximation parameter? You used compactness there—what is compact? Is the embedding valid at the endpoint? That function is only defined almost everywhere; what exactly are you evaluating?

The questions can feel pedantic until one of them kills the proof.

A proof assistant turns that colleague into infrastructure.

The analogy to a compiler is not exact, but it is useful. Source code that looks plausible to a programmer still has to satisfy a language. Types must match. Names must resolve. Syntax must parse. Dependencies must exist. The compiler does not accept a program because the intention is obvious.

Formal mathematics adds a deeper contract. The checker must establish that the proof term inhabits the type corresponding to the theorem. A tactic can be clever, probabilistic, generated by a language model, or written by a human. None of that gives it authority. The small trusted kernel checks the resulting object.

This is a beautiful arrangement for AI because it separates creativity from permission.

Let the model guess.

Let the kernel refuse.

The standard fear around generative systems is that they hallucinate. In ordinary language, hallucination is difficult to contain because truth is open-ended. There is no universal compiler for a market analysis, policy memo, or historical interpretation. Mathematics is not completely different—specification and relevance still require judgment—but local proof steps can often be placed inside a hard verifier.

That changes the training loop.

A model trying to formalize a theorem does not need a human to say, “line seven seems suspicious.” Lean can reject the proof immediately. The error message is not always pedagogically perfect, but it is objective enough to drive another attempt. The model edits. The checker rejects again. The loop can run thousands of times without consuming a mathematician’s afternoon.

This is one reason formal theorem proving can scale differently from ordinary mathematical conversation.

Human feedback is expensive.

Kernel feedback is cheap.

Cheap feedback permits waste.

Waste permits search.

Search is what open problems need, provided the search occurs in a space connected to the real target.

Anthropic’s Fermat formalization illustrates this at industrial scale. The company reported agents generating and repairing Lean until an enormous dependency graph checked. Much of the work was not a sequence of pristine insights. It was interaction with a formal environment: try a statement, discover a missing hypothesis, search for a library theorem, repair a type mismatch, split a goal, generalize a lemma, refactor a dependency, try again.

To a mathematician accustomed to blackboard proofs, this can look like bureaucracy.

To a software engineer, it looks like development.

The comparison becomes more interesting when the mathematics is novel.

Imagine an agent proposing a new Navier–Stokes inequality. In prose, the derivation seems clean. It differentiates a functional, integrates by parts, uses incompressibility, applies Hölder, interpolates, and arrives at a favorable differential inequality.

A human expert might scan the argument and think, “maybe.”

Formalization begins asking rude questions.

Is the solution regular enough to justify differentiating this quantity at the stage where the theorem needs it?

Can the integration by parts be performed on the domain and function class stated?

Does the pressure term vanish or merely change form?

Which exponents satisfy the interpolation relation?

Is the embedding continuous at the endpoint?

Does the constant depend on the norm being estimated?

Did the argument use decay at infinity that was never assumed?

If the proof survives, confidence rises.

If it fails, that failure has information content.

The formal checker may expose the exact assumption the informal argument was borrowing from the desired conclusion. This circularity is common in regularity problems because smoothness makes many manipulations legal. One can accidentally prove that a smooth solution stays smooth by performing operations justified only while it is already smooth and never deriving a bound that prevents the breakdown time from arriving.

Humans understand this danger conceptually. Formalization can make it operational.

The system should mark every lemma according to the regularity it assumes.

If an estimate requires three derivatives of velocity, while the global a priori control provides only one derivative in an integrated sense, the gap should be visible in the dependency graph. An agent should not be able to invoke the high-regularity identity downstream and have the graph pretend the assumption disappeared.

This is the mathematical equivalent of type safety.

A smooth solution is a richer type than a weak solution.

A finite-energy solution carries fewer operations than a classical one.

An estimate valid on the torus is not automatically an estimate on all of space.

A critical norm is not a subcritical norm wearing similar notation.

The more of these distinctions the formal environment enforces, the less room remains for linguistic slippage.

There is a temptation to conclude that the solution is simply to formalize everything.

That would be a mistake.

Formalization has costs. Large portions of advanced PDE theory remain expensive to encode. Libraries are uneven. The human mathematical literature routinely uses objects and arguments whose formal counterparts require substantial infrastructure. An agent can reduce that cost, but it cannot make missing foundations disappear.

More important, proof search often needs to move faster than formalization.

A researcher testing a speculative idea should be allowed to write a rough derivation, run a symbolic calculation, and discover that the route is nonsense in twenty minutes. Requiring a full Lean development first would be like asking a physicist to machine a production instrument before doodling the experiment.

The compiler belongs at the right stages.

I imagine a trust ladder.

At the bottom is unrestricted speculation. Models generate conjectures, transformations, analogies, and possible proof plans. Most are discarded cheaply.

Next comes computational sanity checking. Symbolic algebra verifies identities. Scaling tools reject dimensionally impossible estimates. Numerical tests search for counterexamples or near-extremizers. Literature systems identify known results.

Then informal mathematical review. Specialized agents and humans inspect the logic at a higher level. The candidate is restated precisely.

Then formalization of the strategic core. The theorem statement and decisive lemmas enter Lean. Proof gaps become explicit obligations.

Finally, independent formal and human verification of the completed dependency chain.

The ladder prevents two opposite failures.

Formalize too early and exploration becomes expensive.

Formalize too late and error compounds through a giant informal edifice.

The right moment to invoke the compiler is when a claim becomes expensive to believe.

That principle can guide resource allocation.

A local algebraic identity that is easy to rederive may not need immediate formalization.

A theorem that sits under fifty downstream branches does.

A novel estimate whose failure would invalidate months of search does.

A target-equivalence statement absolutely does.

A computational certificate used in the final proof needs a rigorous checking path.

Formalization becomes risk management.

This is a better way to think about proof assistants than as replacements for mathematicians.

They are systems for moving trust from reputation and readability into mechanically checked interfaces where possible.

The phrase *where possible* matters.

There are things a proof assistant cannot decide efficiently because the relevant formalization does not exist or the search space is enormous. There are questions of mathematical significance and interpretation outside the kernel’s concern. There are choices about which abstractions produce understandable mathematics.

The compiler does not tell the programmer what software to build.

But it changes what kinds of mistakes survive until deployment.

The analogy suggests another underappreciated benefit: refactoring.

Human proofs are difficult to refactor because dependencies are partly implicit. Change an early lemma and an expert rereads the paper to determine what breaks. Formal developments expose dependencies more mechanically. If a hypothesis can be weakened, the checker reveals which downstream proofs still pass. If a general theorem replaces several specialized ones, the library can be reorganized with confidence that no logical connection was lost.

For a multi-year Navier–Stokes effort, this would be transformative.

Research directions mutate. A quantity introduced for one branch may later become useful elsewhere. A theorem initially proved on the torus may be generalized to the whole-space setting. A better pressure decomposition may simplify several chains. An agent may discover that three different lemmas are instances of one abstract estimate.

Formal infrastructure allows the project to consolidate rather than only accumulate.

Accumulation is a hidden enemy of AI-generated mathematics.

Models can produce artifacts much faster than humans can curate them. Without aggressive refactoring, a theorem library becomes a junk drawer: duplicate definitions, nearly equivalent lemmas, inconsistent notation, specialized results, stale proof plans, and layers of wrappers nobody understands.

Software repositories suffer the same fate. More code can reduce capability when maintenance costs dominate.

A mathematical codebase needs librarians.

Some agents should spend their time deleting redundancy, proving equivalences, renaming objects consistently, generalizing duplicated lemmas, and shrinking dependency chains. This is not glamorous. It may be essential to keeping the research state legible.

The proof compiler makes that cleanup safer.

One can ask the system to minimize a theorem’s dependencies and see what remains. One can extract the axiom footprint. One can test whether removing a helper theorem forces anything important to change. One can rebuild the project from scratch in a clean environment.

Reproducible builds are a scientific idea disguised as software hygiene.

If Anthropic eventually announces a Navier–Stokes proof, I would want an independent group to be able to clone the formal repository, pin the toolchain, build the theorem from source, inspect the target, and reproduce every computational certificate without access to Anthropic’s private systems.

A proof that exists only inside a company environment is not ready for a Millennium problem.

This is not because companies are uniquely untrustworthy. It is because public mathematics depends on portable verification. A theorem must survive the disappearance of the lab that produced it.

The same standard should apply to proprietary AI models in the discovery process. The model itself may be closed. The final certificate cannot depend on “trust us, Claude saw something.” The proof has to exit the model.

This is one of formal methods’ great democratic features.

A small checker can verify output from an enormously expensive generator.

The asymmetry matters. Frontier AI may remain concentrated in a few laboratories because training and inference are costly. Mathematical truth should not become equally concentrated. If the output can be reduced to public proof objects and certificates, independent researchers do not need the original billion-dollar system to verify the result.

They need the theorem, the dependencies, and the checker.

This is a version of proof-carrying computation.

Do whatever wild thing you like upstream.

Bring a certificate downstream.

The Navier–Stokes problem is particularly suited to this separation because the claim is pure mathematics. If the proof is valid, it does not matter whether the discovery process involved intuition, brute force, random search, a dream, a language model, a thousand agents, or a very stubborn graduate student. The origin affects credit and understanding. It does not alter the logical standard.

That creates an unusual possibility for AI science.

In many scientific domains, a model can propose a molecule, climate mechanism, or biological hypothesis, but reality must still adjudicate through experiments and measurements. The world is the ultimate checker, and experiments are expensive.

In formal mathematics, a significant portion of adjudication can itself be digitized.

Not all of discovery. Not all of meaning. But enough of validity to support enormous machine search.

This could make mathematics the first serious research domain in which agentic systems can close a large fraction of their own local feedback loop without lowering the standard.

That is why the compiler belongs in the room from the beginning.

Not at the head of the table.

Not deciding what matters.

Not claiming understanding.

Just sitting there, waiting for the sentence “therefore the result follows.”

Then asking for the object.
