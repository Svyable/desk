# Back Matter

*Fluidity* is a work of technical speculative nonfiction built around a real unsolved mathematical problem and a rapidly changing AI research frontier.

As of the manuscript research cutoff on September 6, 2026, the Clay Mathematics Institute continues to list the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes existence-and-smoothness problem as unsolved. This book does not claim that Anthropic, Claude, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, or any other laboratory has solved it. The title asks how such a resolution could plausibly happen if modern AI systems become materially involved.

The manuscript draws a hard line between several things that public discussion often compresses together: numerical solutions of Navier–Stokes used in engineering; numerical evidence of extreme or singular behavior; a candidate singularity; a computer-assisted proof; an informal mathematical proof; a machine-checked formal theorem; and a solution that matches the exact Clay target and survives publication, independent scrutiny, time, and broad mathematical acceptance.

That distinction is central rather than defensive. The most interesting development in 2026 is not a hidden solution. It is the rapid convergence of capabilities that could change how a famous open problem is attacked: long-running scientific agents, multi-agent coordination, machine-generated research mathematics, large-scale Lean formalization, independently implemented proof checkers, public formal conjecture registries, rigorous numerical methods, and machine-learning-assisted searches for difficult structures in nonlinear equations.

Anthropic is the book's protagonist because its 2026 work makes an especially coherent version of that pipeline visible. Its reported formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem demonstrated machine-scale formal reconstruction and theorem-graph coordination. Its Riemann-related work demonstrated that an unsuccessful attempt on a grand problem can still produce validated adjacent mathematics. Those achievements do not imply that the conceptual obstacle in Navier–Stokes will yield to the same methods.

The book therefore treats every proposed architecture as a forecast under test. The research notes preserve counterevidence and state what would make the strongest claims false. Readers who want to inspect the source trail should begin with `research/README.md`, `research/source-registry.md`, `research/frontier-update-2026-09-05.md`, `research/formal-verification-frontier-2026-09-05.md`, `research/pde-formalization-frontier-2026-09-05.md`, `research/open-claim-watch-2026-09-05.md`, and `research/augmentation-2026-09-06.md`.

A special caution applies to public preprints claiming progress on Navier–Stokes. The research process deliberately records some such claims because they illuminate the verification problem described in the manuscript. Inclusion in the research trail is not endorsement. Until a claimed result survives serious independent expert scrutiny and changes the accepted mathematical status of the problem, it remains a claim under evaluation.

The same caution applies to proof assistants. Formal verification can dramatically reduce the burden of checking a long argument, but it does not remove the need to verify the theorem statement, definitions, trusted computing base, axioms, numerical certificates, and equivalence between the formal target and the intended mathematical problem. The manuscript's later chapters therefore advocate independent checker diversity and external reproduction for any high-stakes AI-generated proof.

The durable wager of *Fluidity* is not that a particular model will inevitably solve one particular equation. It is that machine-scale discovery will force science to build stronger institutions of verification, memory, adversarial criticism, provenance, and public evidence.

If Navier–Stokes falls to AI, that institutional architecture may be the larger discovery.
