# Who Gets the Million Dollars?

The first argument after an AI solves a Millennium problem may not be about mathematics.

It may be about the check.

Clay’s prize is one million dollars. That number is small relative to the potential cost of a frontier-lab research program and enormous as a symbol. It converts a theorem into a public object with a winner attached.

Artificial intelligence complicates the noun.

Who is the winner if a proof emerges from a system containing hundreds of agents, a proprietary foundation model, public formal libraries, human PDE experts, formal-methods researchers, numerical analysts, open-source contributors, external critics, and decades of mathematics encoded in the literature?

The easy answer is “Anthropic.”

The easy answer is probably wrong.

A company can own the infrastructure that generated a result without being a human recipient in the ordinary sense of mathematical credit. Clay’s published rules govern prize consideration and give the Institute discretion within its procedures. They were not written to resolve every future case of machine-assisted authorship. The actual determination, if such a case arises, will depend on the form of the submission, the authorship of the qualifying publication, the recognized contributors, and Clay’s interpretation of its rules at the time.

This chapter will not predict that decision.

It will ask what a fair scientific record should contain before anyone reaches it.

Start with the oldest principle: proofs have ancestry.

Andrew Wiles did not create elliptic curves, modular forms, Galois representations, or the entire theory beneath Fermat’s Last Theorem. His proof is rightly associated with him because of the decisive original work connecting and extending that machinery, not because every lemma in the dependency tree was his.

The same will be true of any Navier–Stokes solution.

Jean Leray’s weak-solution framework remains part of the intellectual landscape. Caffarelli, Kohn, and Nirenberg shaped what we know about partial regularity. Generations of analysts developed continuation criteria, harmonic-analysis tools, geometric approaches, and blowup exclusions. Numerical researchers built methods for extreme flows. Formal-methods communities built proof assistants and libraries. A machine-generated proof would sit on this accumulated capital.

Citation is how mathematics remembers much of that ancestry.

AI creates a second ancestry inside the project.

Which human chose the strategic route?

Which agent generated the decisive conjecture?

Which researcher recognized its importance?

Who designed the numerical experiment that revealed the candidate?

Who formalized the target?

Who discovered the counterexample that forced the proof into its final shape?

Who wrote the computer-assisted enclosure?

Who built the general library theorem the proof imports?

Who verified the result independently?

The conventional author list may become a poor compression of this graph.

Scientific fields already face similar problems in large collaborations. Experimental physics papers can have thousands of authors. Genomics projects coordinate large consortia. Software and datasets complicate traditional authorship. Contribution taxonomies have emerged to identify roles such as conceptualization, methodology, software, validation, and supervision.

AI mathematics may need an even more granular ledger.

The theorem graph itself can provide one.

Every node has provenance.

Human-authored.

Agent-generated.

Machine-checked.

Human-revised.

Numerically discovered.

Externally validated.

Library-derived.

The final proof can carry a contribution map showing how the decisive nodes entered the project.

This is not bureaucratic overkill. It protects both people and machines from mythology.

Without provenance, the company will naturally become the protagonist. “Anthropic solved Navier–Stokes” fits on a headline. The hundreds of contributors become invisible.

With provenance, a more accurate story can emerge.

Perhaps a Claude agent proposes the key functional, but a human analyst notices why it matters and repairs the statement.

Perhaps the crucial idea comes from a 2003 paper the literature engine connects to a newer regularity criterion.

Perhaps the machine contribution is mostly exhaustive formalization, while one mathematician supplies the central estimate.

Perhaps the decisive theorem is genuinely generated by an agent and then formalized autonomously, with humans serving mainly as auditors.

These are different historical events.

They should not be flattened into “AI-assisted.”

The word *authorship* becomes especially difficult if the model produces a novel idea.

Current legal systems do not generally treat AI systems as human authors or inventors in the ordinary sense required by many intellectual-property regimes. Mathematical credit is not identical to copyright or patent law, but social categories often borrow from legal ones. A model cannot receive money, attend a prize ceremony as a legal person, or consent to publication in the way a human can.

This can tempt us to ignore machine contribution entirely.

That would also distort history.

If a model generated the decisive argument without a human supplying the mathematical idea, the scientific record should say so plainly.

Credit need not imply personhood.

We already credit instruments in descriptive ways. A telescope “discovered” an object in popular language while named scientists and teams receive formal credit. Algorithms can be recognized as enabling methods. An AI agent can be documented as the origin of a conjecture or proof branch without pretending it has a bank account.

The harder question is human credit inside the lab.

Machine abundance can concentrate recognition upward.

A company funds the models. A senior researcher leads the program. Thousands of small contributions become invisible because agents perform them or because junior people curate the agents. The public sees one laboratory name.

This is familiar from industrial science. AI could make it worse because the visible output may look more autonomous than the human support actually was.

The contribution ledger should therefore record human interventions that are easy to erase:

who corrected the target statement;

who rejected the false singularity;

who designed the evaluator;

who supplied the missing dataset;

who noticed the formal proof used an unacceptable assumption;

who built the open-source library feature on which the result depended;

who persuaded the team to abandon the attractive wrong route.

Negative contributions count.

This is one of the recurring themes of *Fluidity*: the person who prevents a false theorem can matter as much as the person who proposes the surviving one.

The prize itself may force a simpler decision than the history deserves.

Institutions need recipients. A prize cannot be split into thirty thousand graph nodes. Clay may identify one or more people under its rules. Whatever happens, the scholarly record can remain richer.

There is another ownership question: does Anthropic own the proof?

Mathematical facts are not owned in the way products are. Copyright can protect the particular expression of a paper, and software can have licenses, but the theorem itself becomes part of public knowledge once disclosed. A company may possess private research artifacts before publication. It may choose how and when to release them subject to obligations. It cannot make a generally accepted mathematical truth function like a proprietary API forever.

This is one reason pure mathematics is a fascinating frontier for corporate AI research.

The most valuable output escapes.

A solved theorem becomes usable by everyone.

The company can capture reputation and capability gains. It cannot charge a royalty each time a professor invokes global regularity.

That economic feature aligns, imperfectly, with openness.

A lab wanting credit has reason to publish enough for independent acceptance. The theorem’s status improves as control over verification spreads.

This differs from many AI products where proprietary advantage grows through secrecy.

The tension will still be real. Anthropic may want to protect details of its agent architecture. Competitors will want to learn how the research system worked. Scientists will want enough provenance to evaluate claims. The right boundary is not obvious.

My proposed rule is simple: discovery machinery may be proprietary; proof machinery should not be.

The model weights can stay private.

The final theorem statement, proof artifact, dependencies, numerical certificates, and reproduction instructions should be public.

The human-level exposition should be public.

Meaningful formal library contributions should be upstreamed where licensing and community practice allow.

The research can be independently checked without access to Anthropic’s internal model.

If this condition cannot be met, the result is not ready to be the solution of a public Millennium problem.

The prize process reinforces the point through time.

Clay’s rules require publication in a qualifying outlet, a waiting period of at least two years, and general acceptance by the global mathematics community before the Institute considers a proposed solution. This is wonderfully inconvenient for corporate communications.

A company cannot compress two years into a launch event.

That is healthy.

The waiting period turns novelty into durability. Independent researchers have time to reconstruct the proof. Formal builds can be reproduced. Numerical certificates can be attacked. Alternative expositions can emerge. Subtle target mismatch can surface. The community can discover whether the argument has become a tool rather than a curiosity.

An AI-generated proof may need that time more than a conventional one because the production scale can exceed human reading capacity.

The final paper might be short, but the underlying artifact could contain millions of formal steps and large computational certificates. Trust will be layered and distributed.

Two years is not slow if the alternative is a permanent false theorem.

The social process also determines what counts as “general acceptance” in an age of proof assistants.

Would a machine-checked proof with a clean target be accepted even if few humans understand the whole argument?

Probably, if the formal ecosystem and dependencies are trusted and experts understand enough of the structure. Mathematics already accepts computer-assisted results whose exhaustive computational portions no person checks by hand.

Would community acceptance require a human proof independent of the formal one?

Not necessarily as a logical matter, but it may become important culturally and scientifically.

Could another proof assistant independently verify the core theorem?

That would strengthen trust.

Would the community demand open-source code for every computational component?

I hope so.

These norms will be negotiated through actual cases.

Navier–Stokes could become one of them.

Then there is the question of naming.

Theorems often acquire the names of people associated with them. What do we call an AI-discovered regularity mechanism?

The Anthropic estimate?

The Claude inequality?

The Benson conjecture if this book somehow annoys the right person into proving it?

Names are accidents of history and power. The safest answer is not to decide in advance. Let the field name what it uses.

The same humility should govern the title *How Anthropic Solves Navier–Stokes*.

If a university team solves it tomorrow without AI, this book becomes an artifact of a wrong forecast. If DeepMind solves it first, the title becomes corporate misdirection. If Anthropic contributes infrastructure but a mathematician elsewhere closes the decisive lemma, “Anthropic solves” may be too strong.

That possibility belongs inside the book, not outside it.

Prediction is not entitlement.

The title is a hypothesis about the form of future research.

The theorem will choose its own history.

Who gets the million dollars is therefore almost the least interesting credit question.

The more important question is whether the final record tells the truth about how the mathematics was made.

AI gives us the tools to preserve that record with unusual precision.

Every commit.

Every theorem dependency.

Every failed branch.

Every model generation, if retained appropriately.

Every human correction.

Every formal check.

Every numerical certificate.

We could know more about the provenance of this proof than any major theorem in history.

Or we could compress it into a corporate headline and forget almost everything.

That choice is ours.

The million dollars will be easy to allocate compared with the credit.
