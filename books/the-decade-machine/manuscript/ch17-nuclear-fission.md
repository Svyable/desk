# 1936–1945: Nuclear Fission

The most important American technology of this decade was not born in America.

Nuclear fission emerged from European physics. In late 1938, Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann found evidence that bombarding uranium with neutrons produced much lighter elements. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch supplied the physical interpretation and the name. The scientific insight crossed borders through papers, letters, refugees, conferences, fear, and war.

The American achievement came next.

It was not simply “the atomic bomb.”

It was the ability to take an unprecedented physical possibility and build an industrial system around it at wartime speed.

That is why nuclear fission wins 1936–1945.

The decade had technologies that saved more lives in ordinary medical practice. Penicillin moved from laboratory curiosity toward mass production during World War II. Radar transformed air defense, naval warfare, weather observation, and later aviation. Synthetic rubber helped the United States survive the loss of natural-rubber supplies. Jet propulsion began changing military aviation.

Fission wins because it crossed a threshold none of the others did.

It made civilization capable of destroying a city with one weapon.

It also demonstrated that the federal government could coordinate basic science, industrial chemistry, electrical power, mining, construction, secret cities, military logistics, universities, private corporations, and tens of thousands of workers around a technical objective whose operating principles were still being discovered while the factories were built.

The science and the organization became inseparable.

The Manhattan Project is often remembered through a few places and a few physicists: Los Alamos, J. Robert Oppenheimer, Enrico Fermi, General Leslie Groves, the Trinity test.

That memory understates the machine.

In 1942, the project began acquiring and transforming huge sites. Oak Ridge, Tennessee, became a secret city devoted largely to uranium enrichment. Different enrichment technologies were pursued in parallel because nobody could be fully confident which method would work at the scale and schedule war demanded. Electromagnetic separation, gaseous diffusion, and thermal diffusion all entered the industrial gamble. The X-10 Graphite Reactor produced small quantities of plutonium and trained operators for larger production systems.

Hanford, Washington, became the plutonium-production complex. Los Alamos became the weapons laboratory. Chicago hosted the first controlled self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction in December 1942 under Fermi’s team.

The project did not choose one elegant path and execute it.

It bought uncertainty with redundancy.

That is a revealing form of technological capability.

In normal business, parallel plants pursuing different methods can look wasteful. In a wartime program where the cost of technical failure is judged existential, duplication becomes insurance. The project could spend extraordinary sums, consume extraordinary materials, and run competing approaches because schedule risk mattered more than conventional efficiency.

This was industrial policy under maximum pressure.

A laboratory experiment asks whether something can happen.

The Manhattan Project had to answer different questions.

Can it happen reliably?

Can enough fissile material be produced?

Can facilities be built before the underlying process is fully mature?

Can the work be compartmentalized so that most participants do not know the whole program?

Can purity be measured at scales that matter?

Can new reactors be operated safely enough to produce material?

Can weapons be designed around materials with different properties?

Can the entire system outrun enemy programs and the end of the war?

Those questions turn science into infrastructure.

That conversion had enormous human costs before the bomb was used.

Communities were displaced to create the secret sites. Workers faced chemical, radiological, industrial, and construction hazards they did not always understand. Segregation and racial discrimination shaped employment and housing at project sites. Indigenous lands and communities were affected by federal acquisition and later by nuclear contamination and waste across the broader atomic complex. Uranium mining would impose severe health burdens on workers, including many Navajo miners in later years.

The technology’s hidden labor force was larger than its famous scientific cast.

Women performed essential technical and industrial work, from calculation and laboratory analysis to plant operation and clerical systems. Black workers migrated to project sites under segregated conditions. Construction workers built giant facilities whose purpose they often did not know. machinists, chemists, electricians, pipefitters, engineers, military personnel, and administrators turned equations into material flow.

The bomb did not emerge from a laboratory bench.

It emerged from an economy organized around secrecy.

Secrecy itself became a management technology.

Compartmentalization limited who knew what. Workers were assigned narrow tasks. information moved on a need-to-know basis. The project had to coordinate thousands of people while preventing most of them from understanding the system they collectively constituted.

That is the opposite of the information-sharing logic celebrated in many innovation stories.

Sometimes coordination depends on openness.

Sometimes it depends on structured ignorance.

The Manhattan Project demonstrated both. Scientists at Los Alamos needed intense interdisciplinary exchange. Workers elsewhere often received only the information necessary for their jobs. The organization had different information architectures for different risks.

Modern security systems still operate this way.

Access control is a technology of organizational design. Give enough information to act, but not enough to compromise the whole system. Financial firms separate duties. militaries classify information. software companies restrict production credentials. hospitals limit patient-data access. AI systems increasingly create similar questions around model weights, training data, system prompts, and tool permissions.

The nuclear project made information boundaries strategic.

The physical process itself was stranger than any earlier winner in this book.

A fissioning nucleus releases energy and additional neutrons. Under the right conditions, those neutrons cause more fissions. The system becomes a chain reaction.

That means scale can change the nature of the process.

One event produces the conditions for more events.

The technology is therefore partly about controlling multiplication.

In a reactor, the chain reaction is managed.

In a bomb, it is driven explosively.

The difference is not simply one of amount. It is architecture and timing.

This makes fission a grim counterpart to the network effects seen elsewhere in the book. A telephone network becomes more valuable as endpoints multiply. A broadcast network scales one signal across receivers. An assembly line scales repeated production. A nuclear chain reaction scales physical events through feedback.

Not all multiplication is social.

Some is physical enough to end the argument.

The first controlled chain reaction in Chicago was a remarkable act of engineering under uncertainty. The first nuclear detonation at Trinity in New Mexico on July 16, 1945, demonstrated something categorically different.

The weapon worked.

Less than a month later, the United States dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima. Three days later, it dropped another on Nagasaki.

Any account of nuclear technology that races from Trinity to “the Atomic Age” without staying in those cities is morally and historically defective.

The bombs killed enormous numbers of people, many immediately and others through burns, injuries, radiation sickness, and later effects. The overwhelming majority of victims were civilians. The attacks occurred inside a brutal total war in which conventional bombing had already destroyed cities and killed civilians at enormous scale. Historians continue to debate the relationship among the bombings, Soviet entry into the war against Japan, Japanese decision-making, invasion planning, surrender terms, and American political and military motives.

The argument over whether the bomb was necessary is not solved by calling the technology important.

Importance is not exoneration.

The bomb matters because after August 1945 every major power had to confront the possibility that industrial war could cross into national annihilation.

A technology changed strategy before most countries possessed it.

That is an unusual form of influence.

The existence of the capability reorganized behavior through anticipation.

Nuclear weapons would produce arms races, deterrence theory, civil defense, command-and-control systems, submarines, early-warning networks, arms-control regimes, proliferation fears, and a permanent political relationship between technical expertise and national survival.

The bomb did not need to be used repeatedly to become infrastructure for decision-making.

This is why a narrow adoption metric would badly mismeasure its importance.

No household needed a bomb.

Every government had to think about one.

The technology also created a civilian promise: nuclear energy.

If fission released extraordinary energy in weapons, perhaps controlled reactors could produce electricity in large quantities. That promise would generate research, regulation, power plants, naval reactors, medical isotopes, and debates about waste, accidents, cost, and proliferation.

The civilian legacy does not make the weapon origin less central.

It shows how general physical capability refuses to stay inside its first application.

Military investment created scientific and industrial capacity that later moved into other domains.

The same pattern will appear immediately in semiconductors. Military and communications demand helped early transistor development. The integrated circuit would receive major support from missiles and Apollo. GPS would begin as a military system before becoming a civilian utility. The internet would grow from defense and research networking before becoming commercial infrastructure.

War repeatedly finances capabilities whose later uses are broader than war.

That fact is uncomfortable because it means destructive competition has often accelerated useful technology.

It does not follow that war is necessary for innovation.

It means governments at war are willing to spend, coordinate, standardize, and accept risk at scales peacetime institutions often resist.

The Manhattan Project is the extreme case.

The project’s cost, secrecy, urgency, and technical ambition produced a managerial model that later American programs would imitate, sometimes too eagerly. The Cold War state learned to believe in “big science”: giant laboratories, federal contracts, national missions, research universities, classified programs, and industrial partners organized around frontier technologies.

This was a new relationship among science, state, and industry.

The federal government had supported technology long before 1942. Armories, surveys, canals, railroads, agriculture, aviation research, and military procurement all had public roles.

The Manhattan Project changed the scale and intimacy of the relationship.

The government was not merely buying finished equipment.

It was creating the technical ecosystem in which finished equipment became possible.

That is the organizational breakthrough beneath the mushroom cloud.

Radar is the strongest rival because it was arguably more important to winning the war itself. Radar improved detection of aircraft and ships, shaped the Battle of Britain before U.S. entry, aided anti-submarine warfare, fire control, navigation, and weather observation. American microwave radar development through the Radiation Laboratory at MIT became an extraordinary wartime science-and-engineering effort.

If the chapter asked which technology contributed most broadly to Allied battlefield effectiveness between 1936 and 1945, radar might win.

Penicillin is the moral rival.

Mass-produced penicillin transformed the treatment of bacterial infections. The United States helped industrialize fermentation and production during the war. Its legacy is measured in lives extended rather than cities threatened.

If importance were weighted heavily toward health benefit, penicillin could defeat fission.

Fission wins because it changed the ceiling of organized violence and the floor of national strategy simultaneously.

There was no going back to a pre-nuclear world after 1945.

A country could decide not to build nuclear weapons.

It could not decide that nobody else knew how.

Knowledge had become geopolitical fact.

That irreversibility is rare.

The printing press did not make handwriting impossible. Railroads did not erase roads. Radio did not erase newspapers. The refrigerator did not make drying and salting vanish.

Nuclear fission added a capability civilization could not uninvent.

The only durable response would be institutional: deterrence, treaties, inspections, command systems, nonproliferation regimes, political restraint, and continual attempts to make catastrophic use less likely.

Technology expanded the possible.

Institutions inherited the burden.

That may be the darkest recurring lesson in this book.

The next decade’s winner would emerge from a very different laboratory, but it would share one important characteristic with the bomb: it began as a solution to a technical bottleneck inside a large institution.

Bell Labs wanted a better electronic switch and amplifier than the vacuum tube.

In 1947, a small piece of germanium would demonstrate the path.

The nuclear age expanded destructive power through enormous installations.

The transistor would expand electronic power by shrinking the device.

**What would prove this choice wrong?** Nuclear fission loses if “importance” is defined primarily through realized productive or life-saving effects during 1936–1945 rather than strategic irreversibility, in which case radar or penicillin may be more defensible. It also loses if the Manhattan Project should be treated as an organizational achievement rather than a technology, with fission only the underlying scientific mechanism. I keep it because the decade ends with a permanent change in the maximum destructive capability of states and with a new American capacity to industrialize frontier science before the science itself had fully stabilized. The bomb was the terrible product. The deeper technology was controlled access to the atomic nucleus at national scale.
