# 1776–1785: The Printed Page

The declaration left the room before most Americans knew it existed.

On the evening of July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress had a political decision and a practical problem. The delegates could adopt language in Philadelphia, but a declaration that remained in Philadelphia was not yet much use to an army, a state government, a newspaper editor, a town meeting, or a person wondering whether the colonies were still asking for redress or had crossed into something irreversible.

A text that claims to speak for thirteen colonies has to become thirteen-colony information.

John Dunlap’s printing shop was one of the places where that transformation began. During the night, the words attributed to a continental political body became pieces of paper. Copies could be dispatched. One went toward General George Washington. Others went to political authorities. Newspapers reprinted the declaration. Public readings carried it beyond the literate population. The message acquired a physical life independent of the room that approved it.

There were technologies in 1776 with more obvious force.

A musket could kill a man at a distance. A cannon could break a position. A ship could carry soldiers, weapons, and information across an ocean. Mills converted water and grain into food and work. Roads, horses, wagons, and coastal vessels held the colonies together in the literal sense that without them no document traveled anywhere.

But the press did something stranger.

It made authority reproducible.

That capability is so ordinary now that it is difficult to recover its importance. A modern person encounters copies as a nearly free condition of life. A file can be duplicated without a visible machine. A message can be sent to a thousand people with less effort than addressing one letter. A political statement can appear simultaneously on screens scattered across a continent. We tend to treat information as if it naturally multiplies.

In 1776, multiplication had machinery.

Type had to be set. Paper had to exist. Ink had to be applied. Presses had to be operated. Errors had to be noticed or allowed through. Finished sheets had to dry, be bundled, carried, posted, read, recited, copied, and sometimes set in type again. The speed of the network remained constrained by physical transport, but the press changed the economics at every node. Once a text arrived, one copy could become many.

That is why the technology of the decade is not simply “the Declaration of Independence.” A declaration is content. Printing is the capability that lets content escape its authors.

The distinction matters because the Revolution was unusually dependent on language.

The colonies had no inherited national bureaucracy capable of issuing commands down a settled administrative hierarchy. They were separate political communities with different legislatures, newspapers, churches, commercial interests, local disputes, and experiences of British rule. The revolutionary coalition had to be constructed while the conflict was already underway. Arguments about rights, taxation, representation, military necessity, independence, constitutional design, paper money, loyalty, and public order were not side conversations. They were part of the operating system of the rebellion.

Pamphlets mattered. Newspapers mattered. Broadsides mattered. Printed sermons mattered. State constitutions mattered. Military orders and forms mattered. Bills of credit mattered. A political movement stretched across hundreds of miles required more than ideas. It required repeated, durable representations of ideas.

The printing press did not create revolutionary sentiment by itself. That would be a wonderfully simple story and a false one.

Political grievances had economic and constitutional roots. British policy mattered. War mattered. Local committees mattered. taverns, churches, marketplaces, correspondence networks, riders, and face-to-face persuasion mattered. Oral culture remained powerful. Many people learned political news from somebody else’s voice rather than from a page they read themselves.

The page nevertheless gave the voice an object to work from.

A printed broadside could be read aloud repeatedly without relying on the reader’s memory. A newspaper could quote a document, print an argument beside it, and move that bundle to another town. A pamphlet could travel farther than its author. A constitution could be examined as a text rather than remembered as a speech. A printer could reproduce an argument whose author never entered the colony where it was read.

Printing multiplied strangers.

That sounds impersonal, but republics are built from an enormous number of relationships among people who do not know one another. A voter trusts that votes elsewhere are counted. A soldier acts on behalf of people he will never meet. A merchant accepts paper because other people accept it. A legislature claims authority over citizens absent from the chamber. A constitution binds people not yet born.

Such systems depend on shared representations.

The printed page was one of the first machines that gave the United States a common political surface.

Consider what the press changed about a rumor.

A rumor can move quickly. It can also mutate at every telling. The speaker is part of the message, and the listener cannot easily compare today’s version with yesterday’s. A printed text can also be false, partisan, inflammatory, forged, or misunderstood. Eighteenth-century print culture was not a sanctuary of verified information. But a printed statement has a peculiar property: separate readers can point to the same words.

That is a powerful coordination mechanism.

You can argue about a document because the document persists through the argument.

The Declaration did not mean the same thing to every person who encountered it. Its assertion that all men are created equal coexisted with slavery, exclusion, property requirements, displacement, and a political system that did not treat all humans as equal participants. The printed sentence did not dissolve those contradictions. It made some of them harder to hide.

A claim that can be reproduced can also be turned against the people who first made it.

That happened repeatedly in American history. Abolitionists could quote founding language to expose the distance between proclaimed equality and slavery. Women’s-rights advocates could borrow the form and language of declarations. Civil-rights movements could point backward to promises printed generations before. The durability of print allowed a political text to become evidence against its own era.

This is one reason technologies of information are difficult to own politically.

The press was not revolutionary by nature. Governments printed laws. Loyalists printed arguments. Merchants printed notices. Enslavers printed advertisements for people they claimed as property and notices seeking people who had escaped them. Newspapers circulated both liberty and human sale on adjacent pages. Printing amplified whatever institutions and people could pay for type, paper, labor, and distribution.

The machine did not choose the cause.

It lowered the cost of reproducing a cause.

That difference is the beginning of a pattern we will see again with the telegraph, telephone, radio, internet, and artificial intelligence. Communications technologies are often described by the people who first use them most visibly, but the underlying capability is indifferent. A network that helps abolitionists coordinate can help slave catchers coordinate. A network that spreads scientific knowledge can spread financial panic. A radio that carries music can carry propaganda. A social network that connects dissidents can organize harassment.

Technology expands the possible before institutions decide which possibilities dominate.

In the founding decade, the press also helped solve a more mundane problem: a new political order had to produce paperwork.

Revolutionary drama attracts the eye, but states survive on documents. Laws must be published. Tax obligations have to be communicated. Money has to be printed. Military supplies need receipts and records. Assemblies publish proceedings. Treaties become texts. constitutions must be distributed. Elections require notices. Public credit depends on paper instruments that people recognize and trust.

The United States did not merely need inspirational prose. It needed administrative replication.

This is where the musket loses the decade.

Without weapons, the Revolution may fail militarily. That fact is large enough to make any ranking feel ridiculous. Yet the musket was not a distinctively new capability of 1776–1785, and its basic function remained what it had been before independence: project lethal force. Printing, by contrast, sat inside the formation of the new political entity itself. The war used firearms. The republic used print to become legible to itself.

The postal system is a harder rival.

Information that does not move cannot coordinate distance, and eighteenth-century printers depended on riders, roads, ships, and post routes. Newspapers circulated through exchange networks that were partly logistical rather than mechanical. A press in Philadelphia could multiply a text, but it could not carry the broadside to Boston or Charleston.

Why not give the decade to the post?

Because transport moved individual objects while printing changed the number of objects worth moving.

The postal network is the bloodstream. Printing is the reproductive system. The two are complements, and the analogy should not conceal how deeply they depended on each other. Still, if one technology has to represent the decade, the press captures the more fundamental break between a local decision and a reproducible political world.

The press also changed scale without requiring exact simultaneity.

Later communications systems will become obsessed with time. The telegraph compresses the delay between event and report. Radio broadcasts to people at once. Television makes national spectacle simultaneous. The internet makes simultaneity interactive. In 1776, the crucial achievement was less about everyone hearing the same thing at the same moment than about many communities eventually encountering materially related versions of the same text.

A republic could be asynchronous and still be coordinated.

That is worth remembering because modern communications technologies encourage us to confuse speed with capacity. Faster information is not always better information. A system can coordinate millions of people with delays if it preserves enough fidelity, trust, and repeatability. The founding-era press was slow by modern standards and powerful by any standard.

It made political memory external.

Before print, societies had documents, scribes, inscriptions, archives, and manuscript traditions. The printing press was centuries old by the time of the American Revolution. Nothing about the machine itself was new to 1776. That is precisely why it belongs here.

This book does not award decades to inventions merely for being born.

It asks when a technology became essential to what a society was trying to do.

America in 1776 was trying to become an argument large enough to fight a war.

Printing was mature technology applied to a new coordination problem.

The result was not a unified public. The colonies were full of loyalists, skeptics, opportunists, people trying to survive, and people excluded from the political “we” being constructed around them. Newspapers were partisan. Printers faced economic pressure and political risk. Paper could be scarce. Transportation was unreliable. News traveled unevenly. Literacy was unequal. Printed claims did not float above class, race, gender, geography, or power.

The machine operated inside those realities.

But it changed them because it gave political actors something that oral persuasion alone could not provide at comparable scale: durable copies.

The copy is one of civilization’s underrated technologies.

A copy can be checked against another copy. It can be carried into a courtroom. It can be stored. It can be sold. It can be censored. It can be burned. It can survive the person who made it. It can enter an archive and wait two centuries for somebody to notice a contradiction. It can be translated, excerpted, mocked, endorsed, and stolen.

Most importantly, a copy allows an institution to act at a distance without sending the institution itself.

The order travels instead of the commander.

The law travels instead of the legislature.

The argument travels instead of the author.

The declaration travels instead of the delegates.

That is coordination.

A few years after independence, Americans would face a different problem. The new country had claimed enormous territories whose legal status, ownership, occupation, and governance were anything but simple. Political words on paper would meet land on the ground. The republic would need another technology for turning distant reality into something that offices and markets could process.

It would begin measuring.

Before that happens, though, it is worth staying for a moment in Dunlap’s shop.

Not because the printer single-handedly made a nation. He did not.

Not because the broadside instantly united the colonies. It did not.

The scene matters because it shows the smallest version of a recurring American act: take something that exists in one place and build a machine for making it operative somewhere else.

A declaration becomes copies.

A boundary will become coordinates.

A voice will become current in a wire.

Power will become current on a grid.

A location will become timing signals from orbit.

A sentence will become computation.

The technologies change. The ambition is remarkably stable.

Increase the radius of coordination.

The press did it with pressure, ink, paper, and type.

That was enough to win the first decade.

There is one more reason to linger with the press before leaving the founding decade: printing was never just a machine in a room. It was an economy of scarce materials and skilled judgment.

Type wore down. Paper cost money. Ink had to behave consistently enough to transfer a legible impression. Compositors had to turn manuscript into lines of metal type while deciding what to do with cramped space, damaged sorts, unusual spellings, corrections, and last-minute changes. Pressmen had to ink and pull sheets repeatedly. Apprentices and journeymen moved through shops where production knowledge lived in habits that did not appear on the title page.

That materiality imposed choices on politics.

A printer with limited paper could not reproduce everything. A newspaper with a fixed number of pages could not carry every letter, notice, casualty list, foreign report, advertisement, legislative act, and political essay offered to it. Editors and printers became bottlenecks because reproduction was cheaper than hand copying but not free. Their choices determined which arguments acquired more copies and which remained local.

The technology therefore created a new gatekeeper even as it reduced an older gate.

This will happen repeatedly in the book. The telegraph makes messages faster and gives network owners power over access. Radio makes national distribution cheap and gives license holders and networks unusual control over attention. The internet makes publishing cheap and gives discovery platforms extraordinary influence. Generative AI makes production cheap and raises the value of selection, verification, and trusted distribution.

A cheaper copy does not eliminate scarcity.

It moves scarcity somewhere else.

In the 1770s, one of those scarcities was paper itself. Wartime disrupted trade and placed stress on supplies. Rags, mills, imported material, transport, and local production all mattered to whether a printer could turn political demand into physical sheets. The grand language of independence rested on an industrial base humble enough to overlook: fibers beaten into pulp, dried into paper, cut, stacked, carried, purchased, and finally fed into a press.

That dependence makes the printed revolution less magical and more interesting.

Ideas do not spread because history wants them to spread. They spread through supply chains.

The same is true of the newspaper exchange system. Printers did not need a single centrally managed national newsroom. They copied from one another. A report printed in one town could travel as a physical newspaper to another printer, who might reprint all or part of it. This created a decentralized replication network in which information jumped from press to press.

The process was lossy.

Headings changed. Spelling changed. Editors shortened. Context disappeared. Rumor entered the chain. A printer could favor a partisan account. A late report might be overtaken by a newer one before reaching distant readers. Yet the system had redundancy. Many papers could carry versions of the same event, and readers could compare accounts when multiple papers reached them.

That structure looks surprisingly modern.

There was no master database containing “the news.” There were nodes with local copies, incomplete information, incentives, errors, and relationships with other nodes. The system achieved national reach through repeated local acts of copying.

Its weakness was also its strength.

A government could pressure a printer. An army could interrupt a route. A shop could burn or run out of paper. But there was no single switch that turned off colonial print culture everywhere. The network could reroute socially because other printers could reset the words.

This is why the oral life of print matters so much too.

The number of people touched by a printed page was larger than the number who personally read that page. A broadside nailed in a public place could become a gathering point. A newspaper could be shared. A tavern could turn one purchased copy into a roomful of listeners. A minister or official could read a text aloud. Soldiers could hear orders or declarations through organized reading.

One physical copy could therefore produce many human receptions without another pass through the press.

Printing and speech were complements, not rivals.

The page stabilized the wording. The voice widened the audience.

That matters when thinking about literacy. It is tempting to imagine a clean division between literate citizens inside print culture and everyone else outside it. The real information environment was more porous. People encountered print indirectly through readers, public notices, images, symbols, prices, names, and conversations sparked by documents they did not personally decode line by line.

A communications technology changes society partly through secondary users.

The person who never owns a car can live in a city rebuilt for cars. The person who never buys a radio can live inside politics reorganized around broadcasting. The person who never creates a social-media account can work for an institution responding to social-media pressure. Adoption statistics alone can understate system reach.

Founding-era print worked the same way.

Its political reach exceeded its subscriber list.

The darker side is equally important. A runaway advertisement could circulate a detailed description of an enslaved person’s body, skills, clothing, speech, and likely destination, turning the press into an instrument of pursuit. Commercial notices could normalize buying and selling human beings by placing them inside the ordinary information architecture of the marketplace. Loyalist printers could be threatened because political movements understood that controlling a press meant controlling replication capacity.

The machine did not merely carry lofty arguments about liberty.

It helped administer the contradictions of the society making those arguments.

That is precisely why the printed page belongs at the beginning of this book. The first technology does not offer a clean morality tale in which better communication produces better politics. It offers a more durable rule: the technology that expands coordination expands the capability of institutions and people with very different purposes.

A press can spread a declaration.

It can spread a price.

It can spread a law.

It can spread a lie.

The historical question is not whether copies are good.

It is what becomes possible when copies become easier to make.

**What would prove this choice wrong?** If the founding-era information system is better understood as a logistics achievement than a reproduction achievement—if postal routes, riders, committees of correspondence, and face-to-face institutions did most of the causal work while printing merely formatted messages already moving effectively—then the press is too grand a winner. The other serious challenge is military technology: if “importance” is defined by the immediate survival of the Revolution rather than the construction of a reproducible political order, firearms and naval capability become difficult to beat. I choose the printed page because independence had to become a shared text before it could become a durable state, but the choice rests on a definition of importance that values coordination as much as combat.
