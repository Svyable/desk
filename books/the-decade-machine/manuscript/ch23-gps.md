# 1996–2005: GPS

The blue dot is a clock.

That is the first surprise.

Most people experience GPS as a map technology. A phone shows where you are. A car tells you where to turn. A hiker sees a position. A truck fleet records routes. A farmer steers equipment across a field.

Underneath those uses is a timing system.

GPS satellites carry precise clocks and broadcast signals that tell receivers where the satellites are and when the signals were transmitted. A receiver compares arrival times from multiple satellites and solves for position and its own clock error. The geometry produces location because time is measured with extraordinary precision.

The system therefore gives the world two utilities at once.

Position.

Time.

That combination wins 1996–2005.

The Global Positioning System is older than the decade. It emerged from U.S. military satellite-navigation programs beginning in the Cold War. The first GPS satellites were launched in the 1970s. President Ronald Reagan announced in 1983, after the Soviet destruction of Korean Air Lines Flight 007, that GPS would eventually be made available for civilian aviation and other peaceful uses. The constellation reached full operational capability in the mid-1990s.

Why not give GPS an earlier decade?

Because the crucial civilian phase change happens in 2000.

Until then, the U.S. government intentionally degraded the accuracy available through the standard civilian service using a policy called Selective Availability. Civilian users could improve accuracy through differential techniques, but ordinary standalone receivers operated under the deliberate limitation.

On May 1, 2000, President Bill Clinton ordered Selective Availability turned off.

Civilian accuracy improved dramatically without users buying new satellites.

Policy changed the technology.

That is one of the most revealing moments in the book.

The physical system in orbit was nearly the same before and after the decision. The capability available to civilians changed because the government altered what the infrastructure was allowed to provide.

Technology is sometimes a permission setting.

This sounds like a software-era observation, but it applies to infrastructure generally. A road can exist and be closed. A frequency can be technically usable and legally unavailable. A drug can exist before approval. A cryptographic capability can be export-controlled. A data set can exist behind access rules. A model can have capabilities users are not permitted to invoke.

Policy can change effective technology without changing hardware.

GPS makes that fact planetary.

Turning off Selective Availability helped unleash civilian and commercial uses that had been constrained by poorer standalone accuracy. Navigation devices improved. Mapping applications could place users more precisely. Surveying, logistics, agriculture, construction, emergency response, outdoor recreation, and location-aware systems gained a more dependable common reference.

The important phrase is common reference.

Before satellite positioning, navigation was a patchwork of methods. Celestial observations, maps, compasses, radio beacons, inertial systems, landmarks, surveying networks, dead reckoning, and local knowledge all provided answers suited to different environments.

GPS offers a shared coordinate system anywhere the receiver can obtain sufficient satellite signals.

The same latitude, longitude, altitude, and time framework can be used by a ship, aircraft, truck, surveyor, tractor, phone, telecom network, bank, or power system.

The surveyor’s chain has left the ground.

In the 1786–1795 chapter, the federal rectangular survey made land administratively addressable by putting distant parcels into a common spatial syntax.

GPS gives moving machines a common spatial syntax in real time.

The earlier survey asked: where is the property?

GPS asks: where am I now?

That word now is the second half of the technology.

Precise GPS timing became infrastructure for telecommunications, financial systems, electric-power networks, data centers, scientific measurement, and other systems that need synchronized clocks. Cellular networks use precise timing to coordinate operations. Financial markets use accurate timestamps. Grid applications rely on synchronized measurements. Communications infrastructure can depend on GPS even when no map is visible anywhere in the workflow.

The positioning system quietly became a timing utility.

This hidden use is why GPS beats search engines for the decade.

Search feels larger because people interact with it directly. Google’s rise beginning in the late 1990s changed access to internet information. As the Web exploded, search became the navigation layer for digital abundance. Advertising around intent created an enormously powerful business model.

Search is one of the decade’s defining technologies.

GPS sits deeper.

It becomes a shared dependency across physical and digital systems, including systems that users do not realize rely on satellite signals.

Infrastructure wins when its absence breaks unrelated things.

Turn off a search engine and people can use another search engine, direct links, directories, or known sites, though less efficiently.

Disrupt GPS broadly enough and navigation is only one category of trouble. Timing-dependent infrastructure can degrade too.

That systemic dependence is a measure of importance.

It is also a risk.

GPS signals reaching the ground are weak. They can be jammed. They can be spoofed. Receivers can be poorly designed. Buildings and terrain can block signals. Space weather can disrupt conditions. Reliance on a single external timing source can create common-mode vulnerability across institutions.

The network that gives everybody the same answer can give everybody the same failure.

This is the electric-grid lesson again.

Shared infrastructure pools capability and pools risk.

The more applications assume GPS is always available, the more expensive an outage becomes. Backup navigation and timing systems appear inefficient during normal operation because the primary system works so well. Redundancy is hardest to justify before failure.

Efficiency eats its spare tire.

Modern infrastructure repeatedly makes this trade. Companies reduce inventory until a supply-chain disruption reveals the value of inventory. data centers consolidate workloads until an outage affects many services. utilities interconnect for efficiency and create the possibility of cascading failures. organizations standardize software and expose themselves to common vulnerabilities.

GPS is extraordinarily useful partly because so many users depend on the same standardized signal.

That is also what makes resilience difficult.

The system’s military origin complicates its civilian character.

GPS remained and remains a U.S. government-operated system with military missions even as civilian and commercial users around the world built businesses and critical services around open signals. Other countries and regions developed or expanded their own global navigation satellite systems in part because relying completely on another state’s infrastructure carries strategic risk.

Navigation is sovereignty.

A military that cannot position, navigate, and time accurately loses enormous capability. Precision-guided weapons, coordinated operations, reconnaissance, logistics, and communications all benefit from satellite navigation.

Civilian convenience and military power share the constellation.

This dual-use pattern runs through the modern book.

Nuclear technology can generate electricity or weapons material. transistors go into hearing aids and missiles. integrated circuits serve computers and guidance systems. the internet supports research and warfare. GPS guides ambulances and bombs.

General-purpose capability does not divide cleanly into peaceful and military branches.

The same precision serves the institution holding the receiver.

GPS also changed logistics by reducing uncertainty about moving assets.

A dispatcher no longer needs to know only where a truck was at its last check-in. A fleet can increasingly know where vehicles are now. Estimated arrival times become better. Routes can be optimized with digital maps. stolen assets can be tracked. field service can be dispatched by proximity.

Location becomes data.

That changes management.

The telephone let a manager speak to a distant worker.

The telegraph let headquarters issue rapid instructions.

GPS can let an organization see the position of the worker’s vehicle or device without asking.

Coordination becomes observation.

This is a major shift in the relationship between mobility and privacy.

A map used to tell the traveler about the world.

A networked GPS device can tell the world about the traveler.

That difference would become much more consequential after smartphones combined GPS with cellular data, app ecosystems, advertising identifiers, cameras, accelerometers, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and personal accounts.

The GPS chapter is the location substrate before the smartphone makes location continuous and commercial.

This sequencing matters.

The smartphone does not invent location awareness. It makes a preexisting satellite utility personal, networked, and attached to identity.

The same is true of mapping.

Digital map databases, geocoding, satellite imagery, aerial photography, road networks, and routing algorithms all complement GPS. The receiver can know its coordinates without knowing whether a river, restaurant, interstate, or cliff occupies those coordinates.

Position is not meaning.

A map supplies meaning.

A routing system supplies action.

This is another example of technology stacking.

GPS supplies a low-level service.

Applications turn the service into something a user recognizes.

The economic value can migrate upward.

The government operates satellites. Device manufacturers sell receivers. Mapping companies build databases. software firms build route planners. logistics companies reorganize operations. advertisers monetize location. app developers create services around proximity.

One public infrastructure supports layers of private value.

This makes GPS a particularly interesting American technology because the state created and maintained a system whose downstream commercial value was impossible to capture centrally.

The economic surplus escaped the operator.

That echoes the internet.

Open or freely available infrastructure can generate enormous private markets precisely because users do not have to negotiate a bespoke commercial contract for every signal or packet.

Permissionless use lowers friction.

That does not mean infrastructure is free to build.

The costs are socialized or borne elsewhere in the stack.

The user experiences a free signal because taxpayers funded satellites, launches, control systems, maintenance, modernization, and military requirements.

“Free” often means the invoice moved.

The decade’s other strong rival is broadband internet.

Cable and DSL connections expanded high-speed household access, changing the internet from a session people dialed into toward an always-on environment. Broadband enabled richer websites, media, file sharing, online gaming, e-commerce, and eventually streaming.

If the previous chapter is the internet’s architecture, perhaps 1996–2005 should belong to broadband as the technology that made that architecture ordinary.

That is a compelling case.

Lithium-ion batteries are another rival. Commercialized in the early 1990s and rapidly improving, rechargeable lithium-ion cells enabled laptops, phones, cameras, and eventually electric vehicles and grid-storage systems. Modern portable computing depends on the battery almost as much as the chip.

Wi-Fi deserves a place too. Wireless local networking turned broadband from a cable attached to one desk into a shared ambient household and office service.

GPS wins because it adds a distinct dimension to digital systems.

The internet knows identity and address in network space.

GPS gives machines coordinates in physical space and a precision clock.

Once those worlds combine, software can act differently depending on where a person or object is.

Location becomes computable context.

That is a prerequisite for ride-hailing, turn-by-turn phone navigation, delivery tracking, location-based search, geofencing, precision agriculture, fleet optimization, many emergency services, and enormous parts of the modern mobile economy.

The satellite constellation turns the planet into an input field for software.

This is one of the great crossings from bits back into atoms.

A computer no longer knows only what the user types.

It can know where it is.

The strongest argument against giving GPS the decade is that most consumers did not reorganize life around GPS until the smartphone era.

Standalone car-navigation units and handheld GPS devices mattered, but the blue dot did not become universal until it was bundled into a device people already carried.

That is true.

The chapter survives because infrastructure importance can precede interface ubiquity. The 2000 policy change expanded civilian precision, and industries began building around the capability before the average person understood how many systems were using it.

Invisible dependence is still dependence.

The book has now arrived at a point where technologies begin combining almost faster than chapters can separate them.

Internet, broadband, Wi-Fi, GPS, digital cameras, lithium-ion batteries, cellular networks, touchscreens, flash storage, web services, and semiconductor scaling are all converging.

The next winner is less an invention than a merger.

The smartphone will put these systems in one object and then keep the object against the human body.

GPS gives the machine a location.

The smartphone gives the location an identity.

**What would prove this choice wrong?** GPS loses if its broader timing and infrastructure significance is judged too invisible or too late for 1996–2005, while broadband, search, Wi-Fi, or lithium-ion batteries changed more realized American behavior during the period. It also loses if full operational capability in 1995 means the system’s proper decade is the prior one. I keep GPS here because the 2000 end of Selective Availability is a genuine civilian phase change: without replacing the constellation, policy made the public signal dramatically more useful. A military navigation system became a general civil utility for position and time, and software acquired a common answer to the question “where?”
