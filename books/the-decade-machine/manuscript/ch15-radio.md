# 1916–1925: Radio

A telephone network grows by adding conversations.

Radio grows by adding listeners.

That difference created a new kind of power.

In a telephone call, the sender and receiver are usually both participants. The line exists for their temporary exchange. In broadcasting, one transmitter sends outward and thousands or millions of receivers can listen without adding comparable burden to the sender.

The marginal listener is nearly free.

That economic fact changed American culture.

Radio technology had existed before broadcasting. Wireless telegraphy had become useful for ships, military communication, and point-to-point signaling. Amateur experimenters built transmitters and receivers. Governments understood the strategic importance of the spectrum. During World War I, the U.S. government took control of radio stations and restricted amateur transmitting.

The war demonstrated wireless capability but did not settle what civilian radio would become.

The breakthrough after the war was not one invention.

It was a format.

Broadcasting.

Instead of using radio mainly to send a message to a specific station, transmit programming intended for anybody with a receiver.

On election night in November 1920, Pittsburgh station KDKA broadcast returns from the presidential contest between Warren G. Harding and James Cox. The event is often used as a milestone in regular American broadcasting. It was not the first sound ever transmitted by radio, and arguments over “first station” depend on definitions. What matters is what followed.

People began buying receivers because there was something to hear.

Stations appeared because people were buying receivers.

Manufacturers improved receivers because the audience was growing.

The loop accelerated.

This is the radio version of network effects, but the geometry is different from the telephone.

A telephone subscriber adds potential connections for other subscribers. A radio listener adds audience value for the broadcaster and advertiser without needing a wire back to the station.

Broadcasting scales attention asymmetrically.

That asymmetry became one of the most important political and commercial technologies of the twentieth century.

One voice could become millions of private experiences at once.

The listener might be sitting alone in a farmhouse, apartment, store, hotel lobby, or living room. The sound feels local because it emerges inside the room. The event may be hundreds of miles away.

Radio manufactured intimacy at scale.

This is a strange combination.

Mass media before radio existed in newspapers, magazines, books, posters, and film. Print could reach huge audiences, but readers encountered it at different times and supplied the voice internally. Film could create shared spectacle, but audiences gathered physically in theaters.

Radio delivered a live human voice into private space.

The nation could hear together without gathering together.

That capability changes politics because timing changes persuasion.

A printed speech can be edited, excerpted, or delayed. A live broadcast carries cadence, emotion, hesitation, and personality. The audience hears not only what the speaker says but how the speaker inhabits the words.

Politics becomes acoustic.

The full consequences would emerge later with presidential broadcasting, national networks, wartime speeches, advertising, entertainment, and propaganda. The 1916–1925 period is the formation stage. The architecture is already clear enough to change behavior.

Americans began arranging evenings around programs.

Manufacturers sold sets and parts. Hobbyists built radios. Department stores and newspapers promoted the medium. Churches, universities, companies, and newspapers operated stations. Music, weather, news, sermons, lectures, sports, market reports, and entertainment crossed the same air.

The spectrum became crowded.

That crowding forced a governance problem.

Unlike a newspaper press, a radio transmitter can interfere physically with another transmitter using the same frequencies in the same region. The medium therefore created scarcity not mainly in paper or distribution but in spectrum coordination.

Who transmits where?

At what power?

On which frequency?

With what right to interfere with others?

Radio turned an invisible physical resource into public policy.

The Radio Act of 1912, passed after the Titanic disaster and growing wireless use, had already created federal licensing requirements. Broadcasting’s rapid growth in the early 1920s made the existing regime increasingly inadequate. Congress would pass a new Radio Act in 1927, just outside this chapter’s interval, creating a stronger federal regulatory framework.

The problem arrived before the law.

That sequence is familiar.

Technology creates a new kind of collision. Institutions invent rules after the collisions become expensive.

Automobiles created traffic law. Electricity created utility and safety regulation. radio created spectrum allocation. Aviation created airspace rules. pharmaceuticals created modern drug oversight. internet platforms created new fights over content, privacy, competition, and liability. AI is producing similar institutional lag.

The future often appears first as a regulatory headache.

Radio also created a business-model problem.

If listeners can receive a broadcast without paying for each program, who pays for production and transmission?

Hardware manufacturers could support programming to sell receivers. Stations could be tied to newspapers or other institutions. Sponsorship and advertising emerged as powerful answers. In 1922, WEAF in New York aired an early paid commercial message, a milestone in the development of advertising-supported broadcasting.

The business model would reshape the medium.

When audience attention becomes the asset sold to advertisers, programming is not simply content for listeners.

It is also a machine for assembling listeners into a marketable group.

This is the beginning of a lineage that runs through commercial radio, broadcast television, magazines, web portals, search engines, social networks, streaming platforms, and ad-supported mobile apps.

If the user does not pay the full cost of the medium, somebody else may be purchasing access to the user’s attention.

Radio industrialized that exchange in the home.

Advertising itself was not new. Newspapers and magazines had long sold it. Billboards existed. Catalogs and direct mail existed.

Broadcasting changed the emotional bandwidth.

A brand could have a voice.

A jingle could lodge in memory. A sponsor could be associated with a beloved program. Repetition could reach households at scale. The ad did not wait to be read; it entered the room as sound.

This made attention a scheduled resource.

Programming grids developed because broadcasters wanted listeners to return at predictable times. Households learned the schedule. The clock and the radio reinforced one another.

The assembly line had synchronized workers to production time.

Radio synchronized consumers to program time.

Industrial modernity was acquiring rhythms.

Train time standardized clocks across regions. Factory time disciplined shifts. Radio time organized leisure. Later television schedules would intensify the pattern. Then streaming and on-demand media would partially break it, letting audiences move programming around personal time. Smartphones would create a different discipline: not appointment viewing but continuous reachability.

Every media technology negotiates who controls the schedule.

Early radio gave the broadcaster unusual power.

You listened when the program was transmitted or you missed it.

That scarcity made live attention valuable.

Radio’s reach into rural America deserves particular attention.

Telephones and electrification spread unevenly. Rural households could be isolated from urban entertainment and fast information. A radio receiver, once powered and equipped with an antenna, could pull voices from far away without a dedicated physical line to the house.

Broadcasting jumped the last-mile problem in one direction.

That is important.

Wired networks are expensive partly because every endpoint requires a connection. Radio can cover a large area from one transmitter. The receiver becomes cheap relative to extending a wire to every listener.

Wireless systems trade physical last-mile construction for spectrum and signal constraints.

Cellular networks will later use the same broad advantage with two-way communication, though they require dense networks of base stations and much more complex spectrum reuse.

Radio’s early simplicity was extraordinary.

Build a transmitter tall and powerful enough, and the signal can reach people the broadcaster has never met and cannot count precisely.

That one-way architecture created community and propaganda with the same mechanism.

A shared broadcast can help a dispersed population feel connected. It can deliver emergency information, education, culture, music, and public events. It can also allow governments and demagogues to speak over local intermediaries and cultivate emotional loyalty at scale.

The microphone can become a political instrument.

The United States would not experience radio dictatorship in the form seen in some twentieth-century regimes, but American politicians, corporations, religious leaders, and activists quickly learned the medium’s persuasive power.

Technology changes who can command a room by changing how large the room can be.

The founding-era printer expanded the room asynchronously.

Radio made the room simultaneous.

This is why the tractor loses the decade, though it is a formidable rival.

Gasoline-powered tractors were spreading on American farms and reducing dependence on horses and animal feed. Mechanized power changed acreage, labor, timing, and agricultural economics. The Fordson became particularly important in the early 1920s.

A case for the tractor would be strong if the book prioritized material productivity.

Radio wins because it creates a new national coordination layer that crosses sectors. Farmers themselves used radio for weather and market information. Businesses advertised. politicians spoke. musicians performed. sports became live mass events. families reorganized leisure. The same infrastructure served many unrelated functions.

It was a general-purpose attention network.

Household electrification is another rival.

Electric service was spreading and enabled radio itself. Without power, many receiving sets required batteries or other arrangements. The grid’s expansion was continuing to transform factories and homes.

But electricity had already won its architectural decade.

Radio was one of the applications that showed what a power-and-communications stack could do.

The technology also altered celebrity.

A performer or speaker could become familiar to people who had never seen them. Voice separated from place. Audiences formed parasocial relationships with personalities encountered repeatedly through sound.

This creates a new kind of scale for reputation.

Print authors had achieved national fame. Film stars did too. Radio added recurring domestic presence.

The celebrity does not visit millions of homes.

The signal does.

That distinction is central to modern cultural economics. Once one performance can be distributed to enormous audiences, a small number of performers can capture attention that would previously have been divided among many local entertainers and speakers. Media technology can create superstar markets.

The best performer does not become slightly more valuable.

Distribution makes the best-known performer available everywhere.

This dynamic will intensify with recorded music, television, streaming, social media, and digital platforms. Technology that lowers distribution cost can increase concentration in attention even while increasing the total amount of content available.

More choice can produce bigger winners.

Radio showed the pattern early.

The medium also changed music itself. Performers adapted to microphones and transmission. Genres reached new regions. Recording and broadcasting reinforced one another. Cultural styles crossed local boundaries faster. Regional distinctions did not vanish, but national culture thickened.

That cultural integration can look benign in hindsight.

It also creates pressure toward homogenization. Local voices compete with powerful stations and national programming. Advertisers prefer audiences that can be aggregated. Networks, once formed after this period, favor programs that travel broadly.

Scale rewards common denominators.

The tension between local diversity and national distribution remains unresolved in every later medium.

The internet would appear to reverse the equation by making publishing cheap for almost anyone. Then a small number of platforms would become dominant gateways to discovery and attention.

Distribution decentralizes.

Attention recentralizes.

Radio did a simpler version.

By 1925, the medium had moved beyond curiosity. Receivers were consumer objects. stations were established institutions. Businesses, regulators, and households were organizing around broadcasting even though the mature network era lay ahead.

The technology had become a schedule.

That is often the moment infrastructure becomes socially real.

People stop asking what the machine can do and start asking what is on tonight.

The next decade’s winner would be even more domestic and much less charismatic.

It would hum in the kitchen.

The refrigerator would change food by changing time.

Radio made distant events arrive now.

Refrigeration would let perishable things arrive later.

Both technologies are machines for controlling time, but from opposite directions.

One compresses delay.

The other manufactures it.

**What would prove this choice wrong?** Radio loses if the tractor’s effects on agricultural labor, animal power, land use, and food production were materially larger during 1916–1925, or if household and industrial electrification should continue to dominate the period. It also loses if commercial broadcasting’s truly national reach belongs after the formation of networks beginning in 1926, making this chapter one decade too early. I keep radio because the one-to-many wireless architecture was already socially legible by 1925: Americans were buying receivers, stations were competing for attention, advertising was emerging, and spectrum congestion had become a governance problem. The nation had acquired a machine for hearing itself at the same time.
