# 1986–1995: The Internet

The internet is not one network.

That is the breakthrough.

Computers had been networked for years before 1986. ARPANET had demonstrated packet switching and wide-area computer communication. Universities, companies, government agencies, bulletin-board systems, commercial online services, local-area networks, and research communities operated many kinds of connected systems.

The problem was not whether computers could communicate.

It was whether unlike networks could communicate without becoming one centrally designed network.

The internet’s architecture answered yes.

Protocols provided common rules for addressing, transmitting, routing, and reassembling information across networks whose internal technologies could differ. A packet did not need to know the business model, ownership, or detailed engineering of every network it crossed. Each layer did enough work to pass the packet onward.

Interconnection became the product.

That is why the internet wins 1986–1995.

The key technical foundations are older than the interval. TCP/IP became the standard protocol suite on ARPANET in 1983. Domain naming, packet networking, Ethernet, and other crucial components had earlier histories. If this book ranked invention dates, the internet would belong elsewhere.

But 1986 is a better place to watch the system become national infrastructure.

The National Science Foundation launched NSFNET to connect researchers with newly funded supercomputer centers. The network expanded rapidly and became a major U.S. backbone, linking universities and regional networks. Federal support helped create a common high-speed research infrastructure whose usefulness encouraged more institutions to connect.

Then the network escaped the research world.

That transition is the decade’s real story.

The internet’s power came from not requiring every participant to use the same computer, operating system, local network, or application. Compatibility lived at the protocol layer.

This is the shipping-container principle translated into information.

A container standard lets a box move from truck to crane to ship to rail without unpacking the cargo.

Internet protocols let packets move from one network to another without requiring the networks to become identical.

Both technologies standardize the handoff.

The box hides cargo.

The packet hides application data.

The receiving infrastructure handles the interface.

This abstraction allows heterogeneity to scale.

That is more powerful than standardizing every internal component.

America had spent two centuries learning to coordinate through common interfaces: survey coordinates, interchangeable parts, rail gauges, electrical characteristics, telephone switching, container dimensions, computer buses. The internet is the culmination of that logic in a particularly flexible form.

Agree on enough at the boundary and innovate inside.

This architectural principle made the network unusually generative.

A company could create a new application without asking every network operator to redesign the underlying infrastructure. A university could connect a new kind of computer as long as it spoke the protocols. A new local network technology could emerge without replacing the entire internet.

The network separated innovation at different layers.

That separation is a form of freedom.

It is also a form of indifference.

The packet network does not need to know whether the data represents scientific research, an email, a financial transaction, a photograph, a political argument, pornography, malware, a medical record, or a shopping order. The generality that makes the internet useful also makes governance difficult.

The infrastructure carries purposes it did not design.

This is the same moral neutrality encountered with printing and radio, intensified by interactivity.

A printing press can reproduce opposing arguments.

Radio can broadcast education or propaganda.

The internet lets every connected endpoint become sender and receiver.

That changes the power structure.

Broadcast media are asymmetric. A small number of stations transmit to large audiences because spectrum, capital, and regulation limit the number of broadcasters.

The internet is technically more symmetric. An endpoint that receives data can also send it. The same connection used to read a page can publish one. The same machine that downloads software can host a service.

In principle, the network turns consumers into producers.

That principle would collide with economics later.

Hosting large services requires capital. Search and social platforms gain network effects. app stores, cloud systems, content-delivery networks, and large data centers recentralize important functions. The internet’s protocol architecture remains distributed while business power can become concentrated above it.

Decentralized technology does not guarantee decentralized markets.

This distinction is essential to understanding the digital economy.

The decade’s most visible acceleration came from the World Wide Web.

Tim Berners-Lee developed the core ideas and technologies of the Web at CERN around 1989–1991: URLs, HTTP, HTML, a browser, and a server. The Web is not the internet. It is an application system built on the internet.

That distinction is widely blurred because the Web became the interface through which many ordinary people first experienced the network.

The internet provides connectivity.

The Web provides a navigable publishing system.

Hyperlinks changed the structure of documents by making references executable. A citation or pointer no longer merely told the reader where another document existed. A click could request it.

The reference became an action.

That is a remarkable interface invention.

The printed page had spent centuries building information through linear sequences and explicit indexes. Hypertext made documents traversable as a network.

Reading became routing.

The 1993 release of NCSA Mosaic helped make the Web usable to a wider audience. Mosaic supported graphical pages and versions for common personal-computer platforms. It reduced the technical friction of accessing networked information.

Again, the interface matters as much as the protocol.

A capability can exist for years and remain socially narrow because the user experience demands too much expertise. The browser translated internet protocols into point, click, read, and eventually buy.

The network became visual.

Commercialization followed.

Through the early 1990s, restrictions around commercial traffic on federally supported backbone infrastructure were loosened and the private networking market expanded. NSFNET’s backbone service was retired in 1995 as commercial providers took over core traffic roles.

This is a major institutional transition hidden behind browser history.

Government-supported research infrastructure helped create the network environment.

Private companies then built businesses on, beside, and eventually far larger than the research system.

The internet is another case where the public/private dichotomy fails.

Defense research, university networking, federal standards policy, private telecom companies, software firms, equipment manufacturers, nonprofits, standards communities, and individual programmers all built different layers.

No one actor “invented the internet” in the useful sense.

The system’s strength came partly from the absence of one owner.

Standards communities such as the Internet Engineering Task Force developed protocols through relatively open processes. The motto often associated with internet engineering—rough consensus and running code—captured an institutional culture in which practical interoperability mattered more than centralized decree.

Standards became governance without government in the conventional sense.

That model has limits and politics of its own. Participation requires expertise, time, and institutional support. Technical choices embed values. Protocol decisions create winners and constraints. But compared with proprietary networks, the internet’s standards process allowed a broad community to build shared infrastructure without assigning one corporation permanent control over the core language.

Open interfaces made permissionless innovation more plausible.

A developer did not need the telephone company to approve a new kind of web page.

A retailer did not need NSF to approve an online store.

A teenager could build a website.

This edge freedom is one of the internet’s deepest economic properties.

Innovation can happen at endpoints because the middle mostly moves packets.

The principle became known as end-to-end design in networking debates: keep some functions at the edges rather than building application-specific intelligence into the network core.

The architecture helped the internet absorb applications nobody had anticipated.

Email existed before the Web. File transfer, remote login, discussion groups, chat, games, voice, video, commerce, search, social networking, cloud computing, streaming, and machine-to-machine communication accumulated over time.

The network did not need a new physical build for each application.

This is the digital version of the electrical grid’s socket.

Once the general service exists, applications compete to use it.

The internet also changed the economics of copying.

Digital information can be reproduced perfectly at very low marginal cost. The printed page had dramatically reduced copying cost relative to manuscript reproduction. The internet combined cheap digital copies with cheap distribution.

That pairing destabilized industries built around controlling physical copies.

Music, newspapers, software, books, photography, film, maps, classified advertising, and other information products would be forced to confront a world where distribution no longer required pressing discs, printing paper, or maintaining local storefronts.

The network removes scarcity from one layer and creates scarcity elsewhere.

Attention becomes scarce.

Trust becomes scarce.

Authenticity becomes scarce.

Discovery becomes scarce.

The business capable of helping users navigate abundance gains power.

This is why search becomes so important in the following period even though GPS wins that decade in this book. Once publishing becomes cheap, finding becomes valuable.

The internet also undermined geographic monopolies.

A local newspaper’s classified section benefited from being the place where local buyers and sellers met. An online service can aggregate listings across geography. A software retailer benefits from shelf space; a download bypasses the shelf. A specialist store benefits from local scarcity; online commerce can expose consumers to national inventory.

Connection increases competition by making distant alternatives visible.

It also enables winner-take-most markets by aggregating demand onto large platforms.

The same network both fragments and concentrates.

This duality is central to the internet’s political economy.

The strongest rival for 1986–1995 is cellular telephony.

Mobile phones moved communication away from fixed locations. Digital cellular standards and expanding networks were making personal reachability increasingly practical. The ability to call a person rather than a place is a major coordination change.

Why does the internet win?

Because mobile telephony during this interval is primarily a communications service, while the internet is a general substrate for communication, publishing, software distribution, commerce, collaboration, and future services not yet invented.

The network is broader in function.

Graphical personal computing is another rival. Microsoft Windows and Apple Macintosh systems made computers easier to use, and the PC became standard office equipment. Fiber-optic communications increased network capacity enormously.

The internet depends on these complements.

It wins because it connects them into a shared address space.

This choice again risks hindsight. In 1986, NSFNET was research infrastructure. In 1990, most Americans were not browsing websites. In 1995, commercial internet adoption was accelerating but had not yet transformed every industry.

The chapter earns the decade because the architecture crosses from specialized networking into public commercial possibility before the interval ends.

By 1995, a mainstream observer could see that the network was becoming a new place for business and culture, even if nobody could predict exactly which firms or models would dominate.

The internet had become an addressable environment.

That word—addressable—links back to the surveyor’s chain.

The land survey gave physical territory standardized addresses.

The internet gives machines logical addresses.

A packet can be routed to an endpoint because the endpoint is named inside a system.

Coordinates make distance administrable.

The same pattern has returned in a different medium.

The next decade’s winner will join physical and informational addressability.

GPS will let machines answer two questions with extraordinary precision:

Where am I?

What time is it?

The internet tells a packet where to go.

GPS tells the world where it is.

**What would prove this choice wrong?** The internet loses if its decisive household and commercial effects are judged to belong mostly after 1995, making cellular telephony, graphical personal computing, or fiber optics more important within the interval itself. It also loses if the Web, rather than the internet, is the correct technology because protocols alone remained invisible to ordinary users. I keep the internet because NSFNET, open standards, the Web, Mosaic, and commercialization combined during this period to turn networking from a collection of specialized systems into a general interoperable environment. The important invention was not one network. It was a way for networks to stop caring that they were different.
