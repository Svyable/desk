# 2016–2025: The Transformer

The final winner is the least secure.

That is not a flaw in the chapter.

It is the fact that makes the chapter honest.

The technologies of 1776–1785 have had 250 years to reveal their consequences. The technologies of 2016–2025 have had, at most, a decade. Some are still inside the phase where capital, publicity, scientific excitement, and real capability are difficult to separate.

History has not finished voting.

The provisional winner is the transformer.

Not the electrical transformer from the grid chapter.

The machine-learning architecture introduced in the 2017 paper *Attention Is All You Need* by Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan Gomez, Łukasz Kaiser, and Illia Polosukhin.

The name creates a pleasing historical accident. In the nineteenth century, a transformer made electrical voltage negotiable so power could travel farther through a grid. In the twenty-first, the transformer made attention across sequences computationally tractable in a way that helped machine-learning systems scale language, code, images, audio, and other forms of data.

Both technologies reduce a bottleneck in coordination.

The 2017 paper proposed an architecture for sequence processing built around attention rather than recurrence or convolution as the dominant mechanism. The immediate task was machine translation. The paper did not announce a universal intelligence machine. It did not predict chatbots writing legal memos, students using language models for homework, programmers generating code, image models producing photorealistic scenes, or companies reorganizing software around AI agents.

It solved a technical problem.

The consequences arrived through scale.

Previous language models and sequence systems often processed information in ways that made long-range dependencies and training parallelism difficult. The transformer’s attention mechanism allowed models to weigh relationships among tokens while taking advantage of highly parallel hardware during training.

Parallelism matters because scaling requires throughput.

A model architecture can be theoretically elegant and commercially weak if training takes too long or cannot use available hardware efficiently. The transformer fit unusually well with accelerators already being built for highly parallel numerical computation.

Architecture met hardware.

Hardware met data.

Data met capital.

The combination created a new scaling path.

This is the same pattern seen throughout the book.

The cotton gin meets land, slavery, credit, and textile demand. The automobile meets roads, oil, rubber, manufacturing, and finance. The shipping container meets standards, cranes, ships, ports, and trade. The smartphone meets cellular networks, GPS, batteries, cameras, apps, and cloud systems.

No transformative technology arrives alone.

Generative AI is the most extreme modern example because the user sees almost none of the stack.

A person types a sentence into a box.

Behind the box may sit a model containing billions or more learned parameters, specialized accelerators, high-bandwidth memory, networking fabric, distributed training software, data pipelines, storage systems, inference servers, safety systems, monitoring, and an electrical and cooling infrastructure capable of supporting dense computation.

The interface is smaller than the infrastructure.

That has been a recurring rule since the electric outlet.

The cleaner the interface becomes, the more complexity the system may be hiding.

The transformer’s most consequential effect may therefore be an interface change rather than an intelligence benchmark.

Traditional software requires people to translate intention into explicit structures the computer understands.

A spreadsheet requires formulas.

A database requires queries.

A programming language requires syntax.

A graphical interface lets users point and choose, but the available actions still have to be represented visibly through menus, buttons, icons, fields, and workflows.

Large language models offer another possibility.

Describe the desired outcome.

The system attempts to infer the procedure.

Language becomes an executable interface.

That is a large claim and a dangerous one.

Language is ambiguous. Humans rely on context, shared culture, unstated assumptions, physical knowledge, and social expectations when interpreting requests. Software traditionally avoids that ambiguity by demanding formal input.

Generative AI moves some ambiguity into the machine.

The user can say “summarize this,” “write the code,” “compare these contracts,” “make the chart,” “plan the trip,” “translate this,” or “find the error” without specifying every intermediate operation.

The machine attempts to bridge intention and procedure.

That is a reduction in interface cost.

If reliable enough, it expands who can use computing and what tasks become worth delegating.

The word reliable is doing enormous work.

Large language models can produce fluent falsehoods. They can misread instructions, invent citations, fail silently, expose private information, reproduce bias, generate insecure code, or behave unpredictably under inputs far from expected conditions. A system that sounds certain while being wrong is particularly dangerous because language fluency is itself a trust signal for humans.

The same interface that lowers the expertise barrier can lower the skepticism barrier.

This is why AI cannot be evaluated only through benchmark scores.

The relevant question is whether an organization can safely place a task inside the model’s error distribution.

Some tasks are forgiving.

Generate brainstorming options. Draft a rough email. Reformat notes. Produce code that will be tested. Summarize text that a knowledgeable user will review.

Other tasks are not.

Diagnose a rare disease without oversight. Transfer money. approve a legal filing. control critical infrastructure. make a military targeting decision. impersonate a person. produce a safety-critical engineering calculation without verification.

Capability is not deployment.

Deployment is capability plus acceptable failure.

This distinction is where many early AI forecasts fail.

A model can perform a task impressively in a demonstration while being too unreliable, expensive, slow, legally uncertain, or difficult to integrate for production use.

The path from demo to institution is the same path every technology in this book had to travel.

The steamboat had to become scheduled service.

The automobile had to become reliable enough for dependency.

Electricity had to become a utility.

The transistor had to become manufacturable.

The internet had to become commercial infrastructure.

AI must become trustworthy enough to disappear into work.

The first evidence suggests that useful deployment is real and uneven.

A major field study of more than five thousand customer-support agents found that a generative-AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by about 15 percent on average, with much larger gains for less experienced workers and small or mixed effects for the most experienced. That pattern is more interesting than the headline productivity number.

The technology appears capable, in some settings, of compressing parts of organizational experience into a tool.

Newer workers can access patterns resembling the behavior of stronger workers without spending as long accumulating those patterns individually.

That is a knowledge-transfer technology.

The machine-tool chapter described production knowledge moving from individual craft into equipment, jigs, gauges, and process design.

The assembly-line chapter described task knowledge moving into workflow.

The AI chapter may be describing parts of tacit cognitive knowledge moving into models.

Not all of it.

Not reliably.

Not without distortion.

But enough to matter.

This creates a strange labor effect.

If AI helps novice workers more than experts, it can compress performance differences. That can raise the productivity of lower-skill workers and expand access to difficult tasks.

It can also reduce the scarcity value of expertise.

If the organization can package some expert patterns into a model, the bargaining power attached to years of accumulated experience may change.

Expertise does not disappear. Experts may become more productive too, supervise systems, handle exceptions, set quality standards, and operate at a higher level of abstraction.

But the distribution of value can move.

Automation repeatedly does this.

The reaper changes the value of harvest labor.

Machine tools change the value of craft fitting.

The assembly line changes the value of broad manufacturing skill.

The personal computer changes the value of clerical calculation.

Generative AI changes the cost of producing plausible cognitive work.

Plausible is not the same as correct.

That distinction may define the labor market of the next decade.

A first draft becomes cheap.

Verification becomes valuable.

The system can generate ten options.

Judgment has to choose one.

The model can write code.

Tests have to determine whether it works.

The model can summarize a contract.

A lawyer remains responsible for the consequences.

The model can generate an image.

A publisher must determine rights and authenticity.

Abundance shifts scarcity upward.

This pattern should be familiar by now.

Printing made copies cheaper and distribution more valuable. The internet made publishing cheaper and discovery more valuable. Digital photography made images cheaper and attention more valuable.

Generative AI makes production of some symbolic artifacts cheaper.

Trust, taste, provenance, responsibility, and verification become more valuable.

The technology is therefore not simply “automation of knowledge work.”

It may be a repricing of cognitive stages.

Some tasks become near-zero-cost drafts.

Other tasks become the expensive boundary where somebody must certify reality.

This could reorganize professions in ways that resemble the effects of industrial standardization.

Law firms, consultancies, software teams, advertising agencies, financial firms, universities, media organizations, and government offices are built partly around apprenticeship ladders. Junior people perform large quantities of work while learning patterns from senior people.

If machines perform a large share of the junior work, organizations may gain short-run efficiency and damage the process by which future experts are trained.

The technology can eat its own training pipeline.

That is adaptation debt.

A profession that automates the work novices once used to learn must invent a new way to create experts.

This problem is not solved by telling young workers to “use AI.” The old work was not only output. It was practice.

Technological efficiency can remove rehearsal.

The risk parallels GPS navigation. Turn-by-turn directions make travel easier and can reduce the need to build internal spatial knowledge. Calculators reduce arithmetic burden and can weaken practice if introduced without understanding. Automation transfers competence to the tool and changes what the human rehearses.

Whether that is progress depends on which competence remains useful when the tool fails.

AI makes the problem broad because language touches almost every profession.

The architecture also changes software development.

Code has always been a translation layer between human intention and machine execution. Large language models can generate code from natural-language descriptions, explain unfamiliar code, transform syntax, write tests, search documentation, and increasingly act across software tools.

This creates a recursive loop.

Software helps build AI.

AI helps build software.

That loop can accelerate technical change because the tool used to create digital systems becomes itself partially automatable.

The personal computer let individuals write software.

The internet let software reach the world.

Cloud computing let software rent infrastructure.

AI begins to let intention produce software with less direct coding.

If that trend continues, the population capable of creating useful computational behavior could expand dramatically.

It also raises security risk dramatically.

Lowering the skill threshold for useful coding can lower the skill threshold for malware, phishing, fraud, vulnerability exploitation, and automated abuse. Defensive systems can use the same models.

The capability is symmetric enough that advantage depends on institutions, access, data, incentives, and response speed.

This is another technology without moral direction.

The printed page could publish abolitionist arguments and slave-sale notices.

The internet can connect dissidents and harass them.

Generative AI can tutor a child and generate an industrial-scale scam.

Capability expands the possibility set.

Governance inherits the sorting problem.

The transformer’s scaling also makes physical infrastructure central again.

The public interface appears digital and weightless. The buildout is not.

Training and serving large models requires advanced semiconductors, data centers, networking equipment, transformers in the electrical sense, transmission capacity, generation, cooling systems, construction, water in some designs, and enormous capital spending.

Artificial intelligence returns the information economy to the power grid.

This is a satisfying historical loop.

The electrical grid chapter separated useful power from local generation.

The integrated circuit chapter made computation cheap enough to proliferate.

The internet connected computation.

The cloud reconcentrated large amounts of computation into data centers.

AI makes the electricity consumed by those data centers strategically visible.

The virtual world acquires a utility bill large enough to influence energy planning.

This creates a new geography.

Data centers want power, fiber, land, cooling, permits, tax arrangements, and access to transmission. Regions with attractive combinations can draw investment. regions with constrained grids may delay projects. Utilities face large new loads whose future duration and utilization are uncertain.

The AI boom is therefore partly a construction boom.

That matters because capital-intensive booms can overshoot.

Railroads overbuilt some routes. Electricity had speculative cycles. telecommunications firms laid fiber ahead of demand in the late 1990s. useful infrastructure can survive the investors who financed too much of it.

AI could follow a similar path.

The technology may be real and the economics of every proposed data center may not be.

A revolutionary capability does not guarantee revolutionary returns to current owners.

This book has another title devoted to that distinction for a reason.

The transformer also creates a concentration problem.

Training frontier-scale models can require enormous compute and capital. Advanced accelerators are produced through concentrated semiconductor supply chains. Cloud infrastructure is dominated by a small number of firms. Proprietary model providers can control access through APIs.

At the same time, open-weight models and cheaper inference can distribute capability widely.

AI therefore pulls toward centralization and decentralization simultaneously.

The training infrastructure centralizes.

The resulting model can sometimes be copied, compressed, fine-tuned, and run more locally.

The largest models resemble central power plants.

Smaller models resemble generators at the edge.

The equilibrium is unsettled.

So is the legal framework.

Training data can contain copyrighted works, personal information, public-domain material, code, images, audio, and data collected under terms never written with machine learning in mind. Courts and legislatures are still defining boundaries around training, output, authorship, liability, privacy, competition, and disclosure.

The technology arrived before the contract language.

Again.

Automobiles arrived before traffic law matured.

Radio arrived before spectrum regulation matured.

The internet arrived before modern platform law matured.

AI is forcing institutions to decide what machine learning is allowed to learn from and who is responsible for what it produces.

This lag is not evidence that law is uniquely slow.

It is evidence that new capability changes which conflicts are worth formalizing.

The strongest rival for 2016–2025 is not one technology.

There are at least three that could make this chapter look foolish by 2050.

CRISPR is the first.

Programmable gene editing emerged before this decade, but the period includes the first FDA-approved treatment using CRISPR-based genome editing. The capability to modify a patient’s cells to treat a genetic disease is not an interface trick. It is direct intervention in biological code.

If CRISPR therapies become common across inherited disease, cancer, infectious disease, agriculture, and synthetic biology, the technology may ultimately be more important than generative AI.

The second rival is mRNA.

Decades of research in RNA biology, lipid nanoparticles, immunology, and vaccine development converged in the COVID-19 pandemic. mRNA vaccines moved from platform promise to mass deployment at extraordinary speed. The technology demonstrated that a biological manufacturing platform could be reprogrammed by changing sequence information rather than redesigning an entire conventional vaccine process from scratch.

If mRNA becomes a broad platform for vaccines and therapeutics, 2020 may look more historically important than 2017.

The third rival is the lithium-ion battery ecosystem.

Battery costs fell dramatically over the period, helping electric vehicles move toward mass markets and enabling large-scale stationary storage. Batteries change the relationship between energy generation and time in the same way refrigeration changed the relationship between food and time.

Variable renewable electricity becomes more manageable if cheap storage can shift energy across hours. Transportation becomes electrifiable if batteries carry enough energy at acceptable cost, weight, safety, and longevity.

If batteries become the decisive infrastructure of a low-carbon energy system, the transformer may lose.

Reusable launch systems deserve attention too. So do advances in solar power, robotics, synthetic biology, and quantum technologies.

This is why the final chapter cannot end with a coronation.

The book’s method is strongest where it is most vulnerable.

A decade winner must be an argument against serious alternatives.

For 2016–2025, the evidence remains incomplete.

Why choose the transformer now?

Because it appears to change the interface between humans and computation across an unusually broad range of domains at once.

A gene-editing tool operates on biology.

A battery stores energy.

An mRNA platform produces biological interventions.

A language model potentially mediates software, writing, search, education, customer service, scientific work, design, administration, media, and machine control through one common input: language.

Breadth is its advantage.

Reliability is its weakness.

The winner depends on which matters more over time.

The architecture also fits the book’s 250-year pattern with uncomfortable precision.

The printed page let an argument travel without its author.

The survey let institutions act on land they could not see.

The telegraph let information travel without a body.

The telephone let voice travel without presence.

Radio let one voice occupy millions of rooms.

The personal computer let an individual own computation.

The internet let unlike computers coordinate.

GPS gave machines a common location and time.

The smartphone attached networked computation to the person.

The transformer begins to let human intention travel into software without every step being explicitly programmed.

Each technology increases the distance between intention and execution.

That can feel like empowerment.

It can also feel like loss of control.

The person who prints a document knows exactly what was written before the press copies it.

The person who asks a generative model for an answer may not know what answer will emerge.

Earlier coordination technologies increased reach while preserving relatively clear execution.

AI introduces probabilistic delegation.

That is new enough to deserve caution.

A traditional program follows explicit instructions, even if bugs make the result wrong.

A large language model infers behavior statistically from training and context. Its internal representations are not written as a human-readable procedure for every output.

The machine can surprise its creator.

This makes verification a first-class infrastructure requirement.

The AI systems that matter most may not be the models with the highest benchmark scores.

They may be the systems that combine models with tools, permissions, tests, provenance, retrieval, memory, audit logs, human review, and clear failure boundaries.

The model is becoming one component inside a larger agentic system.

That transition echoes every earlier chapter.

The invention becomes useful when the system absorbs its weaknesses.

Boilers need inspection.

Railroads need dispatch.

Electric grids need protection systems.

Automobiles need traffic rules and brakes.

Airplanes need air-traffic control and maintenance regimes.

Computers need operating systems and security.

AI needs an institutional safety stack we are still inventing.

The decade ends before that stack is mature.

So the chapter ends with an asterisk.

The transformer is the winner for now.

That phrase would be unacceptable in the first twenty-four chapters.

Here it is the only responsible conclusion.

We are living too close to the machinery to know which part becomes infrastructure and which part becomes a transitional design.

The transformer itself may be replaced by another architecture while the broader language-interface capability continues. That would not necessarily invalidate the chapter’s deeper thesis, just as later electric grids improved far beyond the equipment of 1895.

But the rival technologies may also outrun it entirely.

The historian of 2076 may choose CRISPR.

Or mRNA.

Or the battery.

Or a technology whose importance in 2025 was hidden inside a laboratory, factory, standards committee, or obscure paper no bestseller author knew to mention.

That possibility is the best way to finish 250 years.

The people in 1776 did not know the chapter titles either.

They knew only the problems in front of them.

How do we copy the declaration?

How do we measure the land?

How do we move upstream?

How do we cut the harvest in time?

How do we send the message faster?

How do we make the part repeatable?

How do we move power farther?

How do we make the car affordable?

How do we keep food cold?

How do we shrink the switch?

How do we wire the circuit?

How do we move cargo without touching every crate?

How do we put computation on the desk?

How do we connect networks?

How do we know where we are?

How do we put all of it in a pocket?

How do we tell the machine what we want?

The final question is the newest version of the oldest ambition.

Increase the radius at which intention can become action.

That ambition built extraordinary abundance and extraordinary danger.

The technology of each decade did not determine America.

It changed the menu of what America could do next.

The next choice was always human.

Until now, perhaps, the machine is beginning to participate in the choosing.

That is why this decade matters.

And why we should be careful about pretending we already know its name.

**What would prove this choice wrong?** The list is unusually long. The transformer loses if generative AI stalls at unreliable assistance rather than becoming dependable infrastructure; if the economics of training and inference remain too expensive; if energy, chip, data, legal, or trust constraints sharply limit deployment; if most productivity gains remain narrow and fail to aggregate; if CRISPR therapies transform medicine; if mRNA becomes a broad programmable therapeutic platform; if battery systems become the central technology of transport and electricity; or if another architecture replaces transformers so completely that the 2017 design looks transitional rather than foundational. I keep it provisionally because between 2017 and 2025 the architecture helped turn natural language into a practical interface for general-purpose computation at unprecedented scale. The choice is less certain than any other in the book. It should remain that way until history supplies more evidence.
