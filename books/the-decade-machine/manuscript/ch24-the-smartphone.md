# 2006–2015: The Smartphone

The smartphone is what happens when infrastructure disappears into a person.

By 2006, most of the important ingredients already existed somewhere.

Cellular networks carried voice and increasing amounts of data. Personal digital assistants managed contacts and calendars. BlackBerry devices made mobile email culturally important. Digital cameras were shrinking. GPS receivers were becoming cheap. Wi-Fi connected laptops to broadband. lithium-ion batteries powered portable electronics. Flash memory improved. web browsers existed. Touchscreens existed. music players existed. ARM processors and mobile operating systems were developing rapidly.

The smartphone did not invent these technologies.

It combined enough of them behind one interface that users stopped thinking of them as separate systems.

That is the breakthrough.

Apple introduced the iPhone in January 2007 as a phone, widescreen iPod, and internet communications device in one handheld product. The first model lacked several features later users would consider basic. It ran on a comparatively slow cellular data network in the United States. It did not launch with a third-party native app store. GPS would appear in a later model. Competitors had offered advanced mobile devices before it.

The historical importance lies less in technical priority than in convergence.

The large capacitive touchscreen and software-centered interface made the phone’s controls change with the task. A physical keyboard dedicates space permanently to letters. A touchscreen can become a keyboard, map, camera control, web page, game board, document, payment surface, or anything software can draw.

The interface becomes programmable.

This is the smartphone’s integrated-circuit moment.

The integrated circuit changed electronics by manufacturing many components as one object.

The smartphone changes consumer technology by making many interfaces one surface.

Hardware specialization gives way to software-defined behavior.

That makes the device unusually extensible.

The app store model, introduced by Apple in 2008 and developed through competing ecosystems including Android, turned the phone into a distribution platform for functions the manufacturer did not have to invent. A developer could write software for millions of devices already equipped with network access, display, processor, storage, camera, microphone, speakers, accelerometers, and increasingly location services.

The installed base became a sensor network waiting for ideas.

That sentence captures why the smartphone wins 2006–2015.

The device is not simply a better telephone.

It is a standardized bundle of capabilities attached to a person and addressable through software.

The word attached matters.

A personal computer is personal because one individual may own and use it. A smartphone is personal because it travels with the person through the day.

The device knows when the user wakes, where the user travels, whom the user communicates with, what the user photographs, which apps the user opens, what the user searches, and often what the user buys.

The computer becomes proximate to life rather than proximate to the desk.

That changes the economics of attention.

A desktop computer must be visited.

A phone can summon the user.

Notifications reverse the direction of interaction.

Instead of the person deciding to sit down at a machine and begin a computing session, applications can interrupt the person wherever the person happens to be.

The computer gains access to idle moments.

Waiting in line becomes screen time. Walking can become listening time. A commute can become messaging time. A dinner can become notification time. A bedroom can become office, entertainment center, newspaper, map, camera, and social space.

The smartphone monetized temporal cracks.

This is one of the most consequential business-model shifts of the digital era.

Radio sold scheduled attention. Television sold larger blocks of scheduled attention. Desktop web advertising monetized time users spent online.

Mobile computing makes online a condition rather than a destination.

The user is reachable continuously enough that services compete for frequency of return, not merely duration of session.

Attention becomes a stream.

That environment rewards behavioral design.

Push notifications, badges, feeds, infinite scroll, read receipts, typing indicators, streaks, likes, algorithmic ranking, autoplay, and recommendation systems become mechanisms for bringing the user back. The phone’s sensors and identity make measurement unusually detailed.

Media companies had always measured audiences imperfectly.

Mobile platforms can observe behavior at individual scale.

The advertising system shifts from buying a broad audience to targeting a profiled person or device.

The smartphone is therefore also a surveillance infrastructure.

That does not require a conspiracy.

It follows from the economics of a networked personal sensor financed partly through advertising and data-driven services.

Location is especially powerful.

The previous chapter gave GPS a decade because precise position and timing became a common utility. The smartphone combines location with identity, network connectivity, maps, payments, contacts, cameras, and software.

The blue dot becomes a person.

That enables turn-by-turn navigation, nearby search, ride-hailing, food delivery, fitness tracking, emergency location, geotagged photography, dating by proximity, local advertising, asset tracking, and a huge range of convenience.

It also creates a persistent record of movement when applications or platforms collect location data.

The map can look back.

Technology turns navigation into history.

That is a major change in privacy because ordinary movement used to be ephemeral unless someone deliberately observed it. A person could walk across a city without generating a detailed machine-readable trail in multiple corporate systems.

The smartphone changes the default.

The more useful the device becomes, the harder it is to opt out of the infrastructure around it.

This is another sign that a technology has crossed into environment.

A person without a car can live differently in a car-centered region but may face substantial friction. A person without electricity can technically survive but cannot participate normally in modern urban life. A person without internet access loses access to services, information, education, and work.

By the end of this decade, the smartphone was becoming that kind of expectation.

Pew Research found that roughly 35 percent of U.S. adults owned smartphones in 2011. By 2015, the share was about two-thirds. The device moved from affluent novelty toward mainstream infrastructure in only a few years.

Adoption speed matters.

Earlier technologies often needed decades for infrastructure to spread. Electricity required wires to buildings. Railroads required track. telephones required physical local loops. Smartphones rode on existing cellular networks, global semiconductor manufacturing, the internet, GPS, Wi-Fi, and mass electronics supply chains.

The stack was already waiting.

This is why modern technologies can diffuse faster than older ones.

They inherit infrastructure.

A new app can reach millions of users without laying cable, building stores, manufacturing custom hardware, or negotiating separate distribution in every city. The smartphone ecosystem compresses distribution cost for digital services.

The app store is a container port for software.

Standardized packaging, review rules, payment systems, installation, updates, permissions, and discovery allow programs to move from developer to user through one interface.

The analogy also exposes power.

A port authority or dominant carrier can shape trade through access and fees. An app-store operator can shape software markets through approval rules, commission structures, ranking, technical restrictions, and control of the operating system.

The platform reduces friction and becomes the gatekeeper to the reduced-friction world.

This is a recurring technology paradox.

The system that makes entry easier can become powerful enough to decide who enters.

The smartphone also reorganized photography.

The camera had already become digital. Camera phones existed before 2007. The smartphone made the camera networked, geolocated, computational, and permanently available.

That combination matters more than pixel count.

A photograph no longer needs a separate process of transfer before publication. Capture and distribution occupy the same device. The delay between event and audience collapses.

That changed journalism, policing, protest, family memory, dating, advertising, celebrity, evidence, and the ordinary performance of self.

Everyone becomes a potential broadcaster carrying a camera.

The radio chapter had given one transmitter access to millions of receivers.

The smartphone gives millions of people transmitters.

That seems like pure decentralization.

Then social platforms centralize the audience.

Again, architecture and market structure diverge.

The phone enables anyone to record. A handful of platforms determine what becomes visible at scale.

The smartphone also altered labor markets.

Ride-hailing and delivery platforms depend on the device as dispatch terminal, GPS receiver, payment interface, identity token, rating system, communication channel, and management instrument. The worker carries the company’s operational system in a personal device.

This is a new relationship between labor and capital.

The factory once brought workers to machinery owned by the company.

The platform can bring algorithmic management to workers through devices they carry themselves.

Location, route, response time, customer rating, acceptance rate, and other metrics become measurable continuously.

Management becomes software close to the body.

That is one reason the smartphone has consequences far beyond consumer convenience.

It changes how organizations can coordinate people who are not employees in a traditional workplace, not sitting at desks, and not under direct human supervision.

The device turns distributed labor into an addressable workforce.

The smartphone also transforms payments and identity.

A phone can store credentials, receive authentication codes, generate security keys, make contactless payments, scan tickets, board aircraft, unlock cars, and access bank accounts. Losing the device can therefore mean losing access to many unrelated systems simultaneously.

Convenience consolidates failure.

The key ring, wallet, map, camera, phone book, calendar, music player, newspaper, and computer merge.

One dead battery can disable a remarkable fraction of ordinary capability.

Integration reduces the number of objects and increases dependency on the integrated object.

That is the consumer version of systemic risk.

The strongest rival for 2006–2015 is cloud computing.

Amazon Web Services launched major infrastructure services in 2006. Cloud platforms made computing, storage, databases, and later increasingly sophisticated services available on demand without companies owning all underlying hardware. Startups could rent infrastructure rather than build data centers. Enterprises could scale differently. Software increasingly became a service rather than an installed product.

Cloud computing is arguably the deeper economic infrastructure beneath the app economy.

Why does the smartphone win?

Because the smartphone moves the network to the person and thereby changes daily behavior across more visible domains. Cloud computing enables many services; the smartphone creates the ubiquitous endpoint those services can reach.

The two technologies are complements.

The cloud makes the phone thin enough to rely on remote infrastructure.

The phone gives the cloud a continuous user interface.

CRISPR is the scientific rival.

The 2012 demonstration of CRISPR-Cas9 as a programmable gene-editing system opened a profound new capability in biology. It would lead toward new therapies, research tools, agricultural applications, and ethical questions about editing life.

If long-run biological impact outweighs immediate diffusion, CRISPR could eventually replace the smartphone as this decade’s winner.

Shale hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling are another serious American rival. Their combination transformed U.S. oil and gas production, energy markets, regional economies, emissions, geopolitics, and infrastructure. Energy abundance can be as systemically important as digital capability.

The smartphone wins provisionally because its convergence is so broad.

It absorbs prior winners.

Printing becomes digital reading.

Telegraphy becomes messaging.

Telephone becomes one app.

Radio becomes streaming.

Camera becomes sensor.

GPS becomes location service.

Personal computer becomes pocket computer.

Internet becomes ambient connection.

The smartphone is not the replacement for all these systems. It is the interface through which a person increasingly reaches them.

That makes it the most concentrated expression of 250 years of coordination technology so far.

It also prepares the final decade.

The smartphone reduced the interface to touch and apps.

The next technology reduces it again.

Instead of finding the right software control, the user can increasingly describe the desired outcome in ordinary language.

The command line demanded syntax.

The graphical interface demanded navigation.

The smartphone demanded touch.

The transformer begins to make the interface a sentence.

**What would prove this choice wrong?** The smartphone loses if cloud computing is judged the more fundamental general-purpose infrastructure beneath the mobile economy, if CRISPR’s long-run medical and biological effects become vastly larger, or if the U.S. shale revolution deserves greater weight for energy, geopolitics, and industrial transformation. It also loses if the iPhone-centered story overcredits one product in a broader smartphone evolution that began before 2007. I keep the smartphone because the decade marks a rapid shift from computers people visit to computers people carry. By combining network, software, sensors, location, identity, camera, payments, and a programmable interface, the phone made digital infrastructure physically intimate.
