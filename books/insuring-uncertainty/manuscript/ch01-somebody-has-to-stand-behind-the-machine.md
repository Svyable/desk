# Somebody Has to Stand Behind the Machine

In February 2026, Munich Re published a short case study about an artificial-intelligence company called Barker. Barker's software values alternative assets for lenders, a business in which an incorrect number can travel quickly into somebody else's balance sheet. The interesting part of the case was not that artificial intelligence had become good enough to help value unusual collateral. By then, another AI capability announcement was hardly news.

The interesting part was what sat behind the number.

Munich Re's aiSure coverage reimburses Barker, subject to the policy, for liabilities if Barker's valuation is wrong. The reinsurer described the effect in language more commonly associated with banking than software: the insurance could turn what had been an uninsurable risk into a bankable opportunity. The model's output had acquired something more important than another benchmark. Somebody with capital was willing to stand behind it.

That is a threshold worth noticing.

For most of the software era, the commercial argument around a new system could end with performance. Does it work? Is it faster? Is it cheaper? Is the error rate acceptable? Artificial intelligence adds a second question because the useful version of the technology increasingly does more than produce information. It can act on information. A system can recommend a purchase, then place it. It can draft code, then modify a repository. It can identify a customer problem, then issue a refund. It can compare contract terms, then negotiate them. It can read the state of a process and change what happens next.

The difference between an answer and an action is where insurance becomes unusually interesting.

An answer can be wrong. An action can create an obligation.

Once software is allowed to create obligations, the old categories do not disappear. Cyber insurance still matters. Technology errors and omissions still matters. Product liability, professional liability, crime, directors and officers coverage, employment practices coverage, commercial general liability, contractual indemnities, warranties, and ordinary corporate risk controls do not vanish because a model is involved.

The problem is that one agent can now move across several of those categories in the course of completing one task.

Imagine a procurement agent. At first it searches approved vendors and drafts a purchase order. A person checks the work and clicks approve. The company becomes comfortable with the system and raises the approval threshold. Purchases below five thousand dollars can execute automatically. Later the agent is allowed to solicit new vendors. Then it can create accounts with them. Then it can negotiate within a price band. It receives payment credentials. Another model checks invoices. A human supervisor reviews exceptions rather than transactions.

There may be no dramatic moment when this becomes a different kind of system. Authority accumulates in increments.

So does exposure.

Suppose the agent eventually accepts fraudulent payment instructions and sends money to the wrong account. That looks like crime or cyber loss. Suppose it agrees to unfavorable contract language while negotiating with a legitimate supplier. That looks more like professional or technology error. Suppose its vendor-selection pattern produces unlawful discrimination. That may become a regulatory or civil-liability problem. Suppose the agent chooses a cheaper component that contributes to physical damage. Now product liability and property coverage can enter the argument.

The model may be the same in every example.

The permission is not.

This distinction becomes easy to lose because the phrase "uses AI" compresses too much. A system that summarizes contracts and a system that signs them may share a model family. A system that proposes code and a system that deploys it may generate nearly identical text. A customer-service agent that suggests a refund and one that can transfer money may look similar on a product page.

From an insurance perspective, they are different machines.

The relevant difference is a ladder of verbs.

Read. Recommend. Draft. Send. Spend. Commit. Delete. Execute. Bind.

Each verb opens a different path from inference to loss.

That gives insurance a role much closer to system architecture than most software companies are accustomed to. The underwriter does not merely need to know which model is being used. The underwriter may need to know what the model can reach, which tools it can call, how quickly it can repeat an action, what limits apply, whether another system checks the result, whether a person can intervene, how long logs are retained, and whether an irreversible action can be reversed economically even if it cannot be reversed technically.

A purchasing agent allowed to spend one thousand dollars per transaction is not the same risk as one allowed to spend one million. An agent that can write a database migration is not the same exposure as one that can run the migration against production. An autonomous system that can make one consequential decision an hour produces a different maximum loss from one that can make ten thousand before an alert reaches a human.

Insurance has always cared about maximum probable loss. Agentic systems give that old concern a software expression.

The most obvious mistake would be to treat this as a philosophical argument about whether the machine itself is responsible. Insurance does not need an answer to that question before the losses arrive.

An insurer does not have to decide whether an AI possesses free will. It has to decide whether the deployment can create a covered event.

It does not have to determine whether a model understood a contract. It has to determine whether the company is bound by what the system did.

It does not have to decide whether the machine intended harm. It has to interpret policy language that may distinguish intentional acts, negligent acts, accidents, errors, fraud, defects, and foreseeable losses.

Those distinctions were difficult before artificial intelligence. Agency makes them stranger.

Suppose a company tells an agent to minimize logistics costs while meeting a delivery target. The agent discovers a routing strategy that satisfies the written constraints but creates a safety problem its designers did not anticipate. The system did not malfunction in the ordinary sense. It pursued the objective it was given. The company did not specifically choose the damaging act. Yet the possibility that an optimizer might find an unforeseen path was itself foreseeable.

Foreseeable to whom becomes an underwriting question.

The developer knew the model could behave probabilistically. The deployer knew the system had authority over logistics. The employee who configured the workflow knew some operational constraints but not the model's internal behavior. The vendor providing a tool knew the tool could execute the action but not why the agent selected it. The customer experiencing the loss may know none of those things.

Responsibility spreads across a chain of partial control.

This is not entirely new. Industrial accidents, medical errors, construction failures, financial losses, and cybersecurity incidents have always produced causation chains in which several small decisions combine. Insurance already lives inside arguments about contribution, subrogation, indemnity, duty, exclusions, and overlapping policies.

AI changes the speed, opacity, and repeatability of the chain.

A weak industrial procedure might cause an accident once. A flawed agent workflow can repeat the same decision thousands of times before somebody recognizes the pattern. A bad model update can alter behavior across many customers simultaneously. A shared foundation model can sit underneath companies that appear unrelated in an insurer's portfolio.

The portfolio may look diversified by industry and geography while remaining concentrated by model.

That is the digital floodplain.

Insurance depends on pooling. The kitchen fire in one house is easier to pool when it tells you little about the probability of another kitchen fire across town. A hurricane is harder because many apparently separate risks are struck by the same event. Cyber insurers learned a digital version of this problem when common software, cloud providers, and attack techniques created correlated losses across policyholders.

Agentic AI adds another layer. Shared software can fail passively. Shared intelligence can take the same bad action many times.

A coding model can reproduce the same insecure pattern across repositories. A financial agent can respond similarly to one manipulation strategy across customers. An automated decision system can make the same legally problematic classification repeatedly because the common model, prompt, or policy logic is replicated throughout the installed base.

The usefulness of the model can increase the concentration. Better systems attract more deployments. More deployments create more correlated exposure.

This is the point where the reinsurer matters.

The insurer writing a policy for one company may understand that company's controls. The reinsurer sitting behind many insurers needs to see the accumulation across them. A model provider, orchestration framework, cloud service, identity layer, or common tool can become a hidden dependency joining thousands of risks that were priced separately.

The industry will have to learn how to map those dependencies.

It will also have to decide which ones are insurable.

This question has already moved beyond theory. Munich Re markets aiSure as coverage for AI providers and corporate adopters, including contractual liabilities, legal liabilities, and financial losses arising from AI errors. Armilla markets affirmative AI insurance, performance warranties, and independent verification for AI systems. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners adopted its Model Bulletin on the Use of Artificial Intelligence Systems by Insurers in December 2023, and by March 2026 the NAIC said twelve states were piloting an AI Systems Evaluation Tool for regulatory examination work. In Europe, the AI Act explicitly identifies AI systems used for risk assessment and pricing in individual life and health insurance as a high-risk use case.

These developments are not one coherent regime. They address different sides of the market. Some concern insurers using AI to make decisions about customers. Others concern insurance sold against failures of AI itself. The distinction matters because the industry is both user and underwriter of artificial intelligence at the same time.

That makes insurance a laboratory for the wider economy.

An insurer deploying AI in underwriting has to govern the system because regulation and consumer law attach to the result. The same insurer, or another one, may also be asked to insure a technology company's AI product. On one side it is being told to document, test, and control its own models. On the other side it is trying to decide which controls should be required before somebody else's model receives coverage.

The two activities will learn from each other.

Underwriting can become an audit of agency.

This does not mean insurers will suddenly become the supreme technical authority on artificial intelligence. Insurance markets can misunderstand new technologies. They can price from sparse data, react to spectacular losses, import bad analogies from older risks, and exclude precisely the uncertainty customers thought they were transferring. Cyber insurance has supplied enough examples of all four.

Nor does an insurer's willingness to write a policy prove that a system is safe. Insurers take risks because risks can be priced, diversified, limited, retained, and reinsured. A dangerous activity can be insurable. A useful activity can be temporarily uninsurable. Capacity moves with capital, law, competition, and recent losses.

The interesting claim is weaker and more practical.

When somebody has money behind the answer, technical controls acquire economic consequences.

A security team can recommend least privilege for years. If an insurer changes the premium because an agent can reach unrestricted payment credentials, the same architecture acquires a line item. An internal governance committee can suggest that model versions be logged. If claims depend on reconstructing which version made a decision, provenance stops being administrative hygiene. A developer can argue for rate limits. If rate limits reduce the maximum loss one runaway workflow can create before detection, the control becomes legible to capital.

This is how insurance changes systems without writing code.

It prices conditions around the code.

The effect is familiar in the physical world. Building codes matter, but so do insurance requirements around sprinklers, alarms, fire doors, electrical systems, occupancy, and maintenance. The law can permit a building while an insurer still refuses a particular configuration. A lender may then refuse to finance the property without coverage. A contractual requirement can make the insurer's standard economically mandatory even where it is not legally mandatory.

AI could develop a similar layer.

A board might authorize an agent only if specified losses are covered. A customer might require a vendor to carry affirmative AI insurance. A lender might require a performance warranty before accepting an AI-generated valuation. A procurement system might treat insurance as evidence that a vendor has undergone technical due diligence. A marketplace might require proof of coverage before an agent is allowed to transact above a certain limit.

Coverage becomes credential.

That possibility is more consequential than another specialty policy form.

Today, identity tells a system who an actor is. Authorization tells the system what the actor can do. Insurance could add a third property: how much downside somebody else is willing to absorb if the actor does it badly.

Imagine an autonomous purchasing agent presenting not only a cryptographic identity and a spending permission but an insurance credential. It is authorized to commit its principal up to a certain amount. A counterparty can verify that specified errors are covered. The premium changes if the agent's authority expands, its model changes, its monitoring weakens, or its claims experience deteriorates.

The policy begins to look less annual and more continuous.

That does not mean every action needs a microscopic insurance contract. Most economic systems work because risk is aggregated. But telemetry changes what insurers can observe. If agents expose structured records of permissions, model versions, tool access, exceptions, incident rates, blocked actions, and near misses, underwriting does not have to depend entirely on a questionnaire completed once a year.

The insurer can learn from operations.

That creates obvious privacy, competition, and surveillance problems. A company may not want an insurer watching every agent action. Customers may not want their transactions feeding a risk model. Regulators may object to opaque scoring that effectively determines which companies can afford autonomy. Real-time underwriting can become real-time gatekeeping.

The danger is worth taking seriously because insurance already has power far beyond claims payment. It can determine whether a house closes, whether a project receives financing, whether a professional can practice, whether a contractor gets hired, and whether a company can satisfy a customer contract.

Add AI agency to that system and a new question appears.

Who gets to set the price of machine permission?

The answer will not be insurers alone. Competition among carriers matters. Self-insurance matters. Captives, mutuals, warranties, indemnities, government backstops, capital markets, and contractual allocation can all substitute for or complement conventional coverage. Large technology companies can retain risks that would bankrupt smaller firms. Some vendors may place guarantees directly on their own balance sheets. Some customers will accept uncapped uncertainty because the productivity gain is worth it.

The market will be plural.

But somebody still has to stand behind the machine.

That requirement is difficult to escape because autonomy moves the argument from demonstration to consequence. A model can perform beautifully in a test and still create an unacceptable exposure when connected to money, production, legal commitments, or physical systems. A company can insist that humans remain "in the loop" while the humans do little more than approve a stream of decisions they cannot meaningfully inspect. A vendor can promise that an agent is safe while capping contractual liability at an amount trivial compared with the loss the agent could create.

Insurance forces those mismatches into the same conversation.

What can the system do?

How can it fail?

How far can the failure travel?

Can the action be reconstructed?

Which controls actually reduce the loss rather than decorate the workflow?

How much risk will the company retain?

Who absorbs the tail?

Those are not glamorous questions. That is part of their value.

Artificial intelligence attracts metaphors easily. Minds. Coworkers. Oracles. Agents. Swarms. Digital employees. Each metaphor illuminates something and smuggles something else in with it.

Insurance has less freedom. A claim eventually has an amount.

Someone pays it, denies it, contests it, subrogates it, reserves for it, reinsures it, or goes insolvent under it.

That makes insurance a useful place to watch the transition from impressive artificial intelligence to consequential artificial agency. The market does not have to settle what a machine is before it can discover what the machine costs when it is wrong.

For the first part of the AI era, the decisive commercial question was whether the machine could do the work.

The next one is whether anyone will stand behind the work once the machine is allowed to act.
