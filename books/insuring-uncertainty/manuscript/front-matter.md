# Insuring Uncertainty

*Insurance in the Era of AI Agency*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

Insurance has always been a machine for turning uncertainty into permission.

A ship can leave port because somebody will stand behind the cargo. A bank can lend against a building because fire, wind, and liability can be transferred away from one balance sheet. A surgeon can practice, a contractor can build, a company can sign a lease, and a board can authorize a project because the downside does not have to remain concentrated where the decision was made.

Artificial intelligence is creating a new kind of uncertainty for that machine to absorb.

The difficult part is not that AI sometimes fails. Software has failed for as long as software has existed. The difficult part is that increasingly capable systems can act: select tools, spend money, contact customers, negotiate terms, alter code, move through workflows, and make decisions that create obligations before a person has reviewed each step.

That changes the insurance question.

The old question was often whether software performed as promised. The new one is how much authority software can be given before its failures stop looking like ordinary product defects and start looking like delegated conduct.

This book is about that boundary.

It does not require machines to be conscious, morally responsible, or legally personified. Those debates matter elsewhere. Insurance can encounter the problem much earlier. An underwriter needs to know who authorized the system, what it could do, how far one mistake could travel, whether the action can be reconstructed, and which party is supposed to carry the loss.

Those questions sound narrow. They are not.

If autonomy becomes economically important, insurability may become one of the conditions under which autonomy is allowed to scale. A system can be lawful and technically impressive yet remain commercially unusable if customers, lenders, boards, or counterparties cannot obtain a satisfactory answer to a simpler question: who stands behind it when it is wrong?

The answer will not come from insurance alone. Courts, regulators, engineers, vendors, contracts, standards, and corporate governance will all shape it. But insurance has an unusual role because it must convert the answer into capital.

That conversion is where uncertainty becomes expensive enough to change behavior.
