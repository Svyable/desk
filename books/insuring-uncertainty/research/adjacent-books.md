# Adjacent-book novelty check

## The Risk Pool

The closest overlap is Chapter 14, “Who Pays for the Agent?”, in *The Risk Pool*. That chapter already establishes several premises this book should not repeatedly rediscover: AI liability can cross policy lines; “silent AI” may resemble silent cyber; shared models create accumulation risk; agent permissions can affect severity; logs and reconstruction matter; and insurance can price authority.

*Insuring Uncertainty* earns a separate book only by going materially deeper and changing the center of gravity. *The Risk Pool* is about insurance as a general social and economic permission system across houses, climate, cyber, health, public backstops, and other risks. Its AI chapter is one application of that thesis.

This manuscript instead treats AI agency as the whole field of view. The planned contribution is to develop mechanisms that a single chapter cannot carry: affirmative AI policy design, insurance-backed performance warranties, machine-readable proof of coverage, dynamic underwriting from agent telemetry, the relationship between identity/authorization/insurance credentials, agent-to-agent contracting, the legal meaning of delegated conduct, continuous changes in model and permission state, correlated behavioral loss, and reinsurance design for model concentration.

The manuscript should cite or acknowledge *The Risk Pool* where it inherits a premise and avoid lifting its sequence, examples, or conclusions wholesale.

## Agentile

*Agentile* argues that agentic software creates a new economic capability: delegated intention becoming cheap, persistent, parallel, and programmable. It studies firms, labor, capital, management, markets, verification, and public capacity.

*Insuring Uncertainty* starts later in the chain. It assumes economically meaningful delegation exists and asks what happens when a third party must price the downside. Its principal actors are underwriters, reinsurers, brokers, risk managers, procurement teams, boards, lawyers, vendors, regulators, and counterparties rather than the firm and labor market in general.

## The Delegation Machine

*The Delegation Machine* studies delegation as a broad civilizational mechanism. This book should not reproduce its general history of principals, agents, authority, or delegated decision-making. It should use delegation doctrine only where it changes insurance attribution, coverage, or loss control.

## Autonomy Half-Life

*Autonomy Half-Life* studies how a grant of autonomy can become unsafe or inappropriate as facts, goals, counterparties, and surrounding conditions change. *Insuring Uncertainty* can use that observation only where changing autonomy creates a measurable insurance problem: stale underwriting assumptions, policy warranties that no longer match deployment state, or the need for dynamic notice and repricing.

## Distinct contribution test

The book remains distinct if a reader can finish it with a concrete model of how AI agency becomes financially legible: what is insured, how authority changes exposure, who carries which layer of loss, how coverage can function as a transaction credential, and how shared machine intelligence changes portfolio correlation.

If the manuscript drifts into a general explanation of why insurance enables economic activity, or a general explanation of why agents change firms, it has crossed into neighboring books and should be cut or redirected.
