# Research brief — Insuring Uncertainty

## Central question

What happens when software is allowed to create economically consequential actions and the downside of those actions must be transferred, priced, reserved, and reinsured?

The manuscript treats insurance as a bridge between technical agency and economic deployment. Its distinctive object is **insurable agency**: autonomy whose permissions, evidence trail, failure modes, loss severity, and contractual responsibility can be described well enough that capital is willing to stand behind it.

## Reader promise

By the end of the book, the reader should understand why AI liability is not merely a question of whether a model is accurate, why permission architecture can become an underwriting variable, why shared models create accumulation risk, how affirmative AI insurance and performance warranties differ from ordinary cyber coverage, and why proof of coverage could evolve into a credential that helps autonomous systems transact.

## Strongest competing explanation

Existing insurance lines and ordinary contracts may absorb most AI-related losses without producing a durable new category of “AI insurance.” Technology E&O, cyber, professional liability, product liability, commercial crime, contractual indemnities, warranties, captives, and self-insurance already contain much of the machinery required.

The book weakens if the market converges on those existing instruments and if agent permissions, model provenance, telemetry, and model concentration do not materially alter underwriting, coverage language, pricing, or reinsurance practice.

## Falsification boundaries

The thesis should be narrowed if evidence shows any of the following:

- AI-related claims remain economically ordinary within existing software and professional-liability categories even as autonomous execution expands.
- Underwriters do not price scope of delegated authority, reversibility, rate limits, logging, model versioning, or similar controls differently from ordinary IT governance.
- Common model dependencies do not create material portfolio accumulation risk beyond familiar cloud and cyber concentration.
- AI warranties and affirmative coverage remain a niche sales tool rather than an important adoption or procurement mechanism.
- Large deployers mostly self-insure AI losses and counterparties accept that arrangement without demanding external risk transfer.
- Courts and contracts establish sufficiently clear allocation rules that uncertainty about machine agency does not create a meaningful insurance-design problem.

## Research tracks

1. **Affirmative AI insurance.** Munich Re aiSure, Armilla, Mosaic and other specialty products; trigger design; performance warranties; exclusions; capacity; claims experience where public.
2. **Silent AI.** Existing cyber, E&O, professional liability, product liability, D&O, EPL, crime and general-liability language that may respond to AI-caused losses without explicit AI wording.
3. **Agency and causation.** Principal-agent doctrine, apparent authority, contractual authority, negligent supervision, product defect, professional duties, and multi-party causation chains.
4. **Permission architecture.** Spending caps, tool access, rate limits, deterministic policy checks, approval thresholds, rollback, segregation of duties, and reconstruction after loss.
5. **Accumulation and reinsurance.** Shared models, cloud concentration, orchestration frameworks, model updates, correlated behavior, catastrophe layers, and possible public backstops.
6. **Insurance as credential.** Procurement requirements, lender requirements, insurance-backed warranties, verifiable coverage, embedded insurance, and machine-readable proof of risk transfer.
7. **Continuous underwriting.** Whether agent telemetry and operational data move commercial insurance away from annual questionnaires toward dynamic risk assessment, and the privacy/governance costs of doing so.
8. **Insurers using AI.** NAIC/state governance, EU high-risk classification for life and health risk assessment/pricing, unfair discrimination, model governance and examination practice. This is evidence about the industry's own AI governance, not the same question as insuring third-party AI failures.

## Source discipline

Prefer regulators, statutes, rule text, court opinions, policy forms where public, insurer/reinsurer product documents, rating-agency material, and direct company documentation. Trade press can identify cases but should not carry the central legal or product claim when the primary source exists.

For emerging products, distinguish marketing claims from demonstrated underwriting practice and demonstrated claims payment. The existence of a product does not establish a mature market.

For liability examples, do not invent a settled rule where doctrine remains jurisdiction-specific or fact-dependent. Separate legal possibility, contractual allocation, underwriting practice, and speculation about future market structure.

## Initial evidence already established

- The NAIC adopted its Model Bulletin on the Use of Artificial Intelligence Systems by Insurers on December 4, 2023. The bulletin says insurer decisions or actions made or supported by AI remain subject to applicable insurance law and sets governance/documentation expectations.
- The NAIC states that, as of March 2026, twelve states were piloting an AI Systems Evaluation Tool intended to support market conduct, financial analysis, and examination work.
- The EU AI Act treats AI systems intended for individual life and health insurance risk assessment and pricing as a high-risk use case under Annex III.
- Munich Re markets aiSure as coverage for AI providers and corporate adopters, including contractual liabilities, legal liabilities, and financial losses arising from AI errors, and uses insurance-backed performance warranties as part of the product design.
- Munich Re's February 2026 Barker case study describes aiSure backing liabilities arising from incorrect AI-generated valuations and explicitly frames insurance as helping make the opportunity bankable.
- Armilla markets affirmative AI insurance, AI performance warranties, and independent verification for AI systems and describes policies for developers and deployers.
