The working thesis is that AI agency creates a new insurance object: delegated machine action whose downside must be attributed, bounded, priced, and financed before autonomy can spread through the real economy. The book follows the shift from insuring software failures to underwriting permissions, authority, observability, reversibility, model concentration, contractual promises, and the correlated losses that can emerge when many firms depend on the same machine intelligence.

The central claim is narrower than “AI creates new risks.” Insurance may become one of the institutions that determines which forms of machine agency are economically deployable. A regulator can permit a system and an engineer can make it work, yet a board, lender, customer, or procurement department may still refuse the deployment if nobody is willing to stand behind its failures. Premiums, exclusions, warranties, deductibles, reinsurance, and underwriting requirements can therefore become a price on autonomy.

This manuscript deliberately expands one pressure point that appears inside *The Risk Pool*. That book studies insurance as a general permission system across property, climate, cyber, health, and other domains. *Insuring Uncertainty* stays inside AI agency and goes much deeper into attribution, affirmative AI coverage, performance warranties, permission architecture, agent identity, continuous telemetry, model accumulation risk, and the prospect that proof of insurability becomes a credential carried by autonomous systems themselves.

## Working argument

Insurance has always required a workable story about actor, cause, loss, duty, and probability. Agentic AI weakens the clean boundaries among all five. A company may authorize a system to pursue an objective without choosing each action. The system may call tools supplied by other companies, use models built elsewhere, delegate to other agents, and create obligations faster than a human can review them. A loss can therefore be both authorized and unintended, foreseeable in category but not in detail, and produced by a chain in which no single participant controlled the whole event.

The book treats that difficulty as an economic design problem rather than a metaphysical one. An insurer does not need to decide whether an AI system possesses consciousness or free will. It needs to decide whether the deployment can create covered loss, how severe that loss can become, what controls limit it, whether the event can be reconstructed, and which balance sheet should absorb the result.

That turns permission architecture into underwriting material. Read-only access is different from spending authority. Drafting code is different from deploying it. Negotiating a contract is different from signing it. A system that can make ten irreversible decisions per second presents a different tail than one whose actions remain queued and reversible. Model choice matters, but the unit of exposure is increasingly the authority around the model.

If this thesis is right, insurance will do more than compensate failures after the fact. It will influence system design before deployment. Insurers and reinsurers will reward some combinations of logging, authorization, rate limits, independent checks, version control, rollback, and human escalation while refusing or repricing others. Affirmative AI insurance and insurance-backed performance warranties are early evidence that this transition has already begun.

## The book's lane

The nearest Desk books establish important neighboring territory:

- *Agentile* studies agentic software as a new economic capability and force.
- *The Delegation Machine* studies delegation as a civilizational mechanism.
- *The Risk Pool* studies insurance as a broad permission layer and contains a chapter on AI agents.
- *Autonomy Half-Life* studies how grants of autonomy decay as the world around them changes.

*Insuring Uncertainty* asks what happens when delegated machine action must be made financially legible. Its object is not agency alone and not insurance in general. It is **insurable agency**: autonomy whose authority, failure modes, evidence trail, and maximum loss can be described well enough that somebody is willing to put capital behind it.

The strongest competing explanation is that existing cyber, technology E&O, professional liability, product liability, contractual indemnity, and ordinary corporate controls will absorb AI losses without producing a distinct underwriting architecture. The manuscript should take that possibility seriously. A new insurance category is not inevitable merely because the technology is new.
