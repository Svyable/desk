# The Moral Crumple Zone

A car's crumple zone is a piece of engineering built to fail on purpose, and it is worth beginning with the machine before turning to the metaphor it eventually lent to something far less mechanical. The front and rear sections of the frame are designed to buckle and absorb the force of a collision precisely so that the passenger compartment does not have to. When a crash happens, the crumple zone takes the damage that would otherwise reach the people inside. It is one of the more elegant ideas in automotive safety: accept a controlled, visible failure in one place in order to protect something more important in another.

Madeleine Clare Elish borrowed that image for a different kind of system, one built not out of steel but out of shared responsibility between a human operator and an automated one. In a 2019 paper, "Moral Crumple Zones: Cautionary Tales in Human-Robot Interaction," Elish argued that when a joint human-automation system fails, accountability does not distribute itself evenly across everyone and everything that contributed to the failure. It tends to concentrate, instead, on the human closest to the controls at the moment things went wrong, often the person with the least actual power to have prevented the outcome, and frequently the person the system's own designers built in specifically to absorb blame the technology itself could not be made to answer for. In Elish's analogy, the human plays the crumple zone's role. The human absorbs the force of the failure. What gets protected, on the other side of that absorption, is not a passenger. It is the system.

This chapter takes Elish's argument and applies it to a single case, chosen because its record is unusually well documented and because it is the case around which this idea has been argued most directly: the death of a pedestrian struck by a self-driving test vehicle in Tempe, Arizona, on March 18, 2018. It is a hard case to write about carefully, because it involves a real death, a real name, and a real investigation whose findings deserve to be reported without embellishment, dramatization, or speculation about anything the record does not itself establish. This chapter tries to hold that line throughout.

The vehicle was operated by Uber as part of its self-driving development program. It struck and killed Elaine Herzberg, 49, who was walking a bicycle across a road outside a marked crosswalk. The National Transportation Safety Board opened an investigation, as it does for incidents of this kind, and its findings are the record this chapter relies on. They are specific, and they deserve to be stated plainly rather than dramatized.

The system's software could not classify an object as a pedestrian when that object was not near a crosswalk. Herzberg, walking her bicycle across the road at the point where she was struck, was outside a marked crosswalk, and the perception system's categorization logic was not built to reliably recognize a person in that position as a person. The system did not correctly identify the collision as imminent until roughly 1.2 seconds before impact — a window of time that does not allow for a meaningful human response, let alone an automated one, once a determination has finally been reached. Automatic emergency braking, a capability the vehicle's underlying platform possessed, had been disabled while the vehicle was under the self-driving system's computer control. This was a deliberate design choice, made on the reasoning that automatic braking triggered by the self-driving system could produce erratic vehicle behavior, sudden, unpredictable stops that might themselves create hazards, or that might interfere with the system's own decision-making as it was being developed and tested. Uber's design instead relied on a human safety driver, present in the vehicle specifically to intervene when the automated system could not handle a situation on its own. And the investigation found that the safety driver, in the moments before the collision, had been watching a video streaming app rather than monitoring the road.

It is worth taking each of those four findings in turn, slowly, because the temptation with a case this well documented is to compress it into a single sentence and move on to the argument it supports, and something gets lost when that happens. The classification failure was not a momentary glitch; it describes a structural limit in how the system had been built to see the world; it had been trained, in effect, to expect people near the places people are expected to be, and a pedestrian walking a bicycle across an open road, away from a crosswalk, fell outside the categories the software had been given to work with. The 1.2-second detection window describes the gap between the system correctly recognizing that a collision was coming and the moment of impact — not the gap between the pedestrian entering the road and the collision, but the much shorter gap between the software finally arriving at the right conclusion and having no time left to act on it. The disabled braking was not a malfunction; it was the system operating exactly as it had been configured to operate, with a safety feature intentionally left off. And the safety driver's inattention was not disputed in the investigation's findings; it was documented, specifically, as watching a video streaming app rather than monitoring the road.

Those four findings, taken together, are the entire factual foundation this chapter stands on, and it is worth resisting the urge to add anything to them. This is not a case that needs embellishment to carry weight. It is not this book's place to speculate about what Elaine Herzberg was thinking as she crossed the road, or what the safety driver was feeling as the moment of the collision approached, and this chapter will not do either. What the record supports is a description of a system: a perception stack with a specific, documented blind spot; a safety mechanism that had been switched off by design; a detection failure that left only just over a second between recognition and impact; and a human backstop who was not looking at the road when the backstop was needed.

It is also not this book's place to relitigate what happened afterward, beyond what Elish's own analysis makes visible. What can be said, following her argument directly, is that public and legal attention in the aftermath concentrated heavily on the safety driver's inattention, on the fact that a person whose job was to watch the road had, in the critical moment, not been watching it. That focus is not unreasonable on its own terms. The safety driver had a real task, a specific and important one, and the record shows the safety driver was not performing it in the moments that mattered. This chapter is not arguing that the safety driver bears no responsibility for that failure. That responsibility stands. What Elish's framework asks the reader to notice is a different question: whether the concentration of attention on that one failure, however real, produced a complete accounting of everything that made the collision possible — or whether it functioned, whether anyone intended it to or not, the way a crumple zone functions, absorbing the force of the failure at the point of least institutional resistance and leaving the system's own design choices comparatively undisturbed.

Consider what those design choices actually asked of the person in that driver's seat. This book spent chapter five establishing, with research reaching back to a British psychologist studying radar operators during the Second World War, that sustained attention to a system which is right the overwhelming majority of the time is one of the hardest tasks human cognition can be assigned, not because the people asked to do it are careless, but because vigilance decays according to a pattern that has been documented and re-documented for more than seventy years, in laboratory conditions and in the field, across a wide range of tasks and settings. A safety driver monitoring a self-driving system that performs correctly on the overwhelming majority of miles it travels is being asked to do exactly that task: watch, uneventfully, for a rare failure, in a stream of data that is almost always fine, and detect the one moment it isn't. The vigilance research this book has already surveyed says people are bad at this by default, not as a character flaw but as a predictable property of sustained attention under low-signal conditions. A single distracted-prone human being was, by the design of the system itself, the last and nearly the only line of defense against a perception system that could not reliably classify a pedestrian outside a crosswalk and a braking system that had been switched off.

There is a further irony worth sitting with, because it connects this case directly back to the vigilance research rather than merely echoing it. The entire reason a human safety driver was in that vehicle at all was as a backstop against exactly the kind of failure the automated system was known, going in, to be capable of — an acknowledgment, built into the program's own design, that the software could not yet be fully trusted on its own. That acknowledgment was reasonable. What was less reasonable, or at least less examined, was the assumption embedded in it: that a person could reliably supply, through sustained attention alone, the exact capability the automated system lacked, for hour after hour, on a task that seventy years of vigilance research says human attention degrades on within the first half hour of a watch and continues to erode gradually thereafter. The role of safety driver asks a person to be, in effect, a perfect and tireless perception system standing in for an imperfect and occasionally unreliable one, which is very nearly the definition of the task human cognition performs worst.

This is the specific sense in which Elish's concept applies here, and it is worth being precise about what it does and doesn't claim. It does not claim that the safety driver's inattention was irrelevant to the outcome — the safety driver was there to catch exactly the kind of failure that occurred, and did not catch it. It does not claim that Uber's engineers wanted a pedestrian to die, or that the decision to disable automatic braking was made carelessly rather than for a stated engineering reason. What it claims is narrower and, in some ways, more disquieting: that a system can be built, deliberately and for defensible-sounding engineering reasons, in a way that concentrates the consequences of its own blind spots onto a single human whose realistic capacity to catch those blind spots was already constrained by well-documented limits on human attention, and that when the system fails, the resulting scrutiny tends to fall hardest on that same human, because the human is visible, individual, and identifiable in a way that a perception model's classification boundary or a braking-logic decision made months earlier in a design meeting is not.

It is worth dwelling, too, on why the crumple-zone image works as well as it does, because the mechanics of a real crumple zone illuminate something about the human version that a more abstract description might miss. A car's crumple zone is not damaged because it is weak or poorly engineered; it is damaged because it was deliberately engineered to be the part of the structure that gives way first, precisely so that the stronger, more critical structure around the passenger compartment does not have to. The failure is not an accident of the design. It is the design. Applied to a human operator inside a joint human-automation system, the analogy carries an uncomfortable implication: the concentration of blame onto the person closest to the failure is not necessarily an accident of how institutions, the press, or the legal system happen to process a tragedy after the fact. It can be, in effect, exactly what certain design choices set up in advance — not through malice, but through the ordinary logic of building a system that keeps a human in the loop specifically to absorb the cases the automation cannot handle, without equally investing in making sure that human is actually positioned to absorb them.

There is a structural asymmetry worth naming directly. The decision to disable automatic emergency braking under computer control was made by an organization, deliberated on, presumably documented, weighed against engineering tradeoffs its designers judged reasonable at the time. The decision about how a perception system would classify pedestrians outside a crosswalk was, similarly, an engineering choice made in advance, encoded into the software long before that particular day in Tempe. Both of those decisions shaped what happened on March 18, 2018, as much as anything the safety driver did or failed to do in the final seconds before the collision. But neither decision has a face. Neither decision was sitting in a car, in the specific location where a camera or a courtroom could train its attention on it. The safety driver did. That is not a claim that institutional decisions are more culpable than individual ones in some abstract moral calculus this book is not equipped to settle. It is an observation about where scrutiny naturally lands when a failure has both a diffuse, institutional cause and a single, visible human at the point of impact, and Elish's argument is that this natural landing place is not the same as a complete account of what happened.

It is worth returning, briefly, to why Uber's engineers disabled automatic braking under computer control in the first place, because the reasoning was not reckless on its face — the concern was that automatic braking, triggered by the self-driving system's own logic, could itself produce erratic, unpredictable vehicle behavior, potentially creating a different category of hazard or interfering with the very system being developed and tested. That is a genuine engineering tradeoff, not an obviously indefensible one in isolation. What makes it look different in hindsight is not that the reasoning was absurd, but that it was made without, apparently, fully accounting for the compounding effect of the other choices layered on top of it: a perception system with a known category of object it could not reliably classify, a detection process slow enough that only 1.2 seconds separated recognition from impact, and a single human being assigned to catch whatever the automated system missed, doing a task that decades of vigilance research already predicted the safety driver would very likely fail at some point during a long, uneventful shift. Each choice, defended on its own terms, might sound reasonable to the people who made it. Stacked together, they describe a system with almost no margin left for the one thing it was still asking a human being to supply.

None of the individual choices, examined in isolation, looks like the cause of what happened. That is, in a sense, the entire point, and it is what makes a moral crumple zone difficult to see clearly even when the record is as thorough as this one. A single bad decision is easy to locate and easy to hold someone accountable for. A stack of individually defensible decisions, each one reasonable within the narrow frame of the meeting where it was made, is much harder to see as a system at all until something goes wrong at the point where they all converge, and by the time that convergence happens, it happens through a single visible person, in a single visible seat, at a single visible moment, which is where the resulting scrutiny naturally goes to look for an explanation.

None of this is offered as an excuse. It would be its own kind of distortion to read Elish's framework as a way of transferring responsibility entirely from the person in the driver's seat to an abstraction called "the design," as though naming the system's blind spots dissolves the driver's own failure to do the assigned job. The safety driver had a real task that day: watching a road that, in the critical moments, went unwatched. The record does not support pretending otherwise. What Elish's framework offers instead is an argument about completeness — that a full accounting of a joint human-automation failure has to include both halves of the system that failed, the human and the automated, and that a society which has already built the automated half to depend on a human backstop it also knows, from decades of vigilance research, is unreliable at exactly this task, cannot honestly place the entire weight of the outcome on the backstop alone. Both things can be true in the same sentence without canceling each other out: the safety driver did not do the job at hand, and that job had been shaped, by design choices made well before that day, into one of the specific tasks human attention is worst at sustaining.

A reader looking for a clean resolution to that tension — some formula for apportioning exactly how much of the outcome belongs to the driver and how much to the design — will not find one in this chapter, because the record this chapter relies on was not built to supply one. The NTSB's role is to establish what happened and why, in enough factual detail that future systems can be built differently; it is not a court assigning percentages of fault, and this book, following the same discipline, will not manufacture a percentage the investigation itself did not produce. What the record does support is the shape of the failure, not its precise moral arithmetic, and that shape is the crumple zone Elish described: a human positioned, by design, to take the force of a failure that had already been made likely by choices made upstream of that seat.

There is also an asymmetry in how accountability structures already exist, or fail to exist, for each side of a joint human-automation failure, and the Tempe case sits squarely inside it. A safety driver has a name, an employment record, a specific shift, and a specific seat; the apparatus of investigation, reporting, and public attention already knows how to focus on a person like that, because that apparatus was built for an era in which the driver was the only decision-maker who mattered. A perception system's classification boundary, or an engineering team's decision to disable a braking feature during a specific phase of testing, does not have an equivalent, pre-built apparatus of scrutiny pointed at it by default. The NTSB's investigation is, in part, an attempt to build exactly that apparatus after the fact, which is precisely why its findings matter as much as they do here, and why this chapter leans on them rather than on the public narrative that formed before the investigation concluded. But an after-the-fact investigation, however rigorous, does not automatically redirect the informal, immediate current of public and legal attention, which had already found its natural resting place in the driver's seat before the NTSB's more complete account was available.

The phrase Elish coined is useful for a reason beyond this one case, and it is worth naming why before this chapter closes. "Moral crumple zone" names a pattern, not a single incident — a recurring shape that can appear anywhere a human is placed inside a system built to make most of its own decisions, asked to catch the rare cases the system cannot handle, and then held accountable, after the fact, as though catching those cases had been a realistic and simple expectation rather than a design decision with its own costs quietly built in. Aviation has versions of this pattern, in the pilot asked to recover from an automation failure with seconds of warning. Warehousing has a version of it, in the worker disciplined for a missed safety step the algorithmic pace made nearly impossible to observe. What makes the Tempe case the sharpest illustration this book has to offer is not that it is unique, but that its investigation was unusually thorough and unusually public, which means the shape of the pattern — the perception blind spot, the disabled safeguard, the single vigilance-limited human, the concentrated aftermath scrutiny — can be traced with a precision that most joint human-automation failures never receive.

That precision is also what makes this case the right place for the book to pivot, in its final chapters, toward autonomous driving specifically. Everything examined so far — the cockpit, the reading room, the courtroom, the warehouse floor, the trading desk — has involved a human and a machine sharing a task where the human, however imperfectly, still holds the wheel in some meaningful sense. Test-phase autonomous driving programs like the one operating in Tempe in March 2018 represent something further along a continuum this book has been tracing since its opening pages: a system designed to do nearly all of the driving itself, with a human retained specifically for the residual cases the system cannot yet handle — Bainbridge's first irony, examined all the way back in chapter three, in its most literal possible form. The designers distrusted the software enough to keep a human in the vehicle. They did not, on the evidence of what happened that day, fully reckon with how difficult they were making that human's job, or how well the vigilance research already understood, decades in advance, exactly how that kind of job tends to fail.

That is where the record of this specific case has to end, because the record itself ends there — with an investigation, its findings, and the death of a woman crossing a road who deserved a system built to see her. What comes next, in a wider and more contested body of evidence, is the question of what has and has not changed about autonomous vehicle safety since 2018, as more companies have put more such systems on public roads and started publishing their own accounts of how safe those systems are. That evidence, and the real limits of trusting a company's own account of its own system's safety, is where this book turns next.

This chapter has tried to hold two things at once, because the record itself holds two things at once. Elaine Herzberg died crossing a road, struck by a vehicle whose software could not recognize her as a pedestrian in the position she was in, whose emergency braking had been switched off by the choices of the people who built it, and whose one remaining safeguard was not watching the road in the seconds that mattered. That is what the NTSB's investigation found, stated as plainly as the facts allow. What this book takes from Elish's framework, applied to that record, is not a verdict about who was more or less at fault, but a caution about where the reader's attention, like the public's, like the legal system's, tends to go by default, and a request to look, deliberately, at what sits just outside that default view.
