# Two Kinds of Wrong

Sullenberger and Skiles never had to decide whether to trust a machine's recommendation, because no machine on that airplane made one. That is what made the decision over the Hudson so demanding, and also, in a strange way, so clean. There was no second voice in the cockpit arguing for a different answer, no proposal to weigh against their own reasoning, no possibility of following the wrong suggestion because there was no suggestion to follow. There was only the problem, and two pilots working it out together.

That cleanliness is what makes Flight 1549 a useful place to start and a misleading place to stop. It shows human judgment operating at its most exposed, with nothing standing between the decision and the person making it. But it is not the situation most people, in most high-stakes moments, actually face, and it is not the situation this book spends most of its pages examining. Once a recommendation exists — once some system has already proposed an answer before a person has finished reasoning toward their own — a second layer settles on top of the original problem, and that second layer turns out to be harder to navigate than it looks from the outside.

Most of the decisions this book is actually about do not offer that clarity. They offer a recommendation, arrived at by some process the person receiving it may only partly understand, competing for attention with whatever that person would have concluded on their own. And once a recommendation is on the table, a new question opens up that Sullenberger never had to answer: not just what to do, but how much to believe the thing telling you what to do.

People turn out to be bad at answering that question in two distinct and almost opposite ways, and getting a handle on both of them — not just one — is the task of this book.

In 1997, the psychologists Raja Parasuraman and Victor Riley published a paper in the journal Human Factors called "Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse." It ran across pages 230 to 253 of that year's second issue, and it did something deceptively simple: it gave names to failure modes that engineers and operators had been living through for years without a shared vocabulary to describe them. Parasuraman and Riley were not describing a new problem. They were describing an old one clearly enough that it became possible to study.

Automation, by the mid-1990s, was already well established in the settings human-factors researchers spent most of their time studying: aircraft cockpits, industrial process control, air traffic management, and the early digital instrumentation finding its way into hospitals and control rooms. Engineers in those fields had accumulated, over decades, a scattered body of incident reports and near-misses that all pointed toward the same underlying difficulty without ever quite naming it as one difficulty. A pilot who trusted a faulty instrument past the point of good sense and a pilot who ignored a working one both ended up in accident files, but nothing in the way those files were organized suggested the two pilots had made structurally related mistakes, pointed in opposite directions from the same underlying problem. Parasuraman and Riley's contribution was to look across that scattered record and notice the shape repeating.

The shape repeating was not particular to aviation. Process-control operators watching chemical plants, technicians monitoring early computerized diagnostic equipment, and analysts relying on decision-support software in operations centers were all susceptible to the same two failures, described in the specific language of their own fields but recognizable, once set side by side, as the same underlying pattern. That was the paper's real achievement: not the discovery of a new phenomenon, but the recognition that a phenomenon already visible everywhere was, in fact, one phenomenon, with two mirror-image expressions, rather than a grab-bag of unrelated field-specific complaints.

Their starting point was a distinction that sounds almost too obvious to need stating. Automation, in their framework, is something a human operator uses — voluntarily engages or disengages, turns on for a task and off for another, brings into a decision or leaves out of it. That baseline act, use, is not itself the interesting part. The interesting part is what happens when use goes wrong, and Parasuraman and Riley argued it goes wrong in two directions that look nothing alike and produce entirely different kinds of harm.

The first direction, they called misuse. Misuse is over-reliance: trusting an automated system more than its actual reliability warrants, and paying for that excess trust with degraded human performance. Misuse shows up as automation bias, where a person accepts a system's output with less scrutiny than they would give a colleague's opinion, simply because the output came from a machine and machines are assumed to be consistent, objective, and free of the ordinary lapses that afflict a tired or distracted human. It shows up as monitoring failure, where a person nominally supervising an automated process stops actually supervising it, because the system has been reliable for so long that continued vigilance starts to feel like wasted effort. And it shows up in the sharpest form when the automation is simply wrong and the human, primed to defer, does not catch the error in time to matter.

The second direction, Parasuraman and Riley called disuse. Disuse is under-reliance: a human operator neglecting, ignoring, or abandoning an automated system that would, in that instance, have been the more accurate guide. Disuse often has an identifiable trigger, and one of the clearest is a system with a history of false alarms. An alert that cries wolf enough times teaches its operator, entirely reasonably, to stop responding to it at full attention — and that learned inattention does not selectively switch back on for the one alarm in a hundred that happens to be real. A tool abandoned after a visible mistake, a recommendation ignored out of habit or frustration, a warning silenced because warnings have so often been noise before: all of it falls under disuse, and all of it can cost exactly as much as misuse does, just in the opposite direction.

The two failures share a peculiar feature that is easy to miss on a first pass through the taxonomy: each one can look, from the inside, like ordinary good sense. The operator who defers to a system that has been right nine hundred times running is not behaving foolishly by any ordinary standard; deferring to a track record that strong is close to what careful judgment usually recommends. The operator who stops trusting an alarm that has been wrong nine hundred times running is not behaving foolishly either. Both are applying a reasonable rule of thumb — trust what has proven reliable, distrust what has proven unreliable — to a situation where the rule of thumb happens, on this particular occasion, to point the wrong way. Misuse and disuse are not, in other words, failures of stupidity or carelessness in any simple sense. They are failures that ordinary, sound reasoning produces under exactly the wrong circumstances, which is part of why they are so difficult to train a person out of, and part of why this book treats them as a permanent feature of the landscape to be managed rather than a defect to be eliminated once and for all.

Parasuraman and Riley named a third category too, one this book will mention less often but should not skip past: abuse. Abuse, in their taxonomy, is not something the operator does at all. It describes designers, managers, or organizations deploying automation in ways that ignore the consequences for the human beings who will have to work with it — automating a task without considering what happens to the person left monitoring the automation, or imposing a system on workers without giving them the training, the feedback, or the authority to use it well. Abuse sits upstream of both misuse and disuse, because a poorly designed or poorly deployed system makes both of the operator-level failures more likely: an operator handed a system with no way to understand its limits is being set up equally well for overtrusting it or for giving up on it, depending on which way the first bad outcome happens to break.

It is worth marking this third category early, because it complicates any account of misuse and disuse that treats them purely as failures of individual judgment. A worker who defers too readily to a system they were never trained to question is exhibiting misuse, in the narrow behavioral sense, but the deeper cause may sit with whoever deployed that system without building in the training, feedback, or time that appropriate skepticism requires. A worker who ignores a system's warnings because the pace of the job leaves no room to act on them is exhibiting disuse, narrowly defined, but the deeper cause may again sit upstream, with whoever set that pace. Abuse will return later in this book, especially when the subject turns from cockpits and reading rooms to warehouses, where the question of who is actually accountable for a joint human-machine failure gets considerably harder to answer, and where the line between an operator's own misjudgment and the conditions that operator was placed under grows genuinely difficult to draw.

But it is misuse and disuse, the two operator-level failure modes, that give this book its title and its shape, because they are the two ways an individual person, doing their honest best in a single moment, gets the trust decision wrong.

It would be tidy to describe these as opposite ends of a single dial — too much trust on one side, too little on the other, with some correct amount of trust sitting safely in the middle, waiting to be found and held. That picture is comforting and it is also wrong, or at least far too simple to survive contact with the actual cases this book examines. The correct amount of trust to place in a given system is not a fixed point that a person can locate once and then keep. It changes with the system, the situation, the operator's own state, and the specific way the automation is likely to fail on this particular occasion rather than the last one. A pilot who is right to trust an autopilot holding altitude in cruise is not automatically right to trust the same autopilot's behavior during an unusual and rapidly evolving failure. A worker who is right to follow a warehouse scanner's routing instruction nine hundred times is not thereby proven right to follow it the nine hundred and first time, if that is the time the instruction happens to be dangerous. There is no dial to set once. There is only a continuous, situation-specific judgment call, made over and over, and misuse and disuse are simply the two directions that call can miss.

The dial metaphor fails for a second reason too, one that matters more as this book goes on: it implies that trust is something a person consciously turns, deliberately raising or lowering their confidence in a system the way a driver adjusts a thermostat. Most of the misuse and disuse this book describes did not arrive through a conscious decision to trust more or less. It arrived through habituation, through the slow accumulation of ordinary experience, through a hundred small moments in which nothing forced the operator to stop and ask whether their current level of confidence still matched the system in front of them. A dial invites the question "where should I set this." The actual phenomenon rarely offers that question the chance to be asked at all.

Nor is it accurate to treat one of the two as the safe default and the other as the dangerous extreme — to conclude, reasonably enough on first instinct, that since misuse means trusting a possibly-wrong machine, the responsible position is always to lean toward disuse, toward skepticism, toward keeping the human in charge. That instinct has real appeal, especially after the litany of automation failures this book will describe. But disuse kills people too, just as reliably as misuse does, and often in less visible ways because an abandoned tool does not generate its own headline the way a trusted one that fails spectacularly does. A useful system ignored, a correct warning dismissed, a capability left unused because the last time it was tried it happened to be wrong: these failures accumulate quietly, one declined recommendation at a time, and they do not announce themselves the way a single catastrophic overtrust does. Both failure modes are real. Both are common. And a book that spent its attention on only one of them would be telling half the story and calling it the whole of it.

This book will spend real time on both, in roughly the order the two failure modes tend to become visible across the domains it visits. The aviation chapters ahead in this section will eventually turn to Air France Flight 447, a case this book will treat with the seriousness it demands rather than summarize here — a crew whose confidence in what their automated systems were doing outran the reality of what those systems were actually telling them, with consequences that could not be recovered from once the confusion set in. That case, when it comes, will be difficult reading, and it deserves to be. Later chapters that turn to labor and to the everyday tools people use and discard will trace disuse instead — people walking away from systems that would have served them well, for reasons that make sense in the moment and look costlier only in retrospect.

It would be a mistake to read those two clusters as proof that misuse belongs to pilots and disuse belongs to everyone else, or that any one profession or setting is reliably prone to only one of the two failures. Real institutions do not settle into a stable equilibrium of appropriately calibrated trust and stay there. They cycle. An organization badly burned by an episode of misuse — a system trusted too far, a failure that should have been caught and was not — tends to overcorrect, tightening procedures, adding mandatory human checks, treating the automation with newly imposed suspicion. That overcorrection, if it goes far enough, produces its own disuse: a workforce trained by the previous failure to distrust a tool that, in its current and perhaps improved form, might deserve more confidence than it is receiving. Wait long enough, and the pattern can run the other way — an institution that suffered through a costly period of disuse, of ignored recommendations and abandoned tools, gradually builds a culture that leans the opposite direction, until a new generation of operators trusts the current systems more than the current systems have earned. Institutions do not find the correct amount of trust and hold it. They swing past it in both directions, at different times, often in response to whichever failure happened most recently and made the deepest impression.

This is not a design flaw unique to any one industry or any one kind of automation. It follows from something simpler: trust in a system is not a fact about the system alone. It is a relationship between a system's actual, situation-dependent reliability and a person's belief about that reliability, and beliefs are shaped disproportionately by whatever happened most recently and most vividly, rather than by the complete and balanced record a careful audit might reveal. A single dramatic failure can undo years of correct performance in an operator's estimation. A long stretch of correct performance can quietly erode the vigilance that would catch the next failure. Neither of those tendencies is irrational, exactly — they are close to how human learning from experience generally works — but neither one reliably tracks the actual, underlying trustworthiness of the system being judged.

Consider, in the abstract, an organization that operates some automated recommendation system across many workers or many decisions — a hospital department, an airline's fleet, a logistics operation, the specific institution does not matter yet. Suppose that system performs well for a long stretch, and the people using it gradually stop treating its output as a proposal to be checked and start treating it as a settled answer. That is misuse taking hold, quietly, without any single decision to trust the system more; it accumulates through ordinary repetition, the same way any habit does. Now suppose the system fails once, visibly, in a way that causes real harm. The organization's response, entirely understandably, is to pull back: new procedures requiring independent human verification, new training emphasizing the system's limitations, a cultural shift toward treating its recommendations with open suspicion. That correction may well be appropriate in the immediate aftermath. But if it persists past the point where it is warranted — if workers five years later are still discounting a system that has, in the meantime, been refined and improved — the organization has not solved its trust problem. It has simply traded one form of miscalibration for the other, and it may not notice the trade has happened, because disuse rarely produces its own headline the way a single dramatic overtrust failure does.

Nothing about that pattern requires bad faith, incompetence, or even a particularly unusual institution. It requires only the ordinary human tendency to weight recent, vivid experience over a complete record — the same tendency, incidentally, that Sullenberger and Skiles did not have the luxury of falling prey to over the Hudson, because they had no track record with the specific failure in front of them at all, no history of prior trust or distrust to lean on, only the bare mechanics of the problem itself. Their decision was, in that narrow sense, uncontaminated by the very bias this chapter has just described. Most of the decisions this book examines are not so lucky. They are made by people who bring a history with the system into the moment, and that history shapes the decision as much as, sometimes more than, the actual situation in front of them.

None of this means the two failure modes are equally dangerous in every setting, or that a writer or a reader should treat them as perfectly symmetrical twins deserving identical attention regardless of context. Some domains punish misuse far more severely than disuse, because the automated system, when it fails, fails in ways no human backup can catch in time. Other domains punish disuse more severely, because the automated system is, on the whole and across the overwhelming majority of cases, considerably more reliable than the unaided human alternative, and walking away from it routinely costs real accuracy or safety that a wary operator never sees because the cost of their wariness is invisible to them. Later chapters will make exactly these domain-specific arguments, in detail, with the actual evidence attached to each case rather than an abstract claim asserted in advance. What stays constant across all of them is the underlying shape of the mistake: a person's trust in a system diverging from what that system, in that moment, actually deserved.

There is also an asymmetry in how visible the two failures are after the fact, and it is worth naming because it will quietly bias every chapter that follows, this one included. Misuse tends to produce a specific, investigable event: a wrong decision was made, a machine's bad output was accepted, and there is usually a moment that can be pointed to, a recommendation that was followed when it should not have been. Investigators can reconstruct that moment, as they will in several of the cases ahead. Disuse rarely offers the same clean object of study. When a useful system is ignored, the cost is a decision that was made worse than it needed to be, not a decision that can be traced back to a single instant of failure — and often nobody realizes the cost was paid at all, because the counterfactual, the better outcome the ignored system would have produced, never happened and so never left a record. This book will describe documented cases of disuse where the evidence is strong enough to support the claim plainly. But the reader should hold, alongside every misuse story that follows, the possibility that disuse is quietly happening in far more places than get written up, precisely because it leaves the fainter trace.

Parasuraman and Riley wrote their paper about automation in general — about the cockpits, control rooms, and early digital tools of the mid-1990s, well before anything resembling the recommendation engines that structure daily life today existed to study. Their vocabulary was built for a narrower moment than the one this book is set in. It has not needed to be replaced. The distinction between misuse and disuse describes, with almost no modification, the choice a radiologist faces when an algorithm flags a mammogram, the choice a judge faces when a risk score arrives with a sentencing file, the choice a driver faces when a car's systems propose to take over part of a maneuver. In every one of those settings, the person involved can go wrong by trusting too much or by trusting too little, and the paper that first separated those two ways of being wrong, cleanly enough that researchers could begin studying each one on its own terms, is nearly three decades old and has not gone out of date.

What it does not yet explain — what it was never trying to explain — is why either failure happens at all: why a competent, attentive person would extend a machine more confidence than the evidence supports, or less. Parasuraman and Riley's paper is a taxonomy, not a mechanism. It tells a reader where to look for the two kinds of wrong and gives each one a name precise enough to be studied, but it does not, on its own, say what it is about the situation of working alongside automation that pushes ordinary, competent people toward one error or the other in the first place. A taxonomy without a mechanism is a filing system. It is useful — genuinely useful, and this book will keep returning to its two central terms because nothing since has replaced them — but it leaves the harder question sitting untouched underneath it.

Naming the two kinds of wrong is the beginning of that explanation, not the end of it. The next step is to ask what it is about automation itself, and about the humans asked to work alongside it, that makes both kinds of error not just possible but, under certain conditions, close to structurally inevitable — built into the relationship between a person and a machine that does most of a job most of the time, rather than an occasional lapse either one of them could simply try harder to avoid. That question leads directly to a short, unglamorous paper about factory control rooms, written six years before Parasuraman and Riley's, that turns out to be the argument underneath almost everything else in this book.
