# The Miss

Not every chapter here can end the way the last one did. The three cases just examined were chosen because each one worked, and because each one illustrated something true about how prepared, well-calibrated judgment intervenes on an automated system at exactly the moment it needs to. This chapter is about two cases where a comparable kind of judgment was attempted and did not work, and where the people trying to exercise it died along with almost everyone around them. It is the hardest material in this book, and it deserves to be handled with more restraint than drama — plain description of what investigators found, not speculation about what cannot be known.

The previous chapter closed by noting how narrow the margin was in each of its three cases, and how easily each could have gone the other way. This chapter is, in part, an answer to that observation. Judgment does not always arrive in time. Preparation is not always adequate to the moment it is finally tested against. And an automated system's design, built years before anyone had to rely on it under real pressure, can foreclose the very possibility of a successful human intervention no matter how capable the people in the cockpit turn out to be. What follows is not a story about pilot error, and the investigation findings do not support treating it as one.

On October 29, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 took off from Jakarta's Soekarno-Hatta International Airport and crashed into the Java Sea a short time later. All 189 people aboard were killed. Indonesia's National Transportation Safety Committee, the country's civil aviation accident investigation authority, spent months examining the wreckage, the flight data recorder, and the aircraft's systems before publishing its findings. What the investigators identified was a piece of flight-control software, part of the Boeing 737 MAX's design, called the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System, or MCAS.

MCAS was designed to adjust the aircraft's pitch trim automatically, without a direct command from the pilots, under certain flight conditions. Its logic depended on data from the aircraft's angle-of-attack sensors, which measure the angle between the wing and the oncoming air. On the accident flight, one of those two sensors was providing erroneous readings. Investigators found that MCAS, relying on the faulty data from that single sensor, activated repeatedly during the flight, each time pushing the aircraft's nose down. The crew responded, repeatedly, by pulling the nose back up. MCAS activated again. This pattern repeated until the system's uncommanded, repeated nose-down inputs progressively overwhelmed the flight crew's ability to keep the aircraft under manual control, and the aircraft crashed into the sea.

The word that matters most in that sequence is "repeatedly." A single erroneous automated input, however dangerous in the moment, is the kind of event a trained crew has some chance of recognizing, correcting, and moving past. What Indonesia's investigators documented was not a single bad command but a cycle: the faulty sensor kept reporting the same erroneous angle-of-attack reading, so the condition that triggered MCAS kept being satisfied, so the system kept reasserting the same nose-down input every time the crew countered it. Each individual correction the pilots made was, in isolation, the right response to the immediate problem in front of them. What defeated the sequence of corrections was that the underlying cause generating the false trigger never went away, and nothing in the crew's available response broke that cycle before the aircraft ran out of altitude.

The crash of Lion Air 610 drew the kind of scrutiny that follows any fatal accident involving a nearly new aircraft type flown by a major commercial carrier. The picture that emerged over the following months, as Indonesia's investigators worked through the flight data and the aircraft's systems, centered on the interaction between a single faulty sensor and a piece of software most of the flying public, and a significant number of the pilots then flying the 737 MAX, had never heard of.

Less than five months later, on March 10, 2019, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed near the town of Ejere, Ethiopia, shortly after taking off from Addis Ababa. All 157 people aboard were killed. Ethiopia's Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau led the inquiry, and its findings described a failure pattern closely resembling what Indonesian investigators had already documented in the Lion Air crash five months earlier. Shortly after liftoff, the aircraft's left angle-of-attack sensor began reporting readings sharply divergent from the right sensor — reaching roughly 74.5 degrees on the left side against 15.3 degrees on the right, a gap far too large to reflect the aircraft's actual attitude. That erroneous data again triggered repeated MCAS activation, consistent with the failure mode already identified in the Lion Air investigation.

Two aircraft of the same type, operated by two different airlines on two different continents, had gone down within five months of each other, killing 346 people between them, after a piece of software neither crew could disable in time repeatedly pushed the nose down based on bad data from a single sensor. The scale of that fact is worth sitting with plainly, without embellishment: 189 people on the first flight, 157 on the second, 346 in total, none of whom had any way to know, boarding those aircraft, that a failure mode already present in one of them would recur in the other before regulators grounded the type. They were passengers and crew on ordinary commercial flights, traveling for the ordinary reasons people travel, on an aircraft type that had entered service only a few years earlier and had no comparable history of this failure before the first of these two accidents occurred.

It would be a mistake to read these two accidents as a simple story about pilots over-trusting an automated recommendation — the pattern of misuse traced through earlier chapters, where a person defers to a system's judgment past the point that judgment deserves. That description does not fit what the flight data and cockpit voice recorders show investigators found happening in either cockpit. The crews were not passively accepting MCAS's inputs. They were actively fighting them, repeatedly countering the nose-down trim with nose-up inputs of their own, for as long as the aircraft's systems allowed them to. What defeated them was not a failure to resist the automation. It was an automated system that kept reasserting uncommanded control faster, and more persistently, than manual countermeasures could permanently overcome — a scenario neither crew's training, nor MCAS's original design, had adequately prepared them to diagnose and resolve within the time available.

This is worth stating precisely, because it complicates the misuse-and-disuse framework introduced earlier in this book rather than simply confirming it. Misuse, as originally defined, describes an operator granting a system more trust than its actual reliability warrants — a monitoring failure, a decision bias, an unwillingness to question a recommendation the system should not have been trusted to make. What happened aboard Lion Air 610 and Ethiopian 302 was not exactly that. The crews were questioning the automation's behavior from very early in each event; the flight data shows them repeatedly overriding it. The problem was not that they trusted MCAS too much. It was that the aircraft they were flying gave a single faulty sensor enough authority to repeatedly reassert an uncommanded, incorrect input, and gave the crews neither the specific knowledge of what was fighting them nor enough time to definitively win that fight before the aircraft ran out of altitude.

If neither crew was simply guilty of misuse, and neither was guilty of disuse either, the taxonomy introduced early in this account still has a category left to reach for: abuse, the least discussed of Parasuraman and Riley's four terms, describing not an operator's failure but a designer's or an organization's decision to deploy automation in a way that disregards its consequences for the humans who will eventually have to work alongside it. That category was named early on and set aside, deliberately, as not the main subject — the central interest so far has been in how individual people calibrate trust, not in how the systems they trust get built. This chapter is the place where that deferred category returns, because the mechanism that put MCAS on the accident aircraft, with the authority it had and the disclosure it lacked, was a set of design and rollout decisions made well before either flight, by people who were never in either cockpit.

That absence of specific knowledge connects directly to an argument made in an earlier chapter, drawn from Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper on the ironies of automation. Bainbridge's second irony holds that an automated system built on the assumption that human intervention will rarely be needed tends to leave the humans responsible for exactly the cases automation cannot handle — which, by the nature of that assumption, are the rarest and least familiar cases an operator will ever face. MCAS was built on precisely that kind of assumption: a system meant to activate quietly, correct a specific flight characteristic, and require no pilot awareness of its existence under normal operation. The difficulty was that many pilots transitioning to the 737 MAX were not made aware, in their training materials, that a system like MCAS existed at all, or how it would behave if it activated on faulty sensor data. When the rare case Bainbridge's argument predicts did arrive — a system this quiet malfunctioning in exactly the way it was never supposed to — the pilots facing it were, in the most literal sense, the least prepared people available to diagnose what was overriding their inputs and why. They were fighting a system whose existence and behavior had not been adequately explained to them, in the middle of a climb, with the ground closing and very little time to work out on their own what was happening and why their inputs kept being reversed.

There is an earlier echo of this same argument in the account of Air France 447 several chapters back, the 2009 accident in which an autopilot disconnected without warning after a pitot tube iced over, handing control back to a startled crew unable to reconcile the automation-era displays in front of them with the raw information a pre-automation pilot would have relied on. The mechanism there ran in something close to the opposite direction from MCAS's failure: AF447's automation quietly stepped away and left the crew with too little guidance, while MCAS actively stepped in and gave the crews too much unwanted intervention to overcome. But the underlying diagnosis, first made by Warren VanderBurgh in 1997 and confirmed by the French investigation into AF447 more than a decade later, was the same one this chapter arrives at again: a crew's ability to respond correctly to an automated system's failure depends on understanding, specifically and in advance, how that system behaves and what its silence or its intervention actually means. The industry had already diagnosed this exact vulnerability, in general form, years before it contributed to two more fatal accidents in a single aircraft type. That a well-documented failure mode can recur, in a new guise, inside an industry that had already named the underlying mechanism, is one of the more uncomfortable facts this account has to sit with.

It is important to be exact about what the investigation findings do and do not establish, and to resist filling the gaps with imagined detail. Indonesia's National Transportation Safety Committee and Ethiopia's Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau documented what the flight data recorders captured: sensor readings, control inputs, trim positions, altitude, and airspeed over time. Those records establish that the crews responded to MCAS's uncommanded nose-down inputs, repeatedly, with nose-up inputs of their own, and that the pattern recurred until the aircraft could no longer be recovered. What is not part of the documented record, and what will not be speculated about here, is what either crew understood, moment to moment, about what was fighting them, or what they were thinking or feeling in the aircraft's final seconds. The investigation reports describe a mechanical and procedural failure with terrible precision. They do not, and could not, give an account of two crews' interior experience, and neither should this account pretend to.

What can be said with confidence is that both accident investigations converged on the same underlying mechanism, in two different countries, working from two different aircraft's data, within the same year — a degree of independent confirmation that leaves little room for the failure to be explained away as one operator's unusual circumstance. A single faulty sensor, feeding a system with the authority to repeatedly move a critical flight control surface without being commanded to, and pilots without adequate awareness of that system's existence or behavior, produced the same outcome twice in under five months. That repetition is itself part of what makes this chapter's material different from the near-misses in the previous one. Sullenberger, de Crespigny, and Garman each faced a genuinely novel emergency, one nobody had specifically trained them to expect in that exact form. The second of these two crashes was not novel in the same sense. By the time Ethiopian 302 took off, the aviation industry already knew, in general terms, that something in the 737 MAX's flight-control design had contributed to a fatal accident five months earlier, even though the full technical understanding of MCAS's behavior, and the training and disclosure gaps around it, was still being worked out.

An earlier chapter introduced Madeleine Clare Elish's concept of the moral crumple zone: the pattern by which accountability for a joint human-automation failure tends to concentrate on the human operator with the least actual control over what went wrong, protecting the system rather than the person. That concept is worth holding up against these two investigations, because it clarifies something about what the record does and does not do. Both the Indonesian and Ethiopian investigation bodies, working independently, located the causal chain in the automated system's design and its reliance on a single sensor, not primarily in the performance of the crews fighting it. That is not a small thing. It would have been possible, in each case, to write an investigation report that concentrated on what the pilots failed to do differently, in the same way public attention sometimes concentrates on the safety driver at the wheel of a self-driving test vehicle rather than on the software that put her in an impossible position. These investigations resisted that framing and named the mechanism instead. The crumple zone Elish describes is a pattern in how blame gets distributed after a joint failure, not a law of nature, and these two reports are evidence that a rigorous, independent investigation can locate responsibility more accurately than the crumple-zone pattern would predict.

The response that followed both accidents is part of this record too, and it belongs in this chapter as more than an afterthought. Within days of the Ethiopian Airlines crash, aviation authorities around the world grounded the entire 737 MAX fleet, an extraordinary step that kept hundreds of aircraft out of service for an extended period while the underlying cause was addressed. Grounding an entire aircraft type worldwide is not a decision regulators reach for lightly; it removes a substantial portion of an airline's available fleet from service all at once, at real operational and financial cost, and it is the kind of step usually reserved for exactly this circumstance — a documented, repeatable failure mode with no interim fix available that regulators are willing to certify as adequate.

Boeing redesigned MCAS during that grounding, in ways that specifically targeted the failure mode both investigations had identified: the redesigned system draws on both angle-of-attack sensors rather than relying on a single one, comparing their readings against each other rather than trusting either sensor's output in isolation, and the aircraft's crew procedures and pilot training were revised as well, to give pilots the specific knowledge about the system's existence and behavior that the original rollout had not adequately provided. This is worth naming precisely because it maps directly onto the failure mechanism described earlier in this chapter: the redesign answers a single point of sensor failure with outsized authority over the aircraft's control surfaces by requiring agreement between two independent sensors before the system can act, closing off exactly the single-point vulnerability the original design allowed. The National Transportation Safety Board, the same American agency whose reconstruction validated Sullenberger's decision on the Hudson, weighed in again on the Ethiopian accident as recently as January 2023, publishing additional comments on Ethiopia's final report — a sign that the technical and regulatory reckoning with these two accidents continued for years after the immediate crisis, not a matter closed and filed away the moment the grounding order was lifted.

This regulatory correction matters, and it would be dishonest to leave it out of the account, because it is evidence that the institutions responsible for aviation safety did respond to what the investigations found — not with a shrug, and not only with litigation and public relations, but with an actual redesign of the failure mode itself, built on the specific mechanism both national investigation bodies had independently documented. The corrected system's reliance on both sensors, rather than one, is a direct engineering answer to the single point of failure that doomed both flights. The revised training requirements are a direct answer to the disclosure gap Bainbridge's argument predicts will matter most exactly when it is least expected to. None of this is a small thing, and a serious account of these two accidents has to include it rather than end the story at the moment of impact.

Consider what the training gap actually meant in practice, as far as the investigation record establishes it. A pilot transitioning to the 737 MAX from an earlier version of the same aircraft family was, in general, being asked to learn a new variant of a type they may have already flown for years — the kind of transition training built around the premise that the new variant behaves, for the most part, like the old one, with a defined and disclosed list of differences. Whether or when a given pilot's specific training program described MCAS and its behavior in enough detail to prepare that pilot for encountering it on faulty sensor data is a question the investigation findings and the broader regulatory response addressed by requiring exactly the kind of explicit disclosure and training update that followed. The redesign's requirement that pilots be trained on MCAS's existence and behavior is itself an acknowledgment that the original transition training had not adequately covered a system capable of taking this much authority over the aircraft's pitch. An automated system a pilot does not know exists cannot be diagnosed by that pilot in real time, no matter how experienced the pilot is with every other part of the aircraft.

It would be equally dishonest, though, to let that correction stand as a form of resolution. The redesign came after both accidents, not before either of them, and no engineering fix delivered afterward restores what was lost in either crash. The 346 people aboard those two flights — 189 on Lion Air 610, 157 on Ethiopian 302 — did not benefit from a system that, by the time of the second accident, aviation authorities and Boeing already had real reason to suspect was capable of producing exactly the failure that killed them again. That gap between the first accident and the grounding of the fleet after the second is itself part of the record, and it sits uneasily alongside the eventual correction, without canceling it out. Both things are true at once: the institutions eventually got the engineering and the training right, and getting it right took a second catastrophic loss of life to force the correction all the way through.

Almost exactly five months separate the two crashes, and that interval is worth pausing on rather than passing over. The Lion Air accident was, by the time Ethiopian 302 took off, already a matter of public record and ongoing investigation. Whatever scrutiny that investigation had generated in the intervening months, it did not translate into a fleet-wide grounding or a training change reaching every 737 MAX pilot in time to prevent the same underlying mechanism from producing a second catastrophic loss, on a different continent, in a different airline's aircraft. Judging exactly how much more could reasonably have been done in that window, with the information available at the time, is a harder question than it looks in hindsight, and no attempt to adjudicate it is made here. What can be said plainly is that the interval between the two accidents measures how long it took the full picture to come into focus, and that the full picture did not come into focus in time for the second flight.

This is, in the end, the chapter this account could not skip, however much easier it would have been to end Part III on the note the previous chapter allowed. The earlier cases examined so far — the algorithm aversion experiments, the radiologists calibrating trust against an AI's confident flag, the judge reading an undisclosed risk score, the warehouse worker racing a scanner's quota — are mostly stories about degrees of harm, serious and sometimes life-altering, but recoverable in a way that a fatal aviation accident is not. Lion Air 610 and Ethiopian 302 sit at the far end of what this whole argument has been building toward: that the gap between a well-calibrated human response to an automated system and a miscalibrated one is not an abstract psychological curiosity. In the specific case of these two flights, closing that gap was a matter of whether 346 people lived or died, and the gap was not closed in time for either of them.

There is no tidy way to hold the two halves of this chapter together — the catastrophic failure and the correction that followed it — without one seeming to soften the other, and it would be a disservice to both halves to try. The correction does not redeem the failure. Nothing engineered after March 2019 changes what happened to the people aboard either flight, and no amount of subsequent regulatory diligence converts 346 deaths into an acceptable cost of learning what a single-sensor system could do under the wrong conditions. At the same time, the correction is not nothing, and treating it as nothing would misstate the record as badly as treating it as sufficient would. Aviation safety, across the history traced in these chapters, has generally improved through exactly this uncomfortable mechanism: a failure, an investigation that assigns cause with real precision, and a redesign that closes the specific gap the investigation identified. That mechanism worked here, eventually, at a cost no engineering fix can retroactively lower. Both of those sentences are true, and the honest response to this chapter is to hold them together rather than resolve them into a single, more comfortable verdict.
