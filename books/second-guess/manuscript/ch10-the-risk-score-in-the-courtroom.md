# The Risk Score in the Courtroom

A sentencing hearing is one of the few places in ordinary life where a stranger is asked to predict your future and then act on the prediction immediately. A judge weighs the offense, the record, the arguments made by both sides, and, increasingly by the middle of the 2010s, a number generated by software: a risk score, produced by an algorithm trained on historical data, meant to estimate the likelihood that this particular defendant will be arrested again within some future window. The number arrives on a printed sheet or a screen, usually as a simple category — low, medium, high — sometimes with a numeric scale behind it. What the judge does not receive, in the case of the most widely used such tool of that era, is any account of how the number was calculated. The company that built it treats the scoring method as a trade secret, and the courtroom is expected to take the output on faith.

This chapter is about what happens to the calibration problem — the gap Chapter 6 named between how much a person trusts a system and how much that system has actually earned that trust — when the system in question is legally shielded from the kind of inspection calibration ordinarily depends on. Aviation automation can be studied by the pilots who fly with it every day. A radiology AI's errors can, in principle, be reviewed case by case by the radiologists working alongside it. A risk-assessment tool sold to a court system can be treated, by the company that built it, as proprietary intellectual property — a product to be protected from competitors, not a public instrument to be understood by the people whose liberty it helps determine. That distinction is where this chapter begins.

The tool at the center of this chapter is COMPAS — Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions — developed by a company called Northpointe, later renamed Equivant. By the time it drew wide public scrutiny, COMPAS scores were in routine use across parts of the American criminal justice system, feeding into decisions about bail, sentencing, and supervision. The core promise was straightforward: replace, or at least supplement, a judge's unaided intuition about a defendant's risk of reoffending with a statistically derived estimate, built from patterns in a large historical dataset rather than from one person's gut sense in one afternoon. The core problem, less advertised, was that no one outside the company could see what those patterns actually were, or how the algorithm weighted them.

On May 23, 2016, the investigative newsroom ProPublica published an analysis, by reporters Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu, and Lauren Kirchner, under the title "Machine Bias." The team obtained COMPAS risk scores for defendants in Broward County, Florida, and matched them against what actually happened to those defendants afterward — whether they were, in fact, arrested again within the follow-up period the scores were meant to predict. The headline finding was stark. Among defendants who were not re-arrested — people the system's own logic should have wanted to score as lower risk — 45 percent of Black defendants had nonetheless been misclassified by COMPAS as higher risk, compared with 24 percent of white defendants who were not re-arrested and were misclassified the same way. The asymmetry ran in the opposite direction for the mirror-image error: among defendants who did go on to reoffend, white defendants were more likely than Black defendants to have been mislabeled "low risk" beforehand — a system, in other words, that erred toward flagging Black defendants as dangerous when they were not, and toward clearing white defendants as safe when they were not.

It is worth sitting for a moment with what a misclassification like this actually costs a person, because the phrase "45 percent misclassified as higher risk" can read, on the page, as an abstraction. A defendant scored higher risk is a defendant more likely to be held rather than released before trial, more likely to face a harsher argument from the prosecution at sentencing, more likely to be viewed by the judge as someone requiring closer supervision after release — consequences that accumulate over the course of a case regardless of whether the underlying number was, for that particular person, correct. A defendant who is never rearrested has, by the plainest possible measure, demonstrated that the "higher risk" label did not describe them accurately. That the label was nonetheless attached to them nearly twice as often if they were Black as if they were white, according to ProPublica's analysis of the Broward County data, is the specific, checkable claim the reporting made — not a vague accusation of algorithmic racism, but a documented pattern in a dataset anyone could, in principle, re-examine.

The reporting landed with force in part because it converted an abstract worry about algorithmic fairness into concrete, checkable numbers about real people's sentences and bail conditions. It also landed at a moment when the criminal justice system's use of proprietary risk tools was already drawing scrutiny for reasons independent of race — the trade-secret opacity itself, the question of what due process means when the state relies on a number nobody outside a private company can fully explain. ProPublica's analysis gave that broader unease a specific, quantified grievance to organize around.

Northpointe did not stay silent. The company published its own response, using the same underlying Broward County data, and arrived at a different conclusion: that COMPAS achieved what it called "accuracy equity" across racial groups, meaning the tool was, in a specific statistical sense, equally accurate for Black and white defendants. This is not simply corporate damage control dressed up in the language of statistics, and treating it that way would miss the actual, uncomfortable substance of the disagreement. Northpointe's defense rested on a real and recognized fairness criterion — calibration, sometimes called predictive parity — under which a risk tool is fair if a given score means the same thing regardless of race: if a defendant of any race scored as "high risk" reoffended at roughly the same rate as any other defendant scored "high risk," and likewise for "medium" and "low," then the score itself is not biased by race even if the population of people who receive each score differs by race. Northpointe's analysis found COMPAS satisfied something close to this standard.

It would be convenient, for the purposes of a clean story, if Northpointe's rebuttal could be dismissed as a company protecting its product with selectively chosen statistics. Some of that motive was almost certainly present; no company facing a damaging investigative report responds with pure scientific detachment. But convenience is not the same as accuracy, and the calibration standard Northpointe invoked is a real one, used seriously by statisticians well outside any dispute over COMPAS specifically, precisely because a score that means substantially different things for different groups — a "high risk" label that turns out to predict a meaningfully different real-world rearrest rate depending on a defendant's race — would itself be a serious and separate kind of unfairness, one a company could just as easily be criticized for if its tool exhibited it.

Both sides, in other words, could point to real numbers derived from the same dataset and claim their fairness criterion was met. This is the part of the dispute worth sitting with carefully, because it is not a case of one side lying and the other telling the truth. Predictive parity — the standard Northpointe emphasized — asks whether a score of a given level predicts the same real-world outcome regardless of race. Equalized error rates — the standard ProPublica's reporting emphasized — asks whether the tool makes the same kinds of mistakes, in the same proportions, across racial groups, independent of whether the overall scores are calibrated. These are both coherent, defensible ways to define a fair risk-assessment tool. The trouble, well understood by statisticians who have studied this exact dispute, is that when the underlying base rate of the outcome being predicted differs between two groups — and in Broward County, as in many jurisdictions, the observed subsequent-arrest rate differed by race, itself a fact entangled with decades of unequal policing, prosecution, and everything upstream of an arrest record — a tool generally cannot satisfy both standards simultaneously. Predictive parity and equalized false-positive and false-negative rates are not two paths to the same destination. They are two different destinations, and a single algorithm, applied to a population with unequal base rates, has to choose which one it will fail to reach.

It helps to see, in simple terms, why the two standards can pull apart even when nobody involved is acting in bad faith. Imagine two groups of defendants with different underlying rates of subsequent arrest — not because one group is inherently more or less law-abiding, but because of any of the many upstream forces, from policing patterns to prosecutorial charging decisions to economic circumstance, that can produce unequal arrest rates between populations for reasons that have nothing to do with the individual defendant in front of the judge. If a risk tool is calibrated so that a "high risk" score means the same real-world probability of rearrest in both groups — Northpointe's standard — then, whenever the two groups' underlying base rates differ, the tool will necessarily sort a larger share of the higher-base-rate group into "high risk," and a larger share of the lower-base-rate group into "low risk," even if the tool is working exactly as calibrated. That sorting difference then shows up, mechanically, as an asymmetry in false positive and false negative rates between the groups — ProPublica's standard — because a group with more people sorted into "high risk" will also contain more people who receive that label incorrectly, in raw numbers and often in proportion. Neither Northpointe nor ProPublica needed to manipulate anything for this to happen. It is closer to an arithmetic property of applying one calibrated instrument to two populations with different underlying rates than it is to a hidden bias planted by either side of the debate — which is precisely what makes the disagreement so difficult to resolve by producing more data, rather than by first agreeing on which fairness standard matters more.

This is why a serious accounting of the COMPAS dispute cannot simply declare a winner. ProPublica was right that COMPAS produced a large, racially patterned asymmetry in its errors — a Black defendant who would not go on to reoffend was nearly twice as likely as a similarly situated white defendant to be wrongly flagged as high risk. Northpointe was right that, judged by a different and equally legitimate statistical standard, the tool's scores meant approximately the same thing across racial groups. Neither side fabricated its numbers. Neither side was applying an incoherent definition of fairness. They were applying different, both mathematically sound, definitions to the same disputed terrain, and arriving, as the underlying mathematics essentially guarantees they would, at different verdicts. A reader looking for this book to resolve that disagreement and tell them which side was correct will not find that resolution here, because the honest answer is that the disagreement is not the kind that resolves through better data or a more careful reading of the same dataset. It resolves, if it resolves at all, through a prior political and moral choice about which kind of fairness a society wants its risk tools to guarantee — a choice statistics alone cannot make.

Set the fairness-definition dispute alongside a second, related legal fight that reached the Wisconsin Supreme Court around the same period. Eric Loomis had pleaded guilty to fleeing an officer and operating a vehicle without the owner's consent, in a case connected to a drive-by shooting investigation. At his sentencing, the trial court had before it a COMPAS risk assessment. Loomis challenged the court's reliance on that score on due-process grounds. Part of his argument turned directly on the opacity this chapter has already described: because Northpointe treats its scoring methodology as a trade secret, neither Loomis nor, in any complete sense, the court itself could examine how the specific number in front of them had been generated, what factors had been weighted, or how much confidence the method actually warranted for a case like his.

Loomis's own case is a useful reminder that these disputes rarely arrive in the abstract; they arrive attached to a specific person with a specific record, in a specific courtroom, where a specific number is about to help decide a specific sentence. He was not challenging his underlying guilt. He had pleaded guilty to the charges against him. What he was challenging was narrower and, in its way, more fundamental: whether the state could rely, at the moment of sentencing, on a number produced by a process neither he nor his lawyers nor the judge could examine, and whether that reliance was consistent with the due-process protections a defendant is owed. It is a claim that does not depend on believing COMPAS is inaccurate. It depends only on the more basic proposition that an instrument used to help decide how long someone spends deprived of their liberty ought to be open to scrutiny by the person it is being used against — a proposition that predates algorithmic risk assessment by centuries and that COMPAS, protected as a trade secret, sits awkwardly beside.

The case became State v. Loomis, decided by the Wisconsin Supreme Court in 2016. The court did not bar the use of tools like COMPAS at sentencing. It upheld their use. But it did not uphold it without qualification. The court held that a COMPAS score could not be the determinative factor in a sentencing decision, and it required that courts using such a tool provide defendants with cautionary information about the tool's limitations — an acknowledgment, built directly into the legal standard, that a risk score of undisclosed provenance deserves to be weighted with real skepticism rather than treated as a neutral, science-backed verdict handed down from outside the ordinary uncertainty of judicial judgment.

Read against the trust-calibration framework this book introduced in Chapter 6, the Loomis decision looks less like a resolution and more like the legal system's own recognition of an unsolved calibration problem, arrived at from the inside. Lee and See's central argument was that trust calibration — the match between how much a person trusts a system and how much that system actually deserves to be trusted — depends on feedback and understanding: an operator learns to calibrate trust in a system by seeing, over time, where and how it succeeds and fails, and by holding some working model of why. A judge handed a COMPAS score has almost none of the raw material calibration requires. They cannot see the weights the model assigned to a given defendant's history. They cannot see the training data the model learned its patterns from, or whether that data reflected the same racial disparities in policing and prosecution that later showed up as the error asymmetry ProPublica documented. They receive a category — low, medium, high — and are asked to decide how much weight to give it, without the tools that would let them tell whether this particular score, for this particular defendant, deserves confidence or doubt. The Wisconsin Supreme Court's cautionary-instruction requirement is best understood as an attempt to inject at least a partial substitute for that missing feedback loop: not a way of actually calibrating trust in the tool, since no instruction read aloud in a courtroom can substitute for the kind of accumulated, verifiable track record calibration in Lee and See's sense would require, but an acknowledgment that without such an instruction, judges were being asked to rely on a black box with no warning label at all.

This is overtrust and undertrust with none of the ordinary machinery available to correct either. In the domains earlier chapters have examined, calibration has at least a chance to develop through repeated exposure: a pilot flies with the same automation across thousands of hours and gradually learns its edges; a radiologist reads enough AI-flagged films to start noticing where the tool tends to be strong and where it tends to be shaky. A judge encounters a defendant's COMPAS score once, for one hearing, and moves on to a different defendant with a different score generated by the same undisclosed process. There is no accumulated personal track record to learn from, because each case is a single data point drawn from a population the judge cannot see, scored by a method the judge is not permitted to inspect. If a judge trusts the number too much, deferring to it as though it were a neutral scientific instrument, that is a straightforward case of the misuse Parasuraman and Riley described — automation bias applied to a sentence rather than a cockpit switch, with a person's liberty on the other side of the decision instead of an airplane's flight path. If a judge distrusts the number too much, dismissing a tool that might in fact be capturing real statistical regularities a lone human intuition would miss, that is disuse, and it forfeits whatever genuine predictive value a risk instrument, however imperfectly built, might offer to a system otherwise reliant on the same unaided human judgment whose limitations were part of the reason these tools were built in the first place. Wisconsin's high court did not choose between these failure modes. It required a warning and moved on, leaving the actual calibration problem for judges to solve, hearing by hearing, without the tools calibration ordinarily needs.

The mechanisms Chapters 7 and 8 established in laboratory settings are worth applying here too, cautiously, as reasoning rather than as documented findings about judges specifically — the brief for this chapter contains no study of how judges as a population actually respond to COMPAS scores over time, and none should be invented. But the shape of the risk is easy enough to see. A judge who receives a "high risk" label and treats it as though it carries the authority of a laboratory result — rather than the authority of an undisclosed statistical model trained on a population the judge cannot inspect — is exhibiting exactly the automation bias pattern Chapter 2 named as a form of misuse, translated from a cockpit warning light to a sentencing worksheet. A judge who has once seen, or heard of, a case where a "low risk" defendant went on to commit a serious offense, and who afterward discounts every subsequent score the tool produces, is exhibiting the aversion pattern Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey documented — a single vivid failure moving trust further than the tool's aggregate track record would justify, if anyone in the courtroom actually had access to that aggregate track record to weigh it against. And judges are, in Logg, Minson, and Moore's sense, exactly the kind of domain experts whose research suggests they are the professionals most likely to discount algorithmic advice relative to laypeople — which cuts in a genuinely ambiguous direction here, since a healthy skepticism toward an opaque, trade-secret-protected score is not obviously the same failure as an expert forecaster dismissing a transparent, well-validated algorithm the Logg experiments actually tested. The expertise-reversal finding was established using algorithms whose track record participants could, in principle, evaluate. COMPAS is not that kind of algorithm, and a judge's distrust of it may be less a psychological bias to correct than a rational response to being asked to trust a black box — which is itself part of what makes the calibration problem in this chapter harder than the version Chapter 8 described, not easier.

It is worth being precise about what this chapter is not claiming. It is not claiming COMPAS is secretly accurate and merely misunderstood, nor is it claiming the tool is secretly worthless and merely defended by a company protecting its product. The honest position, and the one the underlying statistics actually support, is that COMPAS can simultaneously be a tool that satisfies a real fairness standard Northpointe can defend and a tool that produces a racially asymmetric pattern of errors ProPublica documented accurately — because those two things are not, mathematically, mutually exclusive descriptions of the same instrument applied to a population with unequal underlying base rates. What makes the courtroom version of this book's argument distinctive is not that the underlying psychology differs from a cockpit or a reading room. It is that the object being judged is deliberately hidden from the judge doing the judging, by design, as a matter of corporate policy upheld, with reservations, by a state's highest court. A radiologist can, in principle, learn over years of practice where an AI system tends to be strong and where it tends to falter, because the system is visible to study even if its internal reasoning is not fully interpretable. A judge facing a proprietary score has been asked to develop exactly that kind of calibrated judgment about an instrument that was built, on purpose, not to be studied.

The stakes of getting this wrong run in both directions, and neither direction is hypothetical. A court system that leans too heavily on an undisclosed algorithmic score risks encoding whatever biases sit inside that score's training data into thousands of individual sentences, with a false patina of statistical objectivity making the bias harder to see and harder to challenge than an individual judge's own acknowledged prejudice would be. A court system that reflexively distrusts every such tool, discarding the genuine predictive signal even a flawed instrument may contain, risks falling back entirely on the unaided human judgment whose documented inconsistency — different judges, different days, different defendants treated differently for reasons that have nothing to do with the facts of their case — was part of what motivated the turn toward algorithmic risk assessment in the first place. Neither failure mode is speculative. Both are simply the misuse and disuse this book has been tracking since Chapter 2, relocated into a setting where a wrong turn in either direction is measured in years of a person's life rather than a missed diagnosis or a delayed flight.

What makes this particular version of the calibration problem so stubborn is that it cannot be solved the way Chapter 6 suggests other versions can be — through accumulated experience, feedback, and a gradually improving mental model of the system. That path requires the system to be visible enough to build a mental model of in the first place. A pilot who flies the same autopilot for a decade eventually develops real, earned intuition about where it is strong and where it needs watching. A judge who sees a hundred COMPAS scores over a hundred different hearings, for a hundred different defendants whose later outcomes the judge mostly never learns, has no comparable opportunity to learn anything at all about the instrument itself — only about the individual defendants who happened to pass through that one courtroom. The scoring method sits behind Northpointe's trade-secret wall regardless of how many scores a given judge has seen, which means the ordinary machinery of calibration — the machinery that, elsewhere in this book, at least offers a plausible path toward appropriate reliance — has almost nothing to work with here. Loomis did not resolve this tension. It named it, required a warning about it, and left the harder work of actually calibrating trust in an opaque tool to the individual judgment of judges who, case after case, still cannot see what is inside the number they are being asked to weigh.
