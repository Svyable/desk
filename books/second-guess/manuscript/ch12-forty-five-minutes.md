# Forty-Five Minutes

Every domain examined so far in this book has offered its human at least a moment to weigh a recommendation before acting on it. A pilot has seconds, sometimes minutes, to decide whether an automated system deserves trust in a given emergency. A radiologist has the length of a reading session. A judge has the length of a hearing. Even a warehouse worker, however narrow the margin, is operating on a timescale a human nervous system can, in principle, keep pace with — a minute, an hour, a shift. Financial markets do not offer that courtesy. A trading algorithm can place, revise, and cancel an order faster than a human eye can register that an order existed at all, and by the time a person notices something has gone wrong, the machine may have already finished doing whatever it was going to do. This chapter is about two occasions when that speed differential stopped being a theoretical hazard and became an actual one, four minutes and forty-five minutes at a time, in markets that trade the retirement accounts and pension funds of people who had no idea, on either afternoon, that anything unusual was happening at all.

The two events did not share a cause, and this chapter treats that distinction as important rather than incidental. One was a systemic failure, arising from the interaction of several independent actors and structural features of an electronic market operating exactly as each individual piece had been separately designed to operate. The other was a single firm's software deployment error, confined to that firm's own systems, with a cause that a competent audit could trace to a specific and avoidable mistake. Lumping them together as two interchangeable instances of "algorithmic trading gone wrong" would erase precisely the distinction that makes them useful to examine side by side: they demonstrate, from two different directions, that the same underlying problem — automated execution outrunning the possibility of a timely human check — can arise from a market's structure and from a single company's carelessness, and that neither cause is reducible to the other.

The first event is now known, in financial-market shorthand, as the Flash Crash. On May 6, 2010, in the space of roughly half an hour during the afternoon trading session, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell by approximately 9 percent, then substantially recovered, in a move so fast and so severe that it briefly wiped out and then restored a scale of paper value that would ordinarily take a market weeks or months to lose and regain, if it ever regained it at all. The event drew a joint investigation from the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, whose staff issued a detailed report on September 30, 2010, reconstructing what had actually happened inside a market event that, from outside, had looked less like an explainable mechanical failure than a brief and inexplicable panic.

It helps to be specific about the instrument at the center of the sell order, because the choice of vehicle is part of why the effect propagated so quickly. E-mini S&P 500 futures contracts are among the most heavily traded financial instruments in the world, prized by a wide range of market participants precisely because they are liquid enough to be bought and sold in enormous size without, under ordinary conditions, moving the price very much. That same liquidity and centrality are what make the instrument a common proxy the broader market watches, consciously or not, for signals about where prices are heading — which means a large, disorderly move in the E-mini market does not stay contained to that single instrument. It ripples outward into the equities, options, and other derivatives markets that traders and their own automated systems use the E-mini's price as one input among many to price. A sell order large enough and blunt enough to distort the E-mini market was never going to stay a story about one futures contract.

What the investigators found was not a single point of failure but an interaction. A large automated sell algorithm — the report does not attribute this to malicious intent, only to a specific and consequential design choice — had been programmed to sell a substantial position, targeting roughly $4.1 billion in E-mini S&P 500 futures contracts, at a fixed execution rate: approximately 9 percent of trailing one-minute trading volume, sold steadily regardless of price and regardless of the time it took to complete the order. That design detail matters enormously to what followed. An execution algorithm indifferent to price will keep selling into a falling market at the same relative pace it would sell into a rising one, because its instructions say nothing about price; it says only how much of the recent volume to represent. As other participants in the market began to react to the falling prices this selling produced, trading volume itself increased, and an algorithm targeting a fixed percentage of volume responds to rising volume by selling faster, not slower — a feedback loop with no circuit breaker built into the algorithm's own logic to notice that the falling prices it was helping cause were themselves evidence that something had gone wrong.

That mechanical loop alone might not have produced a crash of the severity the market saw that afternoon. The investigators identified two further ingredients. Electronic liquidity providers — firms and systems that ordinarily stand ready to buy and sell continuously, providing the depth of market that lets ordinary trades execute without moving prices sharply — began pulling back their standing quotes as volatility rose, for reasons that made sense from each individual firm's own risk-management perspective but that, in aggregate, thinned the market's capacity to absorb the sell algorithm's steady pressure at exactly the moment that capacity was most needed. And separately, the report identified manipulative "spoofing" activity in the market during this period — the placement of orders not intended to be executed, designed to create a false impression of buying or selling interest that could influence other participants' behavior. A spoofed order works by exploiting exactly the kind of pattern-reading that both human traders and automated systems rely on to infer where a market is heading: a large visible order to sell, sitting in the order book, can suggest to everyone watching that selling pressure is building, prompting other participants — human and automated alike — to sell in anticipation, even though the spoofed order itself may never actually execute and can be withdrawn the instant it has done its work of shifting sentiment. Layered on top of a market already destabilized by a blunt, price-indifferent sell algorithm and liquidity providers pulling back their quotes, this activity compounded the disorder that the interaction of the sell algorithm and the withdrawing liquidity had already set in motion. The combination produced the plunge: the Dow fell roughly 9 percent between 2:30 and 3:00pm, a collapse of a magnitude and speed that, under ordinary circumstances, might unfold across a difficult week rather than a single half hour, followed by a rapid partial recovery once the selling pressure eased and liquidity returned.

Chapter 5's vigilance research offers a useful, if partial, way to understand why no human intervened sooner, even though it is not the whole explanation here. Mackworth's original finding was that sustained attention to a rare signal, embedded in an overwhelmingly uneventful stream of data, is a task human perception performs poorly — and financial markets, on an ordinary trading day, are exactly that kind of stream: mostly routine price movement, with genuinely dangerous anomalies rare enough that a human watching prices scroll by has little practiced basis for distinguishing an early Flash Crash from an ordinary volatile afternoon until well after the distinction has become obvious. But the Flash Crash sharpens the vigilance problem past anything Mackworth's radar operators faced, because the compressed timescale removes not just the human tendency to miss a rare signal, but the time that would otherwise exist, even for an attentive human, to correctly interpret the signal once noticed and translate that interpretation into action before the event had already run its course.

No single actor in this account set out to crash the market, and no single actor, acting alone, could have produced the Flash Crash on their own. The sell algorithm was executing exactly the instructions it had been given, instructions that made sense in isolation and became dangerous only in combination with a market thinning around it in real time. The liquidity providers pulling back were, from their own vantage point, managing risk rationally in a period of rising uncertainty. The spoofing activity was a separate, deliberate distortion layered onto structural conditions it did not create but was well positioned to exploit. What ties the three together, for the purposes of this book's argument, is timescale. Every one of these elements operated at a speed that placed the event's actual unfolding entirely beyond the reach of human intervention while it was happening. A human trader watching prices fall might have recognized within minutes that something unusual was underway; recognizing it and acting on that recognition, inside the roughly thirty-minute window the crash and partial recovery occupied, would have required a reaction time no individual human decision process — however well trained, however calm under pressure — could plausibly have delivered against automated systems executing thousands of decisions in the same span.

Two years later, and for an entirely different reason, a second event demonstrated the same structural vulnerability from a completely different angle. A market-making firm like Knight Capital exists to keep markets liquid — continuously posting prices at which it will buy and sell a given security, profiting on the small spread between the two, and in doing so giving other market participants a reliable counterparty to trade against even when no other buyer or seller happens to be available at that exact moment. Doing this at the scale Knight operated at, across a substantial share of NYSE-listed securities, is not a job a human trading desk could perform manually; it depends, by its nature, on automated systems constantly generating, adjusting, and canceling orders faster than any person could track individually. That dependency is not a flaw specific to Knight. It is close to definitional for what a modern market-making operation is. On August 1, 2012, Knight Capital Group deployed new trading software across its production servers ahead of the market's 9:30am open. The deployment did not go as intended. On one of the firm's eight production servers, the new code activated a piece of dormant legacy software, left over from a discontinued internal function the firm had once called Power Peg, that should not have been running at all. From the moment the market opened, that malfunctioning server began sending a flood of erroneous orders into NYSE-listed securities — not a single bad trade, but a continuous stream of them, executing far faster and in far greater volume than any human trader monitoring the firm's activity could review, understand, and halt in real time.

The malfunction ran for roughly forty-five minutes before Knight's staff succeeded in identifying and stopping it. Forty-five minutes is not, by the standards of an ordinary business day, a long interval — shorter than a single meeting, shorter than a commute, the kind of span that passes almost without notice in most working lives. Inside an automated trading system generating erroneous orders continuously, it was long enough to produce a catastrophe. Unwinding the position the malfunctioning code had accumulated produced a realized pre-tax loss of approximately $440 million — a sum large enough that it came close to destroying the firm outright. Knight Capital was acquired within months of the incident, its independent existence effectively ended by three-quarters of an hour of software behaving in a way no one at the firm had intended or, evidently, anticipated closely enough to catch before the damage was substantially done. The Securities and Exchange Commission later fined Knight $12 million for violations of market-access and supervisory rules — a regulatory finding that the firm's own internal controls over its trading systems had not met the standard required to prevent, or to catch quickly enough, exactly the kind of failure that had occurred.

Bainbridge's second irony from Chapter 3 fits the Knight Capital incident with an almost uncomfortable precision, though the setting could hardly be further from the process-control rooms she originally had in mind. An automated trading system exists, in large part, because the volume and speed of modern market-making cannot be handled reliably by unaided human traders — the very premise that justifies automating the function in the first place. That same premise, though, leaves the humans who remain responsible for the system's failures with the rarest, least practiced, and hardest case of all: a malfunction inside code the firm's staff had not been actively monitoring line by line, because the whole point of automating the function was that no one needed to. The dormant Power Peg code had been discontinued, which is to say it was exactly the kind of forgotten, unexercised corner of the system Bainbridge's argument predicts will be least understood at the moment it unexpectedly reactivates. Nobody at Knight was watching that code, because nobody had reason to believe there was anything left in it worth watching — until, for forty-five minutes, there was.

It is worth dwelling on what "supervisory rules" means in this context, because the phrase risks sounding like a bureaucratic technicality when the underlying failure was anything but abstract. Market-access rules exist, in essence, to require firms with the technical capacity to send enormous volumes of orders into the market to also maintain the technical capacity to detect and halt those orders quickly if something goes wrong — a regulatory acknowledgment, arrived at well before Knight's incident, that automated trading systems can fail faster than any human process can respond to unless a firm has specifically engineered the capacity to respond at a comparable speed. Knight's finding was not that the company had behaved recklessly in some vague, general sense. It was that the specific safeguards required to keep a human capable of intervening within a reasonable window had not functioned as they were supposed to, and forty-five minutes of uncaught, uncorrected automated trading was the direct, measurable consequence.

Set the two events beside each other and a shared structural lesson emerges, even though — and this is worth restating plainly — their causes were not the same event wearing two different faces. The Flash Crash arose from the interaction of multiple independent, legally operating market participants, none of whom, individually, could be said to have caused the crash on their own; it was a property of the market's structure under a specific combination of conditions, not a single company's mistake. Knight Capital's malfunction arose from a single firm's own software deployment error, confined to that firm's own systems, traceable to a specific and, in hindsight, preventable technical failure that had nothing to do with the broader market's structure. A regulator, an engineer, or a historian asking "what caused this" would need entirely different answers for the two events, and a chapter that blurred them into one undifferentiated story about "algorithms behaving badly" would be getting the finance industry's actual lesson wrong in exactly the way this book has tried, throughout, not to get lessons wrong.

What the two events share is not their cause but their temporal shape, and that shared shape is the reason this chapter puts them together. In every other domain this book has examined, the person facing an automated recommendation retains, at minimum, the theoretical possibility of intervening before the recommendation becomes an irreversible outcome — a pilot can still choose to disconnect the autopilot, a radiologist can still choose to look more closely at the image, a judge can still choose to weigh a risk score against other evidence before the sentence is final. The Flash Crash and the Knight Capital incident both foreclosed that possibility structurally, not because any individual human involved failed to exercise good judgment in the moment, but because the systems involved were executing at a speed that placed "the moment" itself outside the reach of ordinary human reaction time. By the time a person at Knight Capital or in the wider market on May 6, 2010, could register that something anomalous was occurring, meaningfully diagnose what it was, and act to stop it, a large share of the damage in both cases had already been done. The human second guess did not arrive too late because anyone was slow, distracted, or poorly trained. It arrived too late because the architecture of the systems involved had never given a human second guess anywhere to land before the consequences were already locked in.

This is the sharpest version yet of a problem this book has been building toward since Chapter 6 introduced trust calibration as a matter of matching a person's confidence in a system to that system's actual trustworthiness. Calibration, in the cockpit, the reading room, and the courtroom, is fundamentally a problem about a person's judgment: whether they trust too much, too little, or about the right amount, given what the system in front of them can actually do. Calibration assumes, underneath that judgment, that a moment exists in which the judgment can be exercised — a window, however brief, in which a human's trust or distrust can still change what happens next. The Flash Crash and Knight Capital both describe conditions under which that window effectively did not exist, not because of any failure of psychology, training, or institutional culture, but because the systems were engineered — separately, for separate purposes, by separate actors — to operate at speeds that removed the window by design, whether or not anyone involved intended that removal as an outcome.

That distinction matters for what this book's later, more constructive chapters can honestly promise. Training a pilot to hand-fly more often, teaching a radiologist to search an image independently of an AI flag, requiring a judge to receive a cautionary instruction about a risk score's limits — all of these are interventions aimed at improving a human's judgment inside a window that already exists. None of them would have done anything at all for the Flash Crash or for Knight Capital's forty-five minutes, because the problem in both cases was not that a human judged the situation poorly. It was that no human was positioned, by the time either event was fully underway, to judge it at all. The appropriate response to a problem like this is not better training for the humans nearest the malfunctioning system; it is design and regulation aimed at the systems themselves — circuit breakers, kill switches, mandated pauses in trading when volatility crosses certain thresholds, the market-access and supervisory requirements the SEC's finding against Knight Capital was itself an attempt to enforce after the fact. Some of that regulatory apparatus existed in some form before these two events; some of it was strengthened because of them. Neither event, this book's sourcing makes clear, was allowed to pass without consequence — the joint SEC-CFTC investigation into the Flash Crash and the SEC's enforcement action against Knight Capital both represent institutions responding to a demonstrated failure, not ignoring one. But the response, in both cases, necessarily took the form of engineering constraints on the systems themselves, because the alternative response this book has spent so many chapters examining — a better-calibrated human judgment exercised in the moment — was never structurally available to begin with.

It is easy, discussing basis points and E-mini futures and market-access rules, to lose sight of whose money was actually moving during these two events. The retirement accounts, index funds, and pension holdings referenced at the start of this chapter were not bystanders to the Flash Crash; some of them executed trades, or had trades executed on their behalf by fund managers and automated rebalancing systems of their own, at the distorted prices that existed for the brief window before the market recovered — the crash's human cost distributed, in most cases, as a small, largely invisible drag on ordinary people's savings rather than as a headline casualty count, which may be part of why the event receded from public memory faster than its actual severity would suggest it should have. Knight Capital's $440 million loss was borne more directly and more visibly, by the firm's shareholders and eventually by the firm's independent existence, but the trades its malfunctioning system executed also moved through the same public markets everyone else's retirement money sits inside, at prices no human trader had approved or even seen in time to stop them.

There is a specific, uncomfortable implication buried in that conclusion, and it is worth stating directly rather than leaving implicit. Everywhere else in this book, the argument has been that the decisive skill is knowing when to trust, override, or double-check the system in front of you — a skill that can be taught, trained, and improved, however imperfectly, through better feedback, better design, better institutional habits. Finance, at the speed these two events demonstrate it can now operate, poses a case where that skill, however well developed, may simply arrive too late to matter, because the system has already finished acting by the time a human has finished noticing. Calibrated trust remains the right goal for the vast majority of the decisions this book examines. But the Flash Crash and Knight Capital both stand as a reminder that calibration is a solution to a problem of judgment, not a solution to a problem of physics — and when a system's execution speed structurally forecloses the possibility of a timely human check altogether, the work of keeping people safe has to happen before the system runs, in its design and its guardrails, because there will be no moment afterward in which a well-calibrated human being can still change the outcome.

That does not make the psychology this book has spent so many chapters examining irrelevant to finance; it relocates where that psychology has to operate. The engineers who set an execution algorithm's parameters, the risk officers who approve a firm's automated trading limits, the regulators who decide how aggressively to mandate circuit breakers and kill switches, are themselves making trust-calibration judgments about automated systems — just earlier in the process, before the code runs live in a market moving too fast for anyone downstream to intervene. Get that earlier judgment wrong, underestimate how a fixed-rate, price-indifferent algorithm might behave in unusually thin liquidity, or overestimate how thoroughly a software deployment had actually been tested against legacy code nobody remembered was still there, and the moment for a well-calibrated human being to catch the mistake has already passed by the time the market opens. The forty-five minutes in the chapter's title, and the roughly thirty minutes the Flash Crash took to fall and largely recover, were not periods in which human judgment failed. They were periods in which human judgment no longer had anywhere left to act.
