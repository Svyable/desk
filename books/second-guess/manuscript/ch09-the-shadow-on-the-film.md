# The Shadow on the Film

A mammogram is a black-and-white image of compressed breast tissue, shot from at least two angles, and reading one well takes years. The tissue itself is not uniform. Dense, fibrous breast tissue and a small tumor can both appear as pale regions against a darker background, and a competent radiologist has to hold in mind not just what a cancer looks like but what a hundred things that are not cancer also look like: a benign cyst, a calcified duct, an old scar, a fold in the compression plate, the normal asymmetry between a person's left breast and their right. The image does not announce which shadow matters. The radiologist has to search the whole field, every time, knowing that the finding they are least prepared for is the one most likely to be missed, and that missing it will not look like an error until months or years later, if it is ever traced back at all.

For most of the history of mammography, this search was performed entirely by trained people, sometimes with a second trained person reading the same films independently, and the record of how often something was missed was the record of human limitation: fatigue, distraction, the vigilance a long day of images demands from an attention span that was never built to sustain it. A radiologist reading screening mammograms for a living does not read one image and stop; they read dozens or hundreds in a session, the overwhelming majority of them unremarkable, which is precisely the condition Chapter 5 identified as the hardest kind of attention task there is — sustained watch over a stream of data where the meaningful signal, when it appears at all, is rare. Radiology did not need artificial intelligence to have a vigilance problem. It had one already, built into the basic shape of the job.

By the end of the 2010s, that record had a new entrant. A handful of technology companies and research hospitals had begun training machine learning systems to read mammograms alongside, or instead of, the radiologists who had always read them, and the question of what such a system was actually worth — not in a press release, but in a peer-reviewed accounting of its errors — became one of the more closely watched contests in medical software.

In January 2020, a team led by Scott Mayer McKinney, working with Google Health, published the results of one such contest in Nature, in a paper titled "International evaluation of an AI system for breast cancer screening." What made the study notable was not simply that an AI system had been built to read mammograms — several groups had tried that by then — but the specific test McKinney and his colleagues designed to find out whether the system had actually learned something general about the disease, or had merely become very good at the particular quirks of one hospital's equipment, one population's tissue density, one set of radiologists' conventions for writing up a report. They trained the system on mammography data from roughly seventy-six thousand women screened in the United Kingdom. Then, without retraining it on a single new image, they turned it loose on more than fifteen thousand women's records from the United States — a different health system, different equipment vendors in places, a different population, and, crucially, radiologists on the other side of the Atlantic whose training and habits the system had never seen.

This is the harder test, and it is worth pausing on why. A model that performs brilliantly on the data it was built from can still be nearly useless anywhere else; medicine has a long history of tools that worked beautifully in the hospital that invented them and disappointed everywhere else. A system trained exclusively on UK mammograms could, in principle, have simply memorized the visual signature of one manufacturer's imaging equipment, the typical density patterns of the specific population it saw seventy-six thousand times over, or even incidental habits in how UK radiologists tended to position a patient or annotate a borderline case. None of that would show up as a flaw on UK data, because the system would be right at home there. It would show up, if it existed, the moment the system met a population it had never seen — different equipment in places, a different mix of patients, radiologists trained under an entirely different set of conventions. Evaluating the Google Health system on that unfamiliar American data, with no second chance to adjust to it, was the closer approximation of what actually matters for a screening tool meant for real, varied hospitals: not whether it can ace an exam it studied for, but whether it holds up when the exam changes.

By that test, the results were genuinely strong. Measured against the original clinical reads already on record for those women, the AI system's judgments would have reduced false positives — cases flagged as suspicious that were not, in fact, cancer — by 5.7 percent in the US data and by 1.2 percent in the UK data. It would have reduced false negatives — the more consequential error, a real cancer read as clear — by 9.4 percent in the US data and by 2.7 percent in the UK data. The improvement was larger in the US test than in the UK one, which is itself a small piece of evidence that the system had learned something reasonably general rather than something narrowly tuned to the country it came from, since it performed better, not worse, on the population it had never trained on for false negatives specifically.

The two kinds of error are not equivalent, and no one who has sat through either side of a mammogram appointment would mistake them for equivalent. A false positive means a callback, more imaging, sometimes a biopsy, and weeks of a specific, gnawing fear before the fear turns out to be unfounded — a real cost, in money, time, and psychological toll, multiplied across however many women a screening program calls back unnecessarily. A false negative means a cancer that was there on the film, undetected, left to grow until it announces itself some other way, later and often worse. A tool that improves both numbers at once, as the McKinney results claim, is not making a tradeoff between the two kinds of harm; it is reducing both. That is worth stating plainly, because it is the reason the study drew the attention it did. It is also, on its own, still silent on the question this chapter is actually asking, which has nothing to do with which error the tool is better or worse at and everything to do with what a human reader does once the tool's opinion is sitting next to their own.

Later, Nature published an addendum to the paper. The public record this book draws on does not specify what the addendum corrected, and this account does not guess. What it establishes plainly enough is that the study, landmark as it was, did not arrive as an unrevisable final word; even research published in one of the most selective journals in science remains subject to correction after the fact, and treating the original 2020 paper as the last thing anyone said about the system would be its own small failure of calibration.

Here is what the McKinney study did not do, and the distinction matters enough to state carefully. It measured the AI system's standalone accuracy against a fixed set of clinical reads already on file. It did not put a working radiologist in a reading room, hand them a live case with the AI's flag already visible on the screen, and measure what that radiologist then did with it — whether they searched the rest of the image as thoroughly as they would have without the flag, whether a confident AI recommendation nudged their read toward agreement, whether a case where the AI disagreed with their instinct made them second-guess themselves or dig in. Nothing in the paper reports radiologist over-reliance or under-reliance on the tool, because the paper was not built to measure that. It measured the tool. It is worth being explicit about this, because the natural temptation — a temptation this book has spent eight chapters trying to train the reader out of — is to read "the AI beat the average clinical read" and quietly assume that settles the practical question of how the tool should be used. It does not, because the practical question was never really about averages.

The practical question, every time a radiologist sits down with a live case and an AI flag on the screen, is the one Chapter 6 introduced: not "is this system good," but "is this system, on this read, right now, more or less trustworthy than my own judgment at this moment" — and whether the radiologist's actual trust in it, in that instant, is calibrated to match. A tool that is right eighty or ninety percent of the time in aggregate offers no guarantee about the read in front of you. It might be one of the reads where it is right and you are tempted to overrule it. It might be one of the reads where it is wrong and you are tempted to defer. Averages describe populations. Radiologists do not treat populations; they treat one patient's film at a time, and the aggregate accuracy of a system, however impressive, cannot tell them in advance which category today's image falls into.

There is an older version of this problem that predates any of the software involved. A second reader — another trained radiologist independently examining the same film — has long been one of the more reliable ways to catch what a single reader misses, precisely because two independent searches of the same image are less likely to share the same blind spot than one search performed alone. An AI system inserted into a reading workflow is, in one sense, simply a new kind of second reader: another set of eyes, examining the same shadow, arriving at its own verdict before comparing notes. But a second human reader and a software system are not psychologically interchangeable, even when their accuracy is comparable on paper. A human colleague's disagreement carries information about their training, their reputation, their track record on cases like this one — context a radiologist can weigh, consciously or not, in deciding how much to update their own read. An algorithm's disagreement carries none of that texture. It offers a number, or a flag, stripped of the professional history that would normally help a reader decide how much a second opinion should move their own. That absence of context is not a flaw in the software; it is simply a different shape of second opinion, and the calibration problem this book keeps returning to is, in part, the problem of learning to weigh a second opinion that arrives without the cues humans have always used to weigh one.

This is where the mechanisms established earlier in the book stop being abstractions and start describing a specific, plausible risk to a specific profession. Parasuraman and Riley's original distinction, from Chapter 2, was between misuse — over-reliance on an automated system — and disuse — under-reliance, neglect, or premature abandonment of one. Radiology, once an AI reader is part of the workflow, is exposed to both failure modes at once, in the same reading room, sometimes in the same radiologist across different weeks depending on what case they saw most recently. Consider automation bias first — the form of misuse, in Parasuraman and Riley's original taxonomy, where a person's judgment quietly defers to an automated recommendation rather than independently verifying it. In a reading room, this could take a concrete shape: a radiologist opens a case, sees the AI system flag a particular region with apparent confidence, and lets that flag organize the search — spending real attention on the flagged area, less on the rest of the image, because the system has already told them where to look. If the flag is correct, nothing is lost. If the flag is wrong — either because it marked an innocuous shadow as suspicious, or, more dangerously, because it missed something the system's training never taught it to weight heavily — a search that has been quietly narrowed by the AI's confidence is a search less likely to catch what the AI missed. This is not a finding from the McKinney paper. No documented case tied to this specific system establishes that it happened. It is the well-established mechanism from earlier chapters, applied to a setting this study makes newly urgent: a radiologist's attention is a finite resource, and any tool confident enough to draw that attention toward one region draws it away from the rest of the film, whether or not the tool intended to.

There is a quieter version of disuse worth naming before turning to aversion proper, because it does not require a dramatic error at all. Chapter 2 noted that disuse is often triggered by a system's false-alarm rate — operators learn, over enough exposure, to tune out a tool that cries wolf too often, even when it is right the moment it matters. An AI system flagging a meaningful share of cases as suspicious, most of which will turn out on further workup to be nothing, can train a radiologist toward exactly this habit without any single dramatic miss ever occurring: the flag becomes background noise, glanced at and discounted by reflex, and the tool's real, measured advantage over the unaided read is quietly forfeited by a reader who has simply stopped taking it seriously. This is disuse without a villain and without a single bad case to point to, which makes it harder to notice and harder to correct than the aversion pattern that follows.

Now consider the opposite failure, the one Chapter 7 spent an entire chapter establishing with hard experimental evidence. Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons, and Cade Massey found that people who watched an algorithmic forecaster outperform a human forecaster overall still lost confidence in the algorithm faster than in the human after each visible mistake — even when the mistake was no larger, and no more frequent, than the human's own errors. Radiology offers an unusually vivid setting for this pattern, because a missed cancer is not an abstract forecasting error measured in aggregate statistics; it is a specific patient, a specific film, a specific moment when the tool was wrong about something that mattered enormously. A radiologist who has seen, or heard about, one case where an AI system cleared an image that later turned out to hide a cancer has been handed exactly the kind of vivid, memorable, singular failure that the Dietvorst research shows erodes trust disproportionately — even if that same radiologist's own individual error rate, measured over a comparable number of cases, would not survive the same scrutiny nearly as visibly, because a human colleague's misses are rarely narrated back to a reader, or a colleague, case by case the way a single dramatic algorithmic failure can be. The risk here is not that radiologists distrust AI too much in some abstract, uniform sense. It is that trust in the tool can swing sharply on the evidence of one bad case, in a way that the tool's actual aggregate performance — the 5.7 and 9.4 percent improvements McKinney's team documented — does not justify.

The two error types are also not equally likely to trigger the aversion pattern, and it is worth thinking through why. A false positive the AI generates — a flag that sends a patient for an unnecessary follow-up — is often resolved quietly and relatively quickly; the extra imaging comes back clear, the case closes, and the radiologist's memory of the false alarm fades into the general background hum of a job full of false alarms. A false negative the AI generates — a case the system cleared that later turns out to have hidden a cancer — surfaces much later, if it surfaces at all, often through a route that makes it memorable: a subsequent diagnosis, a chart review, a colleague mentioning the case. If Dietvorst's research is right that visible, memorable errors erode algorithmic trust disproportionately, the erosion in a reading room is likely to concentrate specifically around the AI's false negatives — which is the direction of error a radiologist can least afford to become numb to catching themselves, precisely because it is also the direction in which their own attention, not the tool's, is the last line of defense.

There is a genuine, if modest, reason for optimism buried in the same research that documents the aversion problem, and Chapter 7 built an entire follow-up finding around it. Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's later work found that giving people even a small amount of ability to adjust an algorithm's forecast substantially restored their willingness to rely on it — not because the adjustment power was used heavily, but because having it at all changed the relationship from blind obedience to something closer to collaboration. Nothing in the McKinney paper describes how the Google Health system's flag is presented to a radiologist, whether as a fixed verdict or as an adjustable input the reader can annotate, agree with, or push back against within the workflow. But the general finding suggests that the interface choice is not a cosmetic detail. A system that hands down an unmodifiable verdict invites exactly the swings this chapter has been describing — overreliance when it is confident, sharp discounting after one visible miss. A system that lets the radiologist register disagreement, flag uncertainty, or adjust the emphasis of a finding, even modestly, gives the reader a stake in the outcome that plain deference or plain dismissal does not.

Both of these risks would matter even if radiologists were an ordinary population of tool users. They are not. Chapter 8 closed on a specific, falsifiable warning that this chapter now has to cash in: Jennifer Logg, Julia Minson, and Don Moore's finding that the preference for algorithmic advice over human advice — the "algorithm appreciation" effect that ran in the opposite direction from Dietvorst's aversion result — weakened as the people receiving the advice became more expert, and reversed among people with real, practiced domain expertise. Professionals who forecasted regularly relied less on algorithmic advice than laypeople did in the same experiments, and that reduced reliance measurably hurt their accuracy. Radiologists are not laypeople evaluating a tool from the outside. They are exactly the population Logg, Minson, and Moore identified as most likely to discount a working algorithm's advice, and most likely to be wrong to do so. A hospital administrator reading a press summary of the McKinney study, or a patient reading a headline about an AI that beats doctors, sits in roughly the position of the laypeople in the Logg experiments — inclined, if anything, to over-trust the algorithm's numbers. The radiologist actually reading the film sits in the opposite position: trained, experienced, confident in a hard-won skill, and — the research suggests — statistically more likely than a novice to wave off a tool that could, on a given case, be catching something they would have missed.

There is a further wrinkle here that Bainbridge's argument from Chapter 3 anticipates almost exactly, even though her 1983 paper never mentions medicine. If a screening program eventually leans on an AI system to triage the bulk of routine, visually unambiguous cases — the clear negatives, the obvious findings — while directing its most uncertain, borderline, hard-to-read images toward the radiologists for a closer human look, the radiologists are left doing an ever-more-concentrated diet of exactly the hardest cases, the ones least like the bulk of what the system was trained and validated on. That is Bainbridge's first irony, transplanted: the automation is deployed because it is reliable on the easy cases, which leaves the humans holding an increasingly unrepresentative, increasingly difficult slice of the work. Nothing in the McKinney paper describes screening programs being restructured this way, and this book is not claiming they have been; the point is that Bainbridge's argument predicts this as a natural pressure once a tool this capable exists, and a profession adopting it would do well to notice the pressure before it quietly reshapes what "reading mammograms" even means as a job.

None of this resolves into a tidy recommendation, because the honest state of the evidence does not support one. The McKinney study demonstrates that a well-built system, tested on a population it never trained on, can measurably outperform the clinical reads already on file — a real result, worth taking seriously, not a marketing claim. It does not demonstrate how radiologists actually behave once that system's flag is sitting on their screen during a live read, because measuring that was never the study's purpose. What earlier chapters of this book establish is that the space between those two facts — a tool's proven aggregate accuracy, and a practitioner's moment-to-moment decision to trust, double-check, or override it — is exactly where trust calibration lives or fails. It is not a gap that closes itself simply because the tool is good. Air France 447's crew had functioning instruments and a functioning airplane under them and still lost the calibration battle in the confusion of the moment; algorithm aversion research shows confidence in a system's aggregate quality does not protect a person from over-reacting to its individual failures; algorithm appreciation research shows that the people most qualified to interpret a tool's advice are not automatically the people most inclined to use it well.

What makes radiology a particularly consequential arena for this problem, rather than merely an interesting one, is scale. A screening program does not read one mammogram; it reads enormous numbers of them, year after year, and a small shift in false positive or false negative rates, multiplied across a population, is not a small number of women. A radiologist's individual calibration failure on any single case is a private tragedy or a private relief. The same calibration failure, systematized across thousands of radiologists reading millions of films with an AI flag increasingly present on the screen, becomes a property of the health system itself — and a health system's calibration, unlike an individual's, is not something that corrects through one person's accumulated experience. It has to be built deliberately, through training that teaches radiologists what the tool is actually good and bad at, through feedback loops that let them see their own calibration rather than guess at it, and through institutional patience for the slow, unglamorous work of getting the working relationship between a skilled reader and a flagged image right — work this book will return to directly when it turns, several chapters from now, to what design and training can actually do about problems exactly like this one. For now, the honest place to leave the reading room is where the evidence actually leaves it: a tool that measurably improves on the record it was tested against, handed to a profession whose particular expertise, this book's own earlier research suggests, may make that tool harder rather than easier to trust exactly when trusting it would help.

The shadow on the film does not know any of this. It sits where it sits, cancer or not, regardless of who or what is looking at it, or how much confidence either the reader or the software brings to the search. What the McKinney study changed was not the shadow. It was the number of eyes on the image, and the question of how those eyes — human and otherwise — ought to weigh each other's judgment before either one decides, for a specific woman, on a specific morning, that a specific pale patch of gray is nothing to worry about.
