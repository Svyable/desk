# The Recommendation Economy

Consider, briefly, everyone this book has not written about. Not the pilots, radiologists, judges, warehouse workers, traders, and drivers whose stories have carried its argument, but the much larger and much less dramatic population who spend an ordinary working day doing something quieter: looking at a screen that has already ranked, flagged, scored, or suggested something, and deciding, usually without much ceremony, whether to go along with it. A hiring manager scanning a shortlist an applicant-tracking system has already narrowed from several hundred résumés to a dozen. An insurance underwriter reviewing a premium a pricing model has already calculated. A customer-service representative deciding whether to send the reply a system has drafted, or to write something different. A delivery driver following, or quietly ignoring, a routing app's suggested turn. None of these moments will end up in an accident report. None of them will produce a peer-reviewed study, a court opinion, or a congressional inquiry. And all of them are the same moment the preceding nineteen chapters have spent examining in their most extreme and best-documented forms.

That is worth saying plainly, because it would be easy to close a book built from plane crashes, cancer screenings, sentencing hearings, and flash crashes with the impression that its subject is something rare and dramatic — a set of edge cases where the stakes happen to be catastrophic, interesting mainly because catastrophe makes for a better story than an ordinary Tuesday. The opposite is closer to true. Aviation, medicine, criminal justice, warehousing, finance, and driving were chosen for these chapters not because the psychology at work in them is unique, but because each of those domains happens to produce the kind of evidence a serious account can actually be built on: government accident investigations that reconstruct a decision down to the second, peer-reviewed experiments that isolate a single variable across hundreds of participants, court opinions that put a dispute over trust and disclosure into the public record, regulatory filings that a company cannot simply decline to produce. The hiring manager and the insurance underwriter face a structurally identical question every time a ranked list or a calculated score appears in front of them. Their version of it almost never generates the kind of rigorous public record the earlier chapters depended on. That is a limitation of the evidence available to write about, not a sign that the underlying psychology stops at the edge of the domains chosen for closer examination.

The machines named across these chapters — a flight-management computer with almost nothing to say about a dual engine failure, a breast-cancer screening system trained on tens of thousands of mammograms, a proprietary risk score a Wisconsin court could not fully inspect, a handheld scanner setting the pace of a warehouse shift, a sell algorithm executing nine percent of a minute's trading volume without regard to price, a perception stack trying to tell a person from a shopping cart at highway speed — are not, in the end, the real subject here, for all the space they have taken up on the page. They were vehicles for a single, recurring question that does not belong to any one of them: does this particular system, in this particular moment, deserve this particular person's trust, skepticism, or override, and how would that person actually know. Swap out the machine, swap out the industry, swap out the stakes, and the question does not change shape. What changes is only how well documented the answer turns out to be.

It is worth retracing, briefly and without re-arguing them, how many different forms that one question has taken across the chapters that came before this one, because the range is itself part of the argument. Parasuraman and Riley's distinction between misuse and disuse gave the question its basic vocabulary: too much trust and too little trust are both failures, and neither is the automatically safe default. Bainbridge's two ironies explained why the question gets harder, not easier, as automation improves — because a system trusted to handle the easy cases leaves a human responsible for the rare, unpracticed, hardest ones, and because the very act of automating a skill erodes the human capacity to perform it when that rare case finally arrives. VanderBurgh watched that erosion happen inside actual cockpits and diagnosed it as automation mismanagement years before Air France 447 confirmed, at catastrophic cost, that the diagnosis had been correct. Mackworth's radar operators, tested decades before any of this book's software existed, showed that sustained attention to a rarely wrong system is one of the specific tasks human vigilance performs worst, a finding that reappeared, translated into an SUV's front seat, in the moments before a self-driving test vehicle killed a pedestrian named Elaine Herzberg. Lee and See gave the whole problem a name — trust calibration — and showed that the gap between how much a person trusts a system and how much that system has actually earned is not fixed, but built or eroded through the accumulated experience of using it.

Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey showed that people abandon algorithms too fast after a single visible mistake, even when the algorithm remains the better bet, and then showed that a small amount of control restores the trust that single mistake had taken away. Logg, Minson, and Moore showed the opposite failure running in the opposite direction — laypeople trusting algorithmic advice more than they trusted the same advice from a person — and then delivered one of the sharpest specific warnings in this whole account: the professionals most qualified to judge an algorithm's advice were also the ones most likely to discount it in a way that measurably hurt their own accuracy, a warning that mattered enormously once the argument moved into rooms full of exactly those professionals. The mammography study showed a system that measurably outperformed the average clinical read on a large international dataset, and showed, just as importantly, that outperforming an average settles nothing about what a specific radiologist should do with a specific flag on a specific morning. The COMPAS dispute showed two credible, mathematically coherent definitions of fairness producing two different verdicts about the same tool, and a Wisconsin court trying, imperfectly, to keep a proprietary score from becoming the determining voice in a room where a person's liberty was at stake. The warehouse chapter showed what happens when the automated system stops being a recommendation a worker can weigh and becomes the standing condition of the worker's employment, collapsing the space in which second-guessing is even possible. The Flash Crash and Knight Capital showed that sometimes the problem is not psychological at all, but architectural: a human's judgment arriving, structurally, too late to matter, because the system executed faster than any second guess could travel. Maguire's taxi drivers showed, with unusual scientific precision, that a skill genuinely exercised leaves a physical trace in the people who exercise it, and that a skill left unused may not — the clearest evidence anywhere in this account for a claim the rest of the deskilling debate can only gesture toward. Eiband's placebic explanations showed that the feeling of understanding a system and the fact of understanding it can be pulled apart by nothing more than the presence of an explanation-shaped sentence. Elish's moral crumple zone showed where accountability tends to land after a joint human-machine failure, and the Tempe investigation showed, about as plainly as any single case in these pages, how a system's own design choices can set a human "supervisor" up to fail a task human vigilance was never well suited to in the first place. Tesla's and Waymo's dueling safety statistics showed that even the evidence meant to help the public calibrate trust in a whole category of machine can itself be shaped by the parties with the greatest interest in how it reads.

And then the last three chapters showed both directions the story can go once judgment is actually put to the test. Sullenberger, de Crespigny, and Garman succeeded not because they trusted or distrusted the automation around them as a matter of general policy, but because each of them, at the decisive moment, knew something specific enough about the system in front of them — what it could do, what it could not, where its logic ran out — to act correctly inside a window that would not have stayed open much longer. The crews of Lion Air 610 and Ethiopian Airlines 302 fought, with everything available to them, an automated system that kept reasserting uncommanded control faster than they could permanently counteract it, a fight neither crew had been adequately prepared to win because the system fighting them had been built, and disclosed, on the assumption that a fight like this would essentially never happen. And the chapter that followed both of those tried to state plainly what the accumulated evidence actually suggests can be built differently: automation broken into separable functions rather than treated as a single dial, adjustability that keeps a person meaningfully engaged rather than either fully deferring or fully checking out, honest feedback over either concealment or manufactured reassurance, and a structural, supported habit of stepping down when uncertainty rises rather than riding a system's confidence past the point it has earned.

None of that adds up to a formula, and it would misrepresent the evidence gathered across these chapters to pretend otherwise. There is no reliable way to predict, in advance, which specific system in a person's specific job will turn out to be the next MCAS, quietly amassing more authority than its actual reliability warrants, or the next Qantas 32, flooding a person with more information than any checklist logic can sensibly prioritize while still leaving room for a well-prepared human to sort it out. What this book has tried to establish, across cases that could not be more different from one another in their stakes and their settings, is that the skill of telling the two apart in the moment is real, learnable in some domains and stubbornly resistant to learning in others, and almost never taught on purpose by the institutions now deploying automated recommendation at the fastest pace in their history.

The second chapter of this account warned against treating misuse and disuse as opposite ends of a simple dial, where more trust is always the wrong answer and less trust the safe default. Nothing in the eighteen chapters since has given a reason to abandon that warning; if anything, the cases examined along the way have deepened it. Institutions do not settle into a single stable posture toward automated recommendation and stay there. They tend to cycle: a period of expanding trust, often justified by real gains in accuracy or efficiency, followed by a visible failure that triggers a period of retrenchment, often overcorrected, in which a previously trusted system is abandoned or hedged past the point its actual reliability warrants, until the memory of the failure fades enough for trust to expand again. Aviation has lived through versions of this cycle for decades, tightening training requirements after a crash and loosening them, gradually, as the crash recedes from institutional memory. There is no obvious reason to expect medicine, criminal justice, warehousing, finance, or driving to escape the same pattern, and by 2027 each of those domains has already produced at least one visible failure severe enough to prompt exactly this kind of overcorrection somewhere within it. The skill these chapters have tried to describe is not a defense against that cycle so much as a way of shortening it — of replacing a blanket swing from trust to suspicion and back with something closer to what Sullenberger, de Crespigny, and Garman each managed to do under far worse conditions: a specific, evidence-based judgment about this system, in this moment, rather than a general mood about automation inherited from whatever happened most recently and most visibly.

By 2027, that pace shows no sign of slowing, and the domains it touches keep widening past the ones examined here in depth. A search engine ranks results and, increasingly, drafts an answer before a person has finished reading the question. A social platform's recommendation engine decides, moment to moment, what a billion people will see next, optimizing for engagement in ways few of the people affected by it could describe with any precision. A writing assistant suggests not just a correction but a rewrite, and a student, a lawyer, or a novelist has to decide, sentence by sentence, whether the suggestion sounds more like them or less. None of these examples comes with an NTSB report or a peer-reviewed study attached, which is exactly the point this chapter opened by making: the underlying psychology documented at length, in its best-evidenced, highest-stakes forms, across the preceding nineteen chapters is not confined to cockpits, reading rooms, courtrooms, warehouses, trading floors, and windshields. It has simply been easiest to see clearly there, because the consequences of getting it wrong were severe enough, often enough, to force an investigation.

Part of what makes 2027 different from the moment Parasuraman and Riley were writing about in 1997, or even the moment Lee and See published their trust-calibration framework in 2004, is that the line between a system recommending a decision and a system performing the underlying work itself has become far blurrier than either paper's authors could have anticipated. A flight-management computer recommends a course of action but leaves a pilot to fly the airplane. A mammography system flags a region of a scan but leaves a radiologist to read it. A generative system asked to draft a memo, a diagnosis note, or a line of legal argument is doing something closer to both at once — producing the artifact and, in the same act, embedding a thousand small judgment calls about tone, emphasis, and framing that its user may never consciously review before signing their name to it. The misuse-and-disuse question raised in this book's second chapter does not disappear when recommendation shades into generation. It becomes harder to notice, because there is no longer a discrete moment where a person looks at a flagged item and decides whether to accept it; the flag and the acceptance can happen in the same keystroke, the same glance, the same unexamined habit of trusting whatever appears first on the page.

It is worth pausing, too, on who is actually positioned to close the gap this chapter has been describing, because the answer is not the same person in every case, and the chapters before this one have tried, throughout, not to pretend otherwise. Sullenberger's judgment on the Hudson belonged to Sullenberger, but it rested on decades of training an airline had invested in and an aviation-safety culture had built up over generations of prior accidents. The radiologist's calibration toward an AI's flag depends partly on that radiologist's own experience and partly on whether the hospital deploying the system has given her honest information about when and how it fails. The warehouse worker's ability to exercise judgment at all depends far less on that worker's individual skill than on decisions made by an employer about how much margin a quota leaves for anything other than compliance. Responsibility for building the skill this account describes does not sit neatly with either individuals or institutions alone; it is distributed, unevenly, across both, in a way that makes it easy for either side to plausibly blame the other when something goes wrong. A designer can say the operator should have known better. An operator can say the system should have been built to make the failure visible. Both statements can be true at once, and neither one, by itself, prevents the next failure.

If there is a single practical implication worth leaving with a reader whose own work rarely touches anything as dramatic as a lunar landing or an uncontained engine failure, it is that the vocabulary developed across these chapters travels. Misuse and disuse are not aviation-specific terms; they describe any relationship between a person and a system that recommends. The vigilance decrement does not require a radar screen; it describes any task that asks a person to watch for a rare exception inside a stream of mostly unremarkable output, whether that stream is airport radar, a warehouse dashboard, or a moderation queue. Trust calibration does not require an airplane or an operating room; it describes the gap between how much anyone trusts any system and how much that system has actually earned, a gap that opens in a hiring manager's shortlist and a judge's sentencing worksheet in exactly the same shape, even though only one of those two produces the kind of public record these chapters could examine directly. The expertise reversal documented among professional forecasters is a warning worth carrying into any field where genuine expertise and genuine impatience with an imperfect tool are likely to travel together — which is to say, most fields worth having expertise in at all.

Building the institutions capable of teaching and supporting that vocabulary, at the scale automated recommendation is now spreading, is a different and much larger undertaking than any individual reader deciding, tomorrow, to think more carefully about the next flagged résumé or drafted email. It requires organizations willing to fund the kind of ongoing training Bainbridge argued for in 1983 and that most institutions still treat as optional overhead rather than a structural cost of automating a task at all. It requires designers willing to break a system's automation into separable, independently examined functions rather than shipping a single black box and calling the result intelligent. It requires feedback loops honest enough to tell an operator when and how a system actually fails, resisting the much easier temptation to manufacture the feeling of transparency with an explanation that satisfies without informing. And it requires accountability structures built before a failure happens, not improvised after one, so that the person nearest the wreckage — a safety driver watching a road a system was never designed to let her watch effectively, a pilot fighting a system whose existence her training never disclosed, a warehouse worker penalized by a quota she had no way to appeal — does not absorb the consequences of decisions made, months or years earlier, by people who were never in the room when the automation finally failed.

There is also a narrower, more immediate obligation worth placing on the institutions examined most closely in these pages, distinct from the larger project of building training and design practices that keep pace with the technology: not letting accountability default, by inertia, to whoever happened to be nearest the failure when it occurred. The moral crumple zone Elish described is not an inevitable feature of joint human-machine systems; it is a pattern in how blame gets assigned after something goes wrong, and patterns in how blame gets assigned can be changed by the same kind of deliberate institutional choice that changes anything else. The Indonesian and Ethiopian investigators who traced two catastrophic accidents to a flight-control system's design, rather than settling for an account that emphasized crew performance, showed that a rigorous investigation can resist the crumple-zone pattern even under enormous public and commercial pressure to find a simpler story. That resistance has to be built into the investigative and accountability structures themselves, in advance of the next failure, because it does not happen by default, and the default, case after case in the record examined here, tends to fall on whichever human was holding the controls, the scanner, or the wheel when the automation's design finally ran out of road.

None of that is a small ask, and there is no tidy way to promise it will happen at the pace the technology itself is moving. That gap — between how fast automated recommendation is spreading and how slowly the training, design, and accountability practices meant to keep pace with it actually change — is, if anything, likely to widen before it narrows. Every case examined in this account involved a system that existed, and was already being relied upon, well before the institutions around it had fully worked out how much reliance it deserved. There is no particular reason to expect the systems that arrive next to behave differently, and every reason, given everything the preceding chapters have shown, to expect that the working out will continue to happen partly in retrospect, after something has already gone wrong badly enough to force an investigation.

That is not a reason for blanket suspicion of automated recommendation, any more than the cases gathered here are a reason for blanket faith in it. The evidence does not support either simple verdict, and a book that reached one anyway would be misrepresenting its own material in its final pages. Mammography AI measurably reduced false negatives and false positives in a large international study. Qantas 32 landed safely because a checklist system, however overwhelmed in one specific emergency, generally does what it was built to do. Waymo's driverless vehicles report a low rate of critical disengagement, even accounting for the limits of self-reported, geographically concentrated data. Automated recommendation has, across nearly every domain examined in these chapters, produced real and substantial benefits, alongside the failures documented at equal length. The honest position available to a reader closing this book is neither trust the machine nor trust yourself, but something less quotable and more durable: learn, case by case, system by system, what this particular tool has actually earned, and keep learning it, because what it has earned will keep changing as the tool itself changes.

That is a smaller promise than a book like this one is sometimes expected to make, and it is the only promise the evidence actually supports. It does not resolve the tension raised in this book's opening chapter, between the moment Sullenberger faced with almost no automated guidance to weigh and the moment awaiting most readers of this one, in which the guidance arrives instantly, confidently, and far more often than not correctly. That tension is not going away, and no chapter here, including this one, has offered a way to make it go away. What the intervening chapters offer instead is a more specific sense of what that tension actually consists of, case by case, so that the next time a recommendation appears on a screen, the question it raises feels less like a vague unease and more like something that can actually be investigated, however briefly, before a decision has to be made. The pilots, radiologists, judges, workers, traders, and drivers in these pages did not have access to a formula that told them, reliably, when to trust the system in front of them and when to override it. Neither does the reader closing this book, and neither, most likely, will the reader facing whatever the next decade's version of a flagged résumé, a routing suggestion, or a drafted reply turns out to be. What they can have, if the argument made across these chapters has done its work, is a sharper, more specific set of questions to bring to that moment than most people are currently equipped with — not a final answer about whether to trust the recommendation on the screen, but a better way of noticing that the question is being asked at all, and a clearer sense of what kind of evidence would actually settle it, this time, in this case, before it is too late to matter.
