# Designing for Appropriate Reliance

Somewhere in the middle of this book, a small experimental finding appeared and then, by design, was left mostly alone. Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons, and Cade Massey had already shown that people abandon a forecasting algorithm faster than they abandon a human forecaster after watching each one make the same mistake, even when the algorithm remains the better bet overall — the algorithm aversion examined at length several chapters back. Their follow-up study added one further twist. When participants were given even a small amount of control over the algorithm's output — the ability to adjust its forecast by a modest margin, even if they rarely used that ability, and even when their adjustments did not meaningfully improve the algorithm's accuracy — their willingness to rely on it recovered substantially. People who had just watched an algorithm err, and who had refused to trust an identical algorithm they could not touch, were willing to trust one they could.

That finding was set aside earlier as a piece of real optimism inside an otherwise uncomfortable chapter, and it deserves to be picked back up now, because it is the clearest small-scale demonstration of something the rest of this chapter is about at a much larger scale: appropriate reliance is not primarily a psychological trait to be coached into individual people. It is, to a significant degree, a property of how a system is designed — what control it gives an operator, what feedback it offers, how honestly it represents its own uncertainty, and how it fails when it does fail. This is the constructive turn the argument has been building toward since Chapter 2 first drew the line between misuse and disuse. The diagnosis has, by now, been made from a dozen different angles. What remains is the harder and less satisfying question: what can actually be built differently, and how much does building it well actually cost.

The most useful available answer comes from a paper published in 2000 by Raja Parasuraman, Thomas Sheridan, and Christopher Wickens, offering a model for the types and levels of human interaction with automation. Their argument begins by rejecting a habit of thought the preceding chapters have already shown to be misleading in case after case: the tendency to talk about "automation" as though it were a single dial, running from fully manual to fully automatic, with every system occupying one point on that line. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens proposed instead that any automated task can be broken into four distinct functions — acquiring information, analyzing that information, selecting a decision or course of action based on the analysis, and implementing the chosen action — and that each of these four functions can be automated independently of the others, at a level chosen from a graduated scale running from fully manual to fully automatic in roughly ten steps.

The value of separating the four functions becomes clear the moment it is applied to cases already examined. A flight-management computer can automate information acquisition almost completely — gathering airspeed, altitude, and navigational data far faster and more reliably than a human pilot ever could — while leaving decision and action selection largely in the pilot's hands during a genuine emergency, exactly the arrangement Sullenberger and Skiles found themselves working within over the Hudson. A mammography AI system, of the kind evaluated in the international study examined earlier, can perform information acquisition and much of information analysis at a level approaching full automation, flagging suspicious regions of a scan with real diagnostic power, while decision and action selection — whether to biopsy, whether to recall a patient, whether to treat a flagged region as decisive or as one input among several — remains, appropriately, a function radiologists retain. A risk-assessment tool like the one at the center of the Wisconsin courtroom case can automate information analysis into a single numeric score, while the Wisconsin Supreme Court's ruling in that case effectively insisted, as a matter of law, that decision and action selection — the sentence itself — could not be handed to the tool, whatever level of automation its scoring function had reached.

Seen this way, a great deal of what went wrong in the cases examined earlier can be redescribed as a mismatch between the level of automation applied to one of these four functions and the level of trust, or the level of scrutiny, that function actually deserved. MCAS, the automated trim system responsible for the two catastrophic accidents examined in the previous chapter, was granted a very high level of automation across three of the four functions at once — it acquired its own information from a single sensor, analyzed that information according to its own internal logic, and selected and implemented a corrective action, all without requiring the pilot's awareness, let alone the pilot's authorization, at any stage. A four-function model built around graduated, independently chosen levels would have flagged that concentration as a specific design choice requiring specific justification: why should information acquisition for a flight-control input with this much authority rest on a single sensor, when the analysis and action stages that follow will simply compound whatever error that single sensor introduces. The Parasuraman-Sheridan-Wickens model does not, by itself, tell a designer what the right level is for a given function in a given task. What it does is force the question to be asked function by function, rather than answered once, for an entire system, with a single global setting.

This vocabulary also gives a more precise shape to Warren VanderBurgh's prescription from earlier in the argument, distilled from his 1997 "Children of the Magenta Line" training class at American Airlines: not less automation, but appropriate stepping down, a deliberate reduction in the level of automation in use as workload and complexity increase, rather than either riding full automation past its usefulness or abandoning it reflexively at the first sign of trouble. VanderBurgh's insight, developed for a cockpit and a specific automation suite, generalizes cleanly into the Parasuraman-Sheridan-Wickens framework: stepping down is, in effect, choosing to lower the automation level of one or more of the four functions when circumstances change, rather than treating the automation setting as fixed once chosen. A pilot who steps down from a fully coupled autopilot to manual flight in deteriorating weather is not rejecting automation; she is recalibrating, function by function, which parts of the flying task she wants the machine handling and which parts she wants to be doing herself, given what the moment actually demands. That same habit, generalized well beyond aviation, is a design principle as much as a personal one: systems that make stepping down easy, visible, and low-cost — rather than systems built around a single all-or-nothing mode — give operators the structural ability to do what VanderBurgh was training pilots to do by judgment alone.

None of this design vocabulary solves the deeper problem traced since Chapter 6, which is that trust calibration is not something a well-designed interface can simply install in an operator. John Lee and Katrina See's 2004 framework, revisited here for its design implications rather than its diagnostic ones, is explicit that calibration is built through a closed loop of experience, feedback, and an operator's evolving mental model of what a system can and cannot actually do. That loop can be helped or hurt by design choices, but it cannot be shortcut by them. A system that gives an operator honest, specific feedback about the conditions under which it fails — rather than either concealing its failures or, just as damagingly, crying wolf so often with false alarms that the operator learns to tune it out, the exact disuse-inducing mechanism named all the way back in Chapter 2 — does more for appropriate reliance over time than any single interface decision could. This is a slower, less satisfying answer than a checklist, and it is the honest one: calibration is earned through accumulated, accurate experience with a system's actual behavior, not granted by a clever screen.

That distinction is exactly what makes the placebic-explanation research examined earlier such an important warning for anyone tempted to treat "explainability" as a design shortcut around the slow work Lee and See describe. Malin Eiband and colleagues found that a fabricated explanation — one that merely gestured at a reason without conveying real, substantive information about how a system had reached its recommendation — produced compliance rates nearly identical to a genuine, substantive explanation, and far above no explanation at all, at least when the effort required of the user to engage with the explanation was low. People rated the placebic and the genuinely informative explanations as equally satisfying, even though only the genuinely informative explanation measurably improved their actual understanding of how the system worked. Read against Lee and See's framework, this finding is close to alarming: it shows that the feeling of calibration — the sense that one understands and appropriately trusts a system — can be manufactured by an interface that supplies the form of an explanation without its substance, entirely independent of whether real calibration, the kind built on an accurate mental model, has occurred at all.

This is worth dwelling on, because it cuts against the most common intuitive fix offered whenever the problems examined throughout this account come up in public conversation: just make the system explain itself, and trust will follow trust's proper object. The Eiband research does not say explanation is worthless — the same body of work shows that genuine, substantive explanations, ones that require real engagement from the user rather than a glance at a plausible-sounding sentence, do improve people's actual mental models of how a system behaves. The danger is specifically the low-effort, placebo-shaped explanation, deployed in exactly the high-stakes domains the middle chapters spent examining — a courtroom where a defendant's attorney is told a risk score reflects "static and dynamic risk factors" without ever learning what those factors are or how they are weighted, a reading room where an AI flag arrives with a confidence percentage attached but no account of what in the image drove that number, a warehouse dashboard that tells a worker their rate has fallen without specifying which of a dozen possible causes triggered the alert. An explanation interface built to make people feel calibrated is a different, and in some ways more dangerous, thing than a system honestly designed to help them become calibrated.

Put together, these strands point toward a working discipline rather than a solved problem, and it is worth being explicit about what that discipline actually asks of the people and organizations that build and deploy automated systems. It asks designers to resist collapsing a task's automation into a single setting, and instead to ask, function by function, whether information acquisition, analysis, decision selection, and implementation each deserve the same level of automation or different ones — a question MCAS's designers, on the evidence of the previous chapter, do not appear to have asked with sufficient rigor about a system granted near-total authority across three of the four functions from a single sensor. It asks designers to build in the kind of low-cost, meaningful adjustability that restored trust in Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's experiment, not because adjustability is a psychological trick for manufacturing trust, but because giving an operator a genuine, if modest, channel for exercising judgment keeps that operator engaged with the system's actual behavior rather than either fully deferring to it or fully disengaging from it. It asks designers and trainers to prioritize honest, specific feedback about failure over either concealment or manufactured reassurance, understanding that real calibration is built slowly, through the kind of closed loop Lee and See describe, and cannot be substituted for by an explanation interface engineered to feel satisfying regardless of what it actually conveys. And it asks organizations to build in VanderBurgh's stepping-down habit as a structural option, not merely a personal virtue possessed by unusually disciplined operators, so that reducing reliance on automation under rising uncertainty is a normal, supported, low-friction choice rather than a departure from procedure that requires unusual nerve to make.

This chapter's design vocabulary also has to reckon honestly with the case in this account where it applies least comfortably: the warehouse worker managed by an algorithmic scanner system, whose productivity rankings and quota enforcement leave, according to the reporting examined earlier, little practical margin for the judgment that keeps a body intact. The four-function breakdown assumes an operator who retains some genuine choice about how much to rely on a system's recommendation, a choice the Parasuraman-Sheridan-Wickens model treats as available across all four functions to at least some adjustable degree. That assumption collapses when the automated system is not offering a recommendation the worker can weigh, but setting the terms of continued employment. A worker cannot meaningfully step down the level of automation governing a scanner's rate quota the way a pilot steps down from a coupled autopilot, because the scanner is not merely providing information the worker analyzes and acts on; it is measuring the worker's performance against a standard the worker has no visibility into and no authority to adjust. This is the abuse category named early on and revisited in the previous chapter's account of MCAS: automation deployed in a way that disregards its consequences for the human required to work alongside it. No amount of interface redesign at the level of the individual scanner solves a problem located in the employment relationship the scanner enforces. The honest conclusion is that appropriate-reliance design, as this chapter has described it, presupposes a degree of institutional willingness to share control that not every deployment of automated recommendation actually grants, and that presupposition itself deserves scrutiny rather than quiet acceptance.

A related limit shows up in the earlier comparison between Tesla's and Waymo's self-reported safety statistics. Lee and See's model treats calibration as something built through a closed loop of experience and feedback, but that loop depends on the feedback being trustworthy and independently interpretable, not merely available. A driver deciding how much to rely on a partially automated driving system has, in practice, almost no access to rigorously independent data about that system's actual failure modes under the specific conditions of that driver's own commute; the driver has whatever the manufacturer chooses to publish, methodological limitations and all, and whatever the driver's own limited personal experience happens to include. That is a much thinner feedback loop than the one available to a pilot flying thousands of hours in the same aircraft type across a career, or a radiologist reading thousands of scans against known outcomes over years of practice. Appropriate reliance is hardest to build precisely where the feedback an operator would need is scarcest, most asymmetric, or most controlled by the party with the greatest interest in a favorable interpretation — a design and institutional problem this chapter's vocabulary can name clearly, even where it cannot resolve it.

The expertise reversal documented in the algorithm-appreciation research examined earlier — professionals who forecast regularly relying less on algorithmic advice than laypeople did, and being measurably hurt by that reduced reliance — points toward a narrower, more tractable design response, one worth naming because it follows directly from the four-function model. If the danger for experts is not blind deference but reflexive dismissal, a well-designed decision-selection stage does not need to automate the expert's judgment away; it needs to make disengagement from the system's input costly enough to require a genuine reason. A radiologist required to note, in some structured way, why a flagged region is being dismissed — not as bureaucratic box-checking, but as a deliberate friction against the kind of instant, unexamined override the expertise-reversal research documents — keeps the decision authority exactly where it belongs, with the trained professional, while making it harder for that professional's own confidence to silently discard information that would have improved the outcome. This is a small design move, not a solution to expert overconfidence in general, but it follows the same logic as the modification-restores-trust finding from earlier in the book: a channel for engaging with the system's output, even a modest one, keeps the human meaningfully in the loop rather than either fully deferring or fully checking out.

None of this design work is free, and the clearest evidence for that claim anywhere in this account is the neuroscience examined in the chapter on deskilling. Eleanor Maguire's finding that London taxi drivers who actively navigate develop measurably more posterior-hippocampal gray matter than bus drivers who follow fixed routes — a difference isolating active navigational practice, not driving itself, as the likely cause — gives Bainbridge's 1983 argument about training an unusually concrete biological anchor. Bainbridge's conclusion, that operators need more training as automation increases rather than less, is often the hardest part of her argument for organizations to actually fund, because automation is frequently adopted specifically to reduce the labor and training costs associated with a task. Maguire's result suggests that the underlying skill a stepped-down operator falls back on in a crisis is not a fixed reserve that simply waits, undiminished, for the rare moment it is needed. It is more plausibly a capacity that erodes, at least in domains resembling active spatial navigation, when it goes unpracticed for long stretches — which means VanderBurgh's stepping-down habit and Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens's graduated automation model are not merely interface choices. They are commitments to ongoing practice time, budgeted and protected, for exactly the skills a well-functioning automated system is otherwise designed to make unnecessary on any given day. An organization that automates a task fully and then declines to fund the practice time needed to keep the underlying human skill intact has built a system that looks well-designed on paper and degrades quietly in exactly the way Bainbridge predicted more than forty years ago.

It is worth being honest, too, about the limits of this chapter's optimism, and about how easily a design vocabulary this tidy can be mistaken for a finished solution rather than a starting point for the harder, slower work of actually building and maintaining it inside a real organization. Human-factors researchers who study appropriate reliance do not describe it as a problem that gets solved once, by the right interface or the right training program, after which the underlying tension goes away. They describe it as an ongoing discipline — closer to the way an organization maintains a safety culture than the way it fixes a software bug — because the systems being relied upon keep changing, the tasks they are applied to keep expanding, and each new deployment reopens the same basic question raised in this book's opening pages: does this system, in this moment, deserve to be trusted, and how would anyone actually know. A four-function design vocabulary and a well-understood theory of trust calibration make that question easier to ask precisely. They do not make it easier to stop asking.

One more limit deserves naming before this chapter turns to its conclusion, and it comes from the finance chapter examined earlier. The Flash Crash and the Knight Capital malfunction both involved automated systems executing at a speed that structurally foreclosed the possibility of a timely human second guess — trades and erroneous orders flowing out faster than any person watching a screen could interrupt them, human oversight able to operate only after the damage was already done. The four-function model assumes that decision and action selection can be meaningfully retained by a human, or at least meaningfully shared, but that assumption depends on the task unfolding on a timescale a human nervous system can operate within. Where a system's action-implementation function runs in milliseconds, stepping down the automation level of the decision-selection function accomplishes very little, because there is no interval left in which a human decision, however well calibrated, could actually be inserted before the outcome is already determined. In cases like these, appropriate reliance has to be designed in further upstream — in circuit breakers, position limits, and pre-deployment testing regimes that constrain what the automated system is permitted to do at speed, rather than in any interface meant to keep a human meaningfully engaged during execution itself, because during execution itself there is no longer a meaningful window for a human to be engaged in at all.

There is a specific and instructive contrast worth drawing between the chapter just concluded and the one before it, because together they illustrate what appropriate design can and cannot guarantee. Qantas 32's crew succeeded in part because the aircraft's automated systems, however overwhelmed by contradictory information in that specific emergency, had not been designed to seize authority over the aircraft's controls without the crew's knowledge; the ECAM system flooded the crew with warnings, but it did not fight them for control of the airplane the way MCAS did. That difference in design — automation that informs and recommends versus automation that acts with independent authority on a single point of failure — did not by itself guarantee a good outcome in either case; de Crespigny's crew still had to do enormous, well-prepared work to land safely, and a system designed only to inform can still overwhelm a crew if it floods them with too much contradictory information at once, as it very nearly did. But it illustrates the stakes of the functional breakdown this chapter has argued for. Where a system's information-acquisition and analysis functions are highly automated but its decision and implementation functions remain meaningfully shared with a trained human, that human retains the structural ability to exercise the kind of judgment the clearest successes examined earlier depended on. Where all four functions collapse into a single, high-authority automatic process, as MCAS's design allowed them to, the human is left with very little room to intervene at all, however well trained or well prepared that human happens to be.

None of the sources of design guidance gathered in this chapter — graduated, function-specific automation; adjustability that keeps an operator meaningfully engaged; honest and specific failure feedback over either concealment or manufactured reassurance; a structural, supported habit of stepping down as uncertainty rises; protected practice time for the skills automation would otherwise let atrophy; and, where a system's speed forecloses human intervention entirely, constraints designed in upstream of execution rather than during it — is new to the reader by this point. Each has appeared already, in a different form, attached to a different case. What this chapter has tried to do is not introduce a new idea, but show that these lessons, taken together, describe an actual design practice rather than a handful of unrelated observations about different industries. That practice does not promise that the next MCAS will be caught before it kills anyone, or that the next radiologist will always know when to trust an AI's flag and when to look past it. It promises only that the systems built this way give the human working alongside them a real chance to exercise the kind of judgment the chapters before this one have tried to describe honestly — no more certain than that, and, given everything examined so far, no less than that either.
