# The Ironies of Automation

Six years before Parasuraman and Riley separated misuse from disuse, a British researcher named Lisanne Bainbridge published a paper that explains why both failures happen at all. It appeared in 1983, in a journal called Automatica, in the sixth issue of that year's nineteenth volume. It ran five pages. It was called "Ironies of Automation," and it has since become one of the most cited papers in the history of human factors research — cited roughly 1,800 times by November of 2016 alone, by one count, and cited steadily more in the years since, as the subject it addressed moved from factory control rooms into nearly every corner of modern work.

Bainbridge was not writing about airplanes, or hospitals, or courtrooms. She was writing about process control: the operators who sit in front of banks of gauges and dials in chemical plants and power stations, watching systems designed to run themselves under normal conditions and to alert a human being only when something goes wrong. It is not, on its face, a dramatic subject. There is no crash in Bainbridge's paper, no named accident, no body count. What there is instead is an argument, laid out with unusual clarity, about a trap built into the very idea of automating a task because a human being cannot be trusted to do it perfectly.

Five pages is a strange length for a paper to be cited roughly 1,800 times, and rising, by researchers working across aviation, medicine, nuclear power, software design, and eventually the algorithmic systems this book spends most of its attention on. Papers that influential are usually longer, denser, built around a large dataset or a novel experimental result that other researchers can build directly on top of. Bainbridge's paper offered neither. It offered an argument short and clear enough to be restated in a sentence, and precise enough that once a reader had absorbed it, they began noticing its shape everywhere — which is itself a kind of evidence for how correctly it had identified something real, rather than something specific to the chemical plants and power stations she happened to be describing.

The trap has two parts, and Bainbridge called them ironies because each one undoes, in practice, the reasoning that motivated it in theory. An irony, in the ordinary sense of the word, is an outcome that mocks the intention behind it — a fire station that burns down, a lock that keeps its owner out. Bainbridge's ironies work the same way. Automation is introduced to make a system more reliable by reducing its exposure to human error. Followed far enough, both of the mechanisms she identified quietly work against that same goal.

The first irony concerns why a task gets automated in the first place. Engineers automate a process, in the ordinary case, because they have concluded that a human operator performing that process manually is not reliable enough — too slow, too inconsistent, too prone to lapses of attention or memory, especially across the long, monotonous stretches where nothing interesting happens and a person's mind is free to wander. The automated system takes over the routine handling of the task precisely because routine handling is where human beings are weakest: the repetitive, the well-understood, the parts of the job that do not demand judgment so much as consistency. That reasoning is not wrong. Machines really are better than people at holding a steady value, executing a fixed procedure the same way every time, and doing so for hours without fatigue.

But automating the routine cases does not automate all of the cases. It cannot, because automation is built by anticipating situations in advance, encoding responses to conditions the designers foresaw and prepared for. Some situations cannot be anticipated, or cannot be automated even when foreseen, because they require exactly the kind of flexible, contextual judgment that machines remain bad at and humans remain comparatively good at. Those situations do not disappear simply because the routine ones have been handed to a machine. They are still going to happen, occasionally, unpredictably, and when they do, somebody has to handle them. That somebody is still the human operator — the same operator the system was built around because designers judged human reliability on routine tasks insufficient. The irony is that the tasks left for the human being are, by construction, the rarest, least practiced, and hardest cases: precisely the ones the original judgment of unreliability would predict a person is least equipped to handle well. The system was built to compensate for human limitations on the easy parts of the job, and in doing so it concentrated the hard parts, the ones those very limitations matter most for, onto the same limited human being.

The second irony follows from the first, and it is, if anything, more corrosive, because it operates slowly and invisibly rather than announcing itself in a single design decision. The first irony is a fact about a moment: a rare case arrives, and someone has to handle it. The second irony is a fact about a process unfolding across months and years, one that has already been quietly at work by the time that moment arrives, shaping how prepared the person handling it turns out to be. An operator who no longer performs a task manually, because a machine now performs it, does not remain as skilled at that task as an operator who continues to perform it by hand. Skill of the kind Bainbridge was describing — the fluent, embodied competence that lets a person recognize a developing problem quickly, diagnose it correctly, and respond with the right action under time pressure — is built and maintained through practice. Practice is exactly what automation removes. An operator watching a system run itself is not practicing the underlying task; they are practicing something else entirely, a different and in some ways harder skill: monitoring. And monitoring a system that is usually correct does not build or maintain the specific competence needed to intervene correctly on the rare occasion the system is wrong. The result is a widening gap between what a situation demands and what the operator, out of practice on precisely this task, is actually equipped to provide, opening exactly when the gap matters most: at the moment of the rare, high-stakes intervention the first irony has already concentrated onto that same operator.

Put the two ironies together and the shape of the trap becomes clear. Automating a task because humans are not reliable enough at it removes the very practice that would keep a human's judgment sharp for the moments the automation cannot cover — and it is precisely those moments, the ones automation cannot cover, that get left to the human being to handle. The system does not eliminate the need for skilled human judgment. It relocates that need to the hardest possible cases and simultaneously erodes the operator's capacity to meet it.

Bainbridge did not present this as an argument for abandoning automation, and the book will not present it that way either. Automation earns its place for good reasons: it genuinely does outperform tired, distracted, or inconsistent human operators on the routine tasks it takes over, and abandoning it wholesale in favor of manual operation would trade a set of manageable, well-understood risks for a set of worse ones. Her conclusion ran in the opposite direction from "automate less." It ran toward "train more" — specifically, that operators working alongside automation need more deliberate training, not less, precisely because automation has quietly withdrawn the ordinary, on-the-job practice that used to build and maintain their skill without anyone having to plan for it separately. When a task was performed by hand every day, the skill needed to perform it well took care of itself, as an incidental byproduct of doing the job. Once a machine performs the task instead, the skill has to be maintained deliberately, through training designed for that purpose, because the job itself no longer builds it as a matter of course. Bainbridge's argument was, in effect, that automation creates a training obligation it does not fulfill on its own, and that organizations which fail to notice this obligation get the worst of both worlds: a system that handles the routine cases well, and an operator who is steadily less prepared for the cases it does not.

That obligation is easy to state and hard to fund. Training that maintains a skill nobody currently needs, for a situation that may not arise for years, competes for money and attention against training that addresses a problem an organization is dealing with right now. A plant running smoothly under automated control has, by definition, no urgent daily evidence that its supervisors' manual skills are eroding, because the automated system is doing the job those skills used to require. The cost of under-investing in that training does not show up on a balance sheet the week it is skipped. It shows up, if it shows up at all, on the one day years later when the rare deviation arrives and the supervisor on duty is less prepared for it than a supervisor trained under the old regime would have been — a cost that is real, and can be severe, but that arrives late enough and rarely enough that it is easy for an organization to keep deferring the investment that would have prevented it. Bainbridge's prescription asked organizations to spend, continuously, against a risk that mostly does not materialize, in order to be ready for the rare occasion it does. That is a difficult thing to ask of any institution, and this book will show, repeatedly, how often the asking went unanswered.

It helps to see this working at a small scale before turning, in later chapters, to the scale of a wide-body jet or a hospital reading room. Picture a supervisor in a control room, watching a wall of gauges that track temperature, pressure, and flow across some industrial process — a plant that runs, for the overwhelming majority of any given shift, exactly as designed. The system holds its own values, corrects its own minor deviations, and asks nothing of the supervisor most of the time beyond a kind of ambient attention. For hours, there is nothing to do but watch dials that do not move in any way that requires action. Then, at some unpredictable moment, one of them does — a value drifts outside its normal band, or several values shift together in a pattern the automated controls were not built to recognize and correct on their own — and the supervisor, who has spent the shift watching rather than doing, has to recognize what is happening, diagnose its cause, and respond, often quickly, often with the plant's actual physical safety at stake.

Everything about that supervisor's situation illustrates both ironies at once. The task was automated because a human being manually adjusting temperature and pressure and flow, continuously, for an entire shift, would eventually make an error a well-tuned automatic controller would not. That reasoning holds up fine for the ordinary, anticipated fluctuations the controller was designed to correct. It does not extend to the unanticipated deviation, the pattern the designers did not foresee or could not economically build a response for, and that unanticipated deviation is exactly what lands on the supervisor's desk, because there is nowhere else for it to land. Meanwhile, the supervisor's actual hands-on experience adjusting the plant manually has been shrinking for as long as the automated controller has been doing that job instead — not because the supervisor has grown careless, but because the job itself no longer calls for that hands-on skill under normal conditions, and skill exercised only rarely does not stay as sharp as skill exercised daily. When the unusual deviation finally arrives, it arrives for a person whose most relevant practical experience is, structurally, older and thinner than it would have been if the plant had never been automated at all.

Consider how this plays out across a career rather than a single shift. A supervisor hired into a plant already running on automated controls may never build the manual skill base that an older colleague, hired before the controls existed, acquired simply by doing the job the hard way for years before automation arrived. That older colleague's competence at handling deviations was not a special talent; it was the ordinary residue of having once had no choice but to run the process by hand. The newer supervisor inherits none of that residue automatically. They inherit training programs, procedures, and whatever deliberate instruction the organization has chosen to provide in its place — and Bainbridge's argument is precisely that organizations tend not to provide enough of it, because the need is easy to underestimate when the automated system is, on any given day, working exactly as intended. A skill nobody has needed in months does not announce its own decay. It simply is not there, quietly, until the day it is needed and is not sufficient.

None of this requires the supervisor to have done anything wrong. It requires only the ordinary passage of time inside a system built exactly as designed. That is what makes Bainbridge's argument an irony rather than simply a criticism: the trap is not a flaw introduced by careless engineering or inattentive operators. It is a consequence of automation doing precisely what it was built to do, competently and as intended, for long enough that the human being kept in the loop for the hard cases gradually becomes less equipped to handle them.

It is worth pausing here to notice what Bainbridge's argument does and does not say about Flight 1549. Sullenberger and Skiles were not victims of either irony that afternoon, and it is important to see why not, because the exception clarifies the rule. Their emergency did not arise from an automated system quietly handling the routine cases while eroding their readiness for the rare one; it arose from a bird strike, an event no onboard automation was managing or mismanaging in the first place, and the decision that followed — where to put the airplane down — was never a task that had been automated away from pilots to begin with. There was no skill to have lost, because deciding among a return, a diversion, and a ditching had never been handed to a machine for pilots to stop practicing. Sullenberger and Skiles were, in that specific narrow sense, still fully in possession of a skill Bainbridge's argument predicts erodes wherever automation takes it over. That is not an accident of this book's choice of opening scene; it is close to the reason the scene works as an opening at all. It shows what unautomated judgment under pressure looks like, in full, before the book turns to the more common situation where that judgment has had something to compete with, and, over time, something eroding it.

The same logic sharpens the two failure modes Parasuraman and Riley named. An operator prone to misuse — extending an automated system more trust than it has earned — is often, on Bainbridge's account, an operator who no longer has the independent skill base needed to recognize when the system's output does not make sense, and so defers by default because deferring is what remains available to them. An operator prone to disuse — abandoning a system that deserved more trust than it received — is sometimes an operator whose eroded skill has left them unable to distinguish a genuine system failure from an unfamiliar but correct output, and who responds to that uncertainty by falling back on their own, possibly also degraded, judgment instead. Bainbridge's ironies do not just coexist with the misuse-disuse distinction from the previous chapter; they help explain why an ordinary, competent operator ends up on one side of it or the other in the first place, rather than landing, as the tidy picture would have it, on a well-calibrated middle ground.

It would be easy to read a paper about 1983 process control and conclude it describes a narrow, dated problem — gauges and dials, an industry that has since modernized, a form of work most readers will never do. That reading misses why the paper has been cited with increasing frequency for four decades rather than fading into a historical footnote. The specific technology in Bainbridge's paper is almost incidental to the argument. What she actually described is a structural relationship between any automated system that handles the routine and any human being retained to handle what the system cannot — a relationship that holds regardless of whether the routine task in question is adjusting a valve, holding an aircraft's altitude, flagging a suspicious pattern on a mammogram, or keeping a car in its lane on a highway. Wherever a system takes over the common cases and leaves a person responsible for the uncommon ones, both of Bainbridge's ironies begin operating, quietly, whether or not anyone involved has read the paper that first named them.

That is the sense in which this short, unglamorous paper about factory control rooms functions as the argument underneath nearly everything else in this book. Every domain visited in the chapters ahead — cockpits, reading rooms, courtrooms, warehouse floors, trading desks, the front seat of a car — involves some version of the same structure: a system built because human performance on the routine version of a task was judged insufficient, and a human being left responsible for the task's hardest, least practiced, most consequential edge cases. Bainbridge's two ironies predict, in the most general terms available, both of the failure modes Parasuraman and Riley would later give names to. Misuse becomes more likely whenever an operator, faced with one of automation's rare failures, has been lulled by long stretches of correct performance into extending it more confidence than the moment deserves. Disuse becomes more likely whenever an operator, no longer skilled enough at the underlying task to trust their own judgment against the system's, either defers past the point of good sense or abandons the system erratically because they can no longer tell, from a position of eroded expertise, when it deserves to be trusted and when it does not.

The domains differ enormously in what the automated system actually does and in what is at stake when it fails, and this book takes those differences seriously rather than flattening them into a single interchangeable story. A flight-management computer holding an aircraft's altitude and a diagnostic tool flagging a mammogram do not fail in the same way, do not put the same kind of harm at risk, and do not leave the human being working alongside them with the same kind of residual task. But in every one of these settings, the underlying architecture Bainbridge described repeats: routine handled by machine, exception handled by a person whose readiness for that exception the machine's own success has been quietly working against. A reader who keeps that architecture in view will recognize it again and again in the chapters ahead, sometimes in a setting where naming it explicitly would feel almost too obvious to bother with, and sometimes in a setting complicated enough — a courtroom, a warehouse floor, a trading desk moving faster than any person can watch — that the architecture is present but harder to see without having encountered it first, here, in its plainest form.

There is also a subtler version of the second irony worth naming before moving on, because it will recur in a more troubling shape later in the book. Bainbridge's argument assumes an operator who once could perform the task manually and is gradually losing that ability through disuse. A newer and, in some ways, harder problem arises when an operator enters a role after automation has already taken over the routine cases, and so never builds the manual skill in the first place — not eroding a competence once held, but never acquiring it at all. That operator has no baseline to fall back on when the rare case arrives, no store of hands-on experience degraded by neglect, because there was never a store to begin with. Later chapters, particularly the ones set in aviation's more automated modern cockpits, return to this distinction directly, because it changes what "more training" actually has to accomplish: not refreshing a skill that practice once built, but building a skill that ordinary practice, under current conditions, no longer builds on its own.

Bainbridge's own answer to the trap was neither pure resignation nor a call to strip automation back out of the systems it had already improved. It was a demand: that organizations deploying automation take seriously, as an ongoing and deliberate obligation, the training needed to keep human operators genuinely capable of the rare interventions automation itself creates. That demand has been available, in print, citable, well known within the field that studies these problems, since 1983. Whether the industries this book visits actually met it — whether the training obligation Bainbridge identified was taken up, ignored, underfunded, or rediscovered too late, after a cost had already been paid for not meeting it — is a question this book keeps returning to, because the honest answer varies enormously by industry, by decade, and by how expensive the lesson had to become before anyone acted on it.

It is worth being honest, too, about what Bainbridge's paper does not settle. Naming a structural trap is not the same as closing it, and the four decades since 1983 have not produced a general solution so much as a long, uneven record of specific organizations noticing the problem, in their own domain, usually after it had already cost something. Some of that record is genuinely encouraging: industries that took the training obligation seriously, built deliberate practice back into jobs that automation had hollowed out, and measurably reduced the rare-case failures Bainbridge's argument predicts. Some of it is not: industries that treated automation's smooth, ordinary performance as evidence the underlying human skill no longer mattered, right up until a moment proved otherwise. The chapters ahead sit inside that uneven record rather than above it, and one of this book's quieter purposes is to let the reader see, case by case, which kind of institution each one turned out to be.

One of those cases arrives in the very next chapter, in commercial aviation, where an American Airlines training captain would spend a year in the mid-1990s cataloguing exactly the failure Bainbridge had described in the abstract fourteen years earlier — not as a coincidence, and not as a debt anyone in the industry openly acknowledged owing to a five-page paper on process control, but as independent confirmation, arrived at through accident investigation rather than journal publication, that the trap Bainbridge named was already closing around pilots trained to manage increasingly capable flight-deck automation. What that captain found, and what the industry did and did not do about it, is where the abstract argument of this chapter starts to acquire a body count.
