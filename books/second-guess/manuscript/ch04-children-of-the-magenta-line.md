# Children of the Magenta Line

In the mid-1990s, American Airlines noticed a pattern it did not like in its own accident and incident records, and it did what a serious airline does when it notices something like that: it assigned someone to find out why. The task fell to Captain Warren VanderBurgh, and across 1996 and into 1997 his team worked through the airline's history of accidents, incidents, and violations looking for a common thread running underneath the individual explanations each event had been given at the time.

Individual incident reports, read one at a time, rarely announce a pattern on their own. A hard landing gets attributed to gusty crosswinds. A course deviation gets attributed to a miscommunication with air traffic control. A violation gets attributed to a specific procedural lapse on a specific flight. Each explanation can be accurate as far as it goes, and each one, taken alone, looks like an isolated event rather than an instance of something recurring. What VanderBurgh's team did was the less obvious and more valuable work: setting a large number of these individually explained events beside one another and asking whether the same underlying condition kept showing up beneath the surface explanations, however differently each individual report had characterized its own cause.

They found one. Roughly 68 percent of the events VanderBurgh's team reviewed were attributable to what the investigation called automation mismanagement — not equipment failure, not a single dramatic error, but pilots misusing, misunderstanding, or losing track of what the airplane's own automated systems were doing. That figure is worth sitting with. It means that more than two-thirds of the incidents severe enough to land in an internal safety review at one of the country's major carriers were, at bottom, not failures of flying in the traditional sense. They were failures of managing a relationship between a pilot and an increasingly capable machine.

By the mid-1990s, that relationship had grown considerably more complex than it had been even a decade earlier. Commercial cockpits had absorbed flight-management computers capable of handling navigation, altitude, speed, and much of the routine work of flying a modern jet from shortly after takeoff to shortly before landing, with a level of precision and consistency no human pilot, however skilled, could match by hand for hour after hour. Airlines had good reasons to build and buy that capability, and pilots had good reasons to rely on it: fuel efficiency, consistency, reduced workload on long flights, fewer of the ordinary small errors that come from a human being managing dozens of variables continuously across a multi-hour flight. None of VanderBurgh's findings amounted to an argument that any of this had been a mistake. What his team's review found was something narrower and, for that reason, more useful: that the pilots managing this new capability were not always managing it well, and that the specific ways they were managing it poorly were common enough, and similar enough across incidents, to describe as a pattern rather than a collection of unrelated errors.

VanderBurgh turned the finding into a training class, delivered in April 1997 at the American Airlines Training Academy in Dallas. He called it "Children of the Magenta Line," a title drawn from the magenta-colored path that a flight-management computer draws across a cockpit navigation display to show the airplane's programmed course — the line a pilot can follow with extraordinary precision simply by letting the automation fly it, without ever needing to look up, out the window, at the actual sky the airplane is moving through. The image is a good one, and part of why the phrase spread as widely as it did: a line on a screen that a pilot can track with total fidelity while progressively losing the feel, built through years of hand-flying, for what the airplane is actually doing underneath that precise, computer-generated path. Following the line well enough, for long enough, can start to substitute for understanding the flight the line represents.

The class was aimed at American's own pilots, but it did not stay contained to one airline. It circulated widely, inside and beyond American, and the phrase it coined outlived the specific training session that introduced it. "Children of the magenta" became a piece of working vocabulary across the industry, shorthand for a particular and by-then recognizable kind of pilot: someone who could manage a flight-management computer with real skill and fluency, programming routes, monitoring modes, catching the system's own errors — and who had, in acquiring that fluency, lost confidence or lost proficiency in the older, more basic skill of hand-flying the airplane without the computer's help. It is a memorable phrase precisely because it does not describe carelessness. The pilots VanderBurgh was describing were not inattentive or poorly trained in any ordinary sense; many of them were excellent at the specific, newer skill their jobs now emphasized. The problem was that the newer skill had, in practice, been allowed to substitute for an older one rather than supplement it, and nothing in the ordinary rhythm of flying a well-functioning, highly automated airplane forced that substitution into anyone's attention before it mattered.

It is worth being precise about what VanderBurgh's finding was and was not claiming. It was not a claim that automation itself made American's airplanes less safe, and VanderBurgh's prescription, when it came, was not "use less automation." His actual conclusion was more specific and, in its way, harder to implement than a simple call for less automation would have been: pilots needed to practice deliberately stepping the level of automation down — moving from full automatic control toward more manual modes — as workload, complexity, or uncertainty increased, rather than either riding full automation past the point where it remained the right tool for the situation, or abandoning it reflexively out of anxiety at the first sign of trouble. Automation, on this account, was not the enemy. The failure was a kind of rigidity: pilots who had stopped treating the level of automation in use as a dial to be actively managed according to the demands of the moment, and had started treating it, instead, as a fixed condition of the flight, left on or taken off as an all-or-nothing choice rather than adjusted with judgment.

"Stepping down" is a modest-sounding phrase for a demanding habit of mind. It asks a pilot, in the middle of an otherwise smooth flight, to notice rising workload or approaching complexity — weather building ahead, a busy arrival into an unfamiliar airport, a system beginning to behave in a way that is not quite what was expected — and to respond by deliberately handing more of the flying back to themselves, before the situation forces the issue, rather than waiting to be forced. That is a different kind of discipline than either of the two easier defaults on either side of it. Riding full automation until something goes wrong is easier, in the moment, than actively managing the level of assistance in use; so is disengaging automation reflexively at the first hint of trouble, without a clear judgment about whether hands-on flying is actually the better response to the specific complexity at hand. Stepping down asks for continuous, situational judgment instead of either default, which is precisely why VanderBurgh built an entire training class around it rather than trusting it to emerge on its own from pilots who already understood, in the abstract, that manual skill mattered.

This is, in other words, close kin to Bainbridge's argument from the previous chapter, arriving independently, through accident investigation rather than academic research, at a description of the same underlying trap. VanderBurgh was describing pilots who had let a flight-management computer absorb the routine handling of a flight — the navigation, the altitude holds, the smooth, precise course-following that used to require continuous manual attention — and who had, in the process, lost some of the readiness that the rare, non-routine moment would demand of them. The 68 percent figure is close to a direct measurement of Bainbridge's second irony operating inside a specific, real airline's safety record: not a hypothesis about what erosion of manual skill might eventually cause, but a retrospective count of how often it already had.

VanderBurgh's training class became well known enough, and useful enough, that its core idea survived well past 1997 as a durable piece of the industry's own self-understanding. That survival is itself worth noting, because it means the argument this book has been building — that automation quietly erodes the skill needed for its own exceptions, and that organizations chronically under-invest in the training that would counteract this — was not a secret in commercial aviation. It had a name. It had a widely viewed training video. It had a specific, internally generated statistic behind it. Pilots across multiple airlines encountered the phrase "children of the magenta" and understood, at least in the abstract, what kind of failure it described.

Twelve years after VanderBurgh's class, that failure arrived in its most consequential documented form.

On June 1, 2009, Air France Flight 447 was cruising at altitude over the Atlantic, en route from Rio de Janeiro to Paris, when it flew into a thunderstorm. Cruise altitude is, ordinarily, the quietest and most heavily automated phase of a long flight — the portion where a flight-management computer holds altitude, speed, and course with a steadiness no crew would attempt to match by continuous hand-flying across an ocean crossing, and where a crew's task is closer to monitoring than to active control. It is also, on Bainbridge's account from the previous chapter, exactly the phase of flight where manual proficiency has the least opportunity to be exercised and the most opportunity to quietly erode.

Inside the storm, a pitot tube — one of the small external sensors an aircraft uses to measure its speed through the air — began icing over. Ice on a pitot tube does not merely reduce its accuracy; it can cause it to report airspeed readings that are simply wrong, sometimes wildly so, and Flight 447's icing produced exactly that kind of unreliable data. The aircraft's autopilot, which depends on trustworthy airspeed information to do its job, responded to the corrupted readings the only way it safely could: it disconnected, handing control of the airplane back to the pilots, without warning, in the middle of a storm, at cruise altitude, with the instruments in front of them now displaying information that could no longer be fully trusted.

The Bureau d'Enquêtes et d'Analyses, France's air accident investigation authority, examined the event in detail and published its findings. Its report described the pilots as startled — a word chosen carefully, in an official investigative document, to characterize the immediate psychological state of a flight crew confronted, with no advance notice, by the sudden loss of the automation that had been managing the airplane's most basic parameters seconds earlier. That word matters more than it might first appear to. A checklist response and a startle response are not the same thing; a checklist assumes a crew with the composure to work through a sequence of steps in order, while a genuine startle response can, at least momentarily, disrupt exactly that kind of orderly processing, at the precise moment orderly processing is most needed.

The BEA's findings went further than describing the moment of surprise: the crew, the report found, struggled to reconcile what the automation-era flight displays in front of them were showing with the kind of raw, fundamental information a pilot trained in an earlier, less automated era would have relied on directly — airspeed, attitude, the basic physical sense of what the airplane was actually doing, apart from what any display was reporting about it. An earlier generation of pilots, trained on aircraft with far less automated assistance, built and maintained a continuous, hands-on feel for these fundamentals simply by flying regularly without much automated help to lean on. A crew operating a highly automated aircraft in cruise, where the automation handles these fundamentals for the overwhelming majority of every flight, has structurally less occasion to build or refresh that same feel — not through any individual failing, but through the ordinary operation of the aircraft they fly, exactly as Bainbridge's argument and VanderBurgh's finding both would have predicted. That struggle proved impossible to resolve in the time available. The crew was unable to recover the aircraft.

The BEA's final report issued twenty-five recommendations in response to what its investigation had found. Prominent among them was a call for more manual-flying training at high altitude — training in the exact skill VanderBurgh had identified as eroding twelve years earlier, in the exact circumstance, a sudden and unannounced loss of automated assistance at altitude, that both his 1997 statistic and Bainbridge's 1983 argument would have predicted was becoming harder for pilots to handle well the longer that skill went unpracticed.

Twenty-five recommendations is a substantial response, and it reflects an investigation that looked well beyond any single pilot's actions in the cockpit that night. Accident investigations of this kind rarely locate a failure in one place; a final report that runs to two dozen distinct recommendations is describing a system with multiple points where a different design choice, a different training requirement, or a different piece of information might have changed the outcome. The specific recommendation for more manual-flying training at altitude stands out for this book's purposes not because it was the only finding that mattered, but because it named, independently and years after VanderBurgh's own investigation, the same underlying gap: a skill that modern, highly automated flying no longer builds through ordinary practice, and that has to be built some other way if it is going to be available when automation itself withdraws without notice.

It would be a serious overstatement, and not one this book is prepared to make, to say that VanderBurgh's training class predicted Air France 447, or that anyone who watched "Children of the Magenta Line" in 1997 could have named, in advance, the specific flight that would someday illustrate its argument in the worst possible way. That is not what happened, and treating it as though it were would turn a serious diagnostic finding into something closer to prophecy, flattering the industry's foresight in a way the record does not support. What can be said, and what the record does support, is something more modest and, in its own way, more damning: the mechanism that killed everyone aboard Air France 447 was not a mystery to the industry that operated the airplane. It had a name, a statistic behind it, and a training class built specifically to address it, more than a decade before the storm over the Atlantic. The industry did not fail to see the failure mode coming in some abstract, unknowable sense. It had already seen a version of it, diagnosed it, given it a memorable label, and circulated that label widely enough that the phrase itself became common currency among working pilots. What it had not done, at least not thoroughly enough, was close the gap VanderBurgh had identified — the gap between managing a flight-management computer fluently and retaining the confidence and proficiency to fly without it when the computer's help disappeared without notice.

This is worth stating plainly, because the alternative — treating VanderBurgh's video as a piece of accidental foresight, a lucky guess later vindicated by tragedy — would let the actual lesson slip away. VanderBurgh was not guessing. He was reporting the result of a systematic review of his own airline's incident history, a review that found a specific, measurable pattern and gave it a name precisely so that the pattern could be recognized and addressed before it produced a worse outcome than the incidents already on file. The gap between that diagnosis and Air France 447 is not a gap in knowledge. It is a gap between diagnosis and correction — between an industry understanding a mechanism well enough to name it and an industry closing the training gap that mechanism depends on, at the pace and with the resources that closing it would actually require.

There is a version of this story that would be more comfortable to tell and less honest. It would credit VanderBurgh's training with having improved matters broadly across the industry in the years after 1997, connecting the dots forward from his diagnosis to some measurable set of later safety improvements, and closing the chapter on a note of quiet institutional competence. That version would overreach the evidence. What can be responsibly claimed is narrower: that the mechanism VanderBurgh described was real, was documented inside one major airline's own safety data, was given a name and a training class that spread through the industry's shared vocabulary, and was, twelve years later, present in the specific failure pattern the BEA's investigators found at the center of a fatal accident. The connection between the 1997 diagnosis and the 2009 crash is diagnostic, not causal — VanderBurgh's class did not cause the accident, and its absence from any specific cockpit that day is not established by anything in this record. What the connection shows is that the industry already possessed, well before 2009, the conceptual tools to understand why a highly automated crew might struggle precisely when automation disappeared without warning. Whether those tools were applied broadly and consistently enough, across every airline and every training department operating aircraft as sophisticated as an Airbus A330, is a separate question, and the honest answer is that the record is decidedly mixed.

That unevenness is, in its way, the real subject of this chapter, more than either individual event is on its own. A well-documented, clearly named failure mode — one with a memorable phrase attached to it, an internal statistic behind it, and a training program built specifically to counteract it — still recurred, more than a decade later, in one of the deadliest and most closely studied accidents in modern commercial aviation. That recurrence is not evidence that VanderBurgh was wrong, or that the diagnosis was somehow incomplete. If anything, it is close to the opposite: it suggests that naming a mechanism correctly, even naming it vividly enough that the name itself spreads through an entire industry's working vocabulary, is not the same as ensuring the training, practice, and institutional follow-through required to actually counteract it. Bainbridge's paper made the same point in the abstract, six years before VanderBurgh's investigation gave it a body of real incident data to stand on: naming the trap is not the same as escaping it.

It is worth asking, honestly, why not — why a diagnosis this clear, this well-circulated, backed by an airline's own internal data and reinforced independently by a foreign accident investigation authority's twenty-five recommendations, would still leave a gap wide enough for a crew to fall into more than a decade later. Part of the answer is almost certainly the same one this book raised at the end of the previous chapter: training against a rare event competes, year after year, against the ordinary and much more visible demands of running an airline, and a skill that is not currently failing does not generate the same institutional urgency as one that visibly is. Part of the answer may be that "children of the magenta" described a tendency rather than a mandate, a cultural observation rather than a specific, auditable training requirement with teeth — a phrase pilots could nod along to in a training room without it necessarily translating into hours of deliberate manual practice at cruise altitude, in weather, with unreliable instruments, the specific combination Air France 447 would eventually present. And part of the answer is simply that the industry VanderBurgh was diagnosing was not one airline. His review covered American's own incident history; the pilots aboard Flight 447 were trained under a different airline's programs, in a different regulatory environment, on different equipment. A finding as vivid as VanderBurgh's can travel a long way as a piece of shared vocabulary without traveling nearly as far as a specific, funded, mandatory training requirement.

That gap — between understanding a failure mode and reliably preventing its recurrence — is the question this book keeps circling back to, in domain after domain, because it turns out to be far more common, and far more stubborn, than any single industry's particular history would suggest on its own. Aviation had, by any reasonable standard, done real work by 2009: a systematic internal investigation, a widely circulated training class, a memorable name for the failure that made it easy to discuss and hard to forget. And still, a crew flying one of the most advanced airliners then in service, confronted with exactly the circumstance VanderBurgh had warned about — automation vanishing suddenly, at altitude, with no advance notice — could not close the gap in time.

If a well-documented, well-named, actively taught failure mode can recur inside an industry that had done this much to guard against it, the reader is right to wonder what chance less thoroughly studied, less institutionally prepared domains — courtrooms, warehouses, trading floors, the ordinary car on an ordinary highway — actually have of doing better. Aviation, whatever its gaps, is one of the most safety-conscious, most heavily investigated industries in existence: it has independent accident boards with subpoena power, mandatory reporting, and a decades-long institutional habit of publishing findings and recommendations that other airlines and regulators are expected to act on. Most of the domains this book visits after this one have nothing resembling that infrastructure. A warehouse rarely gets a BEA-style investigation after a worker is injured following a scanner's rate quota. A courtroom does not commission a systematic review of every sentence influenced by a risk score. If commercial aviation, with all of its institutional advantages, still let a named and documented failure mode recur at the cost this one exacted, there is little reason to assume domains with far less investigative scrutiny are doing appreciably better at closing gaps this book has not yet even shown the reader how to see.

That is not a claim this book can prove chapter by chapter with the same forensic precision the NTSB and the BEA brought to two specific flights; the evidence in most other domains simply is not assembled with that rigor, and the book will say so plainly wherever that is the case rather than borrow aviation's certainty where it has not been earned. What VanderBurgh's finding and the Air France 447 investigation together establish is narrower and, for that reason, more durable: that naming a failure mode, even naming it well, is the beginning of the work of preventing it, not the end. That question does not have a comforting answer waiting at the end of this chapter. It is, instead, the question the rest of this book exists to keep asking, case by case, industry by industry, as automation keeps arriving faster than the training built to keep pace with it.
