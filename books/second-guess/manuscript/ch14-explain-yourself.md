# Explain Yourself

A nutrition app recommends a meal. Underneath the recommendation sits a single line of text, the kind of sentence interface designers add almost reflexively once a system starts making suggestions people might not otherwise follow: a short account of why. In a study built around exactly this scenario, researchers Malin Eiband, Daniel Buschek, Alexander Kremer, and Heinrich Hussmann tested what that line of text was actually doing to the people reading it, and the answer complicates nearly everything this book has said so far about trust.

The study, published at the CHI conference in 2019, used a prototype nutrition recommender and put participants through one of three conditions. One group got no explanation at all, just the recommendation, on its own, take it or leave it. A second group got what the researchers called a placebic explanation: a sentence that had the shape and cadence of a reason without actually conveying any real information about how the system had arrived at its suggestion. A third group got a genuine explanation, one that pointed to substantive, checkable logic behind the recommendation. The researchers then measured how often people complied with the system's suggestion under each condition, and by how much they trusted it.

The result that gives this chapter its argument is not that explanations helped — that part was expected, and it held. Compliance in the no-explanation condition sat at 60 percent. What is unsettling is what happened once any explanation was present, real or not. The placebic explanation, the one that gestured at a reason without actually informing anyone of anything, produced a compliance rate of 93 percent. The genuine explanation, drawing on real, substantive information, produced 94 percent. The two numbers are, for practical purposes, the same. People who received a sentence that sounded like a reason complied at essentially the rate of people who received an actual reason, and both groups complied far more than people who received no sentence at all, under the specific condition, the researchers noted, that the effort the explanation asked of the user to engage with it was low.

It helps to be specific about what separated the placebic condition from the genuine one, because the difference is not that one group got a longer sentence and the other a shorter one, or that one explanation was written more persuasively. Both conditions gave participants a sentence of roughly comparable length and tone, sitting in the same place in the interface, doing the same conversational job of following the recommendation with a reason. The difference was entirely in whether the content of that sentence, examined on its own terms, actually connected to anything about how the system had reached its output. A genuine explanation pointed to real, checkable logic — some feature of the input that plausibly bore on the recommendation. A placebic one supplied the syntax of justification without the connective tissue: a reason-shaped sentence that, if you asked why this particular claim justified this particular recommendation, had no good answer. Participants, under low-effort conditions, were not asked that question, and did not ask it themselves.

It's worth sitting with how counterintuitive that finding is supposed to feel, because the whole appeal of explainability as a fix runs on the opposite intuition. The entire premise of building explanation into an automated system (the premise behind years of research and product design under the banner of "explainable AI") is that people trust systems more when they understand them, and that understanding is what an explanation is supposed to supply. Eiband and the study's co-authors found something narrower and less comfortable: under low-effort conditions, the mere presence of an explanation-shaped sentence moved trust and compliance almost as much as an explanation that actually explained something. The form did most of the work. The content was, in this specific low-effort setting, close to optional.

This is where the term the researchers borrowed matters. A placebic explanation is one that offers a reason without offering real information, the linguistic equivalent of a placebo pill, something that produces an effect through the shape of an intervention rather than its active ingredient. In the nutrition-recommender study, the placebic condition still gave participants a sentence with a because in it, still had the grammar of justification, still occupied the visual and cognitive space in the interface where a real explanation would go. What it lacked was substance: nothing in it, examined closely, actually told the user anything checkable about why this particular meal had been recommended over another. And that absence, in the low-effort setting the study tested, turned out to matter far less to people's behavior than its presence would have predicted.

There is a second finding from the same body of work that sharpens the point even further, and it is worth being precise about what it does and doesn't show. Related research distinguished two things that are easy to collapse into one: how satisfied people say they are with an explanation, and how much that explanation actually improves their understanding of the system generating it. On the satisfaction measure, placebic and genuinely informative explanations came out roughly equal — people rated the fake reason and the real one as comparably satisfying. On the performance measure, the two diverged. Only the genuinely informative explanations measurably improved people's actual mental model of how the system worked, their ability to say something accurate about its logic, or to predict what it might do next. Satisfaction and understanding, in other words, are not the same currency, and a system can pay out generously in the first while delivering almost nothing in the second.

Put those two results together and a specific, narrow danger comes into focus, and it is important to keep it narrow, because the temptation at this point is to overcorrect into a much broader and less defensible claim. The finding is not that explanation is worthless, or that "explainable AI" is a fraud, or that giving people real information about how a system reasons never helps. The same research that produced the unsettling headline also produced the reassuring footnote: genuine, substantive explanations that ask something real of the user — that require actual engagement with the logic behind a recommendation, not just a glance at a justifying sentence, did improve people's understanding, measurably, in a way the placebic explanations did not. The danger is specific: low-effort, placebo-shaped explanation, of exactly the kind that is cheapest and easiest to build into a product, can be mistaken for the real thing, both by the people receiving it and, if a design team isn't careful, by the people building it.

It is also worth being honest about the asymmetry in what it costs to build each kind of explanation, because that asymmetry is a large part of why this problem is likely to persist rather than resolve itself as the technology matures. A placebic explanation can be generated automatically, cheaply, and consistently, often by the same underlying system that produced the recommendation in the first place, with no additional engineering investment in making the system's actual reasoning legible. A genuine explanation, by contrast, usually requires the harder and more expensive work of building interpretability into the system itself, or constraining the system's design from the outset so that its reasoning can be faithfully summarized rather than merely gestured at after the fact — work that many of the most capable modern systems, built on architectures that are not natively interpretable even to the engineers who build them, may not be able to support at all. A product team facing a deadline and a user-satisfaction metric that responds about equally to both options does not face a level playing field between them, and the Eiband findings suggest the market signal that would normally push a team toward the harder, better option (user trust and compliance) will often fail to distinguish the two well enough to apply that pressure.

That specificity is what makes this chapter's warning land where the book's other machinery already lives. Chapter six gave the reader trust calibration as the book's central diagnostic tool: the idea that trust in a system should track what that system can actually do, built through a closed loop of experience, feedback, and an accurate mental model. Explanation interfaces are supposed to be one of the more promising ways to feed that loop, a mechanism for giving people the information they need to calibrate correctly, rather than relying on blind faith or blanket suspicion. What the Eiband study shows is that an explanation interface can instead short-circuit the loop. If a placebic explanation raises trust and compliance to nearly the same level as a genuine one, then from the system's point of view, and often from the user's point of view too, the explanation has "worked" — trust went up, the recommendation was followed, satisfaction scores look fine. But the user's mental model of the system has not improved to match. Trust has moved without calibration moving with it. That is precisely the failure mode chapter six named: trust exceeding actual capability, a form of the misuse problem from chapter two, except now manufactured on purpose or by accident through interface design rather than through the operator's own psychology alone.

Consider what this means once it leaves the nutrition app and enters the domains this book has spent its middle chapters on. A defendant's risk score, of the kind examined in chapter ten, arrives in a courtroom with essentially no explanation at all in the Loomis case: Northpointe's scoring method was treated as a trade secret, and the Wisconsin Supreme Court's response was to require cautionary information about the tool's limits rather than disclosure of its logic. Imagine a version of that same system redesigned, with good intentions, to include a short explanatory sentence alongside the score: something like "elevated risk driven by prior offense pattern," attached to a number a judge is asked to weigh at sentencing. If that sentence has the form of a reason without the substantive, checkable content of one (if it is placebic in Eiband's sense rather than genuinely informative), the redesign could easily increase a judge's confidence in the score without increasing the judge's actual understanding of how the score was computed or how reliable it is in cases like this one. The system would look more transparent while remaining, in the way that matters, just as opaque as it was before, and the false sense of insight could make an already difficult calibration problem worse rather than better, precisely because it would feel solved.

The same risk sits inside the diagnostic setting chapter nine examined. An AI system flagging a mammogram as suspicious, or clear, could easily be built to append a sentence of explanation to its output — something pointing, in general terms, at the region of the image or the feature that drove the flag. Whether that sentence functions as a genuine explanation or a placebic one depends entirely on whether it gives the radiologist real, checkable information that changes how they search the image, or whether it merely gestures at a justification while asking nothing further of the reader. A radiologist working under real time pressure, the condition under which the Eiband study found the placebic and genuine explanations converge most closely, is exactly the reader least likely to notice, in the moment, which kind of sentence they've been given. The low-effort condition that made explanation format outweigh explanation content in the lab is not a laboratory artifact; it describes ordinary professional conditions in exactly the high-stakes settings where getting the distinction right matters most.

The warehouse floor examined in chapter eleven offers a blunter version of the same mechanism, stripped of any pretense of choice. A worker told by a handheld scanner that their rate has fallen below target, with a line of automatically generated text attributing the shortfall to a specific bin or task, is being given something that has the outward form of an explanation and may or may not have its substance. Whether that line of text reflects a real, checkable accounting of the worker's day or a generic, templated justification the system produces regardless of the underlying cause is not something the worker is typically positioned to verify, and the incentive for the system's designers to make it feel sufficient rather than to make it actually accurate runs in a direction this chapter's findings should make a reader suspicious of. And the finance chapter's automated systems raise a related question in the opposite direction: a trading algorithm or an automated sell program does not need to explain itself to a human in real time at all, precisely because, as chapter twelve argued, its speed forecloses that kind of intervention, which means the explanation problem in finance is less about placebic reassurance in the moment and more about what gets reconstructed afterward, in a report like the one the SEC and CFTC produced after the Flash Crash, when the explaining finally happens too late to have changed anything.

Aviation offers a version of the same problem in a different register. A flight-management computer that presents a pilot with a recommended course of action (a suggested descent profile, an alert prioritization, a proposed response to an abnormal condition) could, in principle, attach a brief rationale to that recommendation. Whether that rationale helps a pilot decide when to trust it or substitutes a comforting sentence for the deeper situational understanding a real emergency requires is not a question the interface answers by existing; it is a question that depends on whether the sentence actually informs. A cockpit is an unusually low-effort environment by design — checklists, alerts, and automated logic are built to be processed fast, under stress, with minimal cognitive overhead, which is exactly the condition under which Eiband's research found content and form hardest to tell apart.

It is worth pausing on why a placebic explanation works at all, since the finding would be less troubling if it were simply a quirk of one nutrition-app prototype rather than something with an intuitive mechanism behind it. People do not typically approach a low-stakes recommendation — what to eat tonight, which article to read next, which route the map suggests — by rigorously auditing the justification offered for it. They glance, they register that a reason was given, and they move on, because interrogating every justification behind every minor recommendation a piece of software makes in the course of an ordinary day would be exhausting and, most of the time, pointless. That habit of mind is not a flaw; it is a reasonable adaptation to a world full of low-stakes automated suggestions, most of which genuinely don't warrant scrutiny. The trouble is that the habit does not know, on its own, when it has wandered from a low-stakes nutrition app into a courtroom, a reading room, or a cockpit, where the same glance-and-move-on response to an explanatory sentence carries entirely different consequences. The interface looks the same in both places. The sentence is built the same way in both places. Only the stakes have changed, and the stakes are exactly what a placebic explanation, by design or by accident, gives no signal of.

None of this is an argument that engineers and interface designers who build explanation features are acting in bad faith. Most of them are responding, reasonably, to years of research and public pressure suggesting that opaque systems erode trust and that transparency restores it, a version of the very intuition this chapter opened with, and one that isn't wrong so much as incomplete. The problem is that "add an explanation" is compatible with two very different design decisions that look nearly identical from the outside: building genuine insight into a system's reasoning, which is expensive, difficult, and sometimes limited by the underlying model's own lack of interpretability, or building a sentence that merely performs the function of insight, which is cheap and always available regardless of whether the underlying system can actually be made legible. Under time and budget pressure, the second option is always on the table in a way the first often isn't, and the Eiband findings suggest that, at least in the low-effort settings the study examined, users will often reward the two options almost identically. That is not evidence of bad intent. It is evidence that the market signal a product team receives (did trust and compliance go up) cannot, by itself, distinguish a real fix from a cosmetic one.

It matters, too, that the researchers were careful to attach a condition to their headline result rather than presenting it as universal, and this book should be equally careful in repeating it. The near-identical compliance rates for placebic and genuine explanations held specifically when the effort required of the user to engage with the explanation was low — a glance at a sentence, not a close reading of a technical justification. That qualifier is doing real work, and it is honest to say plainly that this brief's sourcing does not extend to a precise account of what happens as required effort rises: whether a sufficiently motivated, sufficiently expert reader would eventually notice that a placebic sentence doesn't hold up, and discount it accordingly, the way the algorithm-aversion research in chapter seven found people scrutinizing and penalizing a forecasting algorithm's visible errors. It would be convenient to assume that experts and high-stakes readers are naturally exempt from the low-effort trap the study documented, but convenience is not evidence, and the more honest reading is that professional settings often manufacture exactly the low-effort conditions the study tested, not because the people in them are careless, but because time pressure, caseload, and the sheer number of automated recommendations crossing a desk in a given day push everyone toward exactly the glance-and-move-on mode where form and content are hardest to tell apart.

This is also where the tension between chapters seven and eight resurfaces, wearing a different coat. Chapter seven showed people penalizing algorithms disproportionately for visible errors, discounting a forecaster's future value after watching it be wrong even once. Chapter eight showed the opposite pattern, laypeople adhering more closely to advice framed as algorithmic than the identical advice framed as human, right up until real expertise entered the picture and reversed the effect. Explanation interfaces sit at the pressure point between those two findings, because an explanation is one of the main levers a system has for shaping which of the two tendencies a given user falls into. A well-designed, genuinely informative explanation might do what chapter seven's follow-up study found minor modification power did — restoring appropriate reliance by giving the user real purchase on the system's reasoning. A placebic explanation risks manufacturing chapter eight's appreciation effect artificially, encouraging deference dressed as understanding, in exactly the professional and high-stakes settings chapter eight warned are most vulnerable to costly overcorrection. The same sentence, depending only on whether it is genuine or placebic, can nudge a reader toward either failure mode this book has spent its middle chapters describing.

There is also a harder version of this problem lurking underneath the study's specific result, which is that even a genuinely informative explanation is not automatically a genuinely complete one. A real explanation can be accurate as far as it goes and still leave out the parts of a system's reasoning that would matter most to a person trying to decide whether to trust it in this particular case rather than in general. The Eiband research draws a clean, useful line between placebic and genuine explanation, but genuine itself is not a single fixed standard; a sentence can clear that bar and still fall well short of the kind of deep, situationally specific understanding a judge, a radiologist, or a pilot would need to calibrate trust the way chapter six describes. The lesson of this chapter is not that genuine explanation solves the calibration problem. It is that genuine explanation is a necessary condition that a great many real interfaces quietly fail to meet, while still looking, and feeling, like they've met it.

There is a modest, practical implication buried in all of this, even though this chapter is not the book's design chapter and should not pretend to be. If form and content are as easy to confuse as the research suggests, then the burden of telling them apart cannot rest solely on the person receiving the explanation in the moment, glancing at a sentence under real time pressure with every incentive to move on. It has to be built into how a system is evaluated before it reaches that person at all, whether an explanation, tested honestly, actually changes what a user can accurately say about the system's reasoning, not merely whether it raises a satisfaction score or a compliance rate. A metric that cannot distinguish a placebic sentence from a genuine one is not measuring explanation. It is measuring reassurance, and reassurance is cheap to manufacture precisely because, as this chapter has shown, it does not require the thing it appears to promise.

What should unsettle a reader who has been hoping, across the last several chapters, for something like a clean answer to the calibration problem is exactly this: explainability was supposed to be that answer, or at least a large piece of it, and the research says it is not one by default. An explanation can inform. It can also merely reassure. The two outcomes can look identical from outside the system — same sentence, same interface, same apparent transparency — and, per the satisfaction research, can even feel identical to the person receiving them, right up until the moment their accuracy is tested. The genuine version does real work, restoring some of the calibration this book has argued is so hard to build and so easy to lose. The placebic version does something else: it produces the feeling of calibrated trust, the comfort of having been told why, without the substance that would make trusting the recommendation actually a good bet. In a nutrition app, mistaking one for the other costs a person a slightly worse dinner. In the domains this book has been examining since its opening chapters, the same mistake, dressed in the same reassuring sentence, costs a great deal more, and it will look, to nearly everyone involved, exactly like progress.

That is the uncomfortable shape of this chapter's finding, and it is worth stating plainly rather than softening. A courtroom, a reading room, and a cockpit are not immune to a laboratory result obtained from a prototype meal-planning app just because the stakes are higher; if anything, the caseload, the time pressure, and the sheer daily volume of automated recommendations in those settings make the low-effort condition the study tested more likely, not less. The fix this book can responsibly offer here is not a design, which belongs to a later chapter, but a habit of suspicion: before crediting an explanation with having done its job, ask whether it could be swapped for a different, made-up justification without anyone downstream noticing the difference. If the answer is yes, the sentence has not explained anything. It has only sounded like it did.
