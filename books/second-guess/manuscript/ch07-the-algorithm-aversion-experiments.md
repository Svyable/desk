# The Algorithm Aversion Experiments

Imagine being shown, side by side, the track records of two forecasters. One is a person, the other an algorithm. Both have been making the same predictions, on the same cases, for a while now, and you can see exactly how each one has performed: how often each was close, how often each was badly wrong, and how the two compare overall. Suppose the algorithm's track record is better — not perfect, but measurably, consistently better than the person's. You are now offered a choice: put your own money on whichever forecaster you prefer to bet on for the next round. Which do you pick?

The honest answer, if you are treating the evidence the way a careful statistician would, is the algorithm — it has the better record, and nothing about the next round of forecasts gives you a reason to expect that pattern to reverse. This is not a trick question, and it is not designed to expose some obscure subtlety in probability theory that only a specialist would catch. It is the most straightforward possible application of the evidence you were just handed: given two tracks records, favor the better one. This is close to the experiment three researchers, Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons, and Cade Massey, actually ran, across a series of five studies published in 2015 in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, under the title "Algorithm Aversion: People Erroneously Avoid Algorithms After Seeing Them Err." And the honest answer is not, in general, what their participants gave.

Across the five studies, participants were shown both an algorithmic forecaster and a human forecaster performing the same task, and were able to observe both making genuine errors along the way, since forecasting under uncertainty produces errors for anyone or anything doing it honestly. Participants could see, in aggregate, that the algorithm outperformed the human forecaster overall — the researchers made sure of that before asking anyone to place their bet. And then, having seen exactly that, participants who were given the choice of which forecaster to rely on for money that was actually theirs to lose chose the algorithm less often than the evidence justified. More specifically, watching the algorithm make a visible error cost it more of participants' confidence than watching the human forecaster make a comparably sized error cost the human. The same mistake, made by two different kinds of forecaster, did not produce the same loss of trust. It produced more loss of trust when the mistake belonged to the machine.

What makes the design of these studies persuasive, rather than merely provocative, is how carefully it rules out the easier explanations. A skeptic's first instinct might be that participants simply had not yet seen enough of the algorithm's performance to trust it — that the aversion was really just an information problem, solvable by showing people a longer track record before asking them to bet. Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey built their studies to close off exactly that escape route: participants observed both forecasters over a substantial run of predictions, saw the aggregate comparison stated plainly, and only then made their choice about where to place a stake that was genuinely theirs to win or lose. A second instinct might be that the algorithm's errors were, in some hard-to-quantify way, worse or stranger than the human's — the kind of odd, inexplicable miss that makes a person wonder whether something has gone wrong with the process itself, as opposed to an understandable, forgivable human slip. The studies controlled for the size of the errors being compared, so that a given miss by the algorithm and a given miss by the human were, on paper, equivalent misses. Strip away both of those alternative explanations and what remains is the finding in its starkest form: with the information available, with the errors matched, participants still treated the machine's misses as more disqualifying than the person's.

This is worth sitting with before moving to the explanation, because the finding is stranger, and less intuitive, than it first sounds. It is not simply that people distrust algorithms in general, or that they have never seen one perform well — the whole design of the study ensured participants had direct, personal evidence of the algorithm's superior track record before making their choice. It is not that the algorithm's errors were somehow worse or more alarming in content than the human's — the studies were built to compare errors of comparable magnitude. And it is not that participants were being irrational about their own money in some general, undiscriminating way, since the same participants were perfectly capable of using the algorithm's superior track record correctly right up until they personally watched it be wrong even once. The aversion appeared specifically, reliably, and disproportionately at the moment a visible error crossed from an abstract statistic — the algorithm is right more often than the human, overall — into a concrete, witnessed failure. Seeing the algorithm be wrong, even once, mattered more to participants than knowing, numerically, that it was right more often than the alternative.

Consider what this asks us to believe about how differently we forgive the two kinds of forecaster for being wrong. A human forecaster who makes an error is understood, almost automatically, within a framework that has been built into human social cognition for a very long time: people are fallible, forecasting is hard, everyone gets some of these wrong, and one error does not erase a generally solid track record. We extend that framework to human forecasters more or less by default, because we have spent our whole lives dealing with other fallible people and have accumulated, without particularly noticing, a working theory of how much a single mistake should count against someone's overall competence. An algorithm making the identical error does not get the benefit of that same framework. Its mistake reads less like "forecasting is hard" and more like evidence that the machine, despite its impressive numbers, is somehow fundamentally unfit for the job — proof, almost, that the earlier good performance was a fluke or a front, rather than what it actually was: the normal, expected performance of a system whose error rate happens to be lower than a person's, but not zero.

Compare the two forgiveness standards directly, because the contrast is where the finding earns its discomfort. Tell a colleague their forecast was wrong last quarter and the conversation that follows tends to look for context: what information was available at the time, what was reasonable to expect, what everyone else in the field was also getting wrong. That context-seeking reflex does not usually require deliberate effort; it is close to automatic, the ordinary charity extended to a fellow person operating under uncertainty. Tell the same colleague that the algorithm they had started relying on was wrong last quarter, and the reflex that tends to follow is closer to suspicion of the whole apparatus — was it trained on bad data, is the model flawed, should we go back to doing this by hand. The error is structurally the same. The story told about the error is not.

Part of what makes this an uncomfortable finding, rather than merely a curious one, is the specific unfairness of the standard being applied. We do not require perfection from human forecasters before extending them provisional trust. We require it, apparently without noticing that we are doing so, from machines — and the moment a machine fails to meet a standard no forecaster of any kind could meet, we treat that failure as disqualifying in a way we would never apply to a colleague, a doctor, or a friend whose predictions have also, on occasion, been wrong. The algorithm in Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's studies was not being judged against the human forecaster's actual performance. It was being judged against an unstated expectation of infallibility that neither forecaster had ever met, and that only the algorithm was penalized for falling short of.

There is also a quieter irony worth naming: the same participants who penalized the algorithm for a visible error were, by the study's own design, penalizing the more accurate of their two options. This is not a case where an understandable caution led people toward the safer, if less impressive, choice. Choosing the human forecaster after watching the algorithm outperform it overall was, by the studies' own measure, choosing worse odds with eyes open. That is what separates algorithm aversion from ordinary risk-aversion or a reasonable demand for a longer track record before trusting something new. The participants were not hedging against uncertainty about which forecaster was better. They had already been shown which forecaster was better, and chose against that evidence anyway, in a direction that cost them money in expectation.

It is worth asking, honestly, where a double standard like that might come from, since the studies themselves are better evidence for the pattern's existence than for any single explanation of its cause. One plausible piece of the story is that people generally do not expect algorithms to be flawless in the same nuanced way they expect people to be flawed — we do not have a rich, socially calibrated intuition for "this is roughly how often a reasonably good forecasting algorithm should be expected to miss," the way we do, from a lifetime of experience, for how often a reasonably competent person should be expected to miss. Absent that calibrated expectation, a single visible algorithmic error has less context to be absorbed into, and more room to be read as evidence against the whole enterprise. Another plausible piece is that an algorithm's error can feel, to the person watching it, like a more damning kind of failure — not simply "this particular guess was wrong," which is how a human's error tends to be interpreted, but "the process behind this guess is broken," since a machine is not generally credited with the excuses — a bad night's sleep, a distraction, an unlucky guess under pressure — that soften how a person's individual errors get read. Neither explanation is established with the same rigor as the aversion finding itself, and the studies were not designed to adjudicate between them. What they did establish, cleanly and repeatedly across five separate tests, is that the asymmetry exists, that it costs people money they had every incentive to protect, and that it persists even when the underlying statistics unambiguously favor the machine.

It would be easy to read this finding as an indictment of ordinary people's reasoning, and it would also be too easy. The participants in these studies were not being asked to do anything unusual with their trust; they were doing something close to what most people do most of the time when deciding how much to rely on another person or a tool, which is to update heavily on the most recent, most vivid evidence in front of them rather than patiently reweighting a long historical average every time a new data point arrives. That habit of mind is not obviously irrational in general — recent information often is more relevant than old information, and a forecaster who has recently started performing worse might genuinely be worth trusting less than their long-run average would suggest. The problem here was specifically that the habit applied itself unevenly: the same recency-weighted, vividness-driven updating that participants used on the human forecaster's errors, they used more aggressively on the algorithm's errors, producing a bias not because they updated on new evidence, but because they updated asymmetrically on formally identical evidence depending only on its source.

It is worth pausing on the word researchers eventually settled on for this pattern, because the choice of "aversion" is doing something specific. An aversion, in the ordinary psychological sense, is not simply a mistaken belief that can be corrected by supplying better information. It behaves more like a reflex — triggered quickly, resistant to being argued out of, and only loosely responsive to the kind of deliberate, statistical reasoning a person might otherwise be perfectly capable of applying in a calmer moment. That is a meaningfully different claim than "people do not know that algorithms are usually more accurate than humans at narrow, well-defined forecasting tasks." Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's participants generally did know that, in the specific sense that they had just watched the evidence demonstrate it. Algorithm aversion, as their studies define it, is not an information gap. It is closer to a standing bias in how a piece of evidence gets weighted once it is known, one that survives contact with exactly the kind of clear, personally observed data that should, in principle, override it.

That asymmetry is the substance of algorithm aversion, and it connects directly to the diagnostic tool the previous chapter introduced. This is undertrust in Lee and See's sense — confidence falling short of demonstrated capability — and it is undertrust with a very specific trigger: not a general absence of positive experience with the algorithm, since participants had plenty of that, but a single vivid failure allowed to outweigh a larger body of confirming evidence. The closed loop that should have produced accurate calibration did receive good feedback; participants did see the algorithm's superior overall record. What broke the loop was not a lack of information but a distortion in how the most recent, most salient piece of that information got weighted against everything that came before it — precisely the vulnerability the previous chapter identified as one of the ways a calibration loop can fail even when feedback is, in principle, available.

There is a natural, uncomfortable question sitting underneath all of this, which the researchers themselves went on to ask directly: if a single visible error is enough to tank people's trust in an algorithm that remains, overall, the better bet, is there anything that keeps people using imperfect algorithms at all — given that every real algorithm, in every real domain, will eventually be visibly wrong about something? A 2016 follow-up by the same team, later published in Management Science under the title "Overcoming Algorithm Aversion: People Will Use Imperfect Algorithms If They Can (Even Slightly) Modify Them," took up exactly that question, and found something more hopeful than the first paper's headline result would have predicted.

Giving participants even a small amount of control over the algorithm's forecast — the ability to adjust its output, even slightly, before it was used — substantially restored their willingness to rely on it. This held even when participants barely used the adjustment power they had been given; the mere presence of a modification option, not the actual exercise of it, was enough to meaningfully change how much people trusted the algorithm going forward. People did not need to fix the algorithm's mistakes themselves, in other words. They needed to feel that they could, if the algorithm's error had been bad enough to warrant it. A forecasting tool that was rigid — take my output exactly as given, with no room for adjustment — pushed people toward the aversion the first paper documented. The identical tool, wrapped in an interface that allowed the smallest sliver of human discretion over the final number, did not.

This is worth taking seriously as more than a clever fix for an inconvenient bias, because it points toward something real about what the underlying aversion is actually about. If people were simply, rationally distrustful of algorithmic competence, a token adjustment power that went mostly unused should not have moved their trust much at all — the algorithm's actual accuracy had not changed, after all, whether or not a person had the option to tweak its output. What moved instead was something closer to the participant's sense of their own agency in the outcome. An algorithm that fails while the person watching had no say in its forecast reads as an imposition — a mistake made to you. An algorithm that fails while the person watching retained even a small, mostly unused ability to have adjusted it reads differently — a mistake made alongside you, with your own judgment still nominally in the loop. The accuracy of the forecast had not changed between these two framings. The psychological experience of relying on it had.

This helps explain why the fix worked even though it barely changed the forecasts themselves. If algorithm aversion were purely a judgment about competence — a belief that the machine simply could not be trusted to do the job well — then handing a person a mostly unused dial should not have moved anything, because the machine's competence, unchanged, remained exactly as trustworthy or untrustworthy as before. What the adjustment power seems to have changed instead is the story a participant could tell themselves about responsibility for the next error. A forecast delivered by an untouchable algorithm belongs entirely to the algorithm when it goes wrong, and an error that belongs entirely to something outside the person's control is, on the evidence of the first paper, judged unusually harshly. A forecast that passed through even a token opportunity for adjustment belongs a little to the person as well, and an error shared with one's own agency, however slightly, seems to earn back some of the forgiveness ordinarily reserved for a fallible human colleague rather than a fallible machine.

There is a design lesson buried in that distinction, and it is one this book will return to directly once it turns from diagnosis to what can actually be built differently: a small amount of user control, even control that is barely exercised, does real work in sustaining appropriate reliance on an imperfect but genuinely useful system. That is a specific, actionable finding, and a rarer thing in this territory than it might sound — a documented case where a modest, concrete design choice measurably closed a gap between how much people should have trusted a tool and how much they actually did. It counts, honestly, as a piece of real optimism inside a chapter that has otherwise been about an uncomfortable and fairly stubborn human bias, and it deserves to be read as such rather than smoothed over.

It is worth pausing, too, on what this finding implies for anyone responsible for deploying an algorithm into a setting where humans are meant to rely on it rather than merely study it in a laboratory. If ordinary users are going to judge a system's very first visible error far more harshly than they judge a comparable human error, then an institution that installs an algorithmic tool and simply waits for its accuracy to speak for itself is relying on a mechanism that the evidence in this chapter says does not reliably work. The algorithm's aggregate superiority, however real, is not what determines whether people keep using it. What determines that, at least in part, is whether the first salient failure a user personally witnesses gets absorbed the way a human colleague's mistake would be, or treated as proof the whole system cannot be trusted. An institution that wants durable, appropriately calibrated reliance on a genuinely good algorithm cannot simply announce the algorithm's superior track record and expect trust to follow the statistics. It has to design for the moment of the first visible error, because that moment, this research suggests, will matter more to the user than everything that came before it.

Picture the same asymmetry playing out beyond the walls of a psychology lab, in the settings later chapters of this book examine directly. A radiologist who has come to rely on a diagnostic aid, and who then watches it miss a single case, is facing precisely the situation Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's participants faced — a machine's visible error, arriving after a track record that had, up to that point, been genuinely reassuring. A trader who watches an algorithmic model misprice a single position after months of solid performance is facing the same structure. A driver who watches a partially automated car make one uncomfortable, if not dangerous, misjudgment after thousands of smooth miles is facing it too. None of these later cases are drawn from Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's data; they are not evidence for algorithm aversion in radiology, finance, or driving specifically. But the psychological mechanism this chapter has just described was not shown to be specific to the particular forecasting task the original studies used, and there is no obvious reason a radiologist, a trader, or a driver would be exempt from a bias this robust in a controlled setting, simply because their domain carries higher stakes. If anything, higher stakes make a single visible error more memorable, not less, which is exactly the condition under which this chapter's aversion effect was strongest.

It would be a mistake, though, to let that piece of optimism resolve more than it actually resolves. The 2015 findings established that algorithm aversion is real, robust across five separate studies, and costly enough that people gave up money over it even when the better choice was directly in front of them, verified by their own eyes. The 2016 follow-up established one condition — a small measure of retained control — under which the aversion could be substantially reduced. Neither paper claims, and this chapter should not claim on their behalf, that algorithm aversion is a universal law governing every interaction between a person and a machine, or that it operates identically in every domain, every framing, and every population the way the vigilance decrement's basic shape reappears across different monitoring tasks. What these studies show is a specific, well-documented tendency, under specific, well-documented conditions, to punish an algorithm's visible error more harshly than an equally visible human one — a tendency real enough to matter, and fixable enough, at least partially, to matter in the other direction too. Whether that tendency is the dominant psychological pattern governing how people actually relate to algorithms in the world, or only one recurring pattern among others, is a question this book is not yet in a position to answer.

By the framework the previous chapter laid out, algorithm aversion looks like a clean, almost textbook case of undertrust: a system whose demonstrated trustworthiness exceeds the confidence it is actually given, with a specific, identifiable point of breakdown in the feedback loop that should have kept the two aligned. It would be tidy to end the diagnosis here, with disuse named, its trigger isolated, and a partial design fix already in hand from the modification studies. It is, in fact, about to get considerably more complicated, because the very next body of research finds a population of people doing something that looks, on its surface, like the precise opposite of everything this chapter has just described — not avoiding algorithmic advice after seeing it fail, but preferring it, sight unseen, over the advice of another person, in exactly the kind of ordinary laypeople this chapter's own participants also were, tested by researchers who, going in, expected to find something closer to more of the same aversion this chapter has just described.
