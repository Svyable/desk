# Back Matter

## About the Book

*Second Guess* is a book about a single, recurring moment: the instant a
person looks at a machine's recommendation and has to decide whether to
follow it, question it, or overrule it outright. That moment now happens
constantly and largely invisibly — in cockpits, reading rooms, courtrooms,
warehouses, trading systems, and the front seats of cars that mostly drive
themselves.

The book does not argue that automation is dangerous or that human
intuition deserves the last word. The evidence runs against both of those
simple stories. It argues instead that reliance is a skill in its own
right, separate from either the skill the automation performs or the skill
it replaces, and that most of the institutions now deploying automated
recommendation at scale have done very little to teach that skill on
purpose.

## Notes and Sources

The manuscript was researched chapter by chapter using a source ledger
maintained alongside the book. Government accident investigation reports —
from the NTSB, the BEA, Indonesia's NTSC, and Ethiopia's AIB — anchor the
aviation and autonomous-vehicle chapters. Peer-reviewed human-factors and
behavioral-science research anchors the psychological argument. Regulatory
filings and joint agency reports anchor the finance chapter. Investigative
journalism, used carefully and cross-checked where possible, anchors the
criminal-justice and warehouse-labor chapters, alongside the primary court
opinion in the criminal-justice case.

Where a finding comes from a single study rather than a settled scientific
consensus, the manuscript tries to say so. Where two credible sources
disagree — as ProPublica and Northpointe do about how to define fairness in
a risk-assessment tool, or as Tesla's and Waymo's safety statistics do about
what a comparable mile actually is — the manuscript keeps both positions on
the page rather than picking a winner for the reader. The research files
accompanying this manuscript contain the chapter-level source ledger with
titles, dates, URLs, claim summaries, and caveats, and are the canonical
source record for the draft.

## Acknowledgments

This book depends on the unglamorous work of accident investigators,
human-factors researchers, court reporters, and journalists who kept asking
what actually happened after the headlines moved on. Government
investigation agencies that publish full, technical, sometimes
uncomfortable final reports make chapters like this one possible in a way
that no corporate press release ever could. So do the researchers,
going back to a British psychologist studying tired radar operators during
the Second World War, who treated the humble question of how people pay
attention as worth decades of careful study.

Any remaining errors of interpretation belong to the author.
