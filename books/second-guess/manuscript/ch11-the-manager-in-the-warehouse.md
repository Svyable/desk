# The Manager in the Warehouse

Every worker in an Amazon warehouse carries a handheld scanner, an unremarkable-looking device for a task that has become, in the reporting on Amazon's operations, one of the more closely watched instruments of modern workplace management. It is not merely a tool for logging where an item goes. It is also a continuous data feed, reporting back in real time how quickly its holder is moving, how many items they have picked, packed, or stowed in the last hour, and how that pace compares with the rate the system expects. That data does not sit quietly in a back-office database. Reporting has described it surfacing as visible worker rankings, a running, comparative account of who is keeping pace and who is falling behind, feeding into a standing possibility of discipline or termination for workers who fail to "make rate." The manager, in the older sense of a person who watches the floor, adjusts expectations case by case, and exercises judgment about a bad day versus a bad worker, has not disappeared from these buildings. But a second manager, one built out of software and quota logic, now sits alongside — or, workers describe, increasingly in front of — the human one, and it does not take days off, does not know a worker's history, and does not obviously exercise judgment about anything at all.

The older model of warehouse supervision, whatever its own flaws, at least ran through a human being with some discretion built into the role. A supervisor walking a floor could notice that a particular worker was having a bad day, that a particular task was unusually awkward given the layout of a particular aisle, that a near-miss earlier in the shift meant a slower, more careful pace for the rest of it was the right call rather than a lapse to be corrected. None of that discretion disappears entirely once a rate-tracking system enters the picture — human supervisors still exist in these buildings, still make decisions, still exercise judgment within the space the system leaves them. But the space itself has narrowed. A supervisor overseeing workers whose pace, output, and standing against a quota are continuously logged by the same handheld device the worker carries is overseeing people whose performance is already being scored by a system the supervisor did not build and, in the specific case of the algorithmic logic itself, may not be able to alter. The discretion that remains sits downstream of a standard set upstream, by software, at a scale and continuity no walking supervisor could ever have matched even at their most attentive.

This is a different kind of relationship to automated recommendation than anything examined so far in this book. A pilot decides whether to trust an autopilot. A radiologist decides whether to trust an AI's reading of a mammogram. A judge decides whether to trust a risk score. In each of those cases, however imperfect the calibration, the human retains, at least in principle, the standing to make the call: to override, double-check, or set the system's recommendation aside. A warehouse worker managed by an algorithmic rate system does not occupy that position. The system is not offering a recommendation the worker can accept or reject. It is setting the terms under which the worker keeps the job.

The visibility of the ranking is doing work beyond simple record-keeping, and it is worth separating that mechanism from the disciplinary threat it sits alongside. A quota enforced privately, known only to a worker and their supervisor, would already create pressure to keep pace. A quota displayed publicly, as a running comparison against coworkers, adds a second and different kind of pressure entirely — the ordinary human discomfort of visibly ranking below peers, which can push a worker toward a faster pace than management would even need to enforce through formal discipline. This is not a claim this book can attribute a precise psychological mechanism to with the sourcing available here; it is simply worth noting that a publicly visible ranking and a privately tracked quota are not the same intervention, even when the underlying data collection is identical, because the first recruits the worker's own peers into the enforcement of the pace, in a way the second does not.

The injury data attached to this arrangement is not ambiguous in its direction, even where its precise causal chain is harder to pin down than a single accident report. In 2023, Amazon's warehouses recorded injury rates more than 30 percent above the warehousing industry average. More than two-thirds of individual Amazon warehouses had injury rates exceeding that industry average — not a handful of outlier facilities dragging up a company-wide number, but a pattern spread across most of the network. Cited research examining the role of automation specifically found that Amazon's robotic warehouses — facilities where robots move shelving units to stationary human workers, rather than having workers walk the floor themselves — had a 54 percent higher serious-injury rate than Amazon's non-robotic facilities. That last figure deserves a moment's attention, because it cuts against an intuition a reader might otherwise bring to this chapter: that introducing more automation into a warehouse would, by removing walking and lifting from the human side of the job, make the work safer. The robotic facilities were not safer. They were markedly less safe, at least by this measure, and the likeliest explanation reporting has pointed to is not the robots themselves but the pace the robots enable — a system that can present a worker with the next item to pick every few seconds has also removed the natural friction, the walking time between stations, that used to build a small margin of recovery into the job.

There is a specific irony worth naming in that finding, because it inverts the story automation usually tells about itself. Automated systems are typically introduced, and typically marketed, as a way of removing dangerous physical labor from human hands — the heavy lifting, the long walks across a warehouse floor, handed to a machine so the human worker is left with a lighter, safer task. What the injury-rate comparison suggests happened in practice is closer to the opposite: the physical burden shifted, but the pace tightened to fill the time the automation freed up, and the resulting job — less walking, more continuous, algorithmically timed motion — produced more serious injuries, not fewer. Automation did not simply remove strain from the job. It removed the built-in recovery time that used to accompany that strain, and replaced it with a pace a machine, not a body, was calibrated to sustain.

Amazon's own public position complicates the picture, and this chapter is not in a position to resolve the complication for the reader. The company has stated publicly that it does not require employees to meet specific productivity speeds or targets. Set against that statement is a body of reporting — including, in some accounts, internal documents described by other outlets — describing workers who were disciplined and terminated based on data the algorithmic tracking system generated: workers let go, in these accounts, for falling short of a quota that was not, according to the company's own public statements, supposed to exist as an enforceable target in the first place. A Senate investigation examined the underlying conditions and reached a conclusion put in stark terms: that following the safety protocols workers were also required to observe, at the pace the rate-tracking system implicitly demanded, had become, in the investigators' characterization, "nearly impossible." This book does not adjudicate which account is more accurate — the company's description of its own policy, or the reporting and testimony that describe a different lived reality on the floor. What can be stated plainly is that the dispute itself is real, unresolved in public record, and sits at the center of why this chapter is difficult to write with the tidy moral clarity a reader might want from it.

A Senate investigation is not the same kind of evidence as a single news report, and it is worth noting the distinction briefly. Where a piece of investigative journalism draws on documents, interviews, and reporting judgment assembled by a newsroom, a congressional investigation carries subpoena power, testimony taken under something closer to legal accountability, and an institutional process built, at least in principle, for exactly this kind of dispute between a company's public statements and the lived conditions its workers describe. That the investigators' own characterization of the situation used a phrase as blunt as "nearly impossible" — describing the compatibility of required safety protocols with the pace the rate-tracking system implicitly demanded — is not a stray line from an unaffiliated critic. It is the conclusion of a body with the institutional standing to examine internal records most outside reporting cannot reach. That does not resolve the dispute with Amazon's public statement in the reader's favor by itself; a company can dispute a Senate investigation's characterization just as it can dispute a newsroom's, and this book has not been given grounds in its sourcing to declare the matter closed. But it is a reason to take the "nearly impossible" finding seriously as more than a rhetorical flourish.

Amazon is, in this sense, less an outlier than a leading indicator — the largest and most closely studied example of a management style that scanner technology, real-time data, and quota software have made available to any sufficiently large logistics operation, not a bespoke practice unique to one firm's culture. This chapter's decision to name Amazon specifically, rather than gesture toward "warehouse automation" broadly, is not a claim that the underlying dynamic is confined to one company. It is a decision about what the available sourcing can actually support with precision, and a bet that a reader is better served by one company's numbers, examined closely and attributed correctly, than by a vaguer indictment spread across an entire industry the cited research did not actually study.

It is worth pausing on why this book treats Amazon specifically rather than reaching for a vaguer claim about "warehouse automation" in general. Other logistics and warehousing operations use scanner-based productivity tracking, quota systems, and algorithmic management tools of their own; this is not a practice unique to one company, and a reader should not come away from this chapter believing otherwise. But the specific, sourced facts available for this chapter — the injury-rate comparisons, the Senate investigation's characterization, the public statement versus the described internal practice — are about Amazon, drawn from reporting and research that examined Amazon's operations specifically. Generalizing those particular numbers into a broader claim about "the industry" would outrun what the sourcing here actually supports, and this book would rather stay precisely inside what it can defend than gesture at a wider indictment it cannot back with the same rigor.

Set this arrangement against the vigilance research from Chapter 5, and the comparison runs in an unexpected direction. Mackworth's original finding, and the large body of research that followed it, concerns a human asked to sustain attention over a long, mostly uneventful watch, waiting for a rare signal that is easy to miss precisely because it is rare and the surrounding data is not. That is a story about a human failing to stay alert enough over an automated or semi-automated system. The warehouse floor inverts the relationship. The worker here is not failing to watch a machine closely enough; the machine is watching the worker, continuously, and setting a pace the worker has essentially no discretion to slow. The vigilance problem does not disappear in this inversion — a worker moving at a rate set to satisfy an algorithmic quota still has to notice hazards, still has to catch the moment when a heavy box is positioned wrong, still has to register a colleague's near-miss or their own body's warning signs before an injury rather than after one — but the margin available for that noticing has been narrowed from the other direction. It is not that the human is asked to watch too little and given too much idle time in which attention drifts, the classic vigilance-decrement setup. It is that the human is given no idle time at all, no pause in which the ordinary work of catching a hazard, reporting a near-miss, or simply resting an overtaxed set of muscles can occur without falling behind a pace a machine is measuring in real time.

This is where the book's central framework — misuse and disuse, the two failure modes Parasuraman and Riley named in 1997 — stops describing two separate, distinguishable problems and starts describing a single collapsed one. In the domains examined so far, misuse and disuse have been opposite errors: trusting a system too much, or trusting it too little, with the human retaining enough independence from the system to make either mistake as a matter of judgment. A warehouse worker under algorithmic rate management cannot meaningfully choose to distrust the system and slow down, because slowing down is not a judgment call available to them in the way it is available to a pilot choosing to hand-fly rather than engage the autopilot, or a radiologist choosing to search an image more thoroughly than an AI flag suggests is necessary. The system does not present a recommendation a worker weighs and decides whether to follow. It presents a standard the worker's continued employment depends on meeting, monitored by the same technology that sets it. Call it misuse, in the sense that the pace itself may be badly calibrated to what a human body can sustain safely over a full shift. Call it disuse, in the sense that whatever judgment a worker might otherwise exercise about their own safety — the accumulated, embodied knowledge of when a shortcut is dangerous, when a task needs two people rather than one, when a near-miss is worth reporting rather than working through — has no channel through which to act on the system that is setting their pace. The distinction Chapter 2 drew, useful everywhere else in this book, breaks down here specifically because the worker is not in a position to exercise the kind of independent judgment misuse and disuse both presuppose.

It is tempting to describe this as automation simply doing what it was always going to do to labor — a story with a long history, running well before scanners and quota software, about efficiency measures narrowing the discretion of the person doing the work. There is truth in that longer history, but it risks flattening what is actually new here. Earlier generations of workplace time-and-motion management still required a human manager to observe, record, and act on a worker's pace, with all the friction, inconsistency, and occasional mercy that human observation carries with it. What the scanner and the rate-tracking software add is continuity and scale: observation that does not blink, does not have an off day, does not know a particular worker's circumstances, and does not need to be present on the floor to render its judgment. That is a difference in kind, not simply in degree, and it is the difference this chapter has been trying to describe precisely rather than treat as an old problem wearing new packaging.

That collapse is the sharper version of the algorithmic-management problem promised earlier in this book, and it is worth being explicit about what makes it sharper rather than simply worse. In every other chapter, the person facing an automated recommendation retains, even when they exercise it badly, the standing to say no — to override the autopilot, to double-check the AI's read, to weigh a risk score against their own judgment before pronouncing a sentence. The warehouse worker's relationship to the rate-tracking system is not structured that way. Saying no to the pace is not equivalent to overriding a recommendation; reporting described it as tantamount to volunteering for discipline or termination. A worker who notices that the required pace is leaving no time for a safety step — checking a load is stable before lifting, taking the extra few seconds a cramped aisle demands, pausing after a near-miss to report it rather than immediately resuming work — faces a choice the earlier chapters' pilots, radiologists, and judges do not: take the time the judgment calls for, and risk falling behind a metric the system is recording continuously, or skip the judgment and keep pace. The 54 percent higher serious-injury rate at Amazon's robotic facilities, whatever its precise causal mechanism, is at minimum consistent with a floor where that second option gets chosen more often than it would if the pace itself allowed room for the first.

Chapter 6 argued that trust calibration is not a switch a person flips consciously but an emergent property of a closed loop — experience, feedback, and an evolving mental model of what a system can actually do, cycling back into better-matched trust over time. It is worth asking where that loop is supposed to run in a warehouse built around algorithmic rate management, and the honest answer is that it is not obvious the loop closes at all, for anyone positioned to act on it. The worker experiencing the pace has almost no channel to feed information about that pace's real cost back into the system setting it; a near-miss, a strained back, a shortcut taken under time pressure does not automatically register anywhere the rate-setting logic can see it, unless it escalates into a reportable injury that shows up, much later, in a very different kind of report reviewed by very different people on a very different schedule than the one generating the day's quotas. The people positioned to see the injury-rate data in aggregate — the 30 percent, the two-thirds, the 54 percent — are not the ones standing at a station absorbing the pace in real time, and the people absorbing the pace in real time are not the ones with visibility into, or authority over, the aggregate numbers. Calibration requires the same person, or at least the same institution acting coherently, to experience the system's behavior and adjust trust in light of it. Here, the experience and the authority to adjust sit in different places, reporting up through different channels, on different clocks — which is as good a description as any of why a calibration loop that works reasonably well in a cockpit or a reading room can fail to close at all on a warehouse floor.

None of this requires believing that anyone involved intends harm, and it is worth resisting the temptation to write this chapter as a story with a single villain. The rate-tracking system was almost certainly built to solve a genuine business problem — coordinating an enormous, physically distributed workforce moving an enormous volume of goods, with a level of real-time visibility no purely human management structure could match at that scale. The problem this chapter has been describing is not that the technology is malicious. It is that a system built to optimize for throughput, applied without an equally rigorous mechanism for protecting the margin a human body needs to work safely inside that throughput, will optimize for throughput — because that is what it was built and measured to do, and nothing in the system's design obligates it to notice the cost showing up in the injury data on a different report, reviewed by different people, on a different schedule. Bainbridge's argument from Chapter 3, built originally around a very different kind of automation, applies here with unsettling precision: a system designed because human coordination at this scale is hard to trust unaided still leaves the humans responsible for exactly the judgment calls the system was not built to make — when a load is unsafe, when a step cannot be skipped, when a body has reached its limit for the day — and gives them steadily less time in which to make those calls as the system's optimized pace tightens around the schedule.

The unresolved dispute this chapter opened with — Amazon's public statement against the pace of reporting that contradicts it — is not a detail to be smoothed over on the way to a conclusion. It is close to the chapter's actual subject. In the other domains this book has examined, the object of trust is at least visible enough to argue about on shared terms: a study's published numbers, a court's public opinion, an accident investigation's findings. Here, the dispute is partly about what the system's actual expectations even are — whether "no specific productivity speeds or targets" describes the lived experience of the people working under algorithmic rate tracking, or describes a policy that exists mainly as a public statement, sitting at some distance from what the tracking software, the ranking displays, and the disciplinary process built around them actually produce on the floor. A worker cannot calibrate trust in a system whose stated rules and observed behavior are themselves in dispute, any more than a judge can calibrate trust in a risk score whose inner workings are withheld as a trade secret. The two chapters share that structural feature even though almost nothing else about a courtroom and a warehouse floor is alike: in both, the object of judgment has been made harder to see clearly than the stakes of misjudging it would seem to require.

What this chapter can responsibly conclude is narrower than a full accounting of who is right in Amazon's case would require, and narrower than the moral clarity a reader might reasonably want from a chapter about workplace injuries. The injury-rate data is real and specific to Amazon's own operations, drawn from research and reporting that examined those operations directly. The gap between the company's stated policy and the practices described in reporting and a Senate investigation is real and, as of this writing, unresolved in public record. And the structural point underlying both — that a worker managed by an algorithm which sets the pace of their own safety margin occupies a fundamentally different relationship to automated recommendation than a pilot, a radiologist, or a judge, because the standing to say no has been folded into the system being judged — is the sharper, harder version of this book's central question that the warehouse floor was always going to force into view eventually.

Every other chapter in this book has, in one way or another, asked a version of the same question: given a system's recommendation, does the person receiving it correctly judge when to trust it, when to check it, and when to override it. That question quietly assumes the person has somewhere to stand while making the judgment — enough independence from the system, and enough time inside the moment, to exercise a choice at all. A pilot deciding whether to trust an autopilot is still the one flying the plane. A radiologist deciding whether to trust an AI flag is still the one who signs the report. A judge weighing a risk score is still the one who pronounces the sentence. The warehouse worker's relationship to the rate-tracking system does not offer that same standing, because the system is not merely advising the worker's judgment; it is measuring, ranking, and in the accounts this chapter has drawn on, disciplining the worker for the time that exercising judgment would cost. The open question this chapter leaves, unresolved because the record itself leaves it unresolved, is how much of that standing an algorithmically managed workforce actually has left — and what, if anything, restores it once a system built to optimize throughput has been given the authority to decide who keeps their job.
