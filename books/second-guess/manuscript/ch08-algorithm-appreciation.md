# Algorithm Appreciation

A team of researchers set out, a few years after Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey published their findings on algorithm aversion, to test a related question: when people are given a piece of advice and told nothing about its accuracy, only its source, do they weight advice from an algorithm differently than advice from a person? Jennifer Logg, Julia Minson, and Don Moore ran six experiments to find out, publishing the results in 2019 in Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes under the title "Algorithm Appreciation: People Prefer Algorithmic to Human Judgment." Given the previous chapter, a reasonable prediction would be that people discount the algorithm, extend the human forecaster the benefit of the doubt, and adhere more closely to advice they believe came from a person. That is, in fact, what Logg, Minson, and Moore themselves predicted going in. It is also, across six separate experiments, essentially the opposite of what they found.

Laypeople in these studies adhered more closely to advice when they were told it came from an algorithm than when they were told the identical advice came from another person. This held for a task asking participants to estimate a numeric quantity from a visual stimulus — a task with a verifiable right answer, where following better advice meaningfully improved accuracy, and where the identical advice, worded identically, moved people further from their own initial guess when it was labeled as coming from an algorithm than when it was labeled as coming from a person. It held again for forecasting tasks with no comparably objective right answer available in the moment: predicting how popular a song would become, and predicting how compatible two people would be with each other romantically. Across all of these domains, the same basic pattern repeated. Advice labeled as coming from an algorithm moved people's judgments further from their own initial guess and closer to the advice itself than the identical advice labeled as coming from a person. Participants were not simply indifferent between the two sources. They actively preferred the algorithm's judgment to a stranger's, in a study whose authors had bet on the reverse.

Six experiments is a substantial body of evidence to build a single claim on, and part of what makes the result persuasive rather than merely suggestive is the variety across them. A finding that showed up only in one narrow task — say, the visual estimation experiment alone — would leave open the possibility that something peculiar about that specific task, rather than anything general about how people weigh algorithmic versus human advice, was driving the result. Logg, Minson, and Moore instead moved the underlying question across meaningfully different kinds of judgment: a task with an objectively verifiable correct answer, where following good advice has a clear, measurable payoff, and two tasks without any comparably objective ground truth, forecasting a song's future popularity and forecasting romantic compatibility between two people, domains where it is far from obvious that a computational system should have any particular advantage over a thoughtful human judge. That the appreciation effect showed up across this range — not just where an algorithm's edge might seem intuitively plausible, but also where it does not — is what makes the finding read less like an artifact of one clever experimental setup and more like a real, if not fully explained, feature of how people process the word "algorithm" attached to a recommendation.

It is worth sitting with how strange this looks set directly against the previous chapter. There, watching an algorithm make a single visible error was enough to erode trust in it faster than an equally visible human error would. Here, with no errors shown at all — nothing but a label attached to a piece of advice — the same broad population of ordinary people leaned toward the algorithm rather than away from it, in numeric estimation and in two much softer, more subjective kinds of forecasting where an algorithm's supposed advantage is far less self-evident than it would be, say, for basic arithmetic. If algorithm aversion says people erroneously punish machines for their errors, algorithm appreciation says people erroneously credit machines even in the complete absence of any evidence about their errors, on tasks where it is not obvious a machine should have any particular edge over a thoughtful person at all. Neither finding, on its own, resolves cleanly into a simple story about what people generally believe about algorithms. Held together, they resist a simple story altogether.

The choice of song popularity and romantic compatibility as two of the tested domains is worth dwelling on, because these are not tasks where a naive intuition would predict an algorithm's advantage. Estimating a quantity from a visual stimulus is at least the kind of problem people generally associate with computation — measurement, calculation, the sort of thing a machine seems built for. Predicting whether a song will become popular or whether two people will get along romantically sits much further from that intuition. These are domains soaked in taste, chemistry, and the kind of pattern recognition people tend to regard as irreducibly human — the sort of judgment a friend with good instincts might be trusted for precisely because a friend has lived experience a spreadsheet does not. That the appreciation effect held even here, where the case for algorithmic advantage is least obvious on its face, suggests the effect is not simply people correctly intuiting that machines are good at machine-shaped problems. It suggests something closer to a general halo attached to the word itself, extending into territory where there is no obvious a priori reason to expect a computational system to outperform a thoughtful person.

Part of what makes the appreciation finding worth taking as seriously as the aversion finding is that the researchers were testing their own genuinely held expectation and found it wrong. This matters for reasons beyond mere academic humility. Predictions in behavioral science that turn out correct can sometimes reflect confirmation of an intuition already widely shared — of course people distrust machines, everyone already suspected as much. A prediction that turns out backward, built by researchers who had good reason, based on the existing literature including algorithm aversion itself, to expect the opposite result, is a stronger signal that the actual psychology at work is more layered than either the popular intuition about technophobia or the emerging academic consensus about algorithm aversion had fully captured.

One structural difference between the two chapters' experimental designs is worth naming plainly, because it does real explanatory work rather than merely reconciling two studies for tidiness's sake. Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's participants watched their forecasters perform, accumulating direct, personally witnessed evidence — including evidence of failure — before making a choice. Logg, Minson, and Moore's participants, in the core appreciation experiments, were given no comparable track record at all; they received a single piece of advice, a label describing its source, and nothing else. The two studies were not testing the same moment in a person's relationship with a system. One was testing what happens to trust already partly built through observed experience, once a salient failure enters that experience. The other was testing what happens before any experience exists at all — the trust a bare label can manufacture in a vacuum, prior to any evidence whatsoever about the source's actual competence. That people behave differently in these two moments is not, on reflection, so surprising. What is worth taking seriously is that the direction of the difference runs the way it does: skepticism after evidence of fallibility, credulity before any evidence at all.

The label itself deserves attention. Nothing about the advice changed between conditions in these experiments — the same estimate, the same forecast, presented identically in every respect except for a single word describing its origin. That single word was doing an enormous amount of work. It suggests that, absent other information, people carry some baseline assumption that a system described as algorithmic brings a kind of objectivity, consistency, or freedom from the biases and moods that color human judgment — an assumption strong enough to move behavior meaningfully even when nothing about the actual quality of the advice has been demonstrated one way or the other. Whether that assumption is generally justified is a separate question the experiments were not built to answer for every domain; what the experiments do establish is that the assumption exists, that it operates on contact with nothing more than a label, and that it is powerful enough to outweigh whatever default suspicion of machines the previous chapter's findings might have led a reader to expect.

The appreciation effect, though, was not uniform across every condition Logg, Minson, and Moore tested, and the way it varied turns out to matter more than the headline result. When participants were choosing between an algorithm's estimate and another person's estimate, the preference for the algorithm was strong and consistent. When the comparison shifted to a choice between the algorithm's estimate and the participant's own judgment, the preference weakened. People were more willing to defer to an algorithm over a stranger than they were to defer to that same algorithm over themselves. This is not a surprising asymmetry in isolation — most people rate their own judgment more highly than an anonymous stranger's, algorithm or not, for reasons that have nothing to do with automation specifically. But it does mean the appreciation effect the researchers found was not a blanket preference for machine judgment over human judgment in general. It was, more precisely, a preference for machine judgment over the judgment of an unfamiliar other person, a distinction that narrows the finding considerably from the version of "people love algorithms" a shorter summary might suggest.

The effect narrowed further, and in a more consequential direction, when the researchers looked specifically at people with real expertise in forecasting — professionals whose actual job involved making the kinds of predictions the studies were testing, not laypeople recruited for a one-off task. Among this group, the preference for algorithmic advice did not simply weaken. It reversed. Professional forecasters relied less on algorithmic advice than laypeople did, choosing their own judgment, or a fellow human forecaster's judgment, over the machine's more often than the untrained participants in the earlier experiments had. And this reduced reliance was not a harmless preference, a matter of professional pride with no real cost attached. The researchers found that it measurably hurt the accuracy of the experts' forecasts. The people with the most relevant experience to judge whether an algorithm's advice was worth taking were also the people most inclined to discount it — and discounting it, in this case, made them worse at the very task their expertise was supposed to make them good at.

This is the finding worth pausing on longest, because it inverts a comfortable assumption running quietly through much of the discussion so far. It would be reasonable to assume that expertise is protective against miscalibrated trust in a machine — that a professional, unlike a layperson recruited for a quick study, has the domain knowledge to correctly judge when an algorithm's advice is good and when it should be ignored, and that expert judgment about when to defer should therefore be more accurate than a novice's, not less. Logg, Minson, and Moore's data does not support that assumption. The experts were not simply more discerning consumers of algorithmic advice, correctly separating the cases where it deserved weight from the cases where it did not. They were, on the whole, worse consumers of it than the laypeople, in the specific sense that their reduced reliance cost them accuracy their expertise should have been protecting rather than undermining.

It is worth asking why expertise would push in this particular direction rather than simply having no effect, or pushing toward better calibration as a naive assumption might predict. One plausible piece of the answer is that expertise generates its own, independently earned confidence, built through years of feedback on the expert's own judgment specifically — a trader who has spent a career learning to read a market, a forecaster who has spent years refining an intuition for a particular kind of prediction, has a calibration loop of their own already running, built entirely around their own track record rather than the algorithm's. Introducing an algorithm's advice into that picture is not like introducing it to a blank slate. It is introducing a competing source of judgment into a system that already has a well-developed, self-referential one in place, and self-referential confidence, however well earned in its own domain, does not automatically make room for an outside source it has not yet had the chance to evaluate on its own terms. A layperson with no comparable track record of their own has less internal confidence competing for the same authority, which may leave more room for an unfamiliar source, bearing a label that suggests objectivity, to be taken at something closer to face value.

It is worth being careful about what this does and does not establish. The finding does not mean expertise is worthless, or that professional judgment is generally inferior to algorithmic judgment across the board — the studies were not built to make that sweeping a claim, and nothing in this chapter should be read as making it on their behalf. What the finding does establish, specifically and importantly, is that possessing real domain expertise did not translate, in these experiments, into better-calibrated trust in the algorithmic advice being offered. If anything, the professionals' greater confidence in their own judgment, presumably built through years of genuine skill and hard-won pattern recognition in their field, seems to have made them quicker to discount an outside source that had not yet had the chance to earn their trust through visible track record inside their own domain — trading a documented statistical advantage for the comfort of relying on judgment they understood and controlled.

Read through the calibration framework introduced earlier in this book, the expert reversal is a strikingly specific case of undertrust — confidence falling short of a system's demonstrated capability — occurring in precisely the population that this book's later chapters will spend the most time examining. The professionals who forecast for a living are not naive users encountering an unfamiliar technology for the first time, the way a layperson recruited for a quick online study might be. They are the radiologists reading a mammogram, the judges receiving a risk score, the warehouse managers overseeing an algorithmically set quota, the traders watching an execution algorithm, the drivers whose vehicle has taken over part of the driving task — the exact population the rest of this book is about. And the pattern Logg, Minson, and Moore found suggests that expert disuse, an underconfidence in algorithmic tools that costs real accuracy, may be a more common and more dangerous default in exactly these high-stakes professional settings than naive overtrust is. That is not a minor footnote to the finding. It is close to a reorientation of where this book's later chapters should expect to find the sharper danger — not primarily among inexperienced people handing too much authority to a machine they do not understand, but among skilled professionals confident enough in their own judgment to discount a tool that, on the evidence, deserved more of their trust than it received.

This reorientation matters enough to state plainly before the book turns to its next several chapters. It would be natural, coming out of a chapter about algorithm aversion and a chapter about algorithm appreciation, to expect the more dramatic professional failures ahead to look like overtrust — a radiologist anchoring too heavily on a confident AI flag, a judge treating a risk score as though it were a verdict rather than one input among several, a driver leaning on a partially automated system past the point its design was ever meant to cover. Those failure modes are real, and this book does not set them aside; automation bias, the tendency of a confident system's output to narrow and anchor a professional's own independent judgment, remains a live danger the chapters ahead will examine directly. But the expert-reversal result adds a second, less intuitive danger sitting right alongside it, in the same professionals, sometimes in the same decision: a radiologist who has seen an algorithm miss once discounting a correct flag on the next case, a judge or a trader more confident in an unaided, experience-built instinct than an unfamiliar score deserves, a driver's practiced hand overriding an assist system that was, in that instance, right. Overtrust and undertrust are not sorted neatly by profession, or by how much training a person has. Both are available to the same expert, in the same domain, depending on what that expert has personally seen and how much confidence their own hard-won judgment has already earned them independent of the machine.

Nor should the expert-reversal finding be read as a case against expertise itself, or as license to treat professional confidence as inherently suspect. The professionals in these studies had presumably earned their confidence the ordinary way, through years of feedback on decisions that mattered, in a domain their algorithm counterpart had no comparable history in. The problem the finding identifies is narrower and more specific than "experts are overconfident": it is that expertise built through one kind of feedback loop, entirely self-referential and unconnected to the specific algorithm now on offer, does not automatically transfer into an accurate read of that particular tool's trustworthiness. An expert's confidence in their own forecasting ability can be perfectly well earned and still tell that expert nothing reliable about how good this specific algorithm happens to be at the same task. Treating the two as substitutable — assuming that being good at the underlying skill makes a person good at judging a machine's competence at that same skill — is the precise error the data suggests experts were making.

It would be tidy, and false to the evidence, to resolve the tension between this chapter and the last one with a single clean rule — something like "people distrust algorithms once burned but trust them blindly beforehand," offered as though that neatly reconciled aversion and appreciation into one coherent story. The studies do not support quite that resolution. Algorithm aversion, in Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's work, persisted even after participants had seen the algorithm's aggregate superiority clearly demonstrated — it was not simply a story about untested, unproven algorithms losing to the benefit of the doubt extended to humans. Algorithm appreciation, in Logg, Minson, and Moore's work, showed up with no errors shown at all, but also weakened and reversed depending on who the algorithm was being compared against and how much relevant expertise the person doing the comparing actually had. The honest description of where this leaves the reader is not a single rule but a genuine, still-open tension: the same broad population of people can be shown, under different conditions, to distrust algorithms more than the evidence warrants and to trust them more than the evidence warrants, sometimes within variations of the very same underlying task.

There is a further asymmetry worth noting between the two chapters' implications for institutions, because it cuts against a comfortable assumption that might otherwise close this chapter too neatly. It would be reassuring to conclude that the two effects roughly cancel out at a population level — that a mix of aversion and appreciation, distributed across enough people and enough decisions, evens out into something close to appropriate reliance on average, even if no individual is perfectly calibrated. Nothing in either study supports that comforting arithmetic. Aversion and appreciation were not shown to be evenly distributed compensating errors drawing from the same pool of decisions; they were shown to depend on specific, identifiable conditions — whether a failure had been personally witnessed, who the algorithm was being compared against, how much relevant expertise the person doing the comparing possessed. A population is not one undifferentiated mass slowly averaging its way to good calibration. It is laypeople encountering algorithms for the first time in one set of conditions, and domain experts encountering the same algorithms in a different set of conditions, and the evidence suggests these two groups are as likely to be miscalibrated in opposite, non-canceling directions as they are to balance each other out.

That tension is not a flaw in either study, and it is not evidence that one of the two findings must be wrong. It is, read through the calibration framework this book has been building since Lee and See, exactly what a genuine calibration failure should be expected to look like at the level of a population rather than a single individual. Calibration, recall, is not a fixed trait a person carries into every situation; it is built through a loop of experience, feedback, and mental model, and that loop can be primed differently by different framings, different domains, and different starting assumptions about what a label like "algorithm" signals before any actual evidence has been supplied. A population without a stable, well-calibrated baseline for algorithmic trust will not produce a single, consistent bias in one direction. It will produce exactly what these two chapters have documented: undertrust triggered by a visible error in one condition, and overtrust triggered by nothing more than a label in another, sometimes in the very same people, depending on what exactly they were being asked to evaluate and against what alternative.

This is not a comfortable place to leave the argument, and it should not be treated as one. It would be easier, and more satisfying as a piece of narrative structure, to let one chapter's finding cleanly correct the other's — algorithm aversion is the problem, algorithm appreciation is the fix, or the reverse. The honest state of the evidence does not offer that resolution, and manufacturing one here would falsify the actual shape of the research to make the chapter feel more finished than it has any right to feel. What can be said, and what the rest of this book will keep testing against harder cases in medicine, criminal justice, warehouses, finance, and driving, is that neither blanket trust nor blanket suspicion of algorithmic judgment is the correct default, that the same population can and does hold both tendencies depending on framing and domain, and that the specific reversal found among expert forecasters — the people whose judgment carries the most weight in exactly the high-stakes settings this book is most concerned with — deserves to be carried forward as a standing warning rather than resolved away. The tension stays open. It has to, because the evidence leaves it open, and a book claiming otherwise would be telling the reader something more comfortable than what the research actually supports. What the next several chapters offer instead of a resolution is a series of settings in which this same unresolved tension plays out for real stakes — a reading room, a courtroom, a warehouse floor, a trading desk, a car — where the question is never simply whether algorithms deserve trust in the abstract, but whether the specific person in front of the specific system, at that specific moment, has any reliable way of knowing which of this chapter's two patterns is currently governing their own judgment.
