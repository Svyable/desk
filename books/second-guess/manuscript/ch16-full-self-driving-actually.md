# Full Self-Driving, Actually

Elaine Herzberg died in 2018, in a test vehicle operated by a company running an early, developmental version of a technology that has since spread widely. The self-driving cars sharing roads with the rest of us today are not the same vehicles, running the same software, under the same rules, as the one in Tempe. Some of them no longer carry a human safety driver at all. The industry's own account of what has changed since then rests heavily on numbers: crash rates, disengagement rates, miles driven, all published by the companies that build and operate the vehicles, offered as evidence that the technology has matured past the failure mode chapter fifteen described. This chapter looks at those numbers directly, from the two companies that publish the most detailed such data, and asks what they can and cannot tell a reader trying to decide how much to trust what is now sharing the road with them.

This chapter's title borrows a phrase Tesla itself uses to market one of its driver-assistance packages, and it is worth pausing on the phrase before turning to the numbers, because the name is doing some of the same work a placebic explanation does in chapter fourteen, shaping trust through form rather than content. A system marketed under a name like Full Self-Driving invites a driver to calibrate trust against the name rather than against the system's documented, and more limited, actual capability, before a single mile has been driven or a single safety figure consulted. This is not, on its own, a claim that Tesla's engineers have misrepresented what their software can do in the technical documentation drivers are required to acknowledge; production vehicles sold with these features still require a licensed, attentive driver in the seat, hands available, ready to intervene. It is a claim about what a name does to a person's prior expectations before the fine print gets read, and about how easily a well-chosen product name can produce exactly the kind of miscalibrated trust chapter six warned about, independent of whatever the safety data underneath the name eventually turns out to show.

Tesla publishes safety data on a recurring basis, comparing crash rates for its vehicles when a driver-assistance system called Autopilot is engaged against crash rates for its vehicles when Autopilot is not active. The company's reported figures describe roughly one crash for every 7.63 million miles driven with Autopilot engaged, against roughly one crash for every 955,000 miles driven without it. Set side by side, that is close to an eightfold difference, and Tesla has consistently used numbers like these to argue that Autopilot makes its cars measurably safer than the same cars driven without the system engaged. More recent quarterly figures complicate the trend even as the company continues to make that argument: the Autopilot-engaged crash rate has been worsening, quarter over quarter, for three consecutive quarters, including a reported rate of roughly one crash per 6.36 million miles in the third quarter of 2025 — still far better than the non-Autopilot baseline, but moving in the wrong direction even as the underlying claim about Autopilot's relative safety has stayed the same.

Waymo, which operates a fully driverless service rather than a driver-assistance feature layered onto privately owned cars, publishes a different kind of figure. Its safety-impact data reports roughly one crash for every 50,000 miles of fully driverless operation, along with a low rate of what the company calls critical disengagements (moments where a human intervention or a safety-relevant handoff was required), at approximately 0.07 per 1,000 miles. Waymo characterizes most of the collisions it does record as low-speed events, and attributes a substantial share of them to another road user's error or illegal driving rather than to a failure of its own system.

It is worth pausing on Waymo's own framing of its collision data before moving to the comparison problem, because the framing itself is a choice worth examining rather than simply reporting. Waymo characterizes most of the collisions its vehicles are involved in as low-speed events, and attributes a substantial share of them to another road user's error or illegal driving rather than to any failure of its own system. That characterization may well be accurate: plenty of collisions genuinely are caused by another driver running a light or a cyclist swerving unpredictably, and a company operating thousands of vehicles has real data to draw on when it makes that attribution. But it is also, notably, the company's own characterization of its own vehicles' involvement in each incident, offered without the kind of independent adjudication a police report contested in court, or an NTSB investigation, would supply. A driverless vehicle that is technically not at fault in a collision, by the traffic-law definition of fault, can still have been involved in a way that a differently designed system, a differently placed sensor, or a different decision in the software might have avoided or mitigated — a distinction that "not at fault" as a category does not, by itself, resolve, and one a company reporting on its own incidents has limited incentive to volunteer.

Set next to each other, these numbers look like they are answering the same question: is the software driving, or partly driving, this car safer than a person doing it alone. They are not, and the differences start with what each figure is actually counting. Tesla's headline comparison is internal to Tesla: Autopilot-engaged miles against non-Autopilot miles, both driven in Tesla vehicles, both reported by Tesla. Waymo's headline figure has no equivalent internal baseline at all; it reports fully driverless miles and a disengagement rate, without an equivalent "same vehicles, human-driven" comparison built into the same report. Even the words each company uses — crash, disengagement, critical incident — do not necessarily mean the same thing from one company's report to the other's; a "disengagement" for a system built around a human safety monitor and a "critical disengagement" for a service built to operate with no human aboard at all are not obviously the same category of event, counted the same way, against the same threshold. A reader trying to place these two companies' safety records on a single shared scale is working with numbers that were not built to be placed on one.

There is also the question of what "engaged" and "not engaged" mean at the boundary, for Tesla's comparison specifically, and it is a question the headline numbers do not answer. A driver approaching a complicated intersection, a construction zone, or an unusual hazard while using Autopilot has the option, and often the practical instinct, to disengage the system and take manual control in exactly the moments a collision is becoming likely — which means some meaningful share of the highest-risk seconds on any given trip may systematically shift from the "Autopilot engaged" column to the "Autopilot not engaged" column at precisely the moment risk peaks, simply because that is when a cautious driver's hand goes to the wheel. If that pattern is happening at any scale, it would inflate the apparent safety of the engaged condition and deflate the apparent safety of the disengaged one, for reasons that have nothing to do with which condition is actually safer in a fixed set of circumstances. This book's sourcing does not include a study isolating how much of the observed gap this effect might account for, and it would be dishonest to assert a specific magnitude it cannot support, but the mechanism is a plausible, structural reason the comparison should be read with more caution than the raw ratio invites on its own.

Start with Tesla's comparison on its own terms, because even accepted at face value it invites a question the company's marketing does not volunteer an answer to: what kind of driving is actually happening in each half of the comparison. Autopilot is a highway-oriented system, engaged predominantly on limited-access roads with few pedestrians, no cross traffic, no intersections, no cyclists weaving between parked cars — structurally one of the lowest-risk driving environments that exists, independent of who or what is doing the driving. Non-Autopilot miles, by contrast, necessarily include everything Autopilot does not cover: city streets, residential neighborhoods, intersections, parking lots, the whole messy mixed environment where most serious crash risk actually concentrates. Comparing a crash rate on the first kind of driving against a crash rate on the second kind is not, primarily, a comparison of automated driving against human driving. It is substantially a comparison of highway driving against everything else, with the automation variable tangled up inside a much larger difference in where and how the miles were actually driven. None of this means Autopilot is unsafe, or that Tesla's numbers are fabricated. It means the eightfold gap the company advertises cannot be cleanly attributed to the software, because the two conditions being compared differ in more than just whether the software was engaged.

The worsening trend compounds the difficulty of reading the number rather than resolving it. A crash rate that gets worse for three consecutive quarters, even while remaining well below the company's own non-Autopilot baseline, is not obviously a story about the technology getting less capable: it could reflect Autopilot being engaged on a wider, riskier range of roads and conditions than it used to be, more drivers using it in circumstances the system was not originally validated for, a changing mix of vehicles and hardware generations in the reporting pool, or several of these at once. Tesla's continued claim that Autopilot remains statistically safer than unassisted human driving may still be true even as the specific number moves in the wrong direction; a declining but still favorable ratio is not the same as a system getting dangerous. But a reader is being asked to trust that interpretation from the same party whose product and reputation benefit from it being the correct one, using a methodology the report itself does not fully disclose, and that is precisely the position chapter six describes as the hardest one to calibrate trust from: no independent feedback loop, no outside audit visible in the figures themselves, only the operator's own account of its own performance, delivered by the party with the largest stake in how that account is read.

Waymo's number invites a parallel, though not identical, kind of scrutiny. A driverless vehicle achieving one crash per 50,000 miles sounds, and may in fact be, a genuinely strong safety record. What the figure does not convey on its own is where those 50,000-mile stretches are actually being driven. Waymo's driverless service does not operate everywhere; it operates in specific, mapped operational areas that the company has chosen, tested, and validated in advance, often cities or neighborhoods selected in part because their road networks, weather, and traffic patterns are more tractable for the technology than harder environments would be. That is a reasonable, even prudent way to deploy a new technology — nobody serious argues a driverless system should be unleashed on every road in the country on day one. But it means Waymo's exposure profile is not the same as an average human driver's unrestricted range of roads, weather, and conditions. A human driver's crash rate, aggregated nationally, includes rural highways at night, unfamiliar cities, severe weather, poorly maintained roads, and every other condition a driverless service has not yet chosen to operate in. Comparing Waymo's driverless crash rate against a general human baseline, the comparison the company's marketing invites even when its own report does not make the comparison explicit, risks measuring the difference between a curated operating environment and an uncurated one nearly as much as it measures the difference between the software and a human being.

The low critical-disengagement rate Waymo reports deserves the same scrutiny its crash figure does, for a related reason: a disengagement is, definitionally, an event the company's own system detects and records, using thresholds and criteria the company itself sets. A low disengagement rate could reflect a genuinely capable system that rarely needs help. It could also partly reflect a company's own definitions and reporting choices about which of its vehicle's on-road struggles rise to the level of a countable disengagement in the first place, a distinction outside observers cannot fully evaluate from a summary figure alone. This is not an accusation that Waymo is manipulating its numbers; it is the same structural point this book has made about self-reported data throughout its middle chapters, applied here: a company grading its own homework is not thereby lying, but the grade it reports is not independently verified, and the space for genuine, good-faith judgment calls in what counts as a disengagement is large enough that two companies, or even two reporting periods from the same company, are not guaranteed to be using the same ruler.

There is a further asymmetry worth naming between the two companies' underlying business models, because it shapes what each company has an incentive to measure and publish in the first place, even setting aside any question of good or bad faith. Tesla sells vehicles to individual owners who then choose, trip by trip, whether to engage a driver-assistance feature; the company's safety report is, among other things, a piece of evidence in an ongoing public argument about whether that feature is a wise choice for an owner to make and whether regulators should let it continue operating as currently designed. Waymo operates a service directly, choosing its own operational areas, setting its own routes, deciding for itself when and where its vehicles drive without waiting for an individual customer's moment-to-moment judgment; its safety report is, among other things, a piece of evidence in an ongoing argument about whether it should be allowed to expand that service into new cities and a wider range of conditions. Both reports are genuine safety data. Both are also, unavoidably, arguments — built, formatted, and released by parties with a direct commercial and regulatory stake in how favorably a reader interprets them, in the specific dispute each company is currently trying to win.

This is worth connecting back to a distinction chapter two drew near the start of this book, between the misuse of automation by an individual operator and its abuse by the designers or organizations that deploy it. A driver who over-trusts Autopilot on a stretch of road it was not built to handle is a case of misuse in Parasuraman and Riley's sense, the operator's own miscalibration. A company that names a system Full Self-Driving, or that publishes an internal safety comparison without disclosing the exposure differences that complicate it, sits closer to the abuse category chapter two named but did not dwell on — an organizational choice that shapes the conditions under which individual misuse becomes more likely, whether or not that was the intent behind the choice. Neither category excuses the other. A driver who takes their hands off the wheel because a name told them they safely could still bears responsibility for that decision. But a book that has spent this many chapters asking how individuals should calibrate trust owes the reader an honest acknowledgment that the companies supplying the evidence for that calibration are not neutral instruments handing over raw data — they are participants in the same trust relationship the data is meant to inform, with their own stake in how the relationship resolves.

It is worth being explicit about what this chapter is not arguing, because the temptation, once the limitations on both sides are laid out, is to conclude that the numbers are worthless or that the companies are being deceptive. Neither conclusion is warranted by what has actually been shown here. Tesla's figures and Waymo's figures are both real data, collected from real operations, published with a level of specificity — precise per-mile rates, quarter-over-quarter tracking, categorized disengagement counts — that most industries never volunteer about their own safety record. That specificity is worth something, and dismissing it entirely would throw away real information along with its real limitations. The honest position sits between credulity and dismissal: these are first-party safety reports, produced by companies whose commercial interest lies substantially in the public reading them favorably, describing operations whose exposure conditions — highway-heavy for Tesla's Autopilot miles, geographically curated for Waymo's driverless miles — are not directly comparable to an unrestricted human driving baseline, and not, in either case, independently audited in the way a regulator-mandated, third-party safety investigation like the one chapter fifteen relied on would be.

It is tempting, faced with two incomparable sets of numbers from two different companies, to ask which one at least seems more trustworthy on its face: Waymo's fully driverless model without an internal human baseline to muddy, or Tesla's model with one built in. That temptation should be resisted as well, because the two companies' figures are not more or less trustworthy as reporting exercises; they are differently limited, in ways that track the different products and business models producing them. Waymo's absence of an internal human-driving comparison is not a mark against its methodology so much as a reflection of what its service actually is — there are no Waymo vehicles driven by a human commuter down the same routes to compare against, because the entire premise of the service is that no human drives them. Tesla's internal comparison exists because its product is built around a human who sometimes drives and sometimes doesn't, in the same car, on an overlapping range of roads. Each report is shaped by the vehicle and business it describes, not by one company having tried harder than the other to tell the reader something true.

That comparison to chapter fifteen's source is worth making directly, because it clarifies exactly what kind of evidence this chapter is working with and what kind it isn't. The NTSB's investigation into the Tempe collision was conducted by an independent federal agency with subpoena power, physical access to the vehicle's systems, and no commercial stake in whether its findings favored the company whose technology it was investigating. Its findings — the classification failure, the 1.2-second detection window, the disabled braking, the distracted safety driver — carry the weight they carry in this book precisely because of who produced them and how. Tesla's and Waymo's safety reports are a different category of evidence entirely: valuable, detailed, and worth taking seriously, but produced, formatted, and released by the companies whose products they describe, without the adversarial scrutiny an independent investigator would bring to bear. A single well-documented federal investigation into one incident and a running set of aggregate company-published statistics about millions of miles are not competing accounts of the same kind of thing; they are different kinds of evidence, with different strengths, and this book has tried to be as clear about the limits of the second kind as it was, in the previous chapter, about the weight the first kind can bear.

This is, in miniature, exactly the calibration problem chapter six named at the book's midpoint, transplanted onto a new kind of evidence: the reader now has to calibrate trust not just in a system, but in the numbers a company offers as proof that the system deserves trust in the first place. Calibration, as chapter six argued, depends on a closed loop of experience, feedback, and an accurate mental model of what a system can actually do, and that loop gets much harder to close when the party supplying most of the available feedback is also the party whose product the feedback is meant to evaluate. A driver deciding whether to trust Autopilot on a given stretch of highway, or a pedestrian deciding how much latitude to extend a Waymo vehicle at an intersection, is not in the position Lee and See's original framework imagined, where trust calibrates against a system's directly observable, independently verifiable track record. They are calibrating against a company's own summary of its own performance, filtered through whatever definitions, exposure conditions, and reporting choices that company has made, largely invisible from the driver's seat or the sidewalk.

Public reaction to autonomous vehicle safety tends to run through exactly the two opposing currents this book named early on, and the aggregate crash-rate figures this chapter has been examining sit awkwardly on top of both of them. Chapter seven showed that people penalize a fallible algorithm more harshly than an equally fallible human after watching it make a single visible mistake, even when the algorithm's overall record remains better. A single dramatic, well-publicized autonomous-vehicle failure, the kind chapter fifteen examined in detail, tends to generate public alarm disproportionate to what an aggregate per-mile crash rate, however imperfect, would suggest about the underlying risk, precisely the pattern the algorithm-aversion research documented in a controlled setting. Chapter eight, meanwhile, showed the opposite tendency running just as strongly among laypeople encountering a system framed as algorithmic rather than human, right up until real expertise entered the picture. A driver's day-to-day experience of a system marketed under a confident, capability-implying name, functioning smoothly across thousands of ordinary miles with no dramatic incident to anchor a correction, plausibly produces exactly the kind of unearned appreciation chapter eight described — a gradual, uneventful drift toward trusting the system more than its documented limitations, including the limitations this chapter has just laid out, would actually justify. Neither reaction is irrational on its own terms. Both are the predictable consequence of trying to calibrate trust in a system using exactly the two kinds of evidence — a single dramatic failure and a long run of uneventful competence — that this book has already shown people are worst equipped to weigh correctly.

None of this settles the question the marketing on both sides implies it should settle: whether Tesla's or Waymo's approach to automated driving is, on balance, safer than the alternative, or safer than a competent human driver operating under comparable conditions. This chapter has not tried to answer that question, because the evidence available to answer it, at the rigor the rest of this book has tried to hold itself to, does not currently exist in a form that would let it be answered honestly. What the evidence supports is narrower and, in its own way, more useful: that both companies' headline safety figures are real, are worth attending to, and are also first-party numbers shaped by exposure conditions, definitions, and reporting choices that neither company's marketing fully surfaces — which means a reader trying to decide how much to trust the car next to them at the light is doing exactly the work this book has described since its opening pages, with less independent evidence to work from than the aviation, medical, and judicial cases examined earlier were able to supply, and with the companies that built the systems controlling much of what evidence exists at all. That is not a verdict on the technology. It is a description of the evidentiary position anyone outside those companies is actually standing in, and an honest book about trusting recommendations has to say so plainly rather than borrowing more certainty from a per-mile ratio than the ratio was built to support.
