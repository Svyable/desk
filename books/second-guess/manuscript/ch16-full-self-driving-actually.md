# Full Self-Driving, Actually

Elaine Herzberg died in 2018 in a test vehicle run by a company using an early, developmental version of automated-driving technology. The systems now sharing public roads with the rest of us are not the same vehicles, not the same software, and not operating under the same rules. Some carry no human safety driver at all. Others are sold to private owners as supervised driver-assistance systems whose names suggest far more independence than their operating instructions permit. The technology has changed enough that the old question—could a machine drive safely at all?—has become less useful than a harder one: how should a person read the evidence when the companies building the systems are also among the main producers of the evidence about whether those systems deserve trust?

That question matters because autonomous-driving debates often collapse into one of two easy positions. One side points to a dramatic failure and treats it as proof that the technology is reckless. The other points to a large mileage denominator and treats it as proof that the technology has already surpassed human driving. Both moves are psychologically attractive. Neither is sufficient.

By 2026, the public evidence is substantially better than it was a few years earlier. It is also more difficult to summarize honestly. Waymo publishes a safety-impact dashboard built around rider-only miles and geography-adjusted human crash benchmarks. Tesla now publishes a separate body of safety evidence for FSD (Supervised) that breaks mileage out by road class and compares collision rates across different control conditions. Those newer materials correct some of the weaknesses in older company reporting. They do not remove the deeper problem this book has been tracing from the beginning: trust calibration depends not only on the number a system reports, but on what the number measures, what it leaves out, and who decided how the comparison would be made.

Start with Waymo, because its present evidence is no longer fairly described as a simple collection of raw crash counts and disengagement statistics. Through March 2026, Waymo reported 220.6 million rider-only miles driven without a human driver across its operating geographies. Its dashboard compares crash outcomes against human benchmarks adjusted to the places where the service actually operates. For serious-injury-or-worse crashes, the all-location rate shown on the dashboard is 0.01 incidents per million rider-only miles for Waymo, against 0.23 for the human benchmark. For crashes involving any reported injury, the dashboard reports 0.71 incidents per million miles for Waymo against 3.91 for the benchmark. In a June 24, 2026 summary, the company described the results as 94 percent fewer serious-or-fatal-injury crashes and 82 percent fewer crashes involving any reported injury than comparable human driving.

Those are not trivial differences. A reader who dismisses them merely because Waymo published them is making the same kind of error as a reader who accepts them without examining the method. The company is not simply dividing its crash count by mileage and comparing the result with a national average. Its current methodology uses crash and vehicle-miles-traveled data from the counties where it operates and applies a dynamic geographic adjustment intended to account for the fact that different parts of a city carry different baseline risks. Waymo also makes downloadable datasets available for miles by geography, crash groupings, and benchmark calculations. That is a more serious evidentiary effort than the generic phrase "company self-reporting" implies.

It still leaves judgment calls.

The first is the operational design domain. Waymo chooses where its service runs. That is not a defect; it is what responsible engineering looks like. A system that performs well in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Austin, and Atlanta has earned something meaningful that it had not earned when the technology was confined to test tracks and safety-driver experiments. But those places are still selected environments, with service boundaries, mapping, weather assumptions, fleet-management rules, remote assistance procedures, and deployment decisions that an ordinary human driver does not get to impose on the world. The correct comparison is therefore not "Waymo drives everywhere better than humans." It is closer to "within the areas and conditions where Waymo currently operates, its rider-only fleet has produced substantially lower rates on several important crash outcomes than a geographically adjusted human benchmark." That claim is narrower. It is also strong enough that there is no need to inflate it.

The second judgment call concerns what kind of outcome deserves the most weight. Any collision is not the same as an injury crash; an airbag deployment is not the same as a fatality; a low-speed contact in a parking environment is not equivalent to a high-energy roadway crash. Safety debates become distorted when every event is pushed into one undifferentiated count. Waymo's current dashboard improves on that by publishing multiple outcome categories. The most persuasive numbers are therefore not the easiest headline counts but the severe-outcome comparisons: serious injury or worse, any reported injury, and airbag deployment. A technology can plausibly reduce the severity distribution of crashes even if it does not eliminate every contact, and that distinction matters more to public safety than whether one denominator produces an impressive-looking number.

The third judgment call is the benchmark itself. Waymo's methodology is better than comparing a robotaxi fleet in a few cities with the entire United States, but a benchmark remains a model. It depends on how crash records are matched to exposure, how geography is weighted, how reporting differences are handled, and what assumptions are made about the human miles that would have occurred in the same places. The company's published methods and downloadable data make those choices more inspectable than they used to be. They do not turn a benchmark into a natural constant.

That is the useful middle position: the evidence has become stronger enough that skepticism must become more specific.

The same is true of Tesla, though the product and the evidence are different. Older discussions of Tesla safety often centered on quarterly Vehicle Safety Reports comparing Autopilot-engaged miles with miles driven without Autopilot. Those figures were always difficult to interpret because the exposure conditions differed. Autopilot use was heavily concentrated on highway driving, while non-Autopilot miles included the more complicated mix of intersections, local streets, pedestrians, cyclists, and urban conflict points. A ratio between those two pools was therefore partly a comparison of driving environments rather than a clean estimate of software effect.

Tesla's newer FSD (Supervised) evidence makes that criticism less complete than it once was. The company's current dashboard reports that FSD (Supervised) accumulated nearly two billion miles in 2025 across all road classes. It also publishes road-class breakdowns that show substantial non-highway use rather than only limited-access freeway exposure. In North America, the dashboard reports hundreds of millions of miles on urban collectors, arterials, and local-access roads. That matters because a critique built on the assumption that the automated miles are almost entirely highway miles no longer describes the present dataset accurately.

Tesla also publishes collision comparisons that separate manually driven vehicles with active safety features from FSD (Supervised) operation. In the company's own analysis, FSD (Supervised) shows lower major and minor collision rates across road classes than manually driven Tesla vehicles with active safety features. It also reports surrogate-safety measures—such as automatic emergency braking activations, harsh acceleration, lateral acceleration, and blinker use—that attempt to capture risk before a collision occurs.

Again, these are not meaningless because Tesla produced them. A dataset approaching two billion miles across multiple road classes is a real evidentiary asset. Road-class stratification directly addresses one of the most obvious confounders in the earlier Autopilot comparisons. Surrogate measures can reveal behavior that crash counts alone miss, especially for rare severe events. Anyone who continues to argue as though the only available Tesla evidence is a highway-heavy Autopilot denominator is arguing with an older version of the record.

But the newer evidence creates different questions rather than ending the need for questions.

One is control selection. Comparing FSD (Supervised) with manually driven Tesla vehicles equipped with active safety features is more informative than comparing one fleet with an unrelated national average, but it is still not random assignment. Drivers choose when to activate FSD. The system may be used more often by certain owners, on certain routes, in certain weather, at certain times, or under conditions where a driver expects it to perform well. Drivers also disengage. Some of those disengagements occur because the human anticipates trouble. If a driver takes over in the seconds before a difficult maneuver, the assignment of subsequent risk between "FSD" and "manual" exposure becomes methodologically important. A comparison can improve dramatically and still remain observational.

Another question is reporting architecture. Tesla owns the vehicles' telemetry, defines the control categories, chooses the inclusion rules, and publishes the analysis. That gives it access to data no outside researcher can independently reproduce at full fleet scale. The asymmetry is structural. It does not imply falsification. It means the reader should distinguish between a company making data available and an independent investigator having equal access to the underlying event stream, software state, and labeling rules.

A third question is what "supervised" means in practice. FSD (Supervised) is not the same product category as Waymo's rider-only service. One system requires a licensed human driver who remains responsible for supervision and intervention. The other is designed to operate without a human driver aboard. A miles-per-collision ratio for one cannot simply be placed beside a rate for the other as if both were trying to solve the same task under the same allocation of responsibility. Tesla's product includes the human as part of the safety architecture. Waymo's product is designed to remove that human from the immediate control loop.

That distinction is not semantic. It reaches back to the problem chapter three called the irony of automation. A supervised system can become safer as automation improves and yet place the human in a more difficult supervisory role precisely because interventions become rarer. A person who drives manually most of the time stays practiced in continuous vehicle control. A person supervising a highly capable system may spend long stretches doing almost nothing before being asked to recognize a novel failure quickly and act well. Better automation can therefore reduce ordinary workload while making the residual human task more abrupt and cognitively strange.

The relevant safety question for a supervised system is not only how often the software makes an error. It is how often the combined human-machine system fails, including failures created by delayed detection, over-trust, confusing handoff, mode misunderstanding, fatigue, distraction, or the simple fact that a driver asked to intervene once every few thousand miles may not be cognitively organized like a driver who has been actively controlling the car for the previous ten minutes.

This is where naming matters. "Full Self-Driving" is still a remarkable product name for a system that requires supervision. A driver can be told in documentation that the system is supervised, limited, and subject to intervention while also being exposed to a name whose ordinary-language meaning points in the opposite direction. The important claim is not that a name hypnotizes users or that people ignore every warning. It is that trust begins with priors, and product language helps create those priors before a driver experiences the system's limits. Chapter six argued that good calibration requires an accurate mental model of capability. A name that suggests autonomy while the operating instructions require vigilance adds friction to that calibration task.

Waymo faces a different communication problem. Its service really is rider-only, which removes the mismatch between product name and supervision role. But the absence of a driver means the public has less direct access to the system's reasoning when something unusual happens. A passenger can experience competence without understanding why the vehicle paused, rerouted, stopped, yielded, or asked for remote assistance. A pedestrian sees behavior but not the decision process. In that sense, Waymo trades one trust problem for another: it removes the human from the driving loop while leaving everyone around the vehicle dependent on a system whose internal state remains largely invisible.

Neither model is automatically superior on trust grounds. They place the burden in different places.

The evidence hierarchy matters because public debate tends to mix unlike sources. A company dashboard built from hundreds of millions or billions of miles can be statistically informative but methodologically dependent on company-controlled data. A regulator's standing-reporting database can cover events across companies but may use broad reporting thresholds that are not designed for fine causal comparison. A police crash report can document an individual event but tell little about fleetwide rates. An NTSB investigation can reconstruct one severe failure with extraordinary depth but cannot, from one case, estimate general safety performance. Peer-reviewed studies may improve methodology while still depending on access to company data or on assumptions needed to build a human benchmark.

The temptation is to choose one source type and declare it "real" while treating the others as propaganda or anecdote. That is rarely the right move. Different sources answer different questions.

The NTSB's investigation of the 2018 Tempe crash, discussed in the previous chapter, could tell us how that failure unfolded: what the system classified, when it detected danger, what braking logic had been disabled, what the safety driver was doing, and how the organization had structured supervision. It could not tell us whether a mature robotaxi fleet in 2026 causes fewer injury crashes per mile than comparable human drivers.

Waymo's 220.6 million rider-only miles can contribute to that latter question. They cannot by themselves tell us what every autonomous system will do outside Waymo's current operating conditions, nor do they erase the need to inspect rare severe failures individually. Tesla's nearly two billion FSD (Supervised) miles can reveal patterns across road classes at a scale outside researchers would struggle to assemble. They cannot by themselves isolate the causal effect of software from every feature of driver selection, activation choice, disengagement behavior, fleet composition, and company-defined event classification.

This is not a weakness peculiar to autonomous vehicles. It is what serious evidence looks like once a technology leaves the laboratory and becomes part of a living system. The cleaner the experiment, the less it resembles the world. The more realistic the deployment, the more confounders arrive with it.

There is another reason to resist easy verdicts: safety improvements can coexist with new categories of failure. A system may reduce rear-end collisions and still create an unfamiliar edge case at a blocked lane. It may reduce drunk-driving exposure while introducing dependence on remote fleet operations. It may avoid many human perception errors while occasionally making a machine perception error no human driver would make. Aggregate improvement does not imply identical failure modes.

That matters psychologically. People are not neutral between familiar and unfamiliar mistakes. Chapter seven's algorithm-aversion experiments showed how quickly a visible machine error can erase trust, even when the machine's average performance remains competitive. A human driver drifting across a lane line after a moment of inattention is tragically familiar. A driverless car freezing in a bizarre position or making an apparently inexplicable maneuver is memorable precisely because the failure is alien. The strangeness of the error becomes evidence in the public mind, even when it says little about the base rate.

The reverse bias is equally real. A system that performs smoothly for thousands of miles can teach a driver that supervision is unnecessary long before the system's formal limitations have changed. Uneventful competence is persuasive. It is also dangerous when the human's role is to remain ready for the rare exception. The better the system becomes at ordinary driving, the less frequent the feedback that reminds the person where its boundary lies.

This is why a supervised system and a rider-only system should be evaluated differently even if both use neural networks, cameras, maps, and large-scale fleet learning. For the supervised system, safety includes the quality of the human-machine handoff and the design of attention management. For the rider-only system, safety includes the operator's deployment boundaries, remote-assistance architecture, incident response, and the rigor of deciding where the service should not yet drive. One keeps the human inside the immediate control loop. The other moves humans outward into design, operations, support, policy, and fleet governance.

The word "autonomy" hides that redistribution of work. No serious automated-driving system is independent of humans in the broader sense. People choose the training data, build the maps, maintain the fleet, define intervention rules, investigate incidents, update software, determine geofences, write reporting standards, set regulatory thresholds, and decide when a new city is ready. The human does not disappear. The human moves.

That observation changes the responsibility question as well. When a supervised Tesla crashes, responsibility can be distributed among the driver's attention, the system's behavior, interface design, warnings, feature naming, software limitations, and the circumstances of the road. When a rider-only Waymo is involved in a collision, there may be no driver to blame or praise, but responsibility still exists across system design, operations, deployment policy, maintenance, and organizational decisions. Automation does not remove responsibility. It makes responsibility less visually obvious at the moment of failure.

A useful reader should therefore ask more than whether one headline rate is lower than another. Ask what population of miles produced the rate. Ask what road classes are represented. Ask whether the comparison is geographic, national, internal, or modeled. Ask who chose the benchmark. Ask whether severe outcomes are separated from minor contacts. Ask whether the system is supervised or rider-only. Ask what happens immediately before a disengagement or intervention. Ask whether the underlying data can be downloaded, whether methods are published, and whether outside researchers can reproduce the calculation. Ask what evidence would change the company's own conclusion.

Those questions are not a way of refusing to believe any result. They are a way of allowing strong evidence to remain strong without asking it to prove more than it can.

By that standard, Waymo's 2026 public record deserves more weight than a skeptical reading based on old disengagement counts would give it. Its rider-only mileage is large, its severe-crash comparisons are favorable, and its methodology makes a serious attempt to align human exposure with the geographies where the service operates. The remaining caveats concern benchmark construction, operational-domain selection, rare-event uncertainty, and the ordinary limits of company-controlled telemetry—not the claim that there is no human comparison at all.

Tesla's newer FSD (Supervised) evidence likewise deserves a more current critique than the old claim that its automated miles are simply highway miles compared with everything else. The dashboard explicitly reports substantial exposure across multiple non-highway road classes and presents road-class collision analyses. The harder questions now concern observational selection, disengagement boundaries, telemetry control, category definitions, and the safety implications of placing the human in a supervisory role inside a system that has become capable enough to invite long stretches of passivity.

That is progress in two senses. The technology has progressed, and the argument about the technology has to progress with it.

The right conclusion is therefore not that autonomous driving is proven safe, nor that company evidence cannot be trusted. It is that the evidentiary position has matured beyond slogans. Waymo can now point to hundreds of millions of rider-only miles and large reductions in several serious crash categories against adjusted human benchmarks. Tesla can point to nearly two billion FSD (Supervised) miles across road classes and favorable collision comparisons within its own fleet. Those facts should update a reasonable person's beliefs.

They should not end the inquiry.

The final calibration problem is the same one this book has returned to in aviation, medicine, courts, warehouses, finance, and every other domain where a recommendation system begins to carry part of a human judgment task. Capability is not the same as evidence. Evidence is not the same as explanation. Explanation is not the same as independent verification. And independent verification is not the same as a guarantee that a system will behave well in the next unfamiliar case.

A driver approaching a strange construction pattern, a passenger sitting in the back of a robotaxi, a regulator considering a new operating area, and a pedestrian stepping into a crosswalk all encounter different slices of the same problem. None can see the entire system. Each has to decide how much trust to place in a machine whose ordinary performance may be excellent and whose failures may be rare, unfamiliar, and consequential.

That is what makes the present autonomous-driving evidence so useful for this book. It is no longer thin enough to dismiss. It is no longer crude enough to caricature. It has become exactly the kind of evidence adults usually have to work with: substantial, improving, interested, incomplete, and good enough to force a real judgment.
