# The Decision on the Hudson

On the afternoon of January 15, 2009, an Airbus A320 registered N106US pushed back from a gate at LaGuardia Airport, bound for Charlotte. Winter had settled hard over New York that week, the kind of cold that makes a runway look longer than it is and a river look colder than it will actually feel. In the cockpit sat Captain Chesley Sullenberger and First Officer Jeffrey Skiles. Behind them, the rest of the 155 people who would be aboard for the flight had found their seats, stowed their bags, and gone back to whatever they had been thinking about before boarding: a meeting in Charlotte, a connection, a nap.

The takeoff itself was unremarkable. US Airways Flight 1549 lifted off LaGuardia's runway and began its assigned climb, the city sliding away beneath it in the flat gray light of a January afternoon. For roughly two and a half minutes, nothing about this flight would have distinguished it from any of the several hundred others departing New York's airports that day.

Then, at about 2,818 feet, the airplane flew into a flock of Canada geese.

Geese travel in numbers, and at altitude and airspeed there is no maneuver available to a pilot that reliably avoids a flock that appears in the windscreen with only a second or two of warning. Birds struck the fuselage. Birds went into both engines. An Airbus A320 has two, mounted under the wings, each one a turbine assembly designed to move enormous volumes of air smoothly and continuously; a bird ingested into that airflow is, from the engine's perspective, indistinguishable from a piece of the engine itself failing. Within seconds, both engines had lost nearly all of their thrust.

A jet engine at climb power is one of the louder machines human beings regularly sit beside. Passengers who fly often enough stop noticing the sound at all; it becomes the ambient hum of getting somewhere. What they notice, if it happens, is the opposite: the abrupt subtraction of a noise that had been present the entire flight, replaced by something closer to the sound of gliding, of wind over metal with nothing pushing back against it. Whatever the passengers aboard Flight 1549 understood about jet propulsion, most of them would have registered that subtraction before they understood its cause. Something had gone wrong. What, and how badly, was not yet clear to anyone sitting behind the cockpit door.

It was entirely clear to the two men in front of it.

This is the part of the story that is easy to state flatly and hard to sit with. A twin-engine passenger jet, climbing out over one of the most densely populated regions in North America, had just lost both of its engines at the same time, less than three minutes after leaving the ground. There was no third engine to fall back on. There was no long runway rolling out ahead of them, the way there might have been on an aborted takeoff. There was altitude, there was airspeed, and there was very little of either.

What Sullenberger and Skiles had, in that moment, was each other, a checklist for restarting an engine that was not going to restart in time to matter, and a very short window in which to decide what an airplane with no thrust and less than three thousand feet of altitude was going to do next.

It is worth pausing on what they did not have, because the absence turns out to matter more to this book than the presence of anything in that cockpit. The A320's flight computers could tell them their airspeed, their altitude, their rate of descent, their position relative to the surrounding terrain and airports. What none of those systems could tell them was whether a stricken jet with two dead engines could actually reach a specific runway from a specific altitude at a specific distance, factoring in the descent profile of an aircraft with no thrust, the time already consumed by diagnosing the failure, and the seconds still required to communicate a decision, get a clearance, and turn. That calculation — not the readout of any single instrument, but the judgment about whether a chain of physical possibilities could still be closed before the ground arrived — belonged entirely to the two men in the cockpit. No system on the airplane was built to answer it, because building a system to answer it had never seemed necessary. Dual engine failures at low altitude, over a major city, were rare enough that nobody had engineered a computer to recommend a landing site for one.

The airplane could, in a sense, tell them everything except the one thing they needed most. It could report numbers with precision — a rate of descent expressed to the foot per minute, an airspeed expressed to the knot — while remaining entirely silent on what those numbers meant for the three choices actually in front of the crew. A pilot reading an altimeter in that moment was not being handed an answer. He was being handed a raw material, one ingredient among several that a human mind still had to combine, weigh, and convert into a decision no instrument on the panel was prepared to make for him.

So Sullenberger and Skiles did the calculation themselves, in real time, with the ground getting closer. Part of that work was procedural: running through the checklist for restarting engines that had ingested enough bird material to be unlikely to restart, because the checklist has to be run regardless of how unlikely success seems, and because abandoning it a second too early is its own kind of error. Part of the work was communicative: Skiles and Sullenberger dividing the cockpit's tasks between them, one flying the airplane while the other worked the immediate emergency procedures, the ordinary division of labor a two-pilot crew exists to provide. And part of it was the harder work underneath both of those tasks — the continuous, silent recalculation of where, exactly, an airplane with this much altitude and this little thrust could still go.

They considered returning to LaGuardia. It was the instinctive answer, the one that matched everything a pilot trains to do when something goes wrong shortly after takeoff: turn back toward the runway you just left. Sullenberger raised the possibility with air traffic control, and controllers began working urgently to clear a path back to the airport, offering a runway, trying to buy him room. But the calculation that mattered was not what the airport could offer; it was what the airplane could still do. A jet gliding without thrust trades altitude for distance at a fixed and unforgiving rate, and by the time the extent of the failure was clear, the airplane had already used altitude it could not get back. Turning around costs distance in both directions — first to turn, then to fly back the way you came — and every foot of that costs altitude an engineless airplane cannot spend twice.

Teterboro Airport, across the Hudson in New Jersey, came up next. Teterboro controllers, alerted to the emergency, began clearing a runway for an aircraft that might be arriving with no engines and no second chance. It was closer than LaGuardia in some respects and further in ways that mattered more. Sullenberger and Skiles weighed it the same way they had weighed the turn back to LaGuardia: not as a hopeful option to try, but as a specific physical proposition to be tested against altitude, distance, and time, and found wanting.

There is a particular cruelty to a decision like this one, which is that it does not present itself as a single choice made once. It presents itself as a chain of smaller choices, each one narrowing what remains available for the next. Every second spent evaluating LaGuardia was a second the airplane was also losing altitude toward the ground, which meant that ruling out LaGuardia did not return the clock to zero for evaluating Teterboro; it started the Teterboro evaluation already poorer than it would have been if the airplane had considered Teterboro first. The three options were not laid out on an even table for Sullenberger and Skiles to compare at leisure. They were tested one after another, in a descending glide, with each rejected option spending some of the resource — altitude, time, distance — that the next option would need to succeed.

What was left, by the time the airplane had descended through both alternatives and found each one wanting, was the river.

The Hudson, in January, is not a soft landing. It is cold enough to kill a person in minutes if the ditching itself does not, wide enough to offer a straight, obstruction-free surface an airport runway cannot always guarantee, and utterly unforgiving of a mistake in bank angle, pitch, or timing. Choosing it was not choosing safety over risk. It was choosing the option that the numbers said was actually achievable over two options the numbers said were not, however much the human instinct to reach solid ground might have argued otherwise.

Sullenberger told air traffic control he would be landing in the Hudson. Then he and Skiles worked the airplane down toward the water, no thrust available to correct a misjudged approach, no second attempt possible if the first one went wrong.

The airplane came down onto the river's surface in a controlled descent, wings level, at a point in midtown Manhattan's stretch of the Hudson where ferries already ran regular commuter routes back and forth across the water. A water landing punishes an uneven touchdown far less forgivingly than a runway does; a wing that catches the surface first, or a nose brought down too steeply, can turn a controlled descent into an uncontrolled one in the last seconds before contact, when there is no more altitude left to correct with. None of that happened here. The airplane met the water roughly level, intact, and floating, which was itself a second decision made correctly under conditions that offered almost no room to make it any other way.

Those ferries already running the Hudson that afternoon were close enough, and their crews attentive enough, to reach the airplane within minutes of it settling onto the surface, well before anyone in the water or on the wings could have been expected to survive prolonged exposure to January water temperatures on their own. Passengers came out onto the wings and into inflatable slides doubling as rafts, standing in a January wind on a floating fuselage while boats converged from several directions at once. The evacuation held together. Nobody was left behind.

All 155 people aboard survived. Of that number, about 100 sustained injuries in the event — the impact, the cold, the evacuation itself — and five of those injuries were serious. No one died. A twin-engine jet airliner, over one of the most crowded urban corridors in the country, had lost all thrust below three thousand feet and put every person aboard back on land alive.

It would be easy, in the retelling, to let that outcome do all the work — to treat the survival of 155 people as proof, on its own, that the decision to ditch had been the correct one. But an outcome is not the same thing as a demonstration, and the National Transportation Safety Board, whose job was to investigate the accident rather than celebrate it, did not treat it that way either. The NTSB's final report on the accident, AAR-10/03, did conclude that the decision to ditch in the Hudson was the appropriate one under the circumstances. It reached that conclusion, though, not by pointing at the survival count, but by reconstructing the decision itself — rebuilding, in simulators, the choice Sullenberger and Skiles had faced, and testing whether the alternative they had rejected might actually have worked.

What the reconstruction found should complicate any tidy version of this story. When professional test pilots flew the scenario in a simulator with advance knowledge that a dual engine failure was coming — knowing, before it happened, exactly what would go wrong and exactly when — they were able to turn back and land at LaGuardia. Barely. The margins were close even under those artificially favorable conditions. But when the simulator runs were adjusted to match the actual timeline a real crew would have faced — accounting for the seconds it takes to recognize that both engines have failed, to diagnose the extent of the failure, to decide what to do about it, the ordinary and unavoidable cost of being human and being surprised — those same test pilots, with the same skill and the same airplane, could not make it back to LaGuardia. The version of the flight that assumed foreknowledge worked. The version of the flight that matched what Sullenberger and Skiles actually experienced did not.

That is the finding that gives this event its weight, and it belongs to the NTSB's reconstruction, not to Sullenberger's own account of what he had decided in the cockpit. It is one thing for a pilot to say, afterward, that he believed the return to LaGuardia was not achievable. It is another thing for accident investigators, working independently, with simulators and timing data and no stake in vindicating anyone, to test that belief against the physics of the situation and find that it held. The decision to ditch was not simply defensible because it worked. It was, on the evidence the NTSB assembled after the fact, very likely the only option that could have worked at all.

The gap between the two simulator conditions is worth sitting with a moment longer, because it says something about human judgment that has nothing to do with skill. The test pilots who flew the foreknowledge scenario were not braver or sharper than Sullenberger and Skiles; if anything, the comparison flatters the real crew, since the test pilots had been told exactly what would happen and when, and still found the margins tight. What separated the two outcomes was time — specifically, the several seconds a real crew has to spend simply confirming that the emergency they suspect is the emergency actually occurring, before they can even begin to act on it. Those seconds do not feel like much when they are described afterward in a paragraph. In an airplane trading altitude for time at a fixed rate, they were the entire difference between a scenario where turning back worked and one where it did not. The NTSB's reconstruction did not just vindicate a decision; it quantified, in a small and specific way, what surprise actually costs.

None of this happened in a vacuum built by one man. Skiles was in that cockpit too, running through the same rapid, joint process of diagnosis and elimination alongside Sullenberger, and the accident record does not describe a single pilot solving the problem alone while a second pilot watched. It is a two-person cockpit for a reason: the workload of flying the airplane, running emergency procedures, and communicating with air traffic control in the same ninety-second span is more than any one person reliably manages, and the accident report reflects a divided, cooperative effort rather than a solitary one. Air traffic controllers at LaGuardia and Teterboro were working the problem from the ground in real time, clearing paths that were ultimately not used but that had to be prepared in case the calculation had gone differently, coordinating with a crew that could not afford to waste a transmission. And the ferries on the Hudson that afternoon were already there, already running their routes, already close enough and manned by people alert enough to close the gap between a successful ditching and a successful rescue before the cold made the distinction meaningless.

It is tempting, and understandable, to compress a story like this one into a single name — to let "Sullenberger" stand in for the whole outcome, the way a headline needs a subject and a photograph needs a face. The record itself resists that compression. Sullenberger's judgment sat at the center of what happened on January 15, 2009, but it did not sit there alone, and the outcome depended on a wider set of people and systems being ready, independently, at the moment they happened to be needed. A different first officer, a slower controller, ferries running a lighter schedule that afternoon: any one of those variables shifted, and the ending most people know might have looked very different, whatever Sullenberger himself had decided in the cockpit.

It is worth returning, now, to the earlier observation about what was missing from that cockpit, because it is the reason this book opens with this flight rather than with almost any comparable event from the years since.

Sullenberger and Skiles were operating one of the most sophisticated commercial aircraft flying at the time, an airplane whose flight computers managed fuel, monitored systems, held attitude, and could, under normal circumstances, fly an approach and landing with minimal pilot input. And yet at the exact moment when the stakes of a decision were highest — when the choice was not how to fly the airplane but where, among three fundamentally different and mutually exclusive options, to try to put it down — none of that computing power had anything to offer them. The instruments could report what the airplane was doing. Nothing on board could tell them which of three landing sites was actually reachable, because no one had built that capability, and building it had never seemed urgent enough to prioritize over the thousand other things a modern flight-management system is asked to do.

This was, in other words, an automation-poor moment inside an automation-rich airplane. The machine had plenty to say about airspeed and altitude and rate of descent. It had nothing to say about the one question that actually determined whether 155 people would live or die that afternoon. That gap was filled entirely by two trained pilots doing arithmetic and judgment under pressure, informed by their training, their experience, and whatever they could read off the instruments in the seconds available to them — but the final call, the actual decision, was made by human minds working without a recommendation from the machine, because the machine had no recommendation to give.

Picture the same failure occurring today, or a few years from now, on an airplane whose systems have grown considerably more capable of proposing an answer rather than only reporting inputs. Picture, more to the point, a version of this problem outside aviation altogether — a radiologist looking at a scan with a diagnostic tool already flagging a region of interest, a judge reading a file with a risk score already printed at the top of it, a driver behind a wheel that is already suggesting, or performing, part of the maneuver ahead. In each of those settings, the person is not starting from the blank instrument panel Sullenberger and Skiles faced. They are starting from a proposal, arrived at by some process they may or may not understand, urging a particular answer before they have finished forming their own. The question is no longer only "what should I do." It has become, first, "should I do what this is telling me to do" — a question layered on top of the original one, answerable in far less time than a gliding A320 bought that afternoon, and often by someone with far less specific command of the problem than two experienced airline pilots brought to a cockpit.

That absence is not a detail to note in passing. It is close to the whole reason this event belongs at the front of a book about trusting, overriding, or double-checking machines. Sullenberger did not have to decide whether to believe a computer's suggestion, because there was no suggestion on offer. He had to reason the problem through from first principles, in real time, the way pilots had done for as long as there had been powered flight going wrong at altitude. The moment of judgment was entirely his and Skiles's to make, unmediated by any system claiming to know better, or even to know at all.

That kind of moment is becoming rarer.

Across aviation and medicine and criminal justice and warehouses and finance and the ordinary act of driving to work, the shape of the decision Sullenberger faced is being handed, more and more, to systems that do have something to say about it — that will, increasingly, offer a recommendation before a person has fully framed the question themselves. A flight-management computer that once could only report instrument readings can now, in more advanced forms, propose a course of action. A diagnostic tool can flag a finding before a physician has finished examining the image. A scoring system can suggest a risk level before a judge has finished reading the file. In each of these settings, the person under pressure is no longer solving the problem from a blank instrument panel the way Sullenberger and Skiles were. They are being handed an answer, often a fast one, often a plausible one, and asked — usually with far less time than a gliding airplane's altitude bought that afternoon, and often with far less specific expertise than two experienced pilots brought to a cockpit — whether to accept it.

Sullenberger's decision has been told, retold, and turned into a familiar kind of American story: a skilled professional, calm under pressure, does the right thing when it matters most. That story is not wrong, exactly. It is, however, an incomplete one if it stops at individual competence, because competence alone does not explain why the return to LaGuardia would have failed for a crew that lacked advance warning, or why a river landing that killed no one required a controlled touchdown, an intact fuselage, and rescue boats already positioned within reach. The fuller story is a story about a decision made in the near-total absence of the very thing this book is actually about. The Hudson gave Sullenberger and Skiles no answer to accept or reject. It only gave them a problem, and the freedom, terrible as that freedom was in the moment, to work out the answer themselves, using training built for exactly this kind of situation and nothing resembling a machine's opinion about which option to choose.

What happens to that freedom — and to the judgment it requires — when the freedom disappears, and the machine has an answer waiting before the person has even finished asking the question? Does the judgment atrophy from lack of practice, the way an unused muscle does, so that when a rare moment finally does demand it, the person called on to exercise it is less prepared than Sullenberger and Skiles were that afternoon, precisely because they have exercised it less often? Or does something else happen — does the presence of a recommendation change not just how much judgment a person exercises, but what kind, shifting the whole task from solving the problem to deciding whether to trust an answer someone, or something, else already reached?

That is the story this book actually needs to tell, and it does not begin over the Hudson River. It begins with what comes after: the growing number of moments when someone in Sullenberger's position looks up from the instruments not to an empty panel demanding a decision, but to a recommendation already glowing on the screen, and has to decide, in far less time and often with far less standing to argue with it, whether it deserves to be believed.
