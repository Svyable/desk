# Trust, Miscalibrated

On the night of June 1, 2009, the crew of Air France 447 did not stop trusting their instruments. If anything, the opposite problem set in: they trusted a set of displays and automated systems that had, in that moment, stopped deserving the confidence built up over thousands of earlier, uneventful flights. A frozen pitot tube had handed the crew unreliable airspeed data; the autopilot, unable to make sense of the readings, disconnected and returned control without warning; and the pilots, startled and working from displays that no longer matched the raw information a pre-automation crew would have relied on, could not reconcile what they were seeing with what was actually happening to the aircraft. The BEA's investigation into the accident, and the broader pattern American Airlines Captain Warren VanderBurgh had already diagnosed more than a decade earlier under the name "children of the magenta," describe the same underlying failure from different vantage points: a trust built for one set of conditions did not update fast enough when the conditions changed.

Trust, in other words, was not simply present or absent that night. It was miscalibrated — pointed at the wrong level for what the system in front of the crew could actually do at that specific moment. That distinction, between trust as a quantity and trust as a correctly or incorrectly calibrated quantity, is the idea this chapter exists to introduce properly, because nearly everything the rest of this book examines is a variation on it.

It is a distinction easy to state and surprisingly easy to lose sight of in practice, because most everyday language about trust in machines does not make it. People speak of trusting a GPS app, a spam filter, a self-checkout scanner, or a medical device the way they speak of trusting a friend — as though trust were a single quantity that simply goes up with good experiences and down with bad ones, and the only interesting question were how much of it a person currently has. That framing misses the piece that actually determines whether a decision goes well: not how much trust someone holds, but whether that amount happens to be right for the system, the task, and the moment in front of them. A crew that trusted their instruments completely through years of routine flying was not behaving foolishly for most of that time; the instruments, under those conditions, generally deserved it. The failure was not a general excess of trust. It was trust that did not update — could not update quickly enough — once the conditions that had earned it stopped holding.

The formal version of the idea comes from a 2004 paper by two human-factors researchers, John D. Lee and Katrina A. See, called "Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance," published in the journal Human Factors. It has become one of the most cited papers in the automation-trust literature — by later bibliometric counts, appearing somewhere in the citation trail of roughly a quarter of the research published on the topic since. Its influence is not really about a single striking finding, the way some famous papers in this book are built around a dramatic experiment or a shocking statistic. Lee and See's contribution was conceptual: they took a word — trust — that engineers, psychologists, and designers had all been using loosely and sometimes inconsistently, and gave it a definition precise enough to build research and design decisions on top of.

Their central move was to separate two things that get blurred together in ordinary speech. One is trustworthiness: how reliable, accurate, and competent an automated system actually is, at a given task, under given conditions. This is, at least in principle, a measurable property of the system itself — its error rate, the range of conditions it performs well in, the ways and frequencies with which it fails. The other is trust: the human operator's subjective confidence in that system, the internal state that leads someone to rely on it, defer to it, or act on its recommendation without further checking. Trust is a belief. Trustworthiness is a fact about the world, or as close to a fact as engineering and testing can establish. Calibration is the word Lee and See use for the relationship between the two — specifically, for how closely a person's trust tracks the system's actual, current trustworthiness.

Stated this plainly, the idea can sound almost too simple to be worth a landmark paper. Of course trust should match reality; who would argue otherwise? But the simplicity is exactly what makes it useful, because it reframes a question this book has so far treated somewhat loosely — should people trust automation more, or less? — into a sharper one that has an actual answer in every specific case: does this person's trust in this system, right now, match what the system can actually do, right now? Phrased that way, "trust more" and "trust less" both stop being coherent general advice. The correct amount of trust is not a universal setting. It depends entirely on the system, the task, and the moment, and the only failure that matters is the gap between confidence and capability, in either direction.

Lee and See were writing into a research field that had, up to that point, tended to treat trust as something close to a personality variable — a stable trait some operators had more of and some had less of, the way some people are naturally more trusting of strangers than others. That framing produced a certain kind of research: surveys measuring how "trusting" a given population was of automation in general, studies correlating dispositional trust with age or occupation or prior experience with technology. None of it was wrong, exactly, but none of it answered the question that mattered most to a designer or a safety regulator, which was not how trusting people are in the abstract but whether their trust, in a particular system doing a particular job, happened to be accurate. A person can be a generally trusting individual and still be badly undertrusting of one specific, highly reliable machine because of a single early bad experience with it. A person can be a generally skeptical individual and still be dangerously overtrusting of one specific system because its interface, its confident tone, or its impressive track record on the easy cases has quietly earned more deference than its performance on the hard cases warrants. Calibration is what happens at the level of the specific relationship between one operator and one system, not at the level of a personality trait, and Lee and See's paper is largely an argument for taking that specificity seriously.

Lee and See's framework names two ways that gap can open. Overtrust is trust that exceeds a system's actual capability — confidence in excess of what the evidence supports. This is the psychological seed of what Chapter 2 called misuse: automation bias, complacency, an operator who stops checking because the system has been right often enough that checking starts to feel unnecessary, right up until the moment it is not. Undertrust is the mirror failure, trust that falls short of a system's actual capability — confidence deficient relative to the evidence. This is the seed of disuse: an operator who ignores, second-guesses, or abandons a system that would, if used, have produced a better outcome than the operator's own unaided judgment. Both failures are still failures of the same underlying process. A person who overtrusts a system and a person who undertrusts it are not making opposite mistakes in the sense of one being cautious and the other reckless; they are both making the same mistake, in opposite directions — failing to match confidence to demonstrated capability.

This is worth dwelling on because it corrects a natural intuition that runs through a great deal of popular writing about automation and artificial intelligence, an intuition this book's earlier chapters have already tried to complicate. The intuitive story treats trust as a dial with a safe direction: turn it down, be skeptical, keep a human in the loop, and the danger of over-relying on a flawed machine goes away. Lee and See's framework does not support that story. Turning the dial down does not produce safety; it produces undertrust whenever the system genuinely deserved more confidence than it was given, and undertrust has its own body count, its own version of a warehouse worker overruled by a rate the algorithm insists is achievable, or a radiologist discounting a correct flag because an earlier one from the same system turned out to be wrong. There is no safe direction to lean the dial, because there is no dial. There is only a target — the system's actual, current trustworthiness — that a person's trust can land on, overshoot, or undershoot.

The word "current" in that sentence is doing real work, because one of the more demanding implications of Lee and See's framework is that trustworthiness is not a fixed property that gets established once and then holds. A system can be highly trustworthy in the conditions it was built and tested for and much less trustworthy outside them — a flight-management computer that performs flawlessly through the great majority of routine flight and far less predictably the moment its sensor inputs go bad, an image-recognition system tuned to one population's data and less reliable applied to another's, a risk model built on historical patterns that stop holding once circumstances change. A well-calibrated operator is not simply someone who has arrived, once, at the correct fixed amount of trust for a given machine. They are someone whose trust tracks a moving target — rising in the conditions where the system reliably performs well, falling in the conditions where it does not — which is a much harder ongoing task than picking a single confidence level and holding it.

There is also a category of system for which the moving target is not merely a matter of conditions but a matter of the operator's own understanding. Two operators can face an identical machine, in identical conditions, and calibrate very differently depending on how well each one grasps what the system is actually doing under the hood. A pilot who understands why a flight-management computer behaves a certain way in icing conditions can calibrate trust to that specific vulnerability; a pilot who has only ever learned which buttons produce which results, without a working model of the underlying logic, has no comparable basis for adjusting confidence when conditions shift outside the system's normal operating envelope. This is close to what "children of the magenta" describes in practice — proficiency with an interface mistaken for understanding of the system behind it, which works fine until the interface stops matching reality and the operator has no independent model to fall back on. Calibration, in other words, is not purely a matter of how much feedback an operator receives. It is a matter of whether the operator has the conceptual tools to interpret that feedback correctly once it arrives.

This is where Lee and See's paper makes its second major contribution, and the one this book will keep returning to: calibration is not something a person decides. It is something that develops, correctly or incorrectly, through a closed loop of experience, feedback, and the mental model an operator builds of how the system actually works. An operator starts with some initial expectation — shaped by a system's reputation, its marketing, a training class, or simply the operator's general disposition toward machines. That expectation shapes how much the operator relies on the system in early encounters. Reliance shapes what the operator observes: someone who defers heavily sees the system succeed often (because most of the time it does succeed) and rarely sees it fail in a way that would prompt reconsideration, while someone who checks constantly sees a more complete picture, including whatever errors the system does make, but pays a real cost in effort and slowed performance for all that checking. What the operator observes then updates the mental model — the operator's working theory of what the system is good at, what it struggles with, and why — and the updated mental model feeds back into how much the operator relies on the system next time. Trust calibration, on this account, is less like a decision than like a habit, formed the way habits usually form: through repeated interaction, feedback, and whatever pattern that feedback happens to reinforce.

The loop structure matters because it explains why miscalibration, once established, tends to be self-perpetuating rather than self-correcting. Consider what happens inside that loop when the operator is also the kind of supervisory monitor described in the previous chapter — someone assigned to watch an automated system for its rare failures, under exactly the attentional conditions vigilance research says erode detection within the first half hour of a watch. If that operator's vigilance decrement causes them to miss some of the system's actual errors, the loop does not register those errors as errors. It registers them as further evidence of a smoothly functioning system, because from the operator's degraded vantage point, nothing appeared to go wrong. Trust drifts upward, not because the system became more trustworthy, but because the feedback loop that should have registered its failures was itself compromised by the very attentional limits Chapter 5 described. Vigilance and calibration turn out not to be two separate problems stacked on top of each other. A failure in one directly corrupts the other: a monitor who cannot reliably see the system fail cannot build an accurate mental model of how often, or under what conditions, it does.

The loop also depends on a resource that is not always available: legible feedback about why the system succeeded or failed. An operator who sees a system produce a wrong output, and can trace that error to a specific, understandable cause, gets to update a genuine mental model — this system struggles with this kind of input, under these conditions. An operator who sees only that the system was wrong, with no visibility into why, is left to guess, and guesses tend to either overgeneralize a single failure into a broad, unwarranted distrust or dismiss it as a one-off fluke that teaches nothing. Some systems make this especially difficult by design, not through malice but through simple opacity — a scoring method treated as proprietary, a model too complex for its own designers to fully explain, an interface that reports a conclusion without reporting a reason. A person facing such a system cannot run the calibration loop the way Lee and See describe it, because one of the loop's essential inputs, legible feedback tied to an interpretable cause, has been removed before the loop even starts. Later chapters in this book return to exactly this condition, in settings where the stakes of an uncalibrated guess are considerably higher than a laboratory task.

The same loop runs in the opposite direction with equally corrosive results. An operator who experiences a single, highly visible error from a system — one that is impossible to miss, unlike the errors vigilance may quietly hide — can update far more sharply than the underlying statistics justify. A system that is right the overwhelming majority of the time can still, after one salient and memorable failure, find its user's trust collapsing well below what its overall track record warrants. The loop does not weigh evidence the way a careful statistician would, discounting a single data point against a long run of successes. It weighs evidence the way lived experience generally does, giving outsized influence to whatever was most recent, most vivid, or most personally costly. This is the undertrust half of the same closed loop that produces overtrust in the vigilance case, and it is the subject the next two chapters take up directly — one showing, in careful experimental detail, exactly how disproportionately a single visible error can move trust in an algorithm; the other showing, just as carefully, the opposite tendency running in the opposite direction within the same population, depending on framing and domain.

It is also worth noting that Lee and See framed calibration as something design, not just individual psychology, bears responsibility for. Their paper is explicitly aimed at people who build automated systems, not only at the operators who use them, and its recommendations are addressed to design choices as much as to training programs: a system's interface, its error messages, the way it signals its own confidence or uncertainty, all shape whether the feedback an operator receives is the kind that can actually build an accurate mental model. This matters because it is tempting, once the concept of calibration is on the table, to treat every instance of overtrust or undertrust as a failure of the person doing the trusting — as though a better-trained, more careful, more skeptical operator would simply have gotten it right. Lee and See's own framework resists that conclusion. A closed loop can be broken at several points, and at least as often as it is broken by an inattentive or overconfident operator, it is broken by a system that gives its user misleading, absent, or uninterpretable feedback to build a mental model from in the first place. Blaming the operator for a miscalibration the system's own design made nearly inevitable is one of the recurring patterns this book will return to directly, once later chapters take up the question of where responsibility actually belongs when a joint human-machine system fails.

It is worth being precise about what this chapter's tool is for, and what it is not. Lee and See's concept of calibration is not a solution to the problem of misplaced trust; it is a diagnostic, a way of asking the right question about a given failure rather than the wrong one. When a person over-relies on a flawed system, or abandons a sound one, the useful question is rarely "was this person too trusting or not trusting enough," phrased as though trust itself were the variable to adjust up or down in general. The useful question is where, specifically, the feedback loop that should have kept their trust tracking the system's real performance broke down — whether they never received the feedback that would have corrected them, whether the feedback they received was misleading or unrepresentative, whether their mental model of the system was wrong from the start, or whether a single dramatic event distorted an otherwise accurate model. Different breakdowns call for different fixes, and none of them is answered by a blanket instruction to trust the machine more, or less, or to "keep a human in the loop" as though the mere presence of a person watching guaranteed a correctly calibrated one.

This is also why calibration, treated honestly, resists the kind of tidy engineering solution that would let this chapter end with a formula or a checklist. A dial can be set. A habit, built over an unpredictable mix of experiences, feedback quality, and the accidents of which failures happened to be visible and which were not, cannot simply be set; it has to be built, case by case, system by system, through design choices this book will examine in far more detail once the diagnostic groundwork is complete. It is tempting to imagine that the fix is simply more information — tell the operator more about the system, show them its error rate, explain its reasoning, and calibration should follow automatically from transparency. That intuition is not baseless, but it is considerably less reliable than it sounds, and one of the more unsettling findings this book takes up later shows that the mere appearance of an explanation can move a person's trust nearly as much as an explanation that actually informs them, which is precisely the kind of gap between the feeling of calibration and the substance of it that Lee and See's framework warns against. Calibration built on a persuasive interface rather than an accurate mental model is not calibration at all. It is overtrust wearing calibration's clothing, and it can be more dangerous than an obviously untrustworthy system, precisely because it does not feel uncalibrated from the inside.

What can be said now, before the two chapters that follow test the idea against real experimental evidence, is that trust in automation is neither a virtue nor a vice in the abstract. It is a claim about the world — this system, in this condition, deserves this much confidence — and like any claim about the world, it can be accurate, it can be wrong in either direction, and the difference between an accurate claim and a wrong one is rarely a matter of how trusting or how skeptical a person's general temperament happens to be. It is a matter of whether the specific loop that built that trust ever had the chance to get it right.

One further complication is worth naming before moving on, because it will matter in nearly every domain the rest of this book visits: the cost of a given calibration error is not fixed, and depends heavily on what is at stake in the decision the trust is attached to. An operator who slightly overtrusts a spam filter pays a trivial cost when it is wrong — an occasional lost email, a minor annoyance. An operator who slightly overtrusts a flight-management computer, a sentencing tool, or a diagnostic algorithm pays a cost measured in lives, liberty, or health. The underlying psychological mechanism that produces the miscalibration can be identical across all three cases — the same closed loop, the same overweighting of a single vivid failure or a long unbroken run of successes — while the consequences of getting it wrong scale by orders of magnitude depending on the domain. This is part of why the chapters ahead keep returning to the same small set of research findings, drawn largely from lower-stakes laboratory settings, and apply them to courtrooms, cockpits, and hospitals: the psychology of trust calibration does not change because the stakes are higher, but the tolerance for miscalibration does, and institutions that treat a high-stakes domain as though ordinary calibration processes will sort themselves out are relying on a mechanism that was never built with those stakes in mind.

The next two chapters put that claim under experimental pressure from opposite sides. One shows people discounting a demonstrably superior algorithm faster than they discount an equally fallible human being, an undertrust so systematic that researchers gave it its own name. The other shows the same kind of people, in different conditions, doing the reverse — preferring algorithmic judgment to human judgment even when they had been told nothing about which was more accurate, an overtrust so consistent that the researchers who found it had predicted the opposite result. Read separately, the two findings can look like a contradiction: does the average person trust algorithms too little, or too much? Read through Lee and See's framework, they are not a contradiction at all. They are the same calibration failure, observed from its two possible directions, in a population whose trust in machines was never well calibrated to begin with — because building that calibration was never simply a matter of choosing to trust more or less. It was always a matter of the evidence, the feedback, and the mental model a person had actually been given the chance to build, and on that count, as the next two chapters make clear, most people are working with far less than they assume.
