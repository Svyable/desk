*How Human Judgment Survives the Age of the Recommendation.*

The working thesis is that automated recommendation has spread into
aviation, medicine, criminal justice, finance, warehouses, and driving
faster than anyone has taught the humans working alongside it how to decide
when to trust it. The decisive skill is no longer simply "make the right
call." It is "know when this system, in this moment, has earned your
trust, your skepticism, or your override" — and the human-factors and
behavioral-science research is clear that people get this calibration wrong
in both directions: sometimes trusting a flawed system past the point of
no return, sometimes abandoning a sound one after a single visible mistake.

The book moves from the cockpit (Air France 447, the 737 MAX accidents, the
successful ditching of US Airways 1549) to the reading room (an AI system
that outperformed radiologists on a large international mammography study)
to the courtroom (a risk-assessment algorithm at the center of a Wisconsin
due-process case) to the warehouse floor, the trading desk, and the front
seat of a car that mostly drives itself — tracing one recurring psychology
across very different stakes, and asking what the evidence says can
actually be designed, trained, and held accountable differently.
