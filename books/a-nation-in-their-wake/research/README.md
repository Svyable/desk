# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*A Nation in Their Wake* asks whether the history of the United States can be made newly visible by following one consequential ship through each named decade touched by the country's first 250 years.

The organizing claim is deliberately narrower than “these are the twenty-six greatest ships in American history.” Importance is not a measurable property of a hull. The book instead selects the vessel that best exposes a transition the country could not avoid: independence, international commerce, naval statehood, technological change, slavery and law, continental expansion, Pacific power, industrial war, migration, logistics, environmental risk, terrorism, or the infrastructure dependencies of a containerized economy.

The selection rule therefore allows a ship to be merchant, naval, passenger, foreign-flagged, improvised, infamous, or lost. The question is not whether the vessel was admirable. The question is whether its story reveals something America became.

The chronology uses the named decades from the 1770s through the 2020s. Because the United States declared independence in 1776 and reaches its semiquincentennial in 2026, those 250 years touch twenty-six named decades: partial 1770s and 2020s at the ends, with twenty-four full named decades between them. The 2020s choice is necessarily provisional as of 2026.

## Selection tests

A candidate ship should satisfy several of these tests, and the final choice should survive comparison with obvious alternatives.

1. **Decade fit:** the decisive American episode involving the vessel begins or occurs substantially inside the named decade.
2. **National consequence:** the episode changes policy, technology, commerce, war, migration, territory, infrastructure, culture, or the country's understanding of itself beyond the ship's own crew or owners.
3. **Explanatory reach:** the vessel opens a door into institutions and people around it rather than serving only as an isolated maritime anecdote.
4. **Counterfactual usefulness:** asking what the United States would have looked like without the voyage, loss, attack, or innovation clarifies the transition even when the ship was not a single cause.
5. **Evidence quality:** primary records, official histories, museum collections, investigations, or serious scholarship are available for the concrete claims.
6. **Portfolio balance:** the final twenty-six should not reduce American maritime history to naval combat. Commerce, forced and voluntary migration, industry, science, energy, logistics, law, and infrastructure must remain visible.

## Working selection

| Decade | Ship | Why it earns the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| 1770s | *Bonhomme Richard* | John Paul Jones's 1779 fight with HMS *Serapis* turns a borrowed French merchant ship and multinational crew into a story of American naval legitimacy. |
| 1780s | *Empress of China* | The first American ship to China in 1784 makes commercial independence physical before the Constitution exists. |
| 1790s | USS *Constitution* | One of the six frigates authorized by the Naval Act of 1794, launched in 1797, embodies the federal government's decision to protect commerce with permanent naval power. |
| 1800s | USS *Chesapeake* | The 1807 *Leopard* attack converts impressment and maritime sovereignty from abstractions into national outrage on the road to the War of 1812. |
| 1810s | *Savannah* | The 1819 steam-assisted Atlantic crossing shows a technological future arriving before customers, infrastructure, or economics are ready for it. |
| 1820s | USS *Vincennes* | Launched in 1826 and sent around the world, she makes the early U.S. Navy a platform for Pacific presence, commerce, navigation, and knowledge. |
| 1830s | *La Amistad* | The 1839 revolt and ensuing American legal battle make a ship the scene of a fight over slavery, personhood, treaties, abolition, and federal law. |
| 1840s | SS *California* | Pacific Mail's first steamer leaves New York in 1848 and reaches San Francisco as the Gold Rush begins, helping turn annexed Pacific territory into an American system. |
| 1850s | USS *Susquehanna* | Commodore Perry's steam flagship enters Tokyo Bay in 1853, making American industrial power and Pacific ambition impossible for Japan to ignore. |
| 1860s | USS *Monitor* | The 1862 battle with CSS *Virginia* compresses the industrial transformation of naval war into a single morning at Hampton Roads. |
| 1870s | SS *City of Peking* | The giant American-built iron steamer places immigration, Pacific commerce, industrial shipbuilding, labor, and empire on the same route. |
| 1880s | USS *Dolphin* | The first completed ship of the ABCD “New Navy” generation marks the federal turn from an aging wooden fleet toward steel, steam, and modern sea power. |
| 1890s | USS *Maine* | Her 1898 destruction in Havana Harbor becomes a political accelerant for the Spanish-American War even though the explosion's cause remains unresolved. |
| 1900s | USS *Connecticut* | Flagship of the Great White Fleet, she carries Theodore Roosevelt's demonstration of American industrial and naval power around the globe. |
| 1910s | RMS *Lusitania* | Her 1915 sinking kills 128 Americans and transforms the political meaning of submarine warfare and neutrality without simplistically “causing” U.S. entry into World War I. |
| 1920s | USS *Lexington* (CV-2) | A battlecruiser converted under treaty limits into a carrier, she makes the interwar pivot from big guns toward naval aviation visible in steel. |
| 1930s | MS *St. Louis* | The 1939 refugee voyage exposes the human cost of immigration restriction and the distance between American refuge mythology and American policy. |
| 1940s | SS *Patrick Henry* | The first Liberty ship turns merchant hulls into a story about mass production, logistics, labor, and the industrial arithmetic behind Allied victory. |
| 1950s | *Ideal X* | The 1956 voyage carrying fifty-eight truck bodies from Newark to Houston demonstrates the container system that will reorder ports, factories, trade, and American geography. |
| 1960s | USS *Hornet* (CVS-12) | Apollo 11 ends in the Pacific in 1969, where a former warship recovers the astronauts and reveals how much terrestrial machinery sat beneath the Moon landing. |
| 1970s | SS *Mayaguez* | Her 1975 seizure and the American rescue operation become the violent coda to the Vietnam era and a test of credibility, force, intelligence, and limits. |
| 1980s | *Exxon Valdez* | The 1989 grounding in Prince William Sound turns the hidden environmental risk of petroleum logistics into a national political fact. |
| 1990s | *Golden Venture* | The 1993 grounding off New York brings Chinese migration, asylum, detention, smuggling networks, and immigration enforcement onto one beach. |
| 2000s | USS *Cole* | The 2000 al-Qaeda attack in Aden kills seventeen sailors and exposes the vulnerability of American power before September 11 reorganizes the national security state. |
| 2010s | SS *El Faro* | The 2015 loss with all thirty-three aboard makes weather information, bridge culture, corporate oversight, aging ships, and domestic maritime regulation painfully visible. |
| 2020s | MV *Dali* | The 2024 Key Bridge collapse, caused after the ship lost power and steering, exposes how one container ship can sit inside a chain of electrical design, port commerce, bridge vulnerability, labor, and metropolitan infrastructure. |

## Falsification and counter-evidence

The manuscript should not pretend the selections are inevitable. Each chapter needs at least one serious alternative and a reason the chosen ship better serves the book's explanatory purpose. A different naval history could choose *Enterprise*, *Kearsarge*, *Olympia*, *Arizona*, *Nautilus*, or *Nimitz*. A migration history could choose an immigrant liner in the 1880s or 1900s. A commercial history could assign more decades to packets, tankers, containerships, or Great Lakes shipping. The disagreement is useful: the selection should illuminate the decade even when a reader would choose differently.

Causation also needs restraint. *Maine* did not mechanically cause the Spanish-American War. *Lusitania* did not send the United States to war the next morning. *Monitor* did not single-handedly invent iron warships. *Ideal X* did not invent every component of containerization. *Dali* did not by itself create the Key Bridge's vulnerability. The ship is the aperture, not always the prime mover.

For every chapter the notes should preserve:

- what participants knew at the time versus what hindsight supplies;
- claims about disputed causes, especially *Maine* and episodes involving intelligence failures;
- the role of institutions, financiers, workers, governments, ports, shipyards, technologies, and people outside the command structure;
- an alternative ship for the decade and the argument for rejecting it;
- a short “What would make this selection wrong?” note.

## Source discipline

Prefer primary records and high-authority secondary sources: Naval History and Heritage Command, National Archives, Library of Congress, Smithsonian museums and libraries, National Park Service, State Department Office of the Historian, U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum, NASA, FBI, EPA, NTSB, Coast Guard, contemporaneous documents, and serious maritime or historical scholarship.

Do not invent bridge dialogue, captain's thoughts, weather, sounds, smells, or quotations. When a vivid detail is not in a source, leave it out. Treat famous phrases such as John Paul Jones's reported reply to *Serapis* with special caution and either source the wording and its evidentiary limits or paraphrase the episode without quotation marks.

The research ledgers in this folder map chapter claims to sources and include uncertainty notes. The manuscript carries the argument; the research trail carries the audit path.
