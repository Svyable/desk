# The Pacific Becomes a Route

SS *City of Peking* was built in America to make the Pacific feel smaller.

The politics that followed were partly about Americans deciding that some of the people using the route were too close.

Launched in 1874 for the Pacific Mail Steamship Company, *City of Peking* was among the largest American-built iron steamships of her time. She represented a mature stage of the commercial transition that *Savannah* had anticipated awkwardly in 1819. Steam was no longer an experimental auxiliary attached to sail in hope of proving a point. Iron hulls, powerful machinery, regular schedules, and large passenger capacity were becoming parts of an integrated transportation business.

The ship connected San Francisco with ports in Japan and China.

That route placed her inside almost every contradiction of the post-Civil War Pacific United States.

California had been American for less than three decades. The transcontinental railroad had been completed in 1869. San Francisco was a major port. Chinese workers had been essential to the western section of the railroad and to mines, farms, factories, laundries, restaurants, construction, domestic service, fisheries, and other parts of the regional economy. American merchants wanted Asian trade. Steamship companies wanted passengers and cargo.

At the same time, anti-Chinese politics was becoming one of the most powerful movements in California.

Connection had produced dependence and resentment together.

*City of Peking* makes the contradiction visible because a passenger liner is a commercial promise that people will move.

Ships earn money by carrying somebody or something. A company serving an international route has an incentive to increase traffic. Ports invest in facilities to handle traffic. Merchants profit from traffic. Governments collect duties from traffic. Employers hire labor made available through traffic.

Then politics can decide the traffic is unacceptable.

That conflict between the economics of movement and the politics of restriction is older than the United States. The 1870s gave it an especially American form.

Chinese migration to California had grown during the Gold Rush and railroad era. Most migrants were men, many intending initially to work and send money home rather than settle permanently. They entered labor markets where employers valued their work and white workers sometimes viewed them as competitors who could be paid less and used to weaken wages.

Economic grievance fused with racial ideology.

Chinese immigrants were described as permanently foreign, unassimilable, servile, diseased, morally suspect, or controlled by labor systems that made fair competition impossible. Political organizers could transform wage anxiety and recession into a campaign against people with less power to defend themselves.

The Panic of 1873 and the economic distress that followed intensified the climate.

California politicians discovered that exclusion could unite voters who disagreed about many other things. Denis Kearney and the Workingmen's Party built agitation around anti-Chinese slogans later in the decade. Violence and intimidation accompanied the politics.

The country had just amended the Constitution after the Civil War to define citizenship and protect civil rights more broadly.

Now it was constructing a racialized federal immigration regime.

That transition is crucial.

Before the late nineteenth century the federal government had relatively limited machinery for excluding categories of immigrants at the border compared with what would follow. States and localities imposed discriminatory measures, and federal law controlled naturalization, but the modern idea that national immigration policy would decide classes of people inadmissible and build enforcement institutions around those decisions was still developing.

Chinese exclusion helped build it.

The Page Act of 1875 restricted the entry of certain immigrants from Asia, especially women suspected of prostitution, and operated in ways that sharply constrained Chinese female migration. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 would go further by suspending immigration of Chinese laborers and creating documentation and enforcement requirements around status.

The ship route became a border before the ship reached land.

That is the conceptual shift *City of Peking* lets us see.

A border is often drawn as a line on land. Maritime immigration turns the border into a process. Passenger manifests, certificates, inspections, interviews, shipping-company rules, port officials, courts, detention facilities, and diplomatic agreements all determine whether the person who physically reaches the harbor is legally allowed to enter.

Arrival is not admission.

The distinction will return brutally with MS *St. Louis* in 1939 and *Golden Venture* in 1993.

In each case a ship brings people close enough to see America and then discovers that geography and permission are separate systems.

For Chinese passengers in the nineteenth century, that system became increasingly hostile.

The importance of *City of Peking* is documented not only in immigration history but in the ship's position inside the wider Pacific economy. Scholars have used her to examine American industrial development, labor, immigration, national policy, and imperial ambition because the vessel carried all of those currents together.

The ship was American-built at a time when iron steamship construction itself carried industrial significance.

After the Civil War, the American merchant marine faced intense competition from British shipbuilding and shipping. Britain possessed major advantages in iron ship production, steam technology, finance, and global commercial networks. The United States remained a large trading nation without automatically dominating the ships carrying its trade.

That mismatch troubled policymakers repeatedly.

A country can be rich in cargo and weak in shipping.

The distinction matters because control over commercial transport has strategic implications in war, influences shipbuilding employment and industrial capability, and determines where freight revenue goes. Debates over subsidies, mail contracts, cabotage rules, shipbuilding protection, and merchant marine policy reflect the recurring fear that a nation dependent on foreign carriers may discover the dependence at the worst possible time.

*City of Peking* was therefore a commercial ship with an industrial-policy shadow.

She represented the possibility of an American-built transpacific steamship system at a moment when maritime technology was shifting decisively away from the wooden sailing traditions in which American builders had once excelled.

Technological transitions can erase national advantages as quickly as they create them.

The United States had built world-class wooden sailing ships. Britain moved aggressively into iron and steam. Skills, yards, financing, supply chains, and policy developed around the new system. An industry that looks naturally competitive can discover that its competence was tied to a technology whose era is ending.

The challenge is familiar in every later industrial transition.

Dominance in internal-combustion vehicles does not guarantee dominance in electric vehicles. Dominance in one semiconductor generation does not guarantee the next. A country leading in one energy system may lag in the infrastructure required for another. The installed base can become a political constituency for preserving the technology that created it.

Ships are unforgiving because the capital lasts for years while the frontier moves.

By the time a large vessel is designed, financed, built, launched, and placed into service, market conditions and technology may already be changing. Shipowners therefore make long bets on trade routes, fuel systems, labor, regulations, and demand.

Pacific Mail's bet was that transpacific movement would grow.

It did.

The political question became who would be allowed to move.

Chinese exclusion demonstrates that a society can want the economic output associated with migration while rejecting the migrants as political equals.

Employers had recruited and used Chinese labor. Railroads depended on it. Agriculture and urban businesses used it. Merchants profited from trade with Chinese communities. Yet exclusionists could argue that the same workers threatened white labor and national identity.

The contradiction was managed by racial hierarchy.

Chinese workers could be useful and unwanted at the same time.

This is not merely hypocrisy, though hypocrisy was abundant. It is a common structure in labor systems: political communities seek workers under conditions that limit the workers' bargaining power, permanence, or membership. Temporary-worker regimes, colonial labor, guest-worker systems, undocumented employment, and racial exclusions all create versions of the arrangement.

The labor is inside the economy.

The worker is kept outside the polity.

Ships make the arrangement unstable because movement creates facts on the ground—or on the pier. People establish homes, families, businesses, friendships, debts, organizations, and claims. The longer they remain, the more absurd it becomes to describe them as a temporary labor input.

Exclusion politics tries to restore the abstraction.

The law turns people back into categories.

“Chinese laborer.”

“Inadmissible.”

“Merchant.”

“Citizen.”

“Alien.”

These terms carry consequences that a ship captain and immigration official can enforce before a court ever sees the human story.

The legal complexity produced litigation. Chinese migrants and Chinese American communities challenged discriminatory laws in American courts, contributing to important developments in constitutional and immigration law. The Supreme Court would eventually recognize some protections while also granting the federal government sweeping power over immigration in decisions that remain consequential.

The border was becoming a federal institution partly through cases that began at ports.

This is the underappreciated constitutional wake of commercial shipping.

The federal government became stronger not only because it taxed, fought wars, or built agencies. It became stronger because movement across national boundaries generated questions that states could not resolve consistently: who enters, who leaves, what documents count, what treaties require, which goods are taxed, what happens to people detained at ports, and which level of government controls the decision.

International shipping pulls authority upward.

The same can happen with disease control, customs, navigation, safety, and environmental rules. Ports create problems with national externalities. A local decision can affect foreign relations or interstate commerce. Federal authority therefore finds practical justification at the water's edge.

*City of Peking* also reveals how race shaped American ideas of the Pacific.

The ocean was economically attractive and demographically frightening to many white Americans. Asia represented markets American merchants wanted and populations exclusionists feared. American policy would spend decades trying to maximize one flow while controlling the other.

Open the market.

Close the border.

That tension would later scale into empire.

The United States annexed Hawaii in 1898, acquired Guam and the Philippines after the Spanish-American War, and asserted growing strategic influence across the Pacific. The country increasingly treated the ocean as a zone of American interest while retaining racial hierarchies that denied full political equality to many of the people living within its new possessions.

Commercial routes helped normalize the geography before formal expansion arrived.

A regular steamship line turns distant ports into recurring places. Sailors know them. Merchants price them. Newspapers report arrivals. Travelers write letters. Consuls develop relationships. Companies invest. Maps gain lines.

The route becomes imaginable as strategic space.

This is the same progression *Vincennes* represented earlier, now intensified by steam and mass passenger movement.

The United States did not wake up in 1898 and discover the Pacific. It had been building commercial, naval, missionary, and diplomatic familiarity for generations.

*City of Peking* carried the familiarity on a schedule.

There is a further reason to choose her for the 1870s: she makes the aftermath of the Civil War look less domestically sealed.

Reconstruction is rightly centered on emancipation, Black citizenship, federal power in the South, white supremacist violence, and the political collapse of efforts to secure equal rights. But the constitutional language of citizenship and equality was being tested simultaneously by western expansion and Asian migration.

The Fourteenth Amendment declared birthright citizenship in sweeping terms. That principle would become crucial for Chinese Americans.

In 1898, the Supreme Court in *United States v. Wong Kim Ark* held that a man born in San Francisco to Chinese parents was a citizen under the Fourteenth Amendment. The decision came decades after *City of Peking* began carrying the very traffic that exclusionists wanted to constrain.

Ships brought people into the jurisdiction whose presence forced abstract constitutional language to acquire concrete meaning.

This is another kind of wake.

The vessel does not cause the doctrine.

It carries the lives that make the doctrine unavoidable.

By the 1880s, the United States Navy was confronting its own question of technological belonging.

The Civil War had produced ironclads and a burst of innovation, but postwar retrenchment left much of the fleet aging while European navies moved into modern steel construction. American policymakers worried that the Navy no longer matched the country's growing economy and overseas interests.

Congress authorized a group of new steel vessels commonly remembered as the ABCD ships: *Atlanta*, *Boston*, *Chicago*, and *Dolphin*.

USS *Dolphin* was the first completed.

Compared with *City of Peking*, she was smaller and less commercially consequential.

Institutionally, she marked a turn.

The country that had built *Monitor* under emergency pressure was about to relearn how to build a modern fleet in peace.

The distinction matters.

War can force innovation.

Peace has to decide whether to keep paying for it.

## The Route Makes the Border

A scheduled transpacific liner did something a one-time voyage could not: it made repeated arrival ordinary.

That ordinariness changed law.

When a ship arrives once with unfamiliar passengers, officials can treat the case as exceptional. When steamships carry people on a schedule, governments need repeatable categories. Passenger lists, certificates, inspection practices, treaty definitions, port procedures, court rules, and detention arrangements begin to harden around the traffic.

SS *City of Peking* belongs to that process even though no single voyage created Chinese exclusion.

The strongest rival for the 1870s is probably a ship associated with Reconstruction or western conquest, but the decade's maritime transformation is more legible in the Pacific route. The transcontinental railroad had just made the continental map feel newly connected. Pacific Mail made San Francisco part of a transoceanic circuit at the same moment. The national network was extending east and west simultaneously.

That created a political contradiction. California businesses and infrastructure had benefited from Chinese labor, while a growing anti-Chinese movement described Chinese workers as a threat to wages, racial order, and citizenship. The system wanted movement and restriction at the same time.

Steamships made the contradiction administrative. Exclusion is easy to advocate in a speech. Enforcement requires deciding which person standing on a deck belongs in which legal category.

The Page Act of 1875 targeted entry by people deemed undesirable and was enforced especially harshly against Chinese women, often through assumptions about prostitution. The Chinese Exclusion Act followed in 1882. Together with later laws and cases, these measures helped transform the federal government into a more active immigration gatekeeper.

Ports became constitutional laboratories because international arrivals forced national questions. Can a state exclude? Can the federal government? What proof of status counts? What happens to a person while the dispute is litigated? Does treaty language constrain domestic law? How does citizenship acquired by birth change the analysis?

The answers built institutions that outlived the original anti-Chinese campaign.

The counterfactual is straightforward. Chinese exclusion politics would have existed without *City of Peking*. The ship does not get causal credit for racism, labor conflict, or federal immigration law. It wins because the route concentrated all three. A modern American-built liner carrying Asian passengers and goods into San Francisco is the physical system around which Americans tried to write one rule for commerce and another for people.

That distinction becomes even more important when placed beside *Ideal X* eighty years later. The twentieth-century United States would celebrate standardized boxes moving frictionlessly through ports while continuing to subject human movement to increasingly elaborate authorization.

The nineteenth-century border was already learning that asymmetry.

Goods were traffic to facilitate.

Some people were traffic to stop.

## The Economics of Exclusion

Anti-Chinese politics is sometimes narrated as if restriction defeated commerce. The relationship was more entangled. Employers could favor access to Chinese labor while white workers and political organizers demanded exclusion. Merchants could profit from Pacific trade while supporting or accommodating a legal regime that constrained the people moving through the same ports. Shipping companies could earn fares from passengers whose admissibility was becoming a federal controversy.

The economy did not speak with one voice.

That is important because “business interests” are often treated as a unified force for open borders. Businesses differ according to what they buy and sell. A railroad needing labor, a white craft worker protecting wages, a shipping line selling passage, a merchant serving Chinese customers, and a politician responding to voters can all have different preferences while participating in the same regional economy.

Exclusion law reorganized those incentives rather than removing them.

Documentation became valuable. Legal status determined which passengers could enter. Categories such as merchant, student, laborer, citizen, or family member became economically consequential. Brokers and lawyers gained roles in proving identity. Inspectors gained discretion. Carriers had reasons to understand which passengers would be accepted because inadmissible passengers created costs.

A border therefore creates an industry around the border.

The federal state expanded through that administrative work. Immigration officers, inspectors, detention facilities, courts, consular documents, and treaty interpretation became parts of a system whose original political energy was explicitly racial. Later immigration law would broaden beyond Chinese exclusion, but some institutional habits were built in this period: the assumption that entry is a federal permission, that categories may be policed at ports, and that carriers can be enlisted into enforcement.

The strongest challenge to choosing *City of Peking* remains immediacy. The ship itself did not produce the Page Act or Chinese Exclusion Act, and another Pacific liner could illustrate much of the same system. The selection is representative rather than singular.

That is acceptable here because representation is the point. The ship's size, American construction, transpacific schedule, and connection to Pacific Mail make it an unusually concentrated object through which to see the same network generating commerce and exclusion at once.

America wanted the route.

It argued over the people who made the route a human one.

One last consequence of regular steamship traffic was demographic memory. A route creates communities at both ends. Letters, remittances, employers, family ties, and return voyages make later migration easier because the next traveler does not begin with the same uncertainty as the first. Restrictionists understood this intuitively even when they described immigration only as a current labor problem. They were trying to shape the future population by controlling the network in the present.

That is why the liner is more than a carrier. Repetition turns movement into relationship. Once the relationship exists, exclusion requires a permanent administrative effort to interrupt something the transport economy continually makes easier.
