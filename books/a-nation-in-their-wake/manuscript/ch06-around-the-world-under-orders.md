# Around the World Under Orders

USS *Vincennes* was launched in 1826 into a United States that had not reached the Pacific Ocean politically but was already learning to think around the world.

The ship was a sloop of war, a sailing naval vessel rather than a technological revolution. That makes her easy to overlook between *Savannah*, which anticipated steam's conquest of ocean travel, and *Amistad*, whose 1839 revolt would force the country into one of the era's most consequential legal arguments over slavery.

But the 1820s were not only a bridge between more dramatic stories.

They were the decade in which the United States began turning distant commerce into routine naval geography.

*Vincennes* helps us see the change because her importance came not from one decisive battle but from where she went and what the Navy expected a warship to do there.

Soon after commissioning, she deployed into the Pacific. Under Commander William Bolton, *Vincennes* sailed through the South Atlantic, around Cape Horn, and into waters where American merchants and whalers were increasingly active. The voyage continued across the Pacific and Indian oceans, around the Cape of Good Hope, and back toward the United States. When she completed the cruise in 1830, *Vincennes* became the first United States Navy warship to circumnavigate the globe.

The date at the end falls just outside the named decade. The voyage began in the 1820s, and the reason for it belonged unmistakably to that decade: the American state was extending its operational attention across oceans faster than its continental map was expanding.

The important word is attention.

A state does not need to own territory in a region to develop interests there. American merchant ships traded in Pacific ports. Whalers worked far from the Atlantic coast. Sailors became entangled in local disputes. Cargoes needed protection. Consuls and merchants needed communication. Navigation required charts and knowledge. The farther private activity moved, the more often public authority received requests to follow.

The pattern had begun with the *Empress of China*.

Merchants create routes. Routes create exposure. Exposure creates demands for institutions.

*Vincennes* was an institution in motion.

A naval vessel on distant station could do things no merchant ship was equipped or authorized to do. It could show the flag, protect citizens, investigate disputes, carry official communication, collect information, survey waters, enforce claims, and create a visible reminder that people doing business under the American flag were connected to a government capable of sending force across an ocean.

None of these actions required battle.

That is why warships are badly understood if measured only by wars.

Most naval service is the management of possibility. A ship arrives before the crisis, after the crisis, or in the hope that its presence prevents a crisis from growing. It protects not by firing but by changing expectations about what might happen if someone attacks, seizes, or refuses.

The nineteenth-century phrase “showing the flag” can sound ceremonial to modern ears. It was practical. Flags carried legal and political claims. A national warship entering a harbor announced that distant commercial activity had a sovereign relationship behind it.

This could protect commerce.

It could also become the early machinery of coercion.

The line between presence and pressure is thin when one side brings guns.

American naval vessels in the Pacific were part of a broader Western maritime expansion into regions with their own political orders and commercial rules. The United States often described its objectives as protection of trade and citizens. Local governments could experience the same arrival as intrusion. A warship's ability to make a demand meaningful is precisely what makes it useful to the government sending it and potentially threatening to the government receiving it.

*Vincennes* therefore belongs to a story that will become less innocent as American power grows.

Three decades later USS *Susquehanna* would serve as Commodore Matthew Perry's steam-powered flagship when the American squadron entered Japan and demanded the opening of relations. The technology would be more imposing and the diplomacy more coercive. But Perry's arrival did not materialize from nowhere. It rested on decades of expanding American Pacific commerce, naval cruising, surveying, and institutional familiarity.

A world presence is accumulated.

First come ships that need protection. Then ships that provide it. Then agreements, charts, depots, consuls, stations, and expectations. The extraordinary voyage slowly becomes a route.

Circumnavigation is a perfect symbol for that process because the trip looks more singular than it is.

Sailing around the world sounds like an adventure detached from bureaucracy. For a naval vessel, it is almost the opposite. The voyage depends on orders, provisioning, repair, discipline, stores, diplomatic arrangements, money, and knowledge gathered from many prior voyages. A warship may be self-contained compared with a land unit, but no wooden hull can carry everything required for years of service without interacting with ports and suppliers.

Global mobility creates global dependence.

That principle becomes clearer as ships become technologically sophisticated. A sailing warship needs water, food, spare material, maintenance, ammunition, and replacement labor. A coal-fired steamer adds fuel networks. An oil-fired fleet adds petroleum logistics. A nuclear fleet changes the fuel problem but increases technical and maintenance demands. Aircraft carriers require aviation fuel, munitions, aircraft maintenance, escorts, replenishment ships, satellites, networks, and bases.

Power projection is a chain of dependencies disguised as autonomy.

*Vincennes* gives us an early form of the chain.

Her cruise also intersected with the growing American appetite for knowledge about the Pacific. Navigation itself required increasingly systematic information. Coastlines had to be charted. Islands and hazards had to be located accurately. Winds, currents, harbors, and routes mattered to merchants and navies alike.

The Navy became a producer of information because information reduced maritime risk.

Matthew Fontaine Maury, who later became famous for his work on winds and currents and for helping systematize oceanographic knowledge, served aboard *Vincennes* early in his career. It would be too neat to make the ship the cause of his later work. His presence is nevertheless suggestive. The nineteenth-century Navy was not merely a collection of guns. It was one of the federal institutions in which navigation, surveying, meteorology, hydrography, astronomy, and practical science could be organized around national needs.

The ocean demanded measurement.

Maps are a form of power when movement depends on them.

A reliable chart lets a ship move where another captain hesitates. Knowledge of currents shortens voyages. Accurate longitude reduces danger. Harbor surveys make operations more predictable. Scientific information can be shared, standardized, and accumulated across voyages, turning individual experience into institutional capability.

This is another way the Navy was becoming more than its ships.

An inexperienced service can have excellent captains and still lose their knowledge when they retire. An institution stores lessons. It creates bureaus, repositories, manuals, charts, training, procedures, and professional communities. The transformation from *Bonhomme Richard* to *Vincennes* is therefore partly a transformation in memory.

Jones had improvised a legendary battle aboard a borrowed merchant ship. Half a century later the Navy was sending purpose-built vessels on recurring distant deployments inside a service that increasingly understood the world as an operating environment.

That phrase sounds anachronistically modern.

The underlying behavior is not.

When American ships sailed into the Pacific, they carried assumptions about what the federal government owed American commerce and what American commerce was entitled to expect abroad. Those assumptions were still developing. The country had strong anti-imperial language in its founding memory and expanding commercial interests that could produce imperial behavior in practice.

The tension was visible in the Monroe Doctrine, announced in 1823.

President James Monroe warned European powers against new colonization or interference in the Americas while stating that the United States would not meddle in existing European colonies or internal European affairs. At the time, American military power was not sufficient to enforce every implication of the doctrine by itself; British naval interests against renewed European colonization in Latin America helped make the practical environment favorable.

Again the United States asserted a principle larger than its independent capability.

The country was growing into its claims.

The 1820s are full of this intermediate scale. The federal government could send a warship around the world but did not possess a worldwide base network. The country declared strategic views about the Western Hemisphere while relying indirectly on a European navy to help sustain conditions compatible with them. American merchants reached distant markets while the state apparatus serving them remained small.

This is why the decade should not be narrated as a period of quiet after the War of 1812.

The machinery of expansion was acquiring routines.

On land, roads, canals, settlement, removal of Native peoples, and the politics of new states were transforming the continent. The Erie Canal opened in 1825, connecting the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and dramatically changing the commercial geography of New York and the interior. On water, American vessels were widening the country's practical horizon beyond the continent entirely.

The canal and *Vincennes* belong to the same national problem at different scales: connection.

How do you make distance economically and politically manageable?

A canal reduces the cost of moving bulk goods inland. A naval deployment reduces some risks of moving people and goods abroad. Both require capital, institutions, maintenance, and a decision that distant places are worth connecting.

Connection is never merely technical.

The Erie Canal helped make New York City richer and shifted regional trade. Pacific naval presence would favor some merchants, strengthen some diplomatic claims, and eventually make American intervention more plausible. Infrastructure and protection distribute advantage.

The people on the receiving end do not always experience connection as benefit.

This matters especially in the Pacific, where American expansion would eventually collide with Native Hawaiian sovereignty, Spanish and then independent Pacific polities, Asian states, and island communities. Whalers and traders brought disease, alcohol, money, missionaries, weapons, and political pressure. Naval ships arrived in the same world claiming to stabilize commerce that was itself changing local societies.

The ship does not have to fire to participate in transformation.

Presence changes what others must plan for.

This may be the most durable lesson of *Vincennes*.

The United States in the 1820s was beginning to discover that maritime power could be continuous rather than episodic. A warship did not have to wait for Congress to declare a major war and then appear. It could cruise. The government could maintain stations and recurring deployments. Officers could learn regions. Merchants could come to expect the flag.

Continuity creates credibility.

It also creates commitments.

Once citizens and merchants believe the Navy will protect them abroad, withdrawal can feel like abandonment. Once foreign governments expect American ships to arrive, absence can be interpreted. A recurring deployment generates its own political meaning.

Modern strategy inherits this problem at a much larger scale. Carrier presence, submarine patrols, freedom-of-navigation operations, alliance exercises, and port visits are often described as signals. The language of signaling makes them sound temporary and deliberate. The deeper institutional reality is that a signal repeated long enough becomes part of the expected environment.

Expectations are expensive to reverse.

The early Navy could not have known how far the logic would run. The United States in 1826 did not possess California. It did not possess Hawaii, Guam, Puerto Rico, Alaska, or the Philippines. It had no transcontinental railroad. It had not fought Mexico. It had not forced Japan into a treaty. Yet a U.S. warship was already setting out on a voyage around the globe.

The sequence matters.

American Pacific power did not begin when the map turned American.

It began when American interests began making claims on distant water.

This makes *Vincennes* a better choice for the decade than a ship tied to one celebrated event. She represents the quiet conversion of range into routine.

Her later career strengthens the case.

*Vincennes* would participate in the United States Exploring Expedition from 1838 to 1842, the large scientific and surveying enterprise commonly associated with Lieutenant Charles Wilkes. That expedition mapped and studied vast stretches of the Pacific and reached the Antarctic region. The specimens and data collected contributed to American science, including collections that would later be associated with the Smithsonian Institution.

The same hull that had carried national presence now carried national inquiry.

Science and strategy were not identical, but they shared logistics.

A government capable of sending ships across the world could use them to collect information as well as exert power. Surveying could improve navigation for commerce and naval operations. Natural history could advance knowledge and national prestige. Geographic discovery could become entangled with territorial claims.

The categories overlap again.

A warship becomes a laboratory.

A chart becomes a commercial asset.

A specimen becomes a museum collection.

A port call becomes diplomacy.

A distant voyage becomes an argument for larger institutions at home.

The federal government's scientific capacity in the nineteenth century grew partly because practical needs such as navigation, coast surveying, weather, land measurement, and exploration created reasons to support systematic knowledge. The ocean was among the places where usefulness and curiosity could finance each other.

That relationship will culminate, in this book, with USS *Hornet* recovering the Apollo 11 astronauts in 1969. The technology will be unimaginably different, but the institutional pattern will be recognizable: military infrastructure supporting a national scientific project whose achievement exceeds the military mission itself.

The sea becomes the platform beneath another frontier.

In the 1820s, the frontier was still the sea.

*Vincennes* sailed around a planet most Americans knew through maps, commodities, newspapers, missionary reports, naval dispatches, and goods in shop windows. The ship brought parts of that world into the operational memory of the American government.

It also carried the government's assumptions outward.

That exchange would become increasingly consequential.

By the end of the next decade, however, one foreign-built schooner would enter American custody not because the United States was trying to project power abroad, but because a revolt at sea placed a profound moral and legal question before American institutions at home.

The vessel was called *La Amistad*.

The question was whether the people aboard her would be treated as cargo.

The United States had spent fifty years learning how to protect the freedom of American ships.

Now a ship would force it to confront the freedom of human beings.

## The State Learns the Pacific Before It Owns a Pacific Coast

The phrase “first U.S. naval vessel to circumnavigate the globe” risks turning *Vincennes* into a trivia answer. The voyage matters because of what had to become routine for the first to be repeatable.

The ship left New York in 1826, crossed into the Pacific, protected American merchants and whalers, reached Macao, moved through the Philippines and Indian Ocean, rounded Africa, and returned in 1830. The route placed a federal warship inside commercial geographies that the United States did not politically control and, in the Pacific, did not yet border with a continental coastline.

A serious rival for the 1820s is a steamboat on the Mississippi system. Those vessels probably changed more American lives immediately by compressing travel time, widening markets, and helping knit the interior into a commercial economy. They also served an expansion bound up with slavery and Native dispossession. If the book were a history of domestic transport, a river steamer would be the stronger choice.

*Vincennes* wins because the chapter asks when overseas interest becomes state habit.

One distant voyage can be adventure. Repeated cruises create expectations. Merchants assume protection may arrive. Officers acquire regional knowledge. The Navy accumulates charts. Consuls and captains develop relationships. Congress receives requests shaped by people who now make livelihoods in waters earlier generations treated as remote.

The resulting commitments are incremental enough to escape notice. No vote declares that the Pacific has become part of American strategy forever. A series of operational decisions makes the statement indirectly.

The counterfactual is revealing. If *Vincennes* had never circumnavigated, American commerce would still have expanded into the Pacific. Whalers and traders were already there. The Navy would eventually have followed. The ship's importance is therefore not causal monopoly. It is documentary clarity: this one hull makes visible how early the federal government learned to think in routes that exceeded its territory.

The information gathered on voyages mattered as much as the flag. Charts, observations, corrected positions, and practical knowledge reduced uncertainty for later movement. The Navy's later surveying and scientific missions grew from the same operating need. A state that wants to move repeatedly across difficult geography eventually becomes a producer of knowledge about that geography.

That is why *Vincennes* later fit naturally into the United States Exploring Expedition. The missions—protection, surveying, science, prestige—were different, but the platform and institutional competencies overlapped.

A warship can carry guns and measurements at the same time.

The 1820s United States remained weak by later standards, yet weakness did not prevent reach. It made reach selective. *Vincennes* shows the country beginning to convert selective reach into memory, and memory into a permanent expectation that American interests could exist anywhere American ships could profitably go.

The coastline would catch up later.
