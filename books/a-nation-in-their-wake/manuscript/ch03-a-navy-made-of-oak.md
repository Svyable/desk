# A Navy Made of Oak

USS *Constitution* is so physically present that it is easy to forget she began as an argument.

The ship survives in Boston. Visitors can walk a deck built into a national icon, look up at masts and rigging, and encounter a vessel whose material continuity seems to make the early Navy inevitable. She has been afloat since the eighteenth century. The name became permanent. The institution around her became enormous.

None of that was guaranteed when Congress authorized her.

In the 1790s the United States had a problem that the *Empress of China* had helped make larger. Americans wanted overseas commerce but possessed limited ability to protect it. The Revolutionary alliance with France had helped defeat Britain, yet the new republic could not outsource maritime security indefinitely. European war disrupted neutral trade. North African states and corsairs threatened vessels in the Mediterranean and Atlantic approaches. American sailors and cargoes could be taken while the federal government debated the cost and constitutional meaning of a naval force.

A navy is expensive before it is useful.

That simple fact has shaped naval politics for centuries. Ships require large expenditures up front, then continue consuming money through crews, provisions, maintenance, repair, ordnance, yards, and administration. A warship sitting peacefully in harbor can look like an extravagance to a taxpayer who cannot see the attack it may be preventing. A merchant vessel seized abroad can make the same taxpayer furious that the government failed to provide protection.

The 1790s forced the United States to decide which complaint mattered more.

The Naval Act of 1794 authorized six frigates, among them the ship that would become USS *Constitution*. The law was not a declaration that the United States intended to rival Britain's battle fleet. Six frigates could not do that. It was an acceptance that commercial independence without some capacity for armed protection was incomplete.

The design of the new frigates made the ambition more interesting.

American naval planners did not simply order copies of ordinary European frigates. Joshua Humphreys and the other men involved in the program pursued ships intended to be powerful enough to defeat typical frigates yet fast enough to avoid ships of the line they could not sensibly fight. The design expressed the strategy of a weaker maritime power: do not compete symmetrically with the strongest navy; create ships with an advantage in the encounters you can choose.

The concept sounds obvious after success. It was not cheap.

Large, heavily built frigates required materials, skilled labor, guns, copper, timber, and a network of suppliers. Their construction became an early federal industrial project dispersed across several ports. The ships were national objects assembled from local economies.

*Constitution* was built in Boston and launched in 1797 after earlier attempts to get her into the water failed. Her hull used dense live oak among other timbers, material prized for strength. Paul Revere's copper work became part of the ship's later public mythology, though the broader point is larger than any famous supplier. A warship this complex concentrated American craft, material, finance, and government purchasing into one object.

The state was learning how to buy capability.

That lesson is less romantic than combat, but it is foundational. Governments can authorize outcomes more easily than they can produce them. A statute does not become a frigate without contracts, yards, timber cutters, foundries, transport, artisans, supervisors, accounting, and time. The difference between wanting a navy and possessing one is an industrial system.

The young federal government was building pieces of that system while also building itself.

George Washington had become president in 1789. The new Constitution redistributed authority between states and the federal government, created executive departments, and gave Congress powers that made national revenue and national defense more coherent than they had been under the Articles of Confederation. Customs duties on maritime trade became a major source of federal revenue. The government therefore found itself in a revealing loop: commerce helped finance the state, and the state was asked to protect commerce.

The wharf and the Treasury were already connected.

The first missions of *Constitution* belonged to the Quasi-War with France, an undeclared naval conflict that grew from the wider European wars and worsening Franco-American relations. That fact is useful because it destroys a tidy version of early American diplomacy. France had been the indispensable ally of the Revolution. Less than two decades later American and French naval forces were capturing each other's ships.

Nations have memories. They also have interests.

The French Revolution and subsequent European wars created a diplomatic environment in which the United States tried to preserve neutral commerce while Britain and France both had reasons to interfere with it. The Jay Treaty with Britain angered France. French privateers seized American merchant ships. The XYZ Affair inflamed American opinion. Congress expanded naval forces and authorized measures that produced an undeclared conflict at sea.

*Constitution* entered service inside this ambiguity.

She was not launched into a cleanly declared war with an enemy whose political meaning was obvious. She was deployed in a limited conflict shaped by commerce, diplomacy, domestic faction, and rules about what naval forces were authorized to do. This would become a recurring American experience. Warships often spend more of their lives managing the space between peace and total war than fighting set-piece battles.

Presence itself is policy.

A frigate can escort merchantmen, deter weaker attackers, capture hostile privateers, carry officials, show the flag, communicate resolve, and create facts that diplomats must manage. The ship's usefulness does not begin at the moment of a broadside. Its existence changes other people's calculations.

That was the institutional improvement over the improvisation of *Bonhomme Richard*.

Jones's famous ship had been a converted French merchantman provided within a wartime alliance. *Constitution* was a purpose-built American federal warship. The difference is not that one crew was more courageous than another. The difference is repeatability.

A state that builds a class of ships is trying to turn exceptional performance into a service.

The 1794 frigates were not identical in every detail, but they represented a program rather than a one-off opportunity. Programs create administrative memory. Officers gain experience. Yards learn construction. Suppliers learn requirements. Congress develops habits of appropriation and oversight. The Navy develops procedures. The political system acquires constituencies both for and against continuing expenditure.

The ship becomes an institution-building machine before it leaves the pier.

*Constitution* would earn her most famous reputation in the War of 1812, when her victories over British frigates transformed her into something larger than a well-designed warship. The nickname “Old Ironsides” grew from the impression that British shot could not penetrate her stout sides during battle with HMS *Guerriere*. The poem written by Oliver Wendell Holmes decades later helped preserve her when scrapping appeared possible. Later restorations and public advocacy turned the ship into an unusually durable artifact of the founding naval era.

Those later episodes make it tempting to assign her to the 1810s.

I put her in the 1790s because the important decision came before the legend.

A country choosing to build a navy is more consequential than a country discovering that one of the ships it built can become famous.

The 1790s are the decade when the United States accepted that ocean commerce required permanent federal capacity. *Constitution* is the surviving body of that choice.

The choice was contested for good reasons.

A large military establishment carried political risks to a generation suspicious of standing forces. Naval construction could feed patronage and debt. Strategic priorities were uncertain. The country had long land frontiers, internal development needs, and weak fiscal capacity. Money spent on frigates was money not spent elsewhere or money that had to be raised. A ship that did not encounter an enemy could be dismissed as idle capital.

There was also no assurance that a modest fleet would solve the underlying problem. A few frigates could protect some routes and punish some attackers. They could not guarantee American commerce against every great power. Building naval capacity might even create pressure for more capacity as overseas interests expanded.

That is exactly what happened over the long run.

Institutions tend to generate new definitions of what adequate means.

Once a navy can protect one trade route, merchants on another route can ask for protection. Once ships operate farther from home, they need supplies, repair facilities, agreements, charts, and intelligence. Once officers acquire professional identities, they argue for doctrine, standards, promotion systems, and investment. Once rivals build new technology, existing ships become obsolete even if they still float.

Naval power is therefore not an asset purchased once. It is a commitment to continual adaptation.

The live oak that made *Constitution* formidable did not solve steam. Steam did not solve armor. Armor did not solve submarines. Battleships did not solve aircraft. Carriers did not solve missiles. Nuclear propulsion did not solve cyber vulnerability. Every generation of maritime technology creates a new bill for remaining capable.

The 1790s are where the United States began accepting that bill in a durable way.

The financing mattered as much as the design.

The new federal government's ability to collect customs revenue made maritime trade both a protected interest and a source of state capacity. This created a relationship that would seem almost circular if it were not so common in history. Governments protect taxable commerce; commerce grows under protection; the resulting revenue can support more government capability.

Successful states often live inside feedback loops like this.

The danger is that the loop can become self-justifying. More interests abroad can become an argument for more force, and more force can make farther interests seem protectable. A navy built to defend commerce can become an instrument for diplomacy, coercion, territorial expansion, and war. The United States would travel that road in the nineteenth century, especially as its interests moved into the Pacific and Caribbean.

Nothing in *Constitution*'s launch predetermined an empire.

It did make a broader range of policy possible.

Capabilities matter because they change the menu. A government without warships cannot credibly threaten many naval actions. A government with them can choose restraint, but restraint becomes a choice rather than an incapacity. The distinction is central to power. Institutions are often built for one purpose and later used for purposes their original advocates did not emphasize.

The frigates authorized to protect commerce became training grounds for officers, tools of diplomacy, combatants in later wars, and symbols of national identity.

*Constitution* herself became almost absurdly adaptable as an institution of memory. She served in active naval roles, circled the world, became a training ship, survived repeated threats of obsolescence and disposal, and eventually became the oldest commissioned warship afloat. Her endurance tempts us to attribute national character to wood.

I would resist that.

Ships survive because people decide to preserve them.

The continuing existence of *Constitution* is evidence of later American choices about memory as much as eighteenth-century craftsmanship. Maintenance is a form of historical authorship. Each generation that spends money and skill keeping an old ship afloat decides that the physical connection to the past has value.

Most important ships do not get that chance.

The sea is destructive. War is destructive. Commercial economics are destructive in another way: ships become obsolete, uneconomic, and are scrapped. Preservation therefore biases public memory toward extraordinary survivors. A visitor can stand aboard *Constitution* and feel the 1790s beneath his feet. There is no comparable intact experience for many vessels whose economic consequences were larger.

That is why a book like this has to keep asking what survives besides the hull.

For *Constitution*, the most important survivor is the idea that national commerce and national power were linked.

The young republic had already learned the commercial side from the *Empress of China*. American merchants wanted to operate globally. The world they entered was not governed by a neutral international police force. Merchant access rested on treaties, local rules, imperial systems, convoy practices, armed coercion, and the calculations of states. A neutral flag could not simply declare itself immune from stronger powers.

The United States could protest seizures diplomatically. It could pay tribute in some circumstances. It could negotiate. It could avoid certain routes. Or it could build ships.

It eventually did all of those things.

The Navy that emerged was not simply a response to national pride. It was a response to transaction costs imposed by insecurity.

That phrasing sounds bloodless, but it helps. Piracy, privateering, impressment, confiscation, and wartime interference were human dangers, yet they were also economic frictions. They changed insurance, freight, wages, routes, investment, and prices. Protection of shipping could therefore be defended as economic policy even when the means were military.

This overlap between military and economic power will recur throughout the book.

The Great White Fleet will carry American industrial capacity around the world. Liberty ships will turn manufacturing into logistics. Aircraft carriers will become floating expressions of both military technology and industrial scale. Container ships will make global trade cheap enough to reorganize factories on land. Attacks on warships will affect foreign policy. Accidents involving commercial vessels will reveal the public consequences of private systems.

At sea, categories leak.

A merchant route becomes a strategic interest. A naval base becomes a local economy. A shipyard becomes industrial policy. A fuel network becomes foreign policy. A commercial technology becomes military logistics. A military innovation migrates into civilian practice, or the other way around.

*Constitution* belongs near the beginning because she shows the federal government stepping deliberately into that overlap.

The ship's construction also exposes an early version of the problem later called the defense industrial base.

A nation cannot build sophisticated military hardware merely by appropriating money. It needs production capacity. In the eighteenth century that meant suitable timber, rope, sailcloth, cannon, copper, skilled shipwrights, yards, transport, and a thousand forms of tacit knowledge. In the twenty-first century the materials differ, but the logic remains. Submarines, missiles, sensors, nuclear systems, and warships depend on supplier networks that can become bottlenecks long before money becomes the limiting factor.

Capability lives in people and processes as much as in inventory.

The six-frigate program helped create and sustain those capabilities in the early republic. The ships were expensive partly because the country was building knowledge along with hulls.

That investment would matter when crises arrived.

The Quasi-War validated the need for naval operations, but the larger institutional story continued into conflict with the Barbary states and then into confrontation with Britain. The issues changed while the core demand remained: American ships and citizens were exposed to powers the government could not ignore.

By the first decade of the nineteenth century, one of those confrontations would become intensely personal.

British naval power had always been the scale against which American maritime ambition was measured. During the Napoleonic Wars, Britain's demand for sailors and its assertion of rights at sea collided repeatedly with American claims of neutrality and citizenship. British ships stopped American vessels and impressed seamen they regarded as British subjects or deserters. Americans regarded the practice as an assault on sovereignty, though the underlying reality of nationality at sea could be complicated by forged papers, desertion, dual identities, and the porous labor market of sailors.

Then a British warship fired on an American frigate close to home.

The ship was USS *Chesapeake*.

The event would show the difference between having a navy and having enough power to make every other navy respect it.

That is the unfinished lesson inside *Constitution*'s oak.

Institutions can create capability.

Capability does not abolish vulnerability.

## What Six Frigates Could and Could Not Buy

The six-frigate program can sound grander in retrospect than it looked against the scale of the maritime world. Britain maintained a navy measured in hundreds of commissioned warships. France remained a major naval power. The United States was authorizing six expensive vessels while still arguing about whether all six should even be completed if the immediate diplomatic crisis eased.

That mismatch is useful because it clarifies what *Constitution* was for. The objective was not command of the Atlantic. It was selective leverage.

A heavy American frigate could make some predatory calculations more expensive. It could escort merchantmen, capture privateers, punish weaker cruisers, protect diplomatic movement, and force an opponent to devote more capable ships to any encounter. The Humphreys design embodied an economics of scarcity: if the republic could afford only a few major vessels, each should be unusually useful across the fights the country was most likely to choose.

That logic has a modern echo in debates over exquisite military platforms. A small number of highly capable systems can produce outsized effects, but scarcity creates its own vulnerability. Maintenance removes a larger share of the force. One loss matters more. Crews and spare parts become concentrated. Political leaders can overestimate what a handful of impressive platforms can accomplish because the platforms look powerful individually.

The early frigates never solved the basic arithmetic of American sea power. They bought room to maneuver while the country learned what kind of navy it wanted.

A serious rival for the 1790s is USS *Constellation*, which won celebrated victories during the Quasi-War and could claim a cleaner combat case for the decade. *Constitution* wins because her importance begins before her later victories. She is the surviving artifact of the procurement choice itself: Congress accepting that federal sovereignty required an industrial capability that could not be summoned after a merchant ship was already in trouble.

The counterfactual is therefore institutional. If *Constitution* had never won a famous fight, the 1794 decision would still matter. If the entire six-frigate program had been cancelled after diplomacy improved, the United States would have entered later maritime crises with less industrial experience, fewer trained officers, and weaker proof that the federal government could turn appropriations into complex machines.

That is the quieter foundation beneath “Old Ironsides.” The ship's eventual fame makes preservation easier. Her deeper importance is that she embodies the moment the republic decided maritime insecurity was not merely a problem for individual merchants to insure around.

The state would build capacity before it knew exactly which war would justify it.
