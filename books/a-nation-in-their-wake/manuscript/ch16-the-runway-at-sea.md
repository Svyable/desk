# The Runway at Sea

USS *Lexington* began as one kind of future and entered service as another.

The ship was laid down as a battlecruiser, part of an American program conceived during the great naval arms competition that surrounded World War I. Battlecruisers promised battleship-scale guns combined with higher speed, generally accepting less armor in exchange. The concept belonged to a world in which the decisive naval question still seemed likely to be answered by fleets of large gunships finding and destroying one another.

Then diplomacy interrupted construction.

The Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22 produced agreements intended to limit naval competition among major powers. The resulting Washington Naval Treaty imposed limits on capital ships and required the cancellation or scrapping of many planned battleships and battlecruisers.

The treaty also allowed certain unfinished hulls to be converted into aircraft carriers.

*Lexington* survived on a technicality large enough to land airplanes on.

She and her sister ship *Saratoga* were redesigned as enormous carriers and commissioned in 1927. Instead of a main battery intended to fight other capital ships directly, *Lexington* carried aircraft that could project force far beyond the horizon.

A limit on one form of power helped create another.

This is why the ship belongs to the 1920s.

The decade is often remembered as a pause between world wars, a period of disarmament conferences, consumer expansion, radio, automobiles, speculation, and social change. Naval history looks quieter than the dreadnought race before 1914 or the carrier battles after 1941.

The quiet is misleading.

The architecture of the next war was being assembled.

Aircraft had already demonstrated military value during World War I, but aviation at sea remained an unsettled proposition. Navies experimented with seaplanes, converted ships, purpose-built carriers, arresting systems, flight decks, deck handling, maintenance, communications, navigation, and tactics.

The hard problem was not getting an airplane to fly from a ship once.

It was turning a ship into an airfield that could move, launch, recover, fuel, arm, repair, and coordinate enough aircraft to matter in war.

That required a new institution inside the old one.

The Navy had already begun the transition with USS *Langley*, converted from the collier *Jupiter* and commissioned as the service's first aircraft carrier in 1922. *Langley* was indispensable as an experimental platform, but her limitations made the value of the larger converted battlecruisers clearer. A small carrier could teach the Navy how to operate aircraft at sea. *Lexington* and *Saratoga* allowed the Navy to ask what aviation looked like when it became a major fleet capability rather than an experiment attached to one converted ship.

That difference is why *Langley* is the strongest runner-up for the decade and still loses the chapter.

First is not always most consequential.

*Langley* made carrier aviation possible to practice.

*Lexington* made it possible to imagine scale.

A carrier is not simply a ship with airplanes.

It is an airport, weapons depot, maintenance complex, fuel system, command center, communications node, hotel, power plant, and floating piece of industrial logistics. Its striking power sits in machines that leave the hull and return. The ship's most important weapon can be hundreds of miles away.

That changes almost everything about naval thought.

For centuries, a warship's horizon roughly defined its immediate physical fight. Guns extended the horizon, but line of sight remained central. Aviation broke the geometry. A carrier could search and strike far beyond visual range. The enemy could be destroyed without either fleet seeing the other's hulls directly.

Information became as important as armor.

You had to find the other fleet before it found you.

That created a different relationship between offense and defense. A battleship protects itself partly through armor and guns carried directly aboard. A carrier protects itself by finding threats early, launching fighters, maneuvering, operating with escorts, and keeping the enemy at distance. The ship's survival therefore depends on a larger formation and an information picture shared across ships and aircraft.

The carrier makes the fleet more networked.

A battleship can be formidable alone.

A carrier becomes formidable through coordination.

This difference helps explain why the new platform required doctrinal change rather than merely procurement. Buying a carrier without changing scouting, communications, command relationships, maintenance, pilot training, and fleet tactics would create an expensive deck rather than sea power.

The United States Navy had to learn an organization, not a hull.

The 1920s were the period in which that learning accelerated through practice.

*Lexington* and *Saratoga* were unusually large and fast because they inherited battlecruiser hulls and machinery. Their size gave them substantial aircraft capacity and made them valuable experimental platforms. Fleet exercises during the interwar years allowed naval aviators and planners to test how carriers might scout, strike, defend, and operate with the rest of the fleet.

Exercises are where institutions get to be wrong cheaply.

That function is easy to underrate because exercises do not count as history the way battles do. No territory changes hands. No casualty list makes the front page. Yet a peacetime war game can expose a doctrine's assumptions before an enemy does.

The Navy used fleet problems to experiment with carrier operations, including mock attacks that challenged expectations about the vulnerability of bases and the possible reach of naval aviation. Some results pointed toward a future in which carriers could do more than scout for battleships.

Exercises also revealed a problem peculiar to aviation: tempo.

A carrier does not possess a fixed number of attacks merely because it possesses a fixed number of airplanes. Capability depends on how quickly aircraft can be fueled, armed, moved on deck, launched, recovered, inspected, repaired, and sent back out. A flight deck can become a traffic jam. A damaged airplane can block movement. Fueling and ordnance create hazards. Weather can stop operations even when the ship itself can maneuver.

The carrier's real weapon is the sortie cycle.

That cycle turns deck choreography into combat power.

The public sees aircraft taking off.

The institution has to master everything that happens between landings and launches.

This made enlisted technical skill strategically central. Mechanics, ordnancemen, fuel handlers, signalmen, deck crews, radiomen, and many others became part of a chain where one mistake could damage aircraft, injure sailors, start a fire, or delay an entire strike.

Naval aviation therefore changed the social organization of the ship as much as the geometry of combat.

The institution did not convert instantly.

Battleships remained central to naval doctrine and prestige. Many officers believed carriers would support the battle line rather than replace it. This was not stupidity. Early carrier aviation had real limitations. Aircraft range, payload, reliability, navigation, communications, and weather performance were far less capable than later systems. Flight operations were dangerous. Carriers were vulnerable if caught.

Technology does not become dominant merely because a later historian knows it will.

People in the 1920s had to allocate scarce budgets among systems with uncertain futures.

The battleship advocates also had evidence on their side. Battleships were proven in war. Large naval guns could destroy ships and bombard shore targets. Armor offered survivability. Fleets already had doctrine, yards, training pipelines, and command structures built around surface combat.

Carrier advocates were asking the Navy to believe in machines whose capabilities were improving rapidly enough that any current performance figure would soon be obsolete.

This is one of the hardest procurement problems an institution can face.

The old technology looks better in today's test.

The new technology may own tomorrow's environment.

How much current capability should be sacrificed to buy learning about a future that may not arrive as predicted?

The answer cannot come from engineering alone because the uncertainty is strategic.

That is what makes the interwar naval treaties so important. They changed the price of experimentation by constraining traditional capital-ship construction.

When an institution cannot simply keep buying the familiar answer, it may become more creative about alternatives.

Constraint can be an innovation policy.

This is not a universal rule. Limits can also starve useful capability. But the conversion of *Lexington* demonstrates how a treaty designed around existing categories can create space at the edges of those categories.

Regulation often works this way.

A rule defines what is controlled. Engineers and lawyers examine what remains possible. Investment moves toward the frontier left open. The new system grows partly because the old system has been capped.

The naval architects of the 1920s were not exploiting a loophole in a cynical sense. The conversion was permitted deliberately as part of the treaty structure. The broader dynamic remains: governance changes technological incentives.

The carrier itself also changed the relationship between navy and industry.

Aircraft had shorter technological cycles than ships. A carrier hull might serve for decades while the planes operating from it changed repeatedly. The platform became a host for faster-moving technology.

This creates a strategic advantage if the host is adaptable.

It creates obsolescence if the host cannot accommodate the next generation.

Modern systems increasingly work this way. A ship's sensors, software, missiles, aircraft, communications, and electronic systems may change many times during the hull's life. The expensive long-lived platform has to survive multiple generations of components whose improvement cycle is faster.

*Lexington* was an early large-scale example of a naval platform whose value depended on what could operate from it rather than only what was built permanently into it.

The carrier's weapons were replaceable in a way battleship guns were not.

This helps explain why naval aviation could improve so rapidly in the interwar period. New aircraft designs could extend range, speed, payload, and mission capability without rebuilding the entire carrier.

That adaptability also creates design tension. A carrier built for one generation of aircraft may encounter heavier, faster airplanes requiring stronger decks, more fuel, different maintenance spaces, improved arresting gear, larger elevators, or new launch systems. The host must have enough margin to absorb a future its designers cannot specify completely.

Margin looks like excess at commissioning.

Years later it becomes adaptability.

This is another place where accounting and engineering can disagree. A platform optimized perfectly for today's mission may have less room to survive tomorrow's technology.

The ship became an ecosystem.

That word is overused in technology discussion, but here it earns its place. Carrier capability depends on pilots, deck crews, maintenance, aircraft manufacturers, fuel, munitions, training bases, doctrine, navigation, radio, weather information, escort ships, and replenishment.

No single component is the carrier revolution.

The revolution is coordination.

The United States had enough time in the 1920s and 1930s to practice some of that coordination before war arrived.

Japan did too.

The two Pacific powers developed formidable carrier aviation communities. The irony of Perry's *Susquehanna* grows sharper here. The United States had once used steam warships to demonstrate a technological gap to Japan. Japan responded through decades of modernization and eventually built a navy capable of delivering a carrier strike against the U.S. Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor.

The target learned.

That does not make Perry responsible for Pearl Harbor. It makes historical superiority less comfortable.

Power provokes adaptation.

The United States and Japan also operated under treaty limits that produced different strategic calculations. Japanese leaders increasingly resented a naval treaty system in which permitted tonnage reflected a hierarchy they believed constrained their status and security. Arms control can reduce aggregate weapons while intensifying political grievance if parties view the allocation as illegitimate.

The 1920s naval settlement therefore contained both stabilizing and destabilizing elements.

It slowed one arms race.

It did not resolve the interests underneath it.

The treaty regime also teaches a subtler lesson about measurement. Arms-control agreements have to count something. Tonnage, gun caliber, ship categories, basing, or numbers become proxies for military power because a treaty cannot limit an abstract concept like “threat.”

Once power is translated into a measurable category, innovation begins outside the category's assumptions.

Aircraft carriers were not invisible to treaty negotiators. They were regulated too. But their future military value was harder to estimate because the key variable was not merely hull tonnage. It was the improving capability of aircraft launched from the tonnage.

A treaty can freeze steel more easily than it freezes learning.

This is another reason *Lexington* is a more revealing choice than a passenger liner associated with the decade's consumer prosperity. The ship sits at the intersection of restraint and preparation.

The United States was trying to avoid another ruinous naval competition while developing capabilities that would become decisive in the next one.

Peace was not demilitarization.

It was experimentation under limits.

The carrier's rise also changed which kind of sailor could become strategically central.

Naval aviation created a professional subculture inside the Navy. Pilots, observers, mechanics, deck handlers, and aviation planners developed expertise unfamiliar to the traditional surface fleet. New career paths formed. Institutional politics followed.

Every transformative technology creates people whose status rises with it.

Those people become advocates, sometimes zealots. Their opponents may defend legacy systems partly from genuine strategic belief and partly because institutions distribute prestige, command, budgets, and identity through technology.

The battleship was not just a ship.

It was a hierarchy.

The carrier was not just a platform.

It was a rival hierarchy being born.

The arguments were not resolved fully by theoretical debate.

War resolved them brutally.

When Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, the U.S. battleships present suffered devastating losses. The American aircraft carriers were not in port. The Pacific War quickly became a carrier war, with Coral Sea, Midway, Eastern Solomons, Santa Cruz, Philippine Sea, and other battles demonstrating that naval air power could decide fleet engagements at ranges battleship admirals of an earlier era had not faced.

*Lexington* herself would be lost in the Battle of the Coral Sea in May 1942.

Her aircraft helped strike Japanese forces, but damage from bombs and torpedoes led to fires and internal explosions. The ship was abandoned and scuttled by an American destroyer.

Like *Bonhomme Richard* and *Monitor*, the platform disappeared while the capability it represented expanded.

The name *Lexington* was quickly reassigned to another carrier then under construction, reflecting the Navy's habit of converting lost ships into institutional continuity.

The replacement, CV-16, would serve through World War II and for decades afterward.

Names cross technology generations because organizations want lineage to survive loss.

The original *Lexington*'s greatest contribution, however, may have come before the war.

She gave the Navy years to learn.

This is a form of strategic value difficult to measure. How much is an experiment worth if it prevents a later mistake? How do you price doctrine refined before combat, deck procedures made safer, maintenance systems improved, or officers trained on a platform they will later use under fire?

Peace produces counterfactual returns.

The disasters that do not happen leave weak evidence.

That is why militaries often underinvest in practice when budgets tighten. Training consumes fuel and wears equipment. Exercises create accidents. Experimental units look inefficient. Readiness spending can be deferred because the enemy has not yet imposed a deadline.

Then the deadline arrives all at once.

The 1920s gave American naval aviation a rare resource: time without combat.

The Great Depression would squeeze budgets in the 1930s, and strategic conditions would deteriorate. Yet the institutional knowledge built around carriers continued growing.

That knowledge had to compete with another kind of urgency as the world entered the next decade. Economic collapse consumed public attention. Authoritarian regimes expanded. Refugees sought routes out of Europe. The United States possessed enough state capacity to experiment with aircraft carriers and negotiate limits on fleets.

Capacity alone did not decide what the country would do with people asking for refuge.

The ship of the next decade in this book is not another warship.

It is a passenger vessel carrying people who desperately needed a country to let them land.

MS *St. Louis* sailed from Hamburg in 1939 with more than nine hundred passengers, most of them Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi Germany. They possessed documents for Cuba. When the ship reached Havana, most were not allowed to disembark. The vessel then approached the United States and sought a solution.

None came.

The ship returned to Europe.

The contrast with *Lexington* is severe on purpose.

The 1920s United States invested years learning how to project force beyond the horizon.

In 1939 the country faced a different test of state capacity: whether it would use administrative discretion and political will to admit people whose danger was increasingly visible.

Power is not measured only by what a country can reach.

It is measured by what it chooses to receive.

## The Carrier Was Not Born at Lexington

The first American aircraft carrier was USS *Langley*, converted from the collier *Jupiter* and commissioned as a carrier in 1922. Any chapter that presents *Lexington* as the beginning of American carrier aviation would be wrong.

*Langley* is the strongest rival for the decade precisely because she establishes the first. She allowed the Navy to learn basic flight-deck operations, carrier landings, handling, and procedures on a platform whose experimental character was explicit.

*Lexington* wins because she changes the scale of the experiment.

A small converted collier can demonstrate that aircraft can operate from a moving deck. A large fast carrier forces the Navy to ask what happens when aviation becomes a major component of fleet power. More aircraft mean more deck cycles, fuel, maintenance, spare parts, munitions, pilots, radios, and command decisions. Scale turns a trick into an institution.

The treaty origin adds another reason. The hull had been intended for a battlecruiser because that was where prestige and capital investment were flowing. Arms control made the original form less useful and permitted conversion. The Navy received a platform large enough to explore a different future because diplomacy stranded an expensive piece of the old one.

That kind of path dependence matters in technology. Organizations rarely design the future on a clean sheet. They inherit sunk costs, treaties, buildings, skills, appropriations, and partially completed hardware. Innovation often begins by asking what the old investment can become.

The counterfactual is therefore practical. Without the Washington Treaty, *Lexington* might have entered service as a battlecruiser, and American carrier experimentation would have relied more heavily on smaller platforms until new construction caught up. Carrier aviation still advances, but the Navy loses years of experience operating a very large air group at sea.

Those years mattered because the next war did not allow time for basic experimentation.

Peacetime exercises supplied failures without enemy fire. Deck crews learned sequencing. Aviators learned navigation and recovery. Commanders learned that the carrier's striking range made scouting and information central. The Navy did not emerge from the 1920s with perfect doctrine, but it entered the 1940s with a professional community that had already made and corrected mistakes.

That is why *Lexington* is more important than being first.

First proves possibility.

Scale teaches consequences.
