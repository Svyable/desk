# The Ship That Sank Into a Nation

In 1779 the United States was a country with a navy in the same way it was a country with a future: both existed, but neither could yet be taken for granted.

The Declaration of Independence was three years old. The war with Britain had already outlasted the first optimism of revolution. France had formally allied itself with the Americans, transforming the conflict from an imperial rebellion into a wider war, but an alliance did not erase the imbalance at sea. Britain possessed the most formidable naval institution in the world. The United States possessed courage, privateers, improvisation, a small Continental Navy, and a recurring shortage of almost everything required to make sea power durable.

John Paul Jones's most famous command entered that mismatch from the French side of the ledger.

The ship had not been built for the American Revolution. She had been the *Duc de Duras*, a French East Indiaman, a large merchant vessel designed for long-distance commerce rather than as a purpose-built warship. The French crown transferred her to American use. Jones renamed her *Bonhomme Richard*, commonly understood as a tribute to Benjamin Franklin and the French rendering associated with Poor Richard. Even before she sailed under her new name, the vessel carried the odd coalition that made American independence possible: a Scottish-born officer fighting for the United States, a French merchant hull, French strategic support, and a crew drawn from multiple nationalities.

A national myth often becomes cleaner as it ages. This one becomes more interesting when the edges remain rough.

Jones did not take to sea in a perfectly American ship crewed by a homogeneous body of patriots operating from an established national base. He commanded an improvised instrument of coalition warfare. His squadron itself was multinational. His authority depended on diplomacy, personal reputation, French support, and the unstable administrative machinery of a country fighting for existence. The United States was already discovering something it would rediscover repeatedly over the next 250 years: national power can be intensely national in purpose while materially dependent on alliances, foreign production, borrowed platforms, international crews, and systems that do not fit neatly inside the flag.

That dependence did not make the mission less American. It tells us what the word American meant at the time.

The new republic was trying to establish facts in a world still entitled to doubt it. A declaration could assert sovereignty. A diplomat could negotiate recognition. An army could defend territory. At sea, sovereignty had another test. Could American authority move beyond American shores? Could it interfere with British commerce, survive contact with the Royal Navy, and force other governments and merchants to treat the United States as more than a temporary insurgency?

Jones understood the political value of carrying war into waters Britain considered home territory. Raiding around the British Isles did not threaten to defeat the Royal Navy outright. That was never realistic. It could, however, impose cost, unsettle public confidence, force defensive attention, capture prizes, and make the rebellion geographically inconvenient. The psychological geometry mattered. Britain was fighting a war across the Atlantic. Jones wanted some British communities to feel the Atlantic disappear.

The cruise that made *Bonhomme Richard* famous reached its climax off the coast of Yorkshire on September 23, 1779.

Jones's squadron encountered a British convoy escorted by HMS *Serapis* and the smaller *Countess of Scarborough*. *Serapis* was a newer, purpose-built warship. *Bonhomme Richard* was larger but older, and some of Jones's heaviest guns became liabilities almost immediately. Two of the eighteen-pounders in his lower battery burst early in the engagement, killing men around them and leading Jones to abandon the remaining guns of that battery. In a conventional exchange conducted at useful distance, the American commander's position deteriorated quickly.

The solution was not elegant. It was proximity.

Jones maneuvered alongside *Serapis* and the ships became locked together. What followed is remembered as a naval duel, but at close range the phrase can sound too orderly. Guns fired across almost no distance. Musketry came from the tops and decks. Men attempted to board. Fire became a recurring danger. The ships' rigging and spars tangled. *Bonhomme Richard* absorbed punishment her merchant structure had never been intended to endure.

At some point in the fight, the British captain, Richard Pearson, believed Jones might be ready to surrender. The exchange that followed became the source of the most famous sentence attached to Jones, usually rendered as “I have not yet begun to fight.” The difficulty is that the exact wording comes through later recollection rather than a clean contemporary transcript. That does not diminish the episode. It improves our view of how national memory works. A messy close-quarters battle produced an answer remembered, polished, and repeated because the new country needed language equal to the story it wanted to tell about itself.

The more secure fact is what Jones did next.

He continued fighting.

The battle turned partly through persistence and partly through the peculiar advantages created by the ships being lashed together. American marksmen and sailors aloft could fire down onto the British deck. Grenades were reportedly dropped into *Serapis*, and an explosion caused severe casualties. Damage accumulated. Pearson eventually struck his colors.

Jones had captured the better ship.

He had also lost his own.

*Bonhomme Richard* was so badly damaged that efforts to save her failed. She sank roughly thirty-six hours after the battle. Jones transferred to *Serapis*. The ship that gave the United States one of its defining naval legends never returned as a trophy. Its victory survived in the form of the captured enemy vessel, the reports that moved through Europe and America, the reputation of its commander, and a story that proved unusually durable.

That ending is one reason *Bonhomme Richard* belongs to the 1770s more than a more conventionally successful ship might.

A young nation had very little physical permanence to spare. Its capital would move. Its currency would fail tests. Its army would disband and be rebuilt in different forms. Political institutions were still experimental. The Articles of Confederation had not even fully taken effect when the battle was fought. Yet the republic was already generating intangible assets that mattered: reputation, credibility, diplomatic usefulness, fear, admiration, and the sense that Americans could do things established powers had assumed were beyond them.

Ships are good machines for producing this kind of credibility because they force claims into foreign space.

An army defending home territory can be interpreted as a local fact. A ship appears elsewhere. It arrives in another nation's port. It raids another nation's coast. It demands supplies, recognition, repair, legal decisions, and diplomatic handling. Every voyage tests whether a flag will be honored, whether papers will be recognized, whether prizes will be accepted, whether neutral governments will cooperate, and whether enemies will divert resources to respond.

The flag on *Bonhomme Richard* did not yet represent the sprawling naval state the United States would become. It represented a political experiment asking the rest of the world to behave as if it would last.

France's role is essential here because American retellings can turn allied assistance into background scenery. It was not scenery. French military and financial support mattered enormously to American independence. At sea, the relationship was visible in wood, guns, ports, and logistics. Jones's command was possible because an ally with greater resources was willing to make material power available to the American cause.

That makes the ship a useful first chapter for another reason. It disrupts the self-sufficiency myth at the beginning rather than correcting it later.

The United States did not emerge from the Atlantic world by becoming independent of the Atlantic world. It emerged by changing its position inside it.

Before the Revolution, colonial commerce had been shaped by British imperial rules and protected, imperfectly but significantly, by British sea power. Independence removed political subordination but also removed automatic access to the commercial protections of empire. The new country wanted the freedom to trade widely while lacking the naval capacity of the system it had left. That gap would haunt the next several decades. North African corsairs, European wars, British impressment, French seizures, and attacks on American shipping would repeatedly force a question the Revolution could not settle by declaration: what did commercial sovereignty cost?

*Bonhomme Richard* supplied one answer before the country was ready to institutionalize it.

It cost ships. It cost allies. It cost trained crews, officers, ports, finance, diplomatic agreements, munitions, repair, intelligence, and a government able to coordinate them. Heroism could substitute for some of this temporarily. It could not substitute forever.

Jones himself is useful precisely because he stands at the border between heroism and institution.

He had gone to sea young and built his career in merchant service before the Revolution gave him a national cause. He was ambitious, difficult, professionally serious, and acutely conscious of status. He wanted American naval service to command respect. His later career, including service in the Russian navy, reminds us that eighteenth-century maritime expertise moved through an international labor market more fluidly than later nationalist biographies sometimes imply.

The sailors around him were even less compatible with a simple national portrait. Crew composition changed through recruitment, transfers, captured men, volunteers, and the ordinary complexity of eighteenth-century seafaring. Europeans and Americans served together. Some men were motivated by politics; others by wages, prize money, adventure, necessity, or the availability of a berth. A ship at war is a political instrument crewed by human beings whose reasons for being there are rarely identical.

That distinction matters because nations like to remember collective action as collective intention.

History is usually more complicated. States assemble power from people with mixed motives. Shipyards employ workers who may care more about pay than strategy. Merchant owners seek profit while governments seek influence. Sailors may be patriots and professionals and economic actors at the same time. Foreign allies can advance their own interests while materially enabling yours. None of that invalidates the national result. It explains how national results are made.

The battle with *Serapis* also reveals something the United States would come to value almost dangerously: the political productivity of tactical audacity.

Americans have often preferred stories in which an inferior or underestimated force closes distance, absorbs punishment, improvises, and wins through refusal. Those stories fit the Revolution unusually well because the country's founding war itself could be narrated as a weaker power outlasting a stronger empire. Jones's battle became one of the maritime versions of that national preference.

The danger is that a memorable exception can be mistaken for a scalable strategy.

A navy cannot plan to have every outmatched ship become *Bonhomme Richard*. Institutions exist partly to reduce the need for miraculous performance. Better ships, standardized training, secure logistics, doctrine, maintenance, intelligence, and reliable funding are ways of replacing heroic improvisation with repeatable competence. The history of American sea power after the Revolution can be read as an argument between these two instincts: admiration for the officer who wins despite the system, and the less dramatic work of building a system that does not require him to.

The 1790s chapter will return to that problem with USS *Constitution*. By then the federal government had accepted that commerce needed permanent naval protection and had authorized frigates designed from the beginning as national instruments. But the distance between *Bonhomme Richard* and *Constitution* is not simply technological. It is constitutional.

In 1779 the United States was improvising power through a revolutionary government. In 1797 it would launch a frigate ordered by a federal law, financed through federal authority, built within a developing naval establishment, and intended to operate as part of a permanent service.

The ship changes because the state changes.

That is the pattern to watch across the book. A vessel can be a snapshot of institutional maturity. What the government can commission, finance, crew, maintain, deploy, regulate, investigate, or rescue at sea says something about what kind of government it has become.

*Bonhomme Richard* says the United States in the 1770s was not yet a naval state.

It says something more surprising: it did not have to be one to create naval consequences.

The victory traveled faster and farther than the wreck. European courts and newspapers paid attention to Jones. Britain had to reckon with the embarrassment of a war brought into home waters and an escorting warship captured after a savage engagement. France could see evidence that its ally was capable of imposing costs. Americans acquired a story in which their flag had survived impossible proximity to British power.

The material object disappeared beneath the North Sea.

The political object became more solid.

This distinction between material loss and strategic effect recurs in maritime history. A ship can sink and still win an argument. It can survive and still become evidence of failure. It can be technologically obsolete but politically explosive. It can be commercially ordinary until one voyage changes the system around it.

The temptation in a book organized around ships is to treat survival as importance. Museums encourage that gently because the ships we can visit are the ships that remain. USS *Constitution* still floats. *Bonhomme Richard* does not. Preservation creates an accidental hierarchy of memory.

But the sea preserves consequences differently.

A wreck may vanish from ordinary sight while its story shapes doctrine, identity, commemoration, and the choices made for the next fleet. The physical absence can even help the legend. There is no aging merchant hull to complicate the mental image of Jones's battle. There is the name, the combat, the sinking, and the transfer to the captured *Serapis*.

Later American warships would carry the name *Bonhomme Richard*, including an aircraft carrier in the twentieth century and an amphibious assault ship in the twenty-first. The reuse is more than naval sentiment. Navies create lineage deliberately because ships are temporary and institutions want memory to survive the steel. Names let a service carry an old story aboard a new technology.

The original ship's own technology was already compromised by the time of the famous fight. She had been a merchant vessel, refitted for war, and her age and construction helped explain how badly she suffered. The very mismatch between vessel and mission became part of the drama. The United States was using what it could obtain, not what an ideal naval planner would have designed.

That, too, is revolutionary history.

Founding generations are often remembered for design: declarations, constitutions, theories, deliberate choices. They also lived through enormous improvisation. Armies needed blankets. Governments needed credit. Diplomats needed ships. Commanders used converted merchantmen. Alliances filled gaps. People made decisions with uncertain information because waiting for a perfect institution would have meant waiting past the moment when the institution could be born.

There is a tendency to look backward from the eventual United States and see those improvisations as steps on a road that had to lead somewhere.

The men aboard *Bonhomme Richard* did not have that luxury.

They could not know that the Constitution would be written eight years later. They could not know that the United States would build one of the world's dominant navies, acquire a Pacific coast, fight wars in Asia, launch aircraft from carriers, put submarines under polar ice, or recover astronauts from the Pacific. They could not know that a later American public would turn the battle's reported words into schoolbook memory.

They knew they were beside *Serapis* and the ship beneath them was being destroyed.

That is enough.

History becomes less interesting when we force participants to understand what their actions would mean to us. Jones wanted a victory, prizes, recognition, strategic effect, and professional standing. France wanted pressure on Britain. Britain wanted its convoy protected. Sailors wanted to survive, fight, obey, earn, escape, or endure according to their circumstances. The nation-making consequence emerged from those immediate purposes without being identical to any one of them.

This is why *Bonhomme Richard* is my ship of the 1770s rather than a vessel associated with a cleaner founding moment.

It contains the contradictions at the start.

The ship is American and French. Merchant and warship. Victorious and lost. National symbol and multinational workplace. Evidence of individual command and evidence of dependence on alliance. It expands the young republic's reputation while demonstrating how little permanent naval infrastructure the republic yet controls.

Most of all, it makes independence look like something other than a document.

Independence had to travel.

It had to cross an ocean carrying papers other governments would honor. It had to enter foreign ports. It had to survive encounters with established powers. It had to create risk for enemies and opportunity for merchants. It had to persuade lenders and allies that the new flag represented an entity likely to exist long enough to repay obligations and keep agreements.

A nation is partly the accumulation of other people's decisions to treat it as real.

On September 23, 1779, a battered converted merchant ship helped make that decision harder to avoid.

Then *Bonhomme Richard* went down.

For a country still fighting to stay afloat, the symbolism is almost too convenient. I would distrust it if the facts were not stranger than the metaphor.

The United States did not need the ship to survive the battle.

It needed the consequence.

The next decade would reveal what consequence could become when the guns were put away and the hold was filled for trade. In 1784, while the political architecture of the new country remained unsettled, an American merchant vessel sailed from New York carrying silver, lead, wine, cordage, furs, and a cargo that must have looked almost eccentric to anyone unfamiliar with the market on the other side of the world: tens of thousands of pounds of ginseng.

Its destination was Canton.

The republic was about to discover that sovereignty could be carried in a cargo hold too.
