# A Nation in Their Wake

**Sven Hardy Benson**

*Twenty-Six Ships, 250 Years, and the Stories That Made America*

---

America is usually drawn from the land inward.

Thirteen colonies become states. A frontier moves west. Roads cross mountains. Rails stitch coasts together. Farms become towns, towns become cities, cities become metropolitan regions. The familiar map fills itself in until the country looks inevitable: a continental republic bounded by two oceans, as if the oceans were merely the blue margins around the real story.

They were never margins.

Before the United States had a Constitution, an American merchant ship was already headed for China. Before federal power had settled into recognizable form, the young republic was arguing over whether it needed permanent warships to protect commerce beyond sight of land. Before railroads reached the Pacific, steamships were carrying mail, passengers, gold seekers, officials, and expectations around Cape Horn and through the Isthmus. Before the country could imagine itself as a world power, its ships were crossing oceans to trade, coerce, explore, fight, rescue, transport, survey, threaten, migrate, pollute, and connect.

The sea kept arriving in American history whether Americans were looking toward it or not.

A naval battle could alter the political meaning of independence. A merchant voyage could create a trade relationship with China while the republic was still governed under the Articles of Confederation. An attack on one frigate could turn impressment from a diplomatic grievance into a national humiliation. A steamship could prove a technology before the market knew what to do with it. A revolt aboard a schooner could force the Supreme Court to decide whether people carried across the Atlantic as property would be treated as property under American law.

Later the ships grew larger and the systems around them harder to see.

They carried immigrants, refugees, oil, boxes, aircraft, astronauts, weapons, and the country's assumptions about itself. Some became instruments of power. Others exposed the limits of power. Some are remembered because they won. Some because they sank. Some because they carried people America wanted. Some because they carried people America refused. Some because a mechanical failure, an attack, a storm, or a grounding suddenly revealed a network that had been functioning quietly until the day it did not.

This book chooses one ship for every named decade touched by the United States' first 250 years.

That produces twenty-six ships, not twenty-five. The country declared independence in 1776 and reaches its semiquincentennial in 2026. Those 250 years touch part of the 1770s, all the named decades between, and part of the 2020s. I have kept the ordinary decades because people remember history that way. The slight asymmetry is useful. America begins this story mid-decade and ends it, for now, mid-decade. Nations do not respect the clean edges we give calendars.

The word important requires a warning.

There is no instrument that can measure the historical importance of USS *Monitor* against the *Golden Venture*, or the *Empress of China* against *Exxon Valdez*. These ships did different things in different systems under different moral conditions. A list pretending otherwise would be trivia wearing a necktie.

The choice in each chapter is an argument.

I am looking for the ship that opens the decade most widely. The vessel must matter, but its greater job is to make the surrounding machinery visible: capital, law, labor, technology, politics, geography, prejudice, logistics, diplomacy, military doctrine, environmental risk, or infrastructure. Sometimes the ship is the cause of change. More often it is where several forces meet clearly enough to see them at once.

That means this is not a book of twenty-six heroic captains.

There are captains here, and admirals, sailors, engineers, passengers, shipyard workers, merchants, refugees, astronauts, regulators, smugglers, soldiers, lawyers, and politicians. But no ship moves through history alone. Even the most commanding figure stands inside a web of financing, orders, weather information, technology, institutions, labor, and decisions made elsewhere.

It also means the ships need not be American.

RMS *Lusitania* was British. MS *St. Louis* was German. The container ship *Dali* was Singapore-flagged. Their stories belong here because a foreign flag does not make an event foreign to American history. A country connected to the world cannot insist that only its own hulls count.

Nor does a ship have to be admirable to be important.

USS *Maine* became politically consequential partly because Americans believed things about its destruction before investigators could know them. The *St. Louis* matters because the United States did not become the refuge its later self-image might suggest. *Exxon Valdez* matters because a routine industrial system failed catastrophically in a place where the consequences could not be hidden. The *Golden Venture* matters because hundreds of people who had crossed an ocean in search of America reached Queens by swimming from a grounded freighter and discovered that arrival and admission were different things.

The selection for the 2020s is more provisional than the rest. We are still living the decade. I chose the *Dali*, which struck Baltimore's Francis Scott Key Bridge in 2024 after losing electrical power and propulsion, because the disaster placed an extraordinary amount of contemporary America into one frame: global container shipping, an aging bridge, electrical systems, port labor, highway logistics, immigration, emergency communication, corporate ownership, regulation, and the vulnerability created when enormous moving objects pass through infrastructure built for another era. Another event may eclipse it before 2030. History is allowed to revise the table of contents.

That admission applies backward too.

A reader will choose differently in some decades. Good. The 1860s could produce a shelf of candidates. The 1940s could reasonably belong to a carrier, a battleship, a troopship, a submarine, or an amphibious vessel. The 1950s could go to USS *Nautilus*, whose nuclear propulsion announced a different future from the one I chose. I chose *Ideal X* instead because a converted tanker carrying fifty-eight truck bodies from Newark to Houston in 1956 eventually changed more of ordinary economic life than most people standing near the dock could possibly have guessed.

That is the kind of ship I am after.

The vessel whose importance is obvious only after the world around it has changed.

The phrase “made America” in the subtitle therefore does not mean that ships made the country by themselves. It means that the making of America repeatedly passed through ships. They were pieces of moving infrastructure before infrastructure became a common word. They extended markets, state power, violence, migration routes, supply chains, communications, and national imagination across water. A harbor could be a border, a factory gate, a military base, a courtroom, an environmental fault line, and a link in a global production system, sometimes at the same time.

We tend to remember ships as objects.

This one had iron armor. That one had four funnels. This one carried so many guns. That one displaced so many tons. Museums understandably preserve dimensions because steel, wood, machinery, and design are part of the story. But specifications can become a way of shrinking history to the hull.

I am more interested in the wake.

A wake is evidence that something has passed and disturbance that continues after it is gone. It widens behind a vessel, crosses other wakes, reaches shores the ship itself never touches, and eventually disappears into water that looks calm again.

History behaves similarly.

The *Empress of China* returned to New York in 1785. The route did not disappear with her. *Monitor* sank the same year she fought *Virginia*. The industrial transformation she represented did not sink with her. The first Liberty ship was gone decades before containerization hollowed out and rebuilt American port geography. The *Golden Venture* was scrapped, but the arguments over asylum, detention, smuggling, and borders that met on its decks did not go with the steel.

A country is partly the sum of disturbances that no longer look like disturbances because we now live inside their consequences.

Ships are unusually good at revealing this. They are bounded objects moving through unbounded systems. A ship has a name, a launch date, a crew list, a route, an owner, dimensions, machinery, and usually a documented fate. Around that finite thing spread systems large enough to become abstract. Trade. Empire. Immigration. Oil. War. Globalization. Infrastructure.

Put the abstraction aboard a ship and it has to become physical.

Trade becomes a hold filled with ginseng and silver on its way to Canton. Industrial mobilization becomes plate steel, welds, shift schedules, and another Liberty ship leaving the ways. Neutrality becomes passengers on a liner moving through a declared war zone. Refugee policy becomes a vessel close enough to Florida for passengers to see a place they cannot enter. Containerization becomes fifty-eight boxes that can move from a truck chassis to a deck without being unpacked. Infrastructure vulnerability becomes a ship without propulsion moving toward a bridge pier at night.

The point is not to make history simpler.

It is to give complexity a hull.

The chapters that follow move decade by decade, but the deeper story is not chronological neatness. It is accumulation. The young country first needs ships to prove that independence can survive beyond its coast. Then it needs them to protect commerce. Then to reach farther markets. Then to carry industrial power and territorial ambition. Then to move people and goods at scales earlier Americans could not imagine. By the twentieth century the ship is increasingly one component inside systems too large for any captain to command: fuel networks, alliance structures, mass production, immigration law, air power, spaceflight, oil transport, terrorism, container logistics.

The vessel remains visible while the surrounding system becomes more abstract.

That inversion may be the real subject of the book.

In 1779, a reader can understand much of *Bonhomme Richard*'s predicament by looking at the ship, the opposing ship, their guns, crews, orders, and political purpose. By 2024, understanding the *Dali* requires us to leave the bridge almost immediately. We have to follow electrical components, maintenance practice, corporate structures, pilotage, bridge-protection standards, port economics, roadway traffic, emergency calls, supply chains, and the history of a piece of infrastructure opened in 1977.

The ships got bigger.

The systems got bigger faster.

And yet when the system succeeds, the ship often disappears from public attention. Most Americans consume the work of ships constantly without seeing one. Clothes, electronics, furniture, vehicles, fuel, machinery, food ingredients, industrial components, and the containers themselves cross oceans and waterways far from the customer. Sea power protects routes whose existence becomes noticeable mainly during blockade or war. Domestic shipping laws matter most to people who rarely encounter the vessels they regulate. Port closures become store inventories, factory delays, commodity prices, trucking demand, and rerouted rail traffic inland.

The water is still in the story even when the water is out of sight.

So we begin where the country begins: not with a coastline behind it, but with an ocean in front of it.

The first ship is borrowed, renamed, undergunned for the fight it chooses, crewed by men from more nations than the young republic could plausibly claim as its own, and destined not to make it home.

America's maritime history starts, appropriately, with a ship it does not really own in the way later Americans would understand ownership, fighting for a nation whose future is still uncertain.

Then it sinks.

The wake continues.
