# The First American Road to China

On February 22, 1784, the *Empress of China* sailed from New York for Canton carrying an unusual proposition: the United States could be commercially independent before it had figured out how to govern itself permanently.

The timing is the first thing to notice.

The Treaty of Paris had formally ended the Revolutionary War only months earlier. The Constitution had not been drafted. George Washington was not president because there was no presidency. The federal government Americans now take for granted did not exist. The country operated under the Articles of Confederation, a system whose weaknesses would soon become a subject of alarm among many of the people who had helped create it.

Yet merchants were not waiting for the political machinery to settle.

They had a problem to solve immediately. Independence had broken the old imperial commercial relationship. American merchants were free of British rule, but freedom was not the same thing as access. Under empire, colonial trade had existed inside a British system of law, naval power, finance, ports, and restrictions. The Revolution removed the restrictions the Americans resented and also removed privileges and protections they had often treated as part of the environment.

Now the new country's merchants had to make their own routes.

The *Empress of China* carried one of the first great attempts.

Her investors included prominent American merchants, among them Robert Morris, the financier and public figure whose career straddled government and commerce in ways common to the founding generation. The voyage's commercial officers included Samuel Shaw and Thomas Randall, veterans of the Revolution who went to China not as diplomats in the modern sense but as supercargoes responsible for the business of the voyage. Captain John Green commanded the ship.

The cargo was assembled around a brutal fact of global trade: wanting Chinese goods did not mean Chinese merchants wanted American goods in return.

Tea, porcelain, silk, and other Chinese manufactures had strong demand in Atlantic markets. The commercial challenge was finding exports that could clear the other side of the ledger. Americans carried Spanish silver dollars and a collection of goods, but the most memorable cargo was American ginseng.

Ginseng grew in North America and had a valued place in Chinese medicine and commerce. The connection turned a plant gathered from eastern forests into a bridge between two economies that had barely begun dealing directly with each other. It is hard to imagine a better symbol for the improvised nature of early American globalization. The country did not enter China trade with a great manufactured export. It went looking through its forests for something Canton would buy.

Thomas Randall later described the voyage in a letter connected to Alexander Hamilton's work. His account identified the *Empress of China* as the first ship to sail from the United States for China and listed the cargo: silver, hundreds of piculs of ginseng, cordage, wine, lead, iron, furs, and smaller articles. The document is useful because it strips away the retrospective grandeur. The first direct American venture to China was a commercial calculation made item by item.

That calculation sent the ship around the Cape of Good Hope and into a trading system much older and more structured than the United States.

Canton was not an open marketplace where a newly independent flag could simply arrive and transact on its own terms. Foreign commerce was managed under the Canton system, concentrated through designated Chinese merchants and regulated by imperial authorities. Europeans had established factories and routines. The British East India Company occupied a position the American merchants could not imitate. The newcomers needed to learn the rules from people who already understood them.

Randall later recalled that the European trading communities received the Americans politely and that the Americans sought information about Chinese commercial procedures. The *Empress of China* had to be measured for port duties and required a Chinese merchant who would assume responsibility for the ship's conduct and trade. The details matter because they invert a familiar American story.

The Americans had just won political independence from a European empire. In Canton they were beginners.

They arrived in a system whose rules they had not written, dealing through institutions they did not control, trying to discover what their goods were worth. Independence had made them sovereign. It had not made them powerful everywhere.

That distinction would shape two centuries of American relations with Asia.

The first China voyage was not a prelude to inevitable dominance. It was an entrance. Americans wanted access to an enormous commercial world and had to learn how to operate inside it. The power balance would change radically over time. The United States would later participate in the coercive opening of Asian markets, take colonies in the Pacific, fight a devastating war against Japan, build an alliance system across the region, and become economically intertwined with China at a scale the *Empress of China*'s investors could not have imagined.

But it began with merchants asking how business was done.

The ship reached Canton in 1784 and returned to New York in May 1785. The voyage was profitable enough to encourage imitation. More American vessels followed. A direct trade with China became established. The United States, still a thin Atlantic republic, had found a commercial road to the Pacific world before it possessed a Pacific coastline.

That geographical paradox is the reason the *Empress of China* is my ship of the 1780s.

Americans often narrate their move into the Pacific as a westward story. Settlers cross the Appalachians. The Louisiana Purchase expands the map. Explorers reach the Pacific. Oregon and California become American. Railroads eventually join the coasts. All of that matters.

Commerce took another route.

It went east to get west.

A ship from New York could cross the Atlantic, round Africa, traverse the Indian Ocean, and enter the China trade without waiting for the United States to occupy the continent between New York and the Pacific. The ocean defeated the apparent logic of the land map. A country with no Pacific coast could already have Pacific interests.

That fact would keep pulling American policy outward.

Merchants do not simply move goods. They create constituencies for routes, rules, protection, information, treaties, ports, and government attention. Once Americans have property and lives exposed abroad, foreign policy gains a new set of domestic claimants. A shipping loss becomes a political issue. A tariff becomes a political issue. Port access, piracy, war between third countries, diplomatic representation, navigation charts, and naval protection become political issues.

The hold creates work for the state.

This was not obvious in 1784 because the American state remained weak. The *Empress of China* voyage was a private commercial enterprise. Its organizers did not need a federal trade ministry to tell them to pursue Canton. That entrepreneurial freedom is part of the story. Early American merchants were capable of extending the country's commercial presence beyond the reach of its institutions.

But the voyage also showed why institutions would eventually follow.

Samuel Shaw's experience is instructive. After participating in the first voyage, he became the first American consul at Canton. The position was unpaid, an arrangement that says a great deal about the limited administrative capacity of the early republic. The government needed representation in a place where American commerce was growing but lacked the modern bureaucratic apparatus that would later staff embassies, consulates, trade offices, naval stations, customs services, and intelligence functions around the world.

So public authority attached itself to private commercial expertise.

That pattern is not unique to the United States or to the eighteenth century. Governments expand along paths first traced by merchants, missionaries, financiers, migrants, soldiers, and technology firms. Private actors encounter foreign laws and risks; the state is asked to protect, formalize, negotiate, or regulate. Commercial geography can become strategic geography before the public notices the transition.

The China trade did exactly that.

It also complicated the economic meaning of independence at home.

Americans had rebelled against British imperial control, but their consumption remained deeply connected to imported goods. Tea, the commodity at the center of one of the Revolution's most famous protests, did not become less desirable because independence had been won. If anything, direct access offered merchants a way to capture value previously mediated through British channels.

The revolution against empire did not become a revolution against global consumption.

That sentence matters because Americans have often paired political independence with fantasies of economic self-sufficiency. The two are not the same. A sovereign country can be highly dependent on international trade. A nation can resent foreign control while enthusiastically buying foreign goods. It can seek domestic production and still rely on imported materials, technology, capital, or markets.

The *Empress of China* made that tension visible almost immediately.

The voyage was an assertion of independence precisely because Americans wanted deeper connection.

They wanted to trade without Britain standing between them and Canton.

This is one of the recurring paradoxes of American maritime history. Sea power is often used to make interdependence possible on preferred terms. Ships protect commerce because commerce crosses political boundaries. Navies exist partly because markets extend farther than law. Merchant fleets tie domestic prosperity to foreign stability. Ports thrive on flows that can become strategic vulnerabilities the moment a war, embargo, epidemic, strike, grounding, or blockade interrupts them.

The more connected the country becomes, the more consequences can arrive from far away.

That is true in 1784 and in 2024.

The scale changes almost beyond recognition. The *Empress of China* carried a cargo that could be inventoried in a letter. A modern container ship can carry thousands of standardized boxes filled with goods whose ownership, insurance, financing, production histories, and destinations span many firms and countries. Yet the underlying question is recognizable: what happens to a nation when its prosperity depends on routes beyond its sovereign control?

The early answer was improvisation.

American merchants hunted for commodities Chinese buyers wanted. Ginseng was particularly ingenious because it converted the ecology of North America into purchasing power in Asia. The plant's role also reminds us that trade routes reach inland long before ships do. A Canton voyage affected people gathering, buying, transporting, financing, and reselling an American botanical product far from the pier.

A ship's economic wake begins before departure.

Cargo has to come from somewhere. Money has to be raised. Risks are priced. Provisions are purchased. Labor is hired. Documents are prepared. News about prices and demand moves through merchant networks. On return, imports move through wholesalers, shops, homes, fashions, and credit relationships.

The ocean voyage sits in the middle of a much longer chain.

This is why I resist describing the *Empress of China* merely as the first American ship to reach China. Firsts are seductive because they create clean trivia. A first can matter enormously, but the historical question is what the first changes.

This one created proof.

It demonstrated that direct American trade with China was possible. The route could be financed. Chinese merchants would transact with Americans. American goods and specie could be exchanged for Chinese products. The return could justify repetition. Information gathered on one voyage could reduce uncertainty on the next.

Commercial networks compound through learning.

The first trader pays for ignorance. Later traders inherit some of what the first trader discovered: routes, contacts, prices, customs procedures, seasonal knowledge, language assistance, financing arrangements, and expectations. That accumulated knowledge lowers the practical distance between markets even when the physical distance never changes.

The country therefore became larger in an economic sense before it became larger on the map.

This is not necessarily a celebratory expansion. Commerce distributes gains unevenly. Early American overseas trade was entangled with a world economy shaped by slavery, empire, coercion, extractive labor, and unequal legal regimes. American merchants participated in morally compromised systems as readily as they pursued legitimate opportunity. The China trade itself would later become entangled with opium through foreign merchants and with a wider Western campaign to force access on Chinese terms unacceptable to the Qing government.

The first voyage should not be asked to carry every later sin, but neither should it be turned into an innocent fable of adventurous capitalism.

It is better understood as an opening.

Openings create possibilities. They do not determine how later generations use them.

In the 1780s the United States needed foreign commerce partly because the war had left an economy disrupted by debt and changing trade patterns. Merchants searched for markets with the opportunism of people who understood that political victory did not pay invoices by itself. The new nation required revenue, credit, exports, imports, and commercial relationships. It had sovereignty to defend and bills to settle.

The *Empress of China* belonged to that practical founding.

Founding stories tend to privilege rooms: Independence Hall, legislatures, convention chambers, houses where famous men wrote famous words. Commerce asks us to look at warehouses and wharves too. A republic that cannot move goods, borrow, insure risk, collect customs, and maintain credit will discover that political theory has material limits.

Ports were where those limits became visible.

New York's later emergence as the country's dominant commercial city was not complete in 1784, but the departure of the *Empress of China* hints at the city's future. The harbor connected inland production, Atlantic finance, and overseas demand. Over the next century infrastructure would intensify that advantage. The Erie Canal would link the harbor to the interior. Railroads would widen the network. Steam would change time. Telegraphy would change information. Immigration would change the labor force and city. Each system would make the port more than a shoreline.

A port is an interface between different kinds of movement.

The first China voyage depended on that interface before Americans had a word like logistics to make the complexity sound technical.

It depended on investors willing to tie up capital for a long voyage with uncertain returns. It depended on a crew capable of crossing multiple oceans. It depended on navigational knowledge, provisions, maintenance, foreign port arrangements, and a cargo mix whose value could change by the time the ship arrived. It depended on the vessel surviving weather, accident, disease, and geopolitical risk.

Long-distance trade was an exercise in delayed information.

There was no live price feed from Canton. Investors could not monitor the ship's position from a phone. Decisions made in New York could be obsolete by arrival, and decisions made in China might not be understood at home for months. Merchants compensated with experience, correspondence, agents, relationships, diversification, and margins large enough to justify the uncertainty.

The slower information moved, the more authority had to be delegated to people aboard and abroad.

That is another link between ships and institutions. Distance creates agency problems. The owners cannot command every transaction. The government cannot supervise every consul. The captain cannot consult investors before every navigational choice. Long voyages require trust in people empowered to decide beyond immediate oversight.

Modern technology changes the speed of communication without abolishing the problem. A container ship's owner can know far more about a vessel in real time than an eighteenth-century merchant could. Yet organizations still depend on delegated judgment at sea, in ports, in control rooms, and across supply chains. More information can narrow distance; it does not eliminate responsibility.

The *Empress of China* lived near the opposite extreme.

Once she left New York, the voyage acquired a degree of autonomy that is hard to recover from a world of constant connection. The men aboard carried the enterprise in both senses. They carried the goods, and they carried the authority to make the voyage work.

When the ship returned in 1785, that authority returned with knowledge.

The voyage produced more than profit. It produced precedent. Shaw's later consular role linked commercial experience to national representation. Other merchants entered the trade. Americans learned the Canton system. Chinese goods became part of the material culture of the young republic. The Pacific world entered American calculations before American settlers reached the Pacific in significant numbers.

This is the wider consequence that makes the ship more important to my purpose than a vessel tied more directly to the framing of the Constitution.

The 1780s were the decade in which Americans discovered that political independence did not reduce the importance of the world beyond their borders.

It increased their responsibility for navigating it.

Under Britain, Americans could complain that imperial policy constrained trade. After independence, there was no parent power to blame for the absence of access, protection, treaties, or strategy. The freedom to choose routes created the burden of sustaining them.

That burden would help build the federal state.

Customs duties became a critical source of early federal revenue. Maritime commerce mattered to arguments over naval power. Threats to shipping pushed the country toward diplomatic and military responses. Merchants became both beneficiaries and petitioners of national authority. The coast and the wider Atlantic were not peripheral to the new constitutional order; they were among the reasons a stronger order mattered.

The next ship makes that institutional turn visible in oak and copper.

USS *Constitution* was not a merchant experiment privately financed to discover a market. She was a federal answer to a recurring political problem. Congress authorized a class of frigates because the country had learned that commerce without protection exposed sovereignty to humiliation.

The transition from the *Empress of China* to *Constitution* is therefore not a turn away from trade toward war.

It is trade generating a demand for power.

The merchant ship goes first.

The warship follows.

That sequence will repeat in different forms for the rest of American history.
