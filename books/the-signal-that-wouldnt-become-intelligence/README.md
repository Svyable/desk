# The Signal That Wouldn't Become Intelligence

## Why the Pentagon kept testing remote viewing after the joke should have been over

**Sven Hardy Benson**

### Thesis

The most interesting fact about the American remote-viewing programs is not that intelligence agencies once investigated psychic perception. It is that the program survived repeated encounters with two apparently incompatible results: laboratory and operational records contained episodes that investigators could not comfortably dismiss as chance, while the same phenomenon repeatedly failed the harder requirement of becoming dependable intelligence. This book investigates that gap. A weak or unstable anomaly can be scientifically interesting and operationally useless at the same time.

The book does not assume extrasensory perception exists. It does not assume every reported success was anomalous. It asks a narrower historical and epistemological question: **what exactly did the government observe that kept the question alive, and why could it not turn whatever it observed into a reliable instrument?**

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified / declassified / documented.** Program records, session reports, contracts, evaluations, official histories, congressional material, and peer-reviewed work whose provenance can be checked.
2. **Credible but incomplete.** Participant recollections, investigator accounts, operational anecdotes, and later reporting where documentary corroboration is partial.
3. **Disputed.** Famous targets, spectacular hits, claims of unique intelligence value, Soviet capabilities, and retrospective success stories lacking adequate prospective records or independent confirmation.
4. **Speculative synthesis.** The book's proposed distinction between *anomalous signal* and *usable intelligence*, plus possible explanations involving protocol leakage, selection effects, human scoring, weak statistical effects, cognition, or unknown mechanisms.

No testimony is promoted to proof. Declassification proves that a document or program existed, not that its underlying claim was true.
