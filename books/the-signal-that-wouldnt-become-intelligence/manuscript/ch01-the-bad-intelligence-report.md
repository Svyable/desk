# 1 — The Bad Intelligence Report

The strangest sentence in the remote-viewing archive is not a psychic sentence.

It is a bureaucratic one.

A session report from March 1980 explains that the viewer's impressions were being supplied as **raw intelligence data** and had not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation, or collation. Interpretation belonged to the requester.

That is a wonderfully dangerous phrase: raw intelligence data.

Not because the viewer was necessarily psychic. We are going to spend a great deal of time refusing that shortcut. It is dangerous because bureaucracies have verbs that quietly promote things. A person sits in a room and reports impressions. Someone types them. A cover sheet appears. Classification markings appear. A file number appears. The packet moves through channels used by things the institution already knows how to call intelligence. Nothing supernatural has happened. Something institutional has.

The impression has acquired a career.

The particular session concerned the American hostages in Tehran. The surviving declassified record says exactly what the exercise was for and, with unusual helpfulness, what it was not. The impressions had not yet been evaluated. They were not findings. They were material offered to a customer who would have to decide whether any of it deserved use.

This distinction is the whole book.

Remote viewing is usually sold in two incompatible genres. In the believer's version, the United States government secretly proved that the mind can reach across space and then, for reasons involving cowardice, conspiracy, materialism, or all three, buried the result. In the skeptic's version, credulous officials burned money on nonsense until grown-ups finally arrived in 1995 and shut the circus down.

Neither story adequately explains the paperwork.

The government did fund the work. It did so under changing organizational homes and names. Operational sessions were conducted for real intelligence questions, including the Iran hostage crisis. Some records describe poor or absent target correlation. Some advocates and later evaluators argued that controlled experiments produced results inconsistent with chance. And when the program received its final independent review in 1995, the dispute did not reduce cleanly to `effect / no effect`. Jessica Utts argued that the statistical evidence for anomalous cognition was compelling. Ray Hyman disputed what could legitimately be concluded and emphasized methodological and replication problems. The operational judgment was harsher than the laboratory argument: whatever was happening had not become sufficiently reliable, specific, and timely to justify intelligence use.

That combination is more interesting than victory for either camp.

Imagine a thermometer that is right more often than chance but wrong whenever you need to know whether the reactor is overheating.

You have two problems, not one.

The scientist wants to know why the instrument departs from chance. The operator wants another thermometer.

Intelligence agencies are not universities with stranger stationery. Their threshold is not merely whether an effect survives a statistical test. Intelligence must alter decisions under uncertainty. It has to arrive in time. It has to distinguish among live possibilities. It has to survive adversarial conditions. Ideally it tells the customer something not already available from ordinary collection. It should not require a sympathetic judge to discover the correct interpretation after the target is known.

This is where remote viewing becomes an unusually clean epistemological specimen.

A phenomenon can be real enough to deserve investigation and useless enough to deserve cancellation.

That sentence will irritate almost everybody who has made remote viewing part of an identity. Good.

## The archive contains its own hecklers

One reason to distrust popular histories of psychic spying is that they are too tidy. The declassified files are not.

A 1978 training-session report records that the viewer showed "absolutely no target correlation." Another 1982 report says a viewer had "very little correlation." These are not skeptical reconstructions written twenty years later. They are program paperwork.

This matters.

Any honest reconstruction has to preserve the misses with the hits. Otherwise we are not studying anomalous cognition. We are studying editing.

The temptation to edit is enormous because remote-viewing sessions produce narrative material. Narrative material is elastic. A viewer reports water, metal, vertical structures, motion, a feeling of confinement. Once the target is revealed, human beings are magnificent correspondence engines. We can find the dock, the antenna, the submarine, the gantry, the silo. The more descriptive material generated, the larger the surface available for retrospective matching.

That does not prove every match is spurious. It explains why the scoring problem is central rather than clerical.

A useful protocol therefore has to do violence to storytelling. Targets must be selected in ways that prevent cueing. Judges should be blind. Decoys matter. Criteria should be fixed before revelation. Repeated trials matter more than the session everybody remembers at dinner. The person who knows the answer cannot be allowed to become the measuring instrument without our noticing.

This sounds obvious now because experimental psychology spent a century learning how easily expectation leaks through a room.

It was less obvious inside an intelligence problem where the customer did not care whether the epistemology was elegant. The hostage is either in the building or not. The aircraft is either under the camouflage or not. The submarine is either in the pen or not. A commander wants coordinates, not a philosophy seminar about correspondence.

The archive repeatedly exposes this mismatch between the shape of the phenomenon being claimed and the shape of the answer the customer needs.

## Why keep going?

Here is the question skeptics often answer too quickly.

If remote viewing was unreliable, why did parts of the American national-security apparatus keep returning to it?

Institutional stupidity is available as an explanation. It is also cheap.

The better answer probably has several layers.

First, the Cold War made small probabilities expensive to ignore. American analysts believed the Soviet Union was investigating parapsychology and related human-performance claims. Whether Soviet claims were good science is a separate question. Intelligence organizations routinely investigate things they doubt because an adversary may believe them, fund them, weaponize them, misunderstand them, or discover something first. Studying Soviet psychotronics does not prove psychotronics. It proves that Soviet interest itself could become an intelligence requirement.

Second, the cost asymmetry was seductive. If a person in a quiet room could acquire even occasionally useful information about a denied target, the collection method would be absurdly cheap compared with satellites, aircraft, submarines, agents, or technical penetrations. An intelligence service does not have to be mystical to become interested in a claimed sensor with no obvious range limit.

Third, there appear to have been results sufficiently odd to keep technically literate people arguing. That claim has to be handled carefully. It does not mean the government certified psychic functioning. It means the historical record contains a persistent dispute that survived more contact with controlled experimentation than the cartoon version suggests.

Fourth, bureaucracies have momentum. Sponsors find sponsors. Programs change names. A handful of striking anecdotes can outweigh a warehouse of mediocrity, especially when success criteria are not disciplined. Secrecy makes external correction harder. Classification can preserve weak ideas from ridicule just as effectively as it protects strong ideas from espionage.

All four can be true at once.

The point of this book is to find out how much explanatory weight each deserves.

## The 1995 problem

The end of the program is often presented as a verdict on whether psychic perception exists.

It was not that clean.

The CIA's later public account says that when the program returned from DIA in the mid-1990s, the agency commissioned an independent evaluation. CIA summarizes the resulting position in an awkward but revealing way: enough accurate remote-viewing experiences existed to resist easy dismissal as randomness, but the phenomenon was too inconsistent and sporadic for intelligence use. The agency did not restore the program.

One should be cautious even with an agency's retrospective summary of its own history. But the structure of the conclusion matters.

There are two gates.

**Gate one:** Is there evidence of an effect that cannot adequately be explained by ordinary chance under the tested conditions?

**Gate two:** Can that effect produce dependable information for a customer who does not know the answer?

Remote viewing could conceivably pass the first and fail the second.

That possibility is strangely underexplored because culture prefers acquittal or conviction. Paranormal claims are expected either to collapse completely or to rewrite physics. But nature is under no obligation to provide phenomena at useful signal strength. Biology is full of effects that are real and noisy. Medicine is full of interventions whose average effects do not let you predict an individual outcome. Human perception itself is an unreliable instrument that nevertheless plainly exists.

The analogy does not establish remote viewing. It only removes a bad inference: operational uselessness does not logically prove experimental nonexistence.

The reverse inference is equally bad. A statistically unusual laboratory result does not give you a spy satellite made of consciousness.

Between those errors sits the entire historical program.

## Tehran

The hostage-crisis sessions make the problem painfully concrete.

In 1980, Americans were being held inside a diplomatic compound whose internal human geography mattered. Where were particular people? What surrounded them? Had they been moved? These are intelligence questions with answers. A remote viewer could produce descriptions. The session report could preserve them. But the existence of a description does nothing by itself.

The customer needs discrimination.

Suppose a viewer describes a low building, trees, a wall, two guards, an interior room and a nearby vehicle. In a large compound, how many configurations could partially satisfy that description? If some details correspond and others fail, what weighting is legitimate? If the viewer gives ten propositions and three are striking, do we score the session by three, ten, or by the operational decision the report actually enabled? If analysts already know much of the compound layout, how do we prevent ordinary knowledge from making vague material look prophetic?

These are not skeptic tricks. They are what turns an impression into an instrument.

The session cover sheets themselves sometimes warn that the material is raw and unevaluated. The bureaucracy knew the conversion problem existed.

What it never solved was the conversion rate.

That is the phrase I want to keep in view: **conversion rate**.

How much strange information becomes correct information? How much correct information becomes specific information? How much specific information becomes novel information? How much novel information arrives before verification? How much verified information changes a decision?

Every step is lossy.

A paranormal literature can stop at the first unusual correlation. An intelligence service has to survive the whole chain.

## The Soviet mirror

There is another distortion in the story, and it came from looking east.

Cold War intelligence was full of mirrors. The Soviets investigated an American program because Americans investigated a Soviet program because the Soviets appeared to be investigating an American possibility. Strategic competition can manufacture seriousness by reflection.

This is not unique to parapsychology. Missile gaps, bomber gaps, exotic weapons, civil defense, antisubmarine systems and space capabilities all lived inside imperfect estimates of what the other side might be able to do. Sometimes the fear was justified. Sometimes it accelerated genuine technology. Sometimes both sides spent money responding to distorted pictures of each other.

Psi research is therefore not only a story about whether anomalous cognition exists. It is a story about **uncertainty as a strategic resource**.

If your adversary might possess a capability with enormous upside and almost no conventional signature, how much evidence do you require before spending a small amount to investigate it?

Very little.

That decision can be rational even when the phenomenon turns out to be false.

This is why the mere existence of a classified program proves so much less than enthusiasts think. Intelligence agencies investigate possibilities. Classification tells us about sensitivity, sources, bureaucratic ownership and adversarial interest. It is not a sacrament that converts the classified proposition into truth.

But the reverse sneer is lazy too. "The government studied it" is not evidence that it worked. "The government studied something silly" is not evidence that everyone involved was silly.

Sometimes investigation is what uncertainty looks like when it receives a budget code.

## Four boxes

From here forward I am going to be irritatingly explicit about evidence.

A declassified session report proving that a remote-viewing session occurred goes in the first box: documented fact.

A participant's later account of a spectacular operational success, when the contemporaneous tasking and scoring record is incomplete, goes in the second: credible but incomplete testimony, assuming the witness and provenance justify even that.

A famous story repeated without a clean documentary chain goes in the third: disputed allegation.

The claim that the government may have encountered a weak anomalous signal that could not be engineered into dependable collection belongs in the fourth: synthesis. It is the book's working hypothesis, not a finding smuggled in through typography.

The boxes will move when evidence moves.

That is important because the history of this subject is full of claims frozen at the most flattering stage of their lives.

We will not do that.

## The instrument that never arrived

There is a brutal way to judge any claimed intelligence technology.

Ask what replaced it.

Successful collection systems create descendants. Better optics lead to better optics. Signals intelligence becomes a sprawling technical ecology. Radar changes aircraft and then changes because aircraft changed. A useful sensor attracts engineering. Engineering attacks noise, calibration, bandwidth, latency, operator error and failure modes. The instrument becomes boring.

Remote viewing never became boring.

That may be the most damaging fact in the story.

After years of experiments, operational tasking, protocol arguments and institutional migration, there was no remote-viewing equivalent of a camera specification. No dependable error bar a commander could use. No mature calibration procedure that made Viewer A interchangeable with Viewer B. No production pipeline where the paranormal input became routine enough to disappear into the intelligence product.

The personalities remained important because the instrument had not detached from the personality.

This does not tell us what happened in the laboratory.

It tells us what did not happen in engineering.

And that is where we begin.
