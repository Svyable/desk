# STARCOM and the Making of a Space Warfighter

Military services reveal what they fear through what they train.

A checklist tells you which mistakes have happened before.

An exercise tells you which mistakes leaders believe might happen next.

A school tells you what the institution thinks cannot be left to experience alone.

Space Training and Readiness Command exists because the Space Force learned early that technical competence did not automatically produce a space warfighter.

The distinction is easy to miss from outside the service. Space Force operates some of the most technically sophisticated systems in government. Many Guardians enter with backgrounds in engineering, intelligence, cyber or space operations. The service is small. It would be tempting to imagine that careful recruiting plus system-specific instruction could produce the force required.

STARCOM is an institutional rejection of that idea.

Its mission is broader: education, training, doctrine, test, exercises and the deliberate development of people who can operate in a contested environment. The command is responsible for preparing Guardians to do more than keep systems functioning.

They must understand what those systems are for when somebody is trying to interfere with them.

This represents a historical change in the meaning of military space professionalism.

For much of the space age, American military satellites often occupied a strange psychological category. They were military assets, but their operations could feel more like infrastructure management than combat. Crews maintained constellations, monitored health, delivered communications, missile warning, navigation and other effects. The satellites enabled war elsewhere.

The United States Space Force was created partly because that separation no longer described the strategic environment.

Space systems could be jammed, dazzled, cyberattacked, targeted or otherwise denied. Adversaries were developing counterspace capabilities. Commercial and military architectures overlapped. A Guardian could not assume the domain above the atmosphere remained a sanctuary merely because no familiar front line crossed it.

A service designed around this reality needed a training command designed around conflict.

STARCOM became that command.

Its evolution is important to the Space Academy because it shows how much of the Academy's apparent mission already belongs elsewhere.

A service academy educates and forms future officers.

STARCOM prepares actual Guardians for actual service.

Those functions overlap, but they are not substitutes.

The clearest example is the Officer Training Course.

The Space Force's new officer-development model begins from a problem created by specialization. Officers can enter space operations, intelligence, cyber, engineering and acquisition-related careers. Under older models, a young officer might become technically competent inside a career field before developing much understanding of how neighboring disciplines fit the mission.

The Space Force decided that was too late.

Officer Training Course gives newly commissioned Guardians a common foundation across multiple operational disciplines. Space operations, cyber, intelligence and acquisition appear together. Students encounter scenarios and exercises intended to force integration.

By 2026, service leaders described the goal in language that sounded almost like an educational rebellion against specialization: Guardian first, specialist second.

This does not mean specialist second in importance.

It means second in sequence.

The officer should understand the service before disappearing into the specialty.

That sequencing has direct implications for the Space Academy.

If the Academy attempts to graduate fully formed Space Force officers, it will fail because no undergraduate institution can reproduce what an operational service learns from current threats, classified systems, exercises and units.

The Academy should graduate people prepared to become Guardians.

STARCOM should still do the becoming.

This division protects both institutions.

The Academy can focus on durable knowledge and broad professional formation. STARCOM can change faster. If an adversary reveals a new tactic, a training course can adapt in months. If a satellite architecture changes, system-specific instruction can change with it. If doctrine evolves, the command can update exercises.

A four-year academic curriculum cannot and should not chase every operational revision.

Trying would destroy its purpose.

The Academy's job is to make students capable of absorbing change.

STARCOM's job is to make the change operational.

This is why the Academy's relationship with STARCOM may matter more than the question of who owns the campus.

A NASA-led school could drift toward civil exploration and technical education if military training is treated as a guest component.

A military-dominated school could drift toward current operations at the expense of long-horizon science and engineering.

STARCOM gives the institution a natural boundary: undergraduate education can stay broad because the service possesses a command responsible for turning graduates into warfighters.

That command itself is evolving.

In 2026, STARCOM graduated the first class of a new Space Component Staff Course designed for Guardians serving in joint command environments. The course focused on operational planning, command and control and the integration of space capabilities into wider campaigns.

The need sounds bureaucratic until one understands the problem.

Space Force does not fight alone.

Its effects support and interact with forces in air, land, sea and cyber. Combatant commanders need space integrated into planning rather than treated as a technical annex. Guardians assigned to joint staffs must understand how other services think, what commanders need and how space capabilities fit decisions happening across domains.

Before the new course, that preparation existed in multiple venues but lacked a single mandatory standardized approach for the roles STARCOM was trying to fill.

So the service built one.

This is what professionalization looks like in real time.

A gap becomes visible.

The organization names it.

A course appears.

The first cohort tests it.

Instructors revise it.

The cycle continues.

There is no marble.

No national site competition.

No presidential signing ceremony.

Yet these quiet institutions may shape Space Force performance more directly than a new academy will for years.

The Space Academy story risks overlooking them because academies are easier to narrate.

A campus has architecture.

A first class has faces.

A graduation has ceremony.

Training commands live in the less glamorous world where doctrine is turned into schedules and exercises, where somebody must decide how long a course should be, what scenario is realistic, how to evaluate performance and whether students actually learned anything.

That world is where the Academy will eventually be judged.

If Space Academy graduates arrive at STARCOM better prepared to integrate disciplines, the school adds value.

If they require the same remediation as everybody else while carrying stronger expectations about their own preparation, the Academy has added prestige without readiness.

This suggests a practical test for curriculum designers.

Ask STARCOM what it wishes new officers understood before commissioning.

Not which tasks they should already perform.

Which concepts they should not be encountering for the first time.

Perhaps systems engineering.

Perhaps orbital mechanics.

Perhaps cyber fundamentals.

Perhaps military history and the logic of joint operations.

Perhaps acquisition and industrial capacity.

Perhaps the law and policy governing civil, commercial and military space.

Perhaps a habit of learning across disciplines.

Those are academy problems.

The technical procedures of a particular weapon system are not.

There is a second relationship the Academy must understand: enlisted education.

The public imagination of service academies is officer-heavy because federal academies commission officers. Space Force, like every military service, depends on enlisted Guardians whose technical expertise can exceed that of the officers leading them in particular systems and specialties.

A new Space Academy could accidentally intensify an old military failure: treating officers as the intellectual class and enlisted personnel as operators who merely execute.

Space Force's own development documents resist that model. Its enlisted framework emphasizes progression from tactical expertise toward broader operational and strategic responsibility. The service expects enlisted Guardians to exercise initiative, critical thinking, problem solving and leadership.

The Academy should teach future officers to respect that expertise before they receive rank over it.

This is professional education in a form that does not appear on a degree plan.

A lieutenant does not need to be the smartest person in the room.

The lieutenant needs to know what the room knows.

Technical services make this lesson urgent. An enlisted Guardian may understand a system's operational behavior more deeply than a newly commissioned engineering graduate. A civilian may possess years of program continuity. A contractor may understand a component in ways no government employee does. The officer's responsibility is not to win an expertise contest.

It is to integrate expertise into a decision for which the officer may still be accountable.

STARCOM trains that reality because operational environments expose hierarchy to friction.

The Academy should prepare students for it by designing teams in which authority and expertise are not always aligned.

The challenge extends beyond people.

STARCOM is also responsible for testing, wargaming and doctrine—activities that connect learning to force design.

A test asks whether a capability works.

A wargame asks how people may use or defeat it.

Doctrine asks what the service currently believes about employing power.

These functions make STARCOM a learning system rather than merely a schoolhouse.

The Space Academy should learn from that model.

Universities often separate research from instruction. A federal technical academy tied to space has an opportunity to connect undergraduate education with real institutional learning. Students could participate in unclassified research derived from operational problems, test assumptions through simulation and encounter doctrine as something provisional rather than scripture.

The word provisional is important.

Military organizations need doctrine precisely because people cannot improvise every principle in crisis.

They also need officers capable of changing doctrine when evidence proves it wrong.

An academy that teaches doctrine as memorized truth creates obedient historians of the last conflict.

An academy that treats doctrine as disposable opinion creates people unable to act collectively.

The intellectual skill lies between them: understand the institution's current method deeply enough to use it, and understand the evidence deeply enough to revise it.

STARCOM lives inside that tension every time a course or exercise changes.

The Space Academy could spend four years teaching students to become comfortable there.

This may be more valuable than any particular major.

Space Force is a service operating inside rapid technological change. Commercial launch, proliferated satellites, artificial intelligence, cyber tools and new sensors can alter the environment faster than military acquisition and education traditionally adapt. A Guardian who graduates believing expertise is a fixed possession will become obsolete.

The service needs people who treat expertise as a practice.

That is why training commands matter.

They institutionalize the idea that development continues after the diploma.

The new Academy should make that humility part of its culture from the first day.

Graduation should not mean a student has become a space professional.

It should mean the student has earned entry into professions that will continue teaching them under higher stakes.

This distinction can also protect the Academy from one of the most predictable political pressures it will face.

When lawmakers spend heavily on a new federal school, they will want evidence that graduates are immediately useful. Administrators may respond by making the curriculum more vocational, adding system-specific material and measuring success by short-term job readiness.

That would be a mistake if it comes at the expense of durability.

STARCOM already exists to create immediate operational usefulness.

The Academy should produce something slower and harder to measure: officers and civil servants who learn faster, connect disciplines earlier and exercise better judgment over long careers.

The two institutions need one another precisely because their clocks are different.

STARCOM works on the clock of readiness.

The Academy will work on the clock of formation.

A warfighting service cannot sacrifice the first.

A serious profession cannot survive without the second.

The Space Academy will attract more attention because a new campus is visible.

But the future of Space Force education may depend on whether the campus understands the institution already doing the quiet work.

The service does not need an academy to teach STARCOM how to train Guardians.

It needs an academy that sends STARCOM people worth training.
