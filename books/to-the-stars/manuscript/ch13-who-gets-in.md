# Who Gets In

An academy begins selecting its future long before the first student enters a classroom.

Admissions is not administrative plumbing. It is institutional design.

Decide who may apply, who may nominate, who must pass a medical examination, who must qualify for a security clearance, who must meet a physical standard, who receives preparatory support and who owes service afterward, and the shape of the school appears before the curriculum does.

The August 2026 executive order recognizes this. It instructs the Space Academy commission to recommend applicant prerequisites, including citizenship status, security clearances, government employment or military status. Those examples are revealing because they are not the usual language of college admissions. They are the language of authority and obligation.

As of September 2026, none of those requirements has been fixed.

There is no application portal.

There is no congressional nomination deadline.

There is no published fitness test.

There is no minimum security-clearance level.

There is no class size.

There is not yet even a final answer to whether every student will hold military status.

That uncertainty creates an opportunity the commission should use carefully. The easiest admissions system would be to copy one of the existing military academies. It would also be the quickest way to decide, before the debate has really begun, that the Space Academy is fundamentally a military service academy.

The Air Force Academy shows what that model looks like.

A prospective cadet does not simply apply as a student. The applicant normally needs a nomination from a member of Congress, a senator or another authorized nominating source. The candidate must satisfy academic requirements, medical standards and physical expectations. Admission becomes an appointment. The student enters a cadet wing, receives a taxpayer-funded education and, upon graduation, commissions into the Air Force or Space Force with a service commitment.

The structure is coherent because the destination is coherent.

The Space Academy proposal is not.

If one student is headed toward a Space Force commission and another toward civilian service at NASA, why should both need a congressional nomination?

If a future scientist will never hold military status, why should a military fitness test determine eligibility?

If the Academy intends to educate people who will work in classified national-security roles, how much security screening should occur before admission rather than before assignment?

If the institution wants entrepreneurs and commercial-space literacy in its community, does that mean admitting students whose immediate service obligation still runs through government, or admitting people who may move directly into private industry?

Each answer creates a different school.

There is a federal academy that offers a useful precedent precisely because its mission does not fit neatly inside one armed service.

The United States Merchant Marine Academy at Kings Point is a federal academy organized around the maritime profession rather than a single branch. Its graduates earn degrees and merchant-marine credentials, accept reserve obligations and may fulfill their service through approved maritime employment or through active duty in an armed service or certain uniformed services. Its admissions process also uses congressional nominations, but the post-graduation pathways are more varied than at West Point or the Air Force Academy.

Kings Point is not a template to be copied mechanically. Maritime law, licensing, reserve obligations and merchant shipping create a structure specific to that profession. But it demonstrates that a federal academy can bind publicly funded education to a domain-wide service obligation rather than a single branch destination.

The Space Academy may need a similarly hybrid logic.

The first question is citizenship.

Most federal national-security roles and many military pathways require U.S. citizenship. Security clearances create additional constraints. A NASA-led academy could also have scientific and diplomatic reasons to admit international students eventually, as existing service academies do under limited programs. The commission will have to separate the eligibility needed for the common academic institution from eligibility needed for particular service tracks.

A simple design would restrict the initial academy to U.S. citizens and consider international participation later. That would reduce complexity while the school establishes itself. But a school intended to strengthen American leadership in a domain defined by alliances and international programs will eventually confront the value of educating foreign partners alongside American students.

The second question is nomination.

Congressional nominations serve several purposes in existing service academies. They distribute appointment opportunities geographically, connect the academies to elected representation and make admission part of a public rather than purely bureaucratic process. The Air Force Academy says the nomination system helps create geographic diversity across the cadet population.

The system also adds complexity and political friction. Students must navigate two admissions processes: the academy's requirements and the nominating authority's. Different congressional offices use different methods and deadlines. Families with experience around service academies often understand the process better than equally talented students encountering it for the first time.

A new Space Academy should not inherit nomination requirements merely because old academies use them.

If Congress eventually creates the school in statute, lawmakers may reasonably want geographic representation. A nomination structure could also deepen congressional investment in the institution. But the Academy's broader civilian mission may make a conventional nomination system a poor fit.

There are alternatives.

Congress could authorize a national merit pool with geographic-balancing rules.

It could create nominations for military-track students and a separate appointment process for civilian-track students.

It could allow agency heads, governors, tribal governments, territorial officials or other public institutions to nominate candidates.

It could reserve a portion of appointments for prior service, community college transfers or preparatory programs.

It could adopt a model in which nominations are one pathway rather than a universal gate.

The commission's job is not to make the system novel for novelty's sake. It is to make the selection mechanism fit the public purpose.

The third question is academic preparation.

A Space Academy built around technical rigor will be tempted to recruit students who already look like young engineers: advanced mathematics, physics, coding, robotics, research competitions and top standardized scores.

That population matters.

It is not sufficient.

The school is supposed to produce leaders for systems where technical decisions become institutional decisions. It will need writers, analysts, future lawyers, strategists, economists and people capable of leading technical organizations without pretending to be the deepest specialist in every room.

Even its engineers should not be selected only for precocious technical polish.

A teenager with access to an expensive robotics program may appear more “space ready” than a student from a rural school who had fewer opportunities but demonstrates unusual mathematical ability, curiosity and persistence. If the Academy confuses prior access with potential, it will recruit the children already closest to the space economy and congratulate itself for finding talent.

Federal academies have preparatory institutions and programs partly because raw potential does not arrive evenly packaged.

The Air Force Academy Preparatory School is one example. It gives selected candidates additional academic, athletic, character and military preparation before they compete again for an Academy appointment. The Class of 2026 included graduates who came through that path as well as prior-enlisted Airmen.

A Space Academy would need an even more deliberate access strategy if it claims to recruit nationally for a technical future.

A preparatory school is one option.

Partnerships with community colleges are another.

Summer technical programs at NASA centers, Space Force installations and universities could identify students before the normal admissions cycle.

Scholarship pipelines could support mathematics and computing preparation.

Regional partnerships could reach applicants far from the existing aerospace clusters in Colorado, Texas, Florida and California.

The country should not confuse widening the pool with lowering the standard.

The academy's standard should be difficult.

The question is whether the country wants to discover who can meet it or merely admit people who already had the chance to prove they could.

The fourth question is physical qualification.

For future Guardians, military medical and fitness standards are part of the profession. A service academy cannot promise a commission to somebody who is unable to meet the legal and operational requirements for that commission.

For civilian students, the case is different.

NASA employs people with a wide range of physical abilities. A federal engineer, scientist, lawyer or program manager does not need to meet the physical standards of a military officer. Designing one fitness gate for all students would narrow the civilian talent pool for no mission-related reason.

This problem pushes the Academy toward multiple service tracks from the beginning.

Students may share a common academic institution while holding different status and obligations.

Military-track students could meet service-specific medical and physical standards.

Civilian-track students could meet standards relevant to their roles, including reasonable requirements for field experiences where necessary, without pretending every graduate will become an astronaut or Guardian.

The distinction would make campus culture more complicated.

It would also make it more truthful.

The fifth question is security clearance.

The executive order explicitly names clearances among possible applicant prerequisites, an unusual feature for undergraduate admission.

Requiring every applicant to qualify for a high-level clearance before entry could create severe practical problems. Security investigations take time. Young applicants may have lived abroad, hold dual citizenship or have family connections that require additional review without making them untrustworthy. A school emphasizing civilian science and open research has little reason to make classified access universal on day one.

A more flexible design would distinguish eligibility for federal service from eligibility for particular classified work. Students destined for national-security tracks could undergo investigations at the appropriate stage. The common curriculum could remain overwhelmingly unclassified, preserving academic openness and reducing the temptation to make secrecy a status symbol.

Security culture still belongs in the education.

Every student should understand why information is classified, how controlled information changes collaboration, how to handle sensitive data and why overclassification can damage institutions as surely as careless disclosure can.

The point is to teach responsibility, not to make a clearance the Academy's version of an exclusive club card.

The sixth question is prior service.

Space Force is young enough that enlisted Guardians and personnel transferring from other services carry much of its operational memory. A new academy designed only for seventeen-year-olds would exclude people who already understand the profession and want to become officers or deepen their technical education.

Existing service academies admit prior-enlisted applicants under defined rules. The Space Academy should consider whether prior military service, federal civilian service or technical experience deserves a larger role than usual.

A twenty-four-year-old Guardian who has spent years on an operational crew may contribute more to a classroom discussion of system failure than a student with perfect test scores.

A NASA technician may understand hardware culture from the floor upward.

A community-college student who has worked in software may bring practical judgment unavailable from a conventional high-school pipeline.

The Academy can become selective without becoming narrow.

This brings admissions to the hardest word in elite education: merit.

Merit is always merit for something.

A conservatory selects for musical potential.

A research university may select for academic performance and intellectual promise.

A service academy is selecting people for education and public authority.

The Space Academy must decide what qualities predict success in a profession that does not fully exist yet.

Grades matter.

Mathematical preparation matters.

Technical curiosity matters.

Leadership matters.

Character matters.

The ability to work with people who know more than you matters.

So does the ability to tell a superior that an assumption is wrong.

So does the discipline to follow a procedure when improvisation would be reckless.

So does the judgment to abandon a procedure when reality has changed.

Admissions systems are much better at measuring the first group than the second.

That limitation should make the Academy humble about precision.

Interviews, essays, leadership records and recommendations can add context, but each creates opportunities for performance and bias. Standardized testing can reveal preparation, but not destiny. Physical tests reveal particular abilities, but not moral courage. Security screening can identify risks, but not whether a person will use authority wisely twenty years later.

No admissions office can solve human character.

The best it can do is build a cohort with enough demonstrated ability, enough variety of experience and enough room for growth that the institution has something worth forming.

Cohort design matters especially for this Academy because the students are supposed to learn from one another across future professions.

If ninety percent of the class consists of future Space Force officers and a small civilian contingent is added for appearance, the school will function culturally as a military academy.

If the military cohort is too small, future Guardians may lack the shared formation that justified the institution for Space Force.

If every student studies the same technical subjects, interdisciplinary promise becomes branding.

If service tracks become social tribes, the Academy may reproduce the very silos it was created to bridge.

Admissions cannot guarantee integration, but it can make integration possible.

The commission will therefore need to think in portfolios rather than just rankings.

How many future Guardians?

How many future NASA civil servants?

Which other agencies?

Which academic fields?

How many prior-service students?

How many students from preparatory pathways?

How much geographic representation?

How much room for international exchange?

Those decisions should flow from workforce needs and educational purpose, not from a desire to make the entering-class photo look balanced.

The Academy will also face pressure to become extraordinarily selective.

Prestigious institutions like low acceptance rates because scarcity is mistaken for quality. A federal academy tied to national mission should resist that vanity.

The goal is not to reject the largest possible number of talented Americans.

The goal is to develop enough of them.

If the country truly faces a shortage of people capable of leading in space, success may eventually require expanding programs, creating affiliates and funding pathways far beyond one campus.

The Academy should be the flagship of a pipeline, not the tollbooth at its entrance.

This is where admissions connects back to the fundamental purpose of the school.

If the Space Academy is merely another elite badge, it will add status to people who already had access to opportunity.

If it is a national institution, it should find talent the existing system overlooks and convert that talent into public capacity.

That does not mean everybody gets in.

It means the gate should be designed around the mission rather than inherited from tradition.

As of September 2026, the gate does not exist.

That is not a defect.

It is one of the few moments in an institution's life when the country can still decide what the gate is for.
