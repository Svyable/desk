# What They Must Learn

The easiest Space Academy curriculum to design is the wrong one.

Start with every important subject in space.

Add orbital mechanics.

Add astronautical engineering.

Add propulsion, remote sensing, communications, cyber, artificial intelligence, software, robotics, nuclear systems, space weather, intelligence, acquisition, economics, law, strategy, leadership, ethics and history.

Then add laboratories, internships, physical training, military instruction and a few courses on entrepreneurship because the executive order used the word.

The result would be a curriculum that looks impressively comprehensive and teaches students to be beginners in twenty fields.

Space is too large for a catalog to become an education.

The commission's real problem is not deciding what matters.

Almost everything matters eventually.

The problem is deciding what every graduate must know, what some graduates must know deeply and what the institution should deliberately leave to later professional education.

That requires a theory of the graduate before a list of courses.

The August 2026 order asks for an academic and leadership curriculum, including degree programs, experiential training and development programs. It does not prescribe majors. It does not say the Academy must be an engineering school. It does not say all students will follow a military core. The design space remains unusually open.

A good place to begin is with what the Academy should not attempt.

It should not train students on every current operational system.

STARCOM and service-specific schools can do that later and update faster.

It should not try to out-specialize MIT, Caltech, Stanford, Georgia Tech, Purdue or dozens of other research universities across every technical field.

It cannot.

It should not turn leadership into a sequence of inspirational seminars detached from technical work.

And it should not treat “space” as an excuse to make every graduate an aerospace engineer.

A national space enterprise needs engineers, but it also needs people who understand software, economics, policy, intelligence, law, organizations and public administration deeply enough to lead alongside engineers.

The curriculum has to make room for specialization without allowing specialization to become isolation.

That suggests a structure with three layers.

The first is a common foundation.

The second is disciplinary depth.

The third is repeated integration through real problems.

The common foundation should begin with physics because space is not metaphorical.

The domain imposes constraints whether institutions acknowledge them or not. Orbits create recurring geometry. Energy and momentum matter. Radiation matters. Heat matters. Vacuum matters. Signal propagation matters. Launch mass matters. Distance matters.

A student who cannot reason quantitatively about the physical environment will eventually depend on specialists for every important technical claim.

Not every graduate needs to derive advanced astrodynamics. Every graduate should understand enough mechanics to know why a satellite cannot simply hover over any point, why changing orbit consumes resources, why low Earth orbit behaves differently from geostationary orbit and why coverage, revisit time and latency are physical design choices rather than marketing language.

This is professional literacy.

The common foundation should include computation for the same reason.

Modern space systems are software systems wrapped around physical systems. Guidance, navigation, control, image processing, autonomy, mission planning, ground networks, cyber defense and data analysis all depend on computation. A future lawyer or strategist does not need to become a professional software engineer. A future leader should understand enough to recognize what software makes possible, what data makes fragile and why a system's codebase may matter as much as its visible hardware.

Students should write code.

Not because coding is morally superior to other intellectual work.

Because the experience of making a machine do exactly what you specified—and discovering that you specified the wrong thing—is one of the cleanest introductions to systems thinking available.

The common foundation should include engineering design even for non-engineers.

Design forces tradeoffs.

More power means more mass or a different power system.

More redundancy means cost, complexity and weight.

A better sensor may demand more data processing or communications capacity.

A faster schedule may require accepting technical risk.

A system built for one orbit may not perform the same mission from another.

Students need to experience a requirement becoming a physical object or software system and then encounter all the compromises hidden by the word *requirement*.

The common foundation should include probability and statistics because uncertainty is not an exception in space work.

Sensors produce imperfect measurements.

Reliability is probabilistic.

Intelligence is incomplete.

Testing samples only part of a system's behavior.

Forecasts carry distributions rather than certainties.

A leader who treats a 70 percent estimate as a fact or a low-probability risk as impossibility will eventually make bad decisions with great confidence.

The Academy should teach students to communicate uncertainty without using it as an excuse for indecision.

Then comes history.

Technical institutions routinely underinvest in history because the past seems less useful than the next system. This is especially dangerous in space, where every generation encounters a new technology and becomes tempted to believe the institutional problems are new too.

The Academy's students should know the history of rocketry, missiles, reconnaissance, communications satellites, NASA, military space, service academies, commercial launch, international cooperation, launch failures, acquisition reforms and strategic competition.

They should know why NASA was made civilian.

They should know how military space grew inside the Air Force.

They should know why the Space Force was created and what critics feared it would duplicate.

They should know how programs failed, not only how famous missions succeeded.

History is a laboratory whose experiments cannot be rerun.

Law belongs in the foundation because technical possibility does not confer authority.

Every student should understand the basic architecture of federal authority in space, international treaty obligations, commercial regulation, contracts, military legal authorities and the distinction between civil, military and intelligence missions.

The purpose is not to produce undergraduate space lawyers.

It is to make it professionally embarrassing for a graduate to propose an action without asking who is allowed to authorize it.

Economics belongs there too.

Space is now an industrial and commercial domain. Students should understand fixed costs, marginal costs, learning curves, capital intensity, markets, procurement incentives, competition, monopoly risk, supply chains and the difference between government ownership and purchasing a service.

A launch vehicle is an engineering object and an economic system.

A constellation is an orbital architecture and a business model.

A factory is a strategic asset and a balance-sheet burden.

People who understand only one description will misread the others.

Strategy should come after enough technical foundation that students cannot use abstraction to escape constraints.

They should study deterrence, escalation, alliances, joint operations, resilience, intelligence, command and control and the role of space in national power. But every strategic proposition should eventually encounter a technical question.

What does “resilient” mean in architecture?

How many satellites?

Which orbits?

What ground network?

What replenishment rate?

What cyber assumptions?

What launch capacity?

What commercial dependencies?

A strategy seminar becomes more useful the moment somebody has to draw the system.

Ethics should be treated the same way.

The Academy should resist generic moral instruction built around admirable nouns.

Integrity matters. Courage matters. Service matters.

Students learn what those words mean when they conflict with incentives.

An engineer discovers evidence that a test schedule is unsafe and the launch date is politically important.

A program manager is asked to describe a troubled program in a way that is technically true and predictably misleading.

An intelligence officer holds uncertain evidence that leaders want converted into certainty.

A commercial partner owns useful data but sharing it creates legal or security concerns.

A scientist receives a result that weakens the hypothesis on which a mission proposal was built.

A junior officer believes a senior operational assumption is wrong.

Ethics becomes professional when telling the truth has a cost.

The Academy should add one more common requirement that technical schools sometimes treat as remedial rather than central: writing.

Space institutions fail in documents as well as hardware.

Requirements are written.

Test findings are written.

Budgets are justified in writing.

Intelligence assessments are written.

Orders, contracts, policy memoranda, scientific papers and anomaly reports all convert thought into institutional action.

A leader who cannot explain a complicated system plainly will either lose the decision or force somebody else to translate it.

Students should therefore write for different audiences: a technical peer, a senior decision-maker, Congress, the public and a team whose members come from another discipline. The same evidence should not produce the same document every time.

Clarity is not decoration around technical competence.

It is how competence moves through an organization.

The second layer is depth.

Every graduate should know one difficult field well enough to experience the difference between familiarity and expertise.

This requirement may be the Academy's defense against producing polished generalists who can discuss everything and build nothing.

Possible degrees could include astronautical engineering, mechanical or electrical engineering, computer science, physics, applied mathematics, systems engineering, cyber, intelligence studies, economics, public policy and other fields justified by mission.

The commission should be cautious about creating fashionable degrees faster than it can recruit serious faculty.

A major exists because a coherent discipline deserves sustained study, not because the title looks futuristic on a brochure.

Artificial intelligence, for example, will be central to future space systems. That does not automatically mean every academy needs an undergraduate “AI major.” Strong foundations in computer science, mathematics, statistics and domain applications may age better. The same is true of whatever technology dominates the next news cycle.

The degree list should be smaller than political enthusiasm wants and deeper than marketing prefers.

Artificial intelligence also changes how the Academy must teach rather than merely what it teaches.

Students will have access to systems capable of generating code, explanations, designs, summaries and prose. Banning those tools wholesale would train graduates for an environment that no longer exists. Treating them as invisible assistants would make it difficult to know what students can actually do.

The institution will need explicit modes.

Some work should be tool-free so that the Academy can verify individual competence.

Some work should require tools so students learn to supervise automation, test outputs, identify fabricated claims and understand where machine assistance changes responsibility.

A future officer who accepts an incorrect generated answer remains responsible for the decision made from it.

A future engineer who cannot reproduce or interrogate an automated result does not possess the result in a professional sense.

The educational standard should be neither purity nor convenience.

It should be accountable use.

A technically serious Academy also needs faculty who are scholars and practitioners rather than an endless rotation of temporary instructors.

Service academies have long mixed military and civilian faculty. The Space Academy may need an even wider mix: permanent civilian professors, military officers, NASA engineers and scientists, visiting practitioners from national laboratories and carefully governed participation from industry.

The balance will determine whether the school develops intellectual memory.

Practitioners bring current problems.

Permanent faculty preserve long-term inquiry and can tell when a “new” problem has appeared before.

Students need both.

Industry participation should be strong enough to keep the Academy connected to production and weak enough that no company gets to turn the curriculum into recruiting or product advocacy.

A federal academy cannot become a showroom.

The third layer is integration.

This is where the Space Academy either becomes distinct or becomes a conventional university with unusual uniforms.

Students should repeatedly encounter problems that no single major can solve.

A first-year exercise might be simple: explain why a proposed satellite architecture cannot satisfy all of its stated requirements.

A later project could require designing a mission within fixed cost, launch and schedule constraints.

A third-year exercise could introduce an adversary, cyber compromise, supplier failure or legal restriction.

A senior capstone could require teams from different disciplines to recommend a real architecture or policy to a mixed panel of technical and operational experts.

The point is not to make school dramatic.

It is to make tradeoffs visible.

Integration should extend into summers and field experiences.

The Naval Academy sends midshipmen to sea because the Navy cannot be understood entirely on campus. A Space Academy cannot send every student to orbit, but it can send them into the institutions that make space real.

NASA centers.

Space Force units.

Launch ranges.

Factories.

National laboratories.

Research observatories.

Mission-control centers.

Cyber units.

Commercial operators.

Federal program offices.

A student should see at least one organization where failure means a delayed paper and another where failure means a lost spacecraft or degraded military capability. The contrast will teach more about risk culture than a lecture on organizational behavior.

Experiential education will also reveal whether the Academy's civil-military integration survives outside campus.

A future Guardian should work with civilian scientists.

A future NASA engineer should see military space operations without pretending to become an operator.

A future acquisition professional should spend time where hardware is manufactured.

A future policy student should encounter engineers who can explain why a proposed rule creates a physical impossibility.

This is how shared language becomes earned.

Leadership development belongs across all three layers rather than beside them.

The Academy should distrust the idea that leadership is a separate subject delivered by a department while academic courses handle knowledge.

Leadership appears when a team is late.

When the best technical expert is difficult to work with.

When a student has authority over someone with more expertise.

When a project fails and the team must decide whether to hide the failure, explain it or learn from it.

When a plan meets data that invalidates it.

Students should lead laboratories, projects, organizations and exercises whose results matter to the community. They should receive feedback from people they led, not only people above them.

Military-track students will require additional formation specific to officership: chain of command, military law, physical standards, field leadership, professional military education and service-specific preparation. Civilian-track students should have equally serious leadership expectations designed around public administration and technical responsibility rather than watered-down military training.

The common institution should not imply that leadership looks identical in every profession.

The Academy will also need to decide how it knows whether the curriculum works.

Course completion is not enough.

Grades are not enough.

Accreditation is necessary but cannot answer the Academy's unique institutional question.

The school should define a small set of capabilities every graduate is expected to demonstrate: quantitative reasoning, disciplinary depth, clear writing, systems thinking, lawful decision-making, ethical judgment, collaborative problem solving and the ability to learn unfamiliar technical material.

Those capabilities should appear repeatedly across courses rather than live in one assessment office.

Later employers can provide another feedback loop. STARCOM can report whether military graduates arrive prepared for integrated officer training. NASA and other agencies can report whether civilian graduates handle complex projects differently from comparable hires. Alumni can reveal which parts of the curriculum survived contact with real work and which became forgotten requirements.

A living curriculum requires evidence from the lives it produces.

There is one more subject every student should study because no curriculum can remain current without it.

Learning.

The Academy will graduate students into careers in which much of their technical knowledge becomes outdated.

A propulsion engineer may work with systems not yet designed.

A Space Force officer may face threats not yet fielded.

A software specialist will certainly use tools that did not exist during freshman year.

A NASA scientist may work on a mission whose instruments are only concepts when the student graduates.

The Academy must therefore make intellectual updating part of professional identity.

Students should learn how to read technical literature, challenge sources, reproduce results where possible, distinguish demonstrations from deployed capability, understand uncertainty and teach themselves new tools without abandoning first principles.

They should graduate suspicious of anyone who says the future has made fundamentals obsolete.

Physics does not move at venture-capital speed.

Institutions do not move at software speed.

Human judgment does not improve because a model generated a faster answer.

The people who lead in space will live among different clocks.

A curriculum should make them comfortable with that mismatch.

This is why the question “What should the Space Academy teach?” has no satisfying list answer.

It should teach enough physics that graduates respect reality.

Enough computation that they understand modern systems.

Enough history that they recognize recurring institutional mistakes.

Enough law that they respect authority.

Enough economics that they understand incentives.

Enough strategy that they understand consequence.

Enough ethics that they know expertise does not remove responsibility.

Enough writing that their judgment can move through institutions without distortion.

Then it should make every student go deep enough in one field to discover how hard expertise is.

Finally, it should force those experts to solve problems together until collaboration stops being a slogan.

Everything else is a course catalog.
