# The Entrepreneur Problem

One word in the executive order does unusual work.

Entrepreneurs.

The proposed Space Academy is supposed to help prepare astronauts, scientists, engineers, operators, entrepreneurs, civil servants and warfighters. Most of those categories fit naturally inside a discussion of public institutions. Entrepreneurs do not.

An entrepreneur is not a federal service category.

The commission is explicitly directed to consider service obligations in the Armed Forces and civilian Federal service. It is not directed to design an obligation in which graduates repay the government by founding companies.

The difference matters.

Commercial space is central enough to American capability that any serious space curriculum must engage it. Launch providers, satellite manufacturers, communications companies, remote-sensing firms, software vendors, component suppliers and newer commercial services are woven through civil and military missions. NASA and the Department of Defense buy from companies. The Space Force has published a commercial-space strategy. Government customers increasingly rely on private systems while also worrying about resilience, concentration, security and whether commercial services will remain available under wartime conditions.

Students who do not understand markets will misunderstand the domain.

That does not mean the federal government should build a publicly funded startup school.

The Academy will need to draw a careful distinction between teaching entrepreneurship and subsidizing private exit.

Entrepreneurship, at its best, is a method of noticing possibilities that established organizations neglect.

A founder asks whether a capability can be delivered differently, whether a cost structure can be changed, whether a customer need is real enough to support a company, whether technology has matured enough for a new service and whether capital can be assembled before certainty exists.

Those are useful questions inside government too.

Public organizations can become trapped by inherited program structures. Acquisition systems can reward compliance with an established process even when the outcome no longer makes sense. Technical teams can optimize what has already been authorized. A graduate who has learned to identify assumptions and test demand may become a better civil servant or officer without ever founding a company.

The Academy could therefore teach entrepreneurial reasoning as one professional lens.

What problem is real?

Who experiences it?

What does the current solution cost?

Which constraint is technical and which is institutional?

What can be tested cheaply before a large commitment?

What risk belongs to the builder and what risk belongs to the customer?

What changes when the government is the customer?

What changes when national security is involved?

Those questions belong comfortably inside a public-service institution.

The difficulty begins when the student wants to leave and build the answer privately.

If the government pays for an expensive Academy education and the graduate immediately joins a startup, has the service obligation been satisfied?

Under the order as written, not necessarily.

The commission is asked to recommend military and civilian federal service. It may propose additional conditions, but until it does, the manuscript should not invent a private-sector pathway.

That creates a recruiting tension.

Some of the students most attracted to commercial space may resist a long federal commitment. They may believe the fastest route to influence is through a company. A talented software engineer could earn more outside government. A prospective founder may value freedom to choose a problem after graduation rather than accept an assigned public-service role.

A service obligation that is too rigid could make “entrepreneur” a decorative word in the Academy's founding language.

A service obligation that is too loose could make public subsidy difficult to defend.

The government cannot resolve this by saying industry serves the nation too.

Sometimes it does.

Sometimes a company serves its shareholders, customers or founders in ways that overlap only partly with public priorities. Commercial incentives can produce extraordinary innovation. They can also produce monopoly, hype, fragile business models, security risks and products optimized for markets rather than public need.

The Academy should teach students to respect commercial capability without treating private activity as automatically equivalent to public service.

That distinction has become harder because the boundaries of the American space enterprise are so porous.

A company can build a system used for civilian science one day and military operations the next. A commercial satellite service can become strategically important during conflict. A founder can affect national security without holding public office. A government program can depend on capital markets and supply chains it does not control.

This makes entrepreneurship relevant to public education precisely because it is not public service.

Future officers and civil servants need to understand what companies can do, what they cannot be compelled to do without legal authority, what contracts actually purchase and where government dependence creates leverage for both sides.

A Space Academy could teach this better than institutions that place business and national security in separate buildings.

Students should examine real acquisition structures.

When should government specify a design and when should it buy an outcome?

When does a commercial product remain commercial after the government becomes its largest customer?

How do milestone payments change incentives?

When does competition disappear because one provider reaches scale first?

How should government value redundancy that looks inefficient in peacetime?

When should it preserve a second supplier?

How should agencies evaluate venture-backed companies that can fail between contract award and mission delivery?

What happens when a strategically important company is controlled by a founder whose decisions the government cannot direct?

These are entrepreneurial questions viewed from the public side.

The students interested in founding companies need the mirror image.

What does it mean to accept government money?

What obligations attach to export controls, cybersecurity, classified work, procurement law and truthful representations to customers?

How should a company communicate technical risk when its survival may depend on the next contract?

What happens when the fastest path to revenue creates long-term dependence for a government customer?

How should founders think about capabilities that can be used for coercion or conflict?

What is the responsibility of a private actor operating infrastructure on which the public increasingly depends?

A startup culture that teaches only speed will answer these questions late.

A federal academy that teaches only caution will answer market questions late.

The educational opportunity is to put the incentives into the same room.

This is another reason experiential training matters.

Students should see commercial operations close enough to understand why companies can move differently. They should also see government operations close enough to understand why public organizations cannot simply behave like startups.

The phrase “government should move at the speed of industry” sounds attractive until the difference in obligations is made concrete.

A venture-backed company can concentrate authority in a founder.

A federal agency is bound by law, appropriations, oversight, public accountability and procedural protections.

A startup can abandon a product line.

A military service may have to maintain a capability because national defense cannot wait for the market to become attractive again.

A company can decide a customer is unprofitable.

A government may have obligations to citizens regardless of profitability.

These constraints can become excuses for bad bureaucracy.

They are not all bureaucracy.

Some are democracy.

Entrepreneurial education inside the Academy should teach students which is which.

The Academy will also face pressure from industry itself.

Companies will want access to students.

That is normal. Universities already host career fairs, sponsored research, internships and laboratories supported by industry. A federal academy may be even more attractive because its students are being selected for technical skill and public responsibility in a strategically important sector.

The institution should welcome commercial exposure and protect its independence at the same time.

A sponsored laboratory should not become a training pipeline for one vendor's stack.

An internship should not create an expectation that public decisions later favor the employer.

Faculty research should disclose conflicts.

Students should encounter competitors, new entrants and critical perspectives, not only the firms large enough to maintain a permanent presence near campus.

The Academy's industry relationships should be designed around learning rather than recruitment volume.

This is particularly important because the school could influence procurement for decades through its alumni.

If future acquisition officers, program managers, NASA leaders and military commanders share formative relationships with a small set of companies, the network can improve communication.

It can also create familiarity bias.

Professional education should make students conscious of that risk before they have authority over public money.

The entrepreneur problem also reveals something deeper about the Academy's proposed identity.

A traditional service academy educates people for a defined institution.

West Point graduates serve the Army.

Annapolis graduates enter the Navy or Marine Corps.

The Air Force Academy sends graduates into the Air Force and Space Force.

The Space Academy order gestures toward an ecosystem.

That ecosystem includes people who may never work for the federal government.

If the school is genuinely trying to strengthen the broader space enterprise, its influence may need to travel beyond the graduates whose tuition it pays.

It could do that without releasing students from service obligations.

Research can be public where appropriate.

Short courses can include industry participants.

Conferences can bring companies and agencies together.

Faculty can study commercial architectures and market structure.

Students completing federal service could later move into industry carrying public-service experience with them.

Alumni networks can bridge sectors over an entire career rather than at graduation.

The Academy can strengthen entrepreneurship indirectly by educating better government customers.

That contribution should not be underestimated.

Commercial space companies often complain about slow or confusing government acquisition. Government officials complain that companies misunderstand mission requirements or underestimate the burdens of security and reliability. Better translation on both sides can produce more competition and more useful products without the Academy incubating a single startup.

This is an unglamorous form of economic policy.

It may be more valuable than a campus accelerator.

There will still be students who want to found companies.

The Academy should be honest with them.

A public-service school can encourage entrepreneurial imagination and still say the public comes first for the period you promised.

That constraint may even improve the future founder.

Several years inside NASA, the Space Force or another federal organization can reveal problems outsiders do not see. It can teach why certain requirements exist. It can build relationships with users and expose the gap between a compelling demonstration and an operational capability.

Public service need not be time lost before entrepreneurship.

It can be domain apprenticeship.

The danger is creating a cynical bargain in which students treat service merely as the price of a prestigious education before leaving for higher salaries.

The Academy cannot prevent that through obligation length alone.

It has to make public work meaningful enough that talented people can imagine staying.

Compensation matters.

Personnel systems matter.

Technical authority matters.

The ability to build and operate real things matters.

The school will not control all of those conditions.

It should not pretend culture can compensate for every institutional weakness.

The word *entrepreneurs* therefore keeps the Academy honest.

It reminds the founders that the national space enterprise is larger than government.

The service-obligation language reminds them that the Academy is being proposed as a public institution.

The tension should remain visible.

If the school resolves it by becoming a government-only academy, it may lose contact with a major source of American space capability.

If it resolves it by treating private success as interchangeable with public service, it may lose the reason taxpayers should fund it.

A useful Academy will teach students to inhabit the boundary without pretending the boundary is gone.
