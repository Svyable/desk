# Guardians Are Born in Borrowed Institutions

The first Space Force did not recruit a generation.

It transferred one.

When the service was created in December 2019, most of the people who knew how to perform American military space missions already wore other uniforms or worked as civilians inside existing organizations. The country could not stop operating satellites while a new personnel system matured. Missile warning still had to work. Communications still had to work. Launches still had to occur. Objects in orbit still had to be tracked.

So Space Force began by inheriting expertise.

Air Force Space Command supplied much of the organizational core. Officers and enlisted personnel transferred. Later, people from other services entered as well. Civilian specialists remained essential. The new service received decades of technical knowledge along with decades of habits formed somewhere else.

This is the normal contradiction of institutional birth.

The people most qualified to create the new thing are the people formed by the old thing.

A startup can hire outsiders and begin with a blank culture. A military service responsible for operational systems cannot. It must change identity without discarding competence.

That is why the Space Force's early education story is less about invention than recombination.

A Guardian could arrive from the Air Force Academy.

Another from ROTC at a civilian university.

Another from Officer Training School.

Another could transfer after years in another service.

The June 2026 Officer Training Course cohort included prior enlisted personnel and graduates of the Air Force Academy, Military Academy and Naval Academy. One officer arrived after more than two decades of Marine Corps service before transferring into Space Force.

Put those biographies in one classroom and the service's problem becomes visible.

They all wear the same uniform now.

They do not arrive with the same professional past.

Older services use this diversity deliberately. Army officers come from West Point, ROTC and Officer Candidate School. Navy officers come from Annapolis, NROTC and other routes. Multiple sources prevent any one institution from owning the officer corps.

Space Force adds another layer because much of its early force was not merely commissioned elsewhere.

It was professionally socialized elsewhere.

An officer who spent years in the Air Force brings assumptions about staff work, command, technical specialization and career progression. A former Marine brings another set. A civilian Guardian may possess deep institutional knowledge without military rank. A young lieutenant arriving directly from college knows Space Force only as the service itself teaches it.

The service needs all of them.

It also needs them to understand one another.

The Officer Training Course emerged as an answer.

Space Force leadership described its officers as commanders, leaders, planners and strategists who needed broad multidisciplinary thinking rather than narrow early specialization. The course combined space operations, intelligence, cyber and acquisition exposure in one integrated experience.

By 2026 the service formalized the philosophy further in an officer career-development path built around the phrase “Guardian First, Specialist Second.”

The wording captures the institutional project precisely.

A profession usually starts by teaching specialty.

Space Force was trying to teach identity before allowing specialty to become destiny.

That choice made sense because the service had deliberately limited its career fields. Its public descriptions emphasized space operations, intelligence, cyber, engineering and acquisition or program management. It relied on the Air Force for many support functions rather than recreating the full organizational machinery of a larger branch.

Lean organizations cannot afford elaborate internal ignorance.

If there are fewer layers between a technical problem and a senior decision, officers need broader context earlier.

A program manager who understands nothing about operations can become a bottleneck.

An operator who understands nothing about acquisition can treat hardware as though it appears when requested.

An intelligence officer who cannot translate threat information into engineering or operational consequence is less useful than the quality of the intelligence suggests.

A cyber specialist who sees networks without mission context can protect the wrong thing perfectly.

The service therefore began teaching officers to see the entire machine before assigning them to one part of it.

This approach creates an awkward question for a Space Academy.

If Space Force already possesses a year-one institution designed to turn graduates from different backgrounds into Guardians, what should an undergraduate academy do?

The wrong answer is to move the same curriculum four years earlier.

A college freshman does not need the same training as a newly commissioned lieutenant preparing for an operational assignment. The undergraduate has not yet acquired a technical discipline. The officer needs to connect existing education to service practice.

The academy's purpose should be complementary.

It should teach the intellectual architecture on which Guardian formation can later build.

That architecture might include physics, computation, systems thinking, history, strategy, law, economics and engineering design. More importantly, it should teach students to move among these fields without confusing familiarity with expertise.

A Guardian officer can be “Guardian First” only if the word *Guardian* means something more than a uniform.

Space Force has spent its first years trying to define that meaning.

Its published values—character, commitment, connection and courage—sound broad because values must travel across specialties. Its education efforts emphasize critical thinking, problem solving and multidisciplinary understanding because a small technical service cannot create a leadership culture around one dominant platform in the way an aviation service once could.

There is no single Guardian experience equivalent to flying an airplane or serving aboard a ship.

Many Guardians operate spacecraft from the ground.

Others develop systems they may never operate.

Others analyze intelligence, defend networks or manage programs.

Civilian Guardians provide continuity and technical depth across occupations far beyond the uniformed career fields.

The common identity must therefore come from mission and method rather than a shared physical experience.

This may be the strongest educational rationale for the Space Academy.

The Academy could give the service a place where the mission is central before specialty separates students.

But the same fact creates a risk.

If Space Academy graduates arrive at Officer Training Course believing they already possess the authentic Guardian identity, the new institution will undermine the integration system Space Force has built.

A cadet academy should not become a shortcut around service formation.

It should produce officers ready to learn from people who came through other routes.

This sounds like etiquette. It is actually force design.

A small service cannot afford caste systems based on commissioning source.

The Space Force needs officers from civilian universities because advanced technical talent is widely distributed. It needs prior enlisted officers because operational experience matters. It needs transfers because other services contain useful expertise. It needs academy graduates because service academies can provide deep leadership formation and continuity.

The Space Academy should strengthen that ecology, not become its dominant species.

This is where the enlisted question becomes impossible to avoid.

Academy debates naturally center officers because federal service academies are officer-producing institutions. But a technical service is not an officer corps with supporting scenery. Enlisted Guardians, civilians, contractors and specialists carry operational knowledge that may be more current than the undergraduate knowledge of a newly commissioned lieutenant.

A Space Academy that teaches future officers about leadership while insulating them from the people who actually maintain, operate, troubleshoot and sustain systems would reproduce one of the oldest institutional mistakes in technical organizations: confusing rank with knowledge.

The remedy does not require turning every enlisted pathway into a four-year academy program.

It requires designing the school so that officer formation includes structured exposure to enlisted expertise and so that enlisted Guardians have meaningful access to academy courses, research, preparatory programs or later educational opportunities where appropriate.

The distinction matters.

A young officer should leave the Academy understanding that authority and expertise overlap imperfectly.

The service will sometimes require that officer to make a decision before possessing the deepest technical knowledge in the room. The educational problem is not to pretend otherwise. It is to teach the officer how to identify expertise, ask better questions, recognize uncertainty and take responsibility for a decision that depends on other people's knowledge.

That is leadership in a technical service.

A new academy has an unusual chance to make that principle structural from the beginning.

There is another reason borrowed institutions matter.

They carry intellectual diversity.

An officer educated at West Point may approach organizational problems differently from one educated at MIT through ROTC. A Naval Academy graduate may bring maritime instincts about distributed operations and command. A prior Marine may understand expeditionary culture. A civilian engineer may have spent years in commercial development cycles where speed, cost and iteration operate differently from government programs.

A new service can treat those differences as contamination and standardize them away.

Or it can create a common language that allows the differences to remain useful.

The Officer Training Course appears designed toward the second outcome.

Its multidisciplinary baseline does not require every officer to become equally skilled in intelligence, cyber, operations and acquisition. It gives specialists enough context to participate in a shared mission.

The Academy should follow the same principle at a broader level.

A future NASA engineer does not need to become a military planner.

A future Space Force officer does not need to become a planetary scientist.

A future civil servant does not need to become a startup founder.

They need enough shared literacy to know when the person from the other profession is saying something that changes their own problem.

This is how complex organizations become faster without becoming reckless.

The alternative is translation through hierarchy.

If specialists cannot speak directly, information moves upward until somebody with authority but less technical depth tries to reconcile it. Every layer introduces delay and distortion. The result is a meeting in which all the necessary expertise is technically present and nobody is actually solving the same problem.

Space systems are especially vulnerable to this failure because the connections are tight.

A change in orbit affects coverage.

Coverage affects operations.

Operations affect requirements.

Requirements affect design.

Design affects mass.

Mass affects launch.

Launch affects cost and schedule.

Software changes can alter capability without changing hardware.

Cyber risk can invalidate assumptions made by system engineers.

Threat intelligence can make a previously acceptable architecture fragile.

No one person can master all of it.

A profession has to make collaboration routine enough that no one needs to.

The Space Force is building that routine after commissioning.

The Space Academy could begin it before.

But there is a deeper question beneath curriculum.

Can an identity be taught before a service has finished becoming itself?

The Space Force of 2026 is not the Space Force of 2019. Its commands have changed. Its training has changed. Its career-development philosophy has changed. Its senior leadership has turned over. Its operational environment has changed as commercial constellations proliferated and military threats evolved.

A new academy would freeze some version of the service into traditions, textbooks and ceremonies.

That is what academies do.

They make temporary judgments durable enough to teach.

If the service is still changing rapidly, the academy must be designed to revise those judgments without treating revision as betrayal.

That means institutionalizing correction.

Courses should have owners but not owners for life.

Operational commands should be able to tell faculty when assumptions have become stale.

Graduates returning from the field should become part of curriculum revision rather than merely alumni programming.

Exercises should change when doctrine changes.

Technical laboratories should not become museums for the systems that happened to be fashionable when the first appropriation arrived.

The Academy will need tradition and revision simultaneously.

That sounds contradictory only if tradition is mistaken for immobility.

The best professional traditions are methods for carrying principles through changing tools.

Integrity does not become obsolete when software changes.

Mission command does not become irrelevant when architectures shift.

Systems thinking remains valuable even when systems become more autonomous.

The Academy should be conservative about principles and aggressive about implementation.

This is another advantage of the borrowed-institution era.

Borrowing forces humility.

A Guardian at the Air Force Academy knows the service is not the academy.

A Guardian from ROTC knows the service is not the university.

A transfer knows the uniform changed while professional experience remained.

The common identity has to be discovered later, through Space Force itself.

A Space Academy will bring the source closer to the service, which may strengthen formation.

It may also tempt graduates to confuse the school's culture with the service's culture.

Older branches have had centuries to learn the difference.

The Space Force does not.

This is why the Academy should be built, if it is built, as one doorway among several.

Its students should train beside ROTC cadets.

Its military graduates should still pass through the same Guardian initial-skills system as other officers.

Its faculty should include people whose careers began outside the Academy.

Its research should partner with universities rather than assume federal ownership of expertise.

Its alumni network should be powerful enough to help the service but not powerful enough to define who belongs.

The healthiest test may be social rather than academic.

Ten years after opening, does a Space Academy graduate look at a ROTC lieutenant and see another Guardian—or a lesser path into the same profession?

Does an enlisted Guardian see the Academy as a place that respects operational knowledge—or as a prestige machine for officers?

Does a NASA engineer see a military graduate as a colleague with a different legal mission—or as somebody trained inside a separate tribe?

Does a civilian graduate believe public service is broader than the institution that issued the diploma?

Those attitudes will reveal whether the school created a profession or a caste.

A new service naturally wants symbols of independence.

An academy may become the largest one.

But the most impressive thing about Space Force's early professional development is not independence.

It is the deliberate attempt to integrate people who arrived through borrowed institutions.

That is a strength worth preserving.

The first Guardians inherited systems, ranks, bases and experience.

They also inherited differences.

The profession began when the service stopped treating those differences as a temporary inconvenience and built a common education around them.

The Space Academy should not erase that origin story.

It should teach it.

Because the sixth service was not born in one school.

It was born in all the schools it learned to outgrow.
