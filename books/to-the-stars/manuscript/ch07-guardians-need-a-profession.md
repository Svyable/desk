# Guardians Need a Profession

A commissioning source answers one question.

Are you an officer?

It does not answer the next one.

What kind?

When the Space Force began receiving newly commissioned officers, they arrived through pathways the United States already knew how to operate. Some graduated from the Air Force Academy. Some came through Reserve Officer Training Corps programs at civilian universities. Some completed Officer Training School. Those systems could select, educate and commission people without waiting for a new service to invent an undergraduate institution.

But a Space Force officer still had to learn the Space Force.

That sounds less obvious than it is.

The service had inherited experienced military space professionals, but new officers were entering a branch whose occupational boundaries were still being reorganized. Space operations, cyber, intelligence, engineering and acquisition were deeply intertwined in practice while traditional personnel systems often trained them as separate tribes. A satellite operator might depend on software maintained by another organization, intelligence analyzed elsewhere, networks protected by cyber professionals, hardware acquired years earlier and commercial services bought through contracts the operator never saw.

The mission was integrated.

The careers were not always integrated enough.

Space Training and Readiness Command was created to work on that problem.

STARCOM's responsibilities stretch across education, training, doctrine and test and evaluation. The command is not the Space Force's academy. It performs a different function. It takes people who are already inside the profession and develops what they need to operate as Guardians.

That distinction is crucial to the Space Academy debate.

The proposed Academy would educate before service.

STARCOM educates for service.

If the two institutions ever coexist, their relationship could determine whether the Academy adds value or merely moves training earlier on the calendar.

The Officer Training Course provides the clearest example.

Beginning in 2025, the Space Force established a common initial skills course for newly commissioned Guardian officers. By June 2026, STARCOM reported more than 350 graduates since the course's inception the previous August. One June class contained 54 officers. The service emphasized that officers attended regardless of commissioning source.

The Academy cadet and the ROTC graduate meet again after the commissioning ceremony.

That is an important design choice.

It says the Space Force does not assume an officer arrives fully formed simply because an undergraduate institution awarded a diploma and a commission. Instead, the service creates a common professional layer after those different educational experiences.

The course crosses disciplines. Newly commissioned Guardians encounter material related to space operations, cyber warfare, intelligence and acquisitions. This does not make each officer an expert in all four. It creates awareness of how the pieces fit.

That word—awareness—can sound weak.

In complex organizations, it is often the difference between a local optimization and a mission decision.

Consider a simplified example.

A satellite system experiences unexpected behavior. The operator sees anomalous telemetry. The first explanation might be a spacecraft malfunction. A cyber specialist considers whether the ground segment or software has been compromised. An intelligence officer asks whether an adversary is behaving in a way that changes the probability of intentional interference. An acquisition professional knows that the system's design reflects choices made years earlier and that a replacement capability cannot appear instantly.

No one perspective is sufficient.

The professional task is not to become everybody else.

It is to know when everybody else belongs in the room.

This is why the Space Force's emerging training system matters to the proposed Academy. It reveals that the service has already identified cross-functional literacy as part of the Guardian profession.

A new Academy could build that literacy earlier.

It could also waste four years teaching what the service can teach more efficiently in months after commissioning.

The difference depends on depth.

A short professional course can introduce institutions, mission concepts and career-field relationships. Undergraduate education can spend years developing the intellectual tools beneath them. The Academy should not try to reproduce STARCOM's course calendar. It should ask what a student needs to understand so that later operational training lands on deeper ground.

Take orbital mechanics.

A new officer can learn the operational effects of an orbit without becoming a celestial mechanician. But a student who has spent years working through the physics and mathematics behind orbital motion develops a different intuition. She can recognize why certain maneuvers cost more than others, why geometry changes visibility, why timing matters and why a phrase like “move the satellite” may conceal hard constraints.

Take computer science.

A professional course can teach cyber responsibilities and current architectures. A four-year education can make a student comfortable enough with code, networks, algorithms and software failure that cyber is not an external specialty somebody else handles.

Take acquisition.

A course can explain the acquisition system. A serious education in economics, engineering, program history and organizational behavior can help an officer understand why programs become late, why requirements expand, why incentives matter and why a technically elegant design can still be an institutional failure.

The Academy's job, then, is not to move technical training earlier.

It is to make later training more intelligent.

That sounds like a traditional liberal-education argument because it is one.

The space profession may be technologically new, but the educational problem is old. Professional schools fail when they confuse immediate utility with durable preparation. If every course must map directly to a first assignment, the curriculum becomes a catalog of current procedures. Students graduate highly prepared for the first job and less prepared for the fifth.

The Space Force changes too quickly for that model.

The first Academy class, if admitted near the end of the 2020s, could spend senior careers operating in a space architecture that does not yet exist. They may work with autonomous systems far beyond present practice. They may oversee proliferated constellations whose scale makes today's satellite operations look artisanal. They may operate in cislunar space. They may manage commercial services so integrated into military architecture that the old distinction between government-owned and commercially provided capability becomes operationally awkward.

The curriculum cannot predict all of this.

It can teach people how to learn inside it.

STARCOM's role demonstrates another professional need: doctrine.

Doctrine is one of those military words that civilians can mistake for dogma. Good doctrine is almost the opposite. It is an organization's best current attempt to explain how it understands its mission and how forces should be employed. It gives people common concepts so they do not have to invent the profession from scratch every time a new unit meets a new problem.

The Space Force has developed doctrine across a growing set of subjects, including mission command, planning, targeting, information operations, missile warning and tracking, sustainment and electromagnetic-spectrum operations.

The list is less important than the existence of the process.

A new service writes down what it thinks it knows.

Then experience tests it.

An Academy could give students enough history to understand that doctrine is neither scripture nor disposable bureaucracy. It is institutional memory under revision.

That is a sophisticated relationship to teach.

Young professionals naturally want rules. Rules reduce uncertainty. A checklist says what happens next. A doctrine publication can appear to provide the approved answer.

Senior professionals eventually learn that high-consequence situations often occur where guidance becomes incomplete.

The purpose of education is not to make people less likely to follow sound rules.

It is to make them less helpless when the rules stop.

This matters especially in space because many operational events are ambiguous.

A satellite anomaly may be accidental, environmental or hostile. An unusual maneuver may signal an operational test, routine repositioning or an action intended to communicate something. A cyber intrusion may have uncertain attribution. Interference may be temporary, reversible and politically sensitive. Debris can create consequences without anyone intending them.

The profession has to think probabilistically while institutions often demand decisions.

This is not solved by more technical detail alone.

It requires education in history, intelligence, law, ethics and decision-making under uncertainty.

STARCOM's expanding educational architecture points toward another feature the Academy would need to respect: professional development does not end at commissioning.

In 2026, STARCOM graduated the first class of a Space Component Staff Course designed to prepare personnel for work in joint and service-component staffs. The course brought together people from intelligence, space operations, cyber, acquisition and engineering and focused on planning and command-and-control problems.

The existence of such a course is a reminder that professional needs change with responsibility.

An undergraduate cannot be taught everything a colonel will need decades later.

Trying would be absurd.

The Academy should instead prepare graduates to return to education repeatedly without seeing it as remediation. A healthy profession treats learning as part of work because responsibility keeps changing the questions.

The military has a name for this broad system: professional military education.

The Space Academy, if it serves both military and civilian students, would need something wider.

Professional space education.

The phrase is useful because it separates the domain from the service. Guardians need military education. NASA leaders need civilian public-service education. Regulators need legal and policy education. Engineers need technical development. Commercial leaders need market understanding. Yet they share enough of the domain that some educational infrastructure could be common.

This is where a NASA-led academy could become more interesting than a separate Space Force academy.

Imagine a student body divided eventually into different public-service tracks but sharing foundational work.

In one exercise, students analyze a satellite architecture. The future engineer concentrates on design and failure modes. The future Guardian asks about mission assurance and adversary action. The future regulator asks about spectrum and authorization. The future acquisition officer asks about cost and vendor dependence. The future scientist asks about data quality and mission opportunity.

The goal is not to make everyone agree.

The goal is to make disagreement informed.

That habit would serve the government because many expensive failures begin when one professional community mistakes its own constraints for the whole system.

The Space Force's training system already attempts a military version of this by exposing new officers to career fields beyond their own.

The Academy could widen the aperture.

But it should be careful not to dilute military seriousness in the process.

A future Guardian is not merely a federal space employee in uniform. Military service carries legal authority, discipline, hierarchy and the possibility of combat. Officers accept obligations civilians do not. They may be ordered into situations they would not freely choose. They are responsible for carrying out lawful orders and refusing unlawful ones. They work inside a chain of command where decisions can involve national violence.

This cannot be simulated away as a generic leadership track.

The Academy would need distinct military formation alongside common education.

Likewise, civilian service should not be treated as the easier path for students who do not qualify for the military. NASA and other federal agencies require serious professional identities of their own. A civilian engineer responsible for flight safety may carry life-and-death responsibility without wearing a uniform. A regulator can make decisions affecting public safety and markets. An intelligence analyst can shape national decisions while remaining a civilian.

The institution will fail if it creates an unspoken hierarchy in which military service is considered the “real” service and civilian pathways are secondary.

It will also fail if military requirements become merely ceremonial additions to a technical college.

The challenge is not equality by sameness.

It is seriousness by purpose.

The Space Force's own professionalization effort makes another point that the Academy commission should absorb: names matter less than sequences.

A student could spend four years at a Space Academy and still need Space Force-specific training after commissioning. That is not duplication if the layers are designed intentionally.

Undergraduate education develops foundations.

Commissioning develops military identity and authority.

Initial skills training teaches the service's common professional language.

Unit qualification teaches a specific mission.

Advanced education develops people for later responsibility.

Wargames and exercises expose assumptions.

Experience supplies consequences.

The profession is the sequence, not any single school.

This observation should restrain the Academy's ambition.

New institutions naturally want to become indispensable. They add programs, claim territory and protect budgets. A healthy Space Academy would know what not to teach.

It would not need to replace unit qualification.

It would not need to duplicate classified operational training that belongs in the service.

It would not need to recreate every NASA technical course.

It would not need to become the sole source of graduate education.

It would not need to claim that its alumni are inherently more professional than Guardians commissioned elsewhere.

In fact, the integration of Academy graduates with officers from ROTC and Officer Training School should be treated as a design requirement rather than an afterthought.

A military service cannot afford an aristocracy of commissioning sources.

Service academies inevitably create strong alumni identities. Those networks can support mentorship and institutional memory. They can also create perceptions of insider status. A small Space Force may be especially sensitive to this because a comparatively small academy graduating a few hundred officers could become a large share of senior leadership over time.

The service should want graduates proud of the Academy and humble about it.

That attitude can be taught only if the institution says it explicitly and lives it in assignments, promotions and culture.

The civilian side faces a similar risk. If Space Academy graduates enter NASA or other agencies with a belief that they are the designated elite of the federal space enterprise, the Academy will damage the cross-institutional trust it was supposed to create.

A profession is not a club.

It is an obligation to standards larger than membership.

STARCOM's current system has one advantage on this point: it receives officers after multiple commissioning paths. The common course begins by acknowledging diversity of origin. Everyone needs the shared professional layer.

A Space Academy should preserve that lesson inside itself.

The institution could recruit students with radically different strengths. Some arrive as programmers. Others as builders. Others as writers, pilots, researchers or organizers. The common curriculum should not sand away those differences in pursuit of an idealized “space person.”

It should teach them how to bring a specialty into a team without allowing specialty to become blindness.

There is a wider strategic reason for this.

The United States' comparative advantage in space has often come from pluralism.

NASA centers developed different specialties. Universities pursued independent research. Military organizations built capabilities around distinct missions. Commercial companies tried competing architectures. Contractors accumulated deep engineering experience. Startups challenged assumptions established by incumbent firms. Allies contributed systems and expertise.

This can be inefficient.

It is also adaptive.

A single professional pipeline should not erase it.

The Academy's common culture must be strong enough to create trust and weak enough to preserve intellectual competition.

That balance is difficult in any elite institution. Students learn what professors reward. Cadets learn which opinions sound professional. Organizations develop phrases that signal belonging. Over time, consensus can become socially easier than dissent even when nobody orders it.

The solution is not a culture of permanent contrarianism.

It is to make evidence a form of loyalty.

A Guardian who identifies a flaw in an operational concept is serving the mission. An engineer who says the schedule is unrealistic is not being uncommitted. An analyst who lowers confidence when evidence weakens is doing the job. A student who challenges a fashionable assumption with better data should not have to choose between intellectual honesty and institutional belonging.

Professionalism is partly the ability to disappoint the room correctly.

The Space Force will need that quality as much as technical skill.

So will NASA.

So will any Academy trying to educate people for both.

The service's first years have already shown that professional identity is built through repeated institutional choices. Call members Guardians. Define values. Build doctrine. Create common training. Establish courses for later responsibilities. Organize commands around education and readiness. None of these steps is decisive alone.

Together they create a profession slowly.

The proposed Space Academy enters that process midway.

It should not pretend to be the beginning.

It should ask where beginning earlier helps.

Perhaps a four-year institution can create deeper technical intuition before STARCOM teaches operational application.

Perhaps it can build relationships between future Guardians and future civil servants that would otherwise never form.

Perhaps it can teach history and law before career incentives narrow attention.

Perhaps it can make commercial literacy normal rather than remedial.

Perhaps it can create leaders who understand from the start that space power is produced by networks of institutions rather than by one service alone.

Those are plausible advantages.

They are not guaranteed by the word academy.

The profession will be created in the details: who teaches, what students practice, what behavior earns status, what mistakes are studied, what dissent is tolerated, which communities are invited in and which graduates are treated as successful.

The Space Force discovered that creating a service was easier than completing a service.

The Academy will discover the same thing.

The executive order can create a commission.

Congress can create authorities.

Appropriations can create buildings.

Only repeated education, practice and correction can create professionals.

Guardians need a profession because satellites do not explain what should be done with them.

The Academy, if it earns a place in that profession, will do so by teaching people not merely how space systems work but how to exercise judgment when the systems, institutions and missions disagree.
