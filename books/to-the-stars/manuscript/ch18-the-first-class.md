# The First Class

There is no first class.

That is the most important fact about the United States Space Academy in September 2026.

There is a presidential order. There is a commission. There is a 120-day deadline. There are arguments about purpose, location, cost, duplication, military service, civilian service, curriculum and governance. There are politicians already imagining what the institution could mean for their states and cities. There are headlines that make the Academy sound more finished than it is.

There are no students.

No applicant can yet ask where to send a transcript.

No parent can calculate the service commitment.

No high-school senior knows whether a congressional nomination will be required.

No faculty member can read a tenure policy.

No accreditation body has evaluated a degree program.

No registrar has issued a course number.

No entering class has been told whether it will wear a uniform, several uniforms, or none.

The absence is useful because it forces the institution to be examined in the correct order.

The first class is not the ceremonial beginning of an academy.

It is the first systems test.

Every unresolved question that sounds abstract in a commission report eventually becomes personal when a student accepts an appointment. Governance determines who can discipline that student. Accreditation determines what the degree means. Service obligations determine what the student owes after graduation. Admissions rules determine who had a fair chance to enter. Security policy determines which laboratories and courses are accessible. Curriculum determines what the government has promised to teach. Housing, medical care, physical training, internships, faculty hiring and academic appeals all move from organizational diagrams into daily life.

Institutions can survive vague founding language.

Students cannot enroll in vague language.

The commission therefore has a choice about what kind of first class it wants to make possible.

It could recommend the grand version: authorize a permanent academy, select a site, acquire land, design buildings, hire a founding faculty, establish degree programs, build admissions machinery, secure accreditation and then open the doors when the institution is physically recognizable as an academy.

That is the intuitive sequence because architecture makes institutions legible.

A campus photographs well.

A cohort in borrowed classrooms does not.

History suggests borrowed classrooms may be the smarter beginning.

The United States Air Force Academy did not wait for the permanent campus in Colorado Springs before educating cadets. Its first class entered in 1955 at Lowry Air Force Base while the new site was still being built. The temporary start did not diminish the institution. It gave the institution time to become real before every physical decision was irreversible.

The precedent does not map perfectly onto the Space Academy. The Air Force Academy had a clearer military identity and a clearer commissioning destination. The Space Academy commission is being asked to consider an institution spanning military and civilian federal service, with NASA in the chair and multiple agencies at the table.

That is an argument for testing the model early, not for waiting longer.

A pilot first class could answer questions no white paper can settle.

Can civilian and military students share a common core without one group feeling that the school belongs to the other?

Can technically demanding majors coexist with serious leadership formation rather than being crowded out by it?

Can students move among NASA centers, Space Force installations, laboratories and industry placements without creating an administrative maze?

Can the Academy teach material that touches classified missions while preserving an academic environment broad enough for science, engineering and public policy?

Can faculty be recruited who are strong enough to compete with elite universities and experienced enough to understand the government missions graduates will enter?

Can a four-year institution remain current in a field where software, sensors, launch systems, satellite architectures, commercial business models and military doctrine can change during the time one class moves from freshman year to graduation?

A pilot would not answer all of those questions.

It would turn them into evidence.

The executive order itself points toward that possibility. The commission is instructed to identify administrative actions available under existing authority, including pilot programs, partnerships and preparatory initiatives. That clause may prove more important than the language about a permanent location.

It gives the founders an escape from the oldest institutional trap: building the monument before proving the method.

The first class could begin as a partnership rather than a campus.

That does not mean an online course with a federal logo.

A serious pilot could still be residential, selective and demanding. Existing federal facilities could provide temporary space. Partner universities could supply accredited coursework while the Academy develops its own authority. NASA centers could host laboratories and experiential rotations. Space Force organizations could provide operational exposure. Students destined for different service pathways could live and study together while retaining different legal statuses.

The arrangement would be administratively inelegant.

That may be an advantage.

A temporary system makes hidden assumptions visible.

If every course requires a different memorandum of understanding, the governance problem is not solved.

If civilian students cannot join operational exercises because of clearance rules, the common curriculum needs redesign.

If military students spend so much time on service-specific requirements that they no longer share an educational core with their civilian classmates, the theory of a national space profession may be weaker than the founders imagine.

If partner universities deliver the strongest technical education at lower cost, the Academy may discover that its permanent comparative advantage is not owning every classroom.

If students build unusually strong cross-agency relationships precisely because they move among institutions, the distributed model may deserve to survive.

The first class should be allowed to teach the founders what kind of school they are actually creating.

That requires humility uncommon in prestige projects.

Governments like to announce institutions in finished form. The name comes first. The seal follows. A building is promised. Leaders describe the future as if it has already been appropriated.

Education punishes that instinct because the product is not a building.

It is a person four years later.

The Space Academy will need to decide what promise it is making to that person.

Consider admissions.

A student entering a traditional service academy generally understands the bargain before arrival. The education is federally funded. The institution is military. The student is entering a system designed to produce commissioned officers. The obligations are substantial, but the category is familiar.

The Space Academy may be more complicated.

If one student intends to commission into the Space Force, another to become a NASA engineer, another to serve in a federal science agency and another to enter a different qualifying public-service role, their legal relationships with the institution could differ from the first day.

The Academy cannot defer that complexity until graduation.

Who pays tuition if a student leaves after two years?

Does a civilian student receive a salary, stipend or scholarship?

Is a military student already a member of the armed forces?

What medical standards apply?

Can students switch service tracks?

What happens if a student loses eligibility for a security clearance?

What happens if the government cannot place a civilian graduate in an appropriate qualifying job?

Can a graduate attend medical school, graduate engineering school or a doctoral program before satisfying the service obligation?

These questions are not administrative debris around the Academy's mission.

They define the bargain.

The first applicants deserve answers before they are asked to become symbols.

The recruiting challenge will be unusual because the Academy will compete against institutions whose reputations are already established.

A student with the mathematics and science preparation to thrive in a demanding space curriculum may have offers from universities with famous engineering departments, mature research laboratories, enormous alumni networks and few restrictions on post-graduation employment. A student drawn to military service may already consider the Air Force Academy, ROTC or another commissioning route. A student drawn to NASA may choose a traditional university and pursue internships, fellowships or civil-service hiring afterward.

The Space Academy cannot recruit excellent students merely by being new.

Novelty attracts attention.

It does not sustain a four-year commitment.

The first class will ask a rational question: what can I become here that I cannot become as well somewhere else?

The answer cannot simply be “a space professional.” Universities already educate aerospace engineers, physicists, computer scientists, roboticists, economists, lawyers and policy specialists. The federal government already trains military officers and civil servants. Industry already develops operators and managers with deep mission experience.

The Academy's answer has to be institutional.

You will learn across boundaries other schools treat as electives.

You will understand orbital mechanics and acquisition.

Software and command.

Engineering and public law.

Civil missions and military missions.

Technical risk and political responsibility.

You will spend enough time inside the organizations that use these disciplines to understand why a technically elegant answer can fail when it meets operations, budgets, law, security or human judgment.

That is a compelling promise.

It is also expensive to keep.

The first class will expose whether the Academy has built integration or merely a crowded course schedule.

There is a difference.

A curriculum can force students to take many subjects without teaching them how the subjects interact. A future propulsion engineer does not become strategically literate because one strategy course appears beside thermodynamics. A future Guardian does not become technically deep because an engineering survey appears beside military studies. A future NASA manager does not understand acquisition because a procurement lecture has been added to a leadership seminar.

Integration requires faculty who talk to one another, assignments that cross departmental boundaries, exercises in which technical choices create operational and legal consequences, and enough common language that students can argue productively across specialties.

That is harder to build than a list of requirements.

The first class should therefore be small enough for the faculty to learn from it.

This is not an argument for permanently small scale. A national workforce institution that graduates only a few dozen people a year may struggle to justify the fixed costs of a standalone federal academy. But the first cohort has a different purpose from the tenth.

It is not merely producing graduates.

It is debugging the institution.

A smaller pilot can reveal where advising fails, which laboratory requirements are unrealistic, how many students can obtain appropriate summer placements, what security procedures interfere with coursework, whether the physical-training model fits civilian students, how service-track switching works, and how much of the academic program can be delivered by partners without losing a common culture.

The Academy should resist the urge to solve prestige with selectivity alone.

Founding classes acquire mythology easily. Their members become firsts by definition. The first graduates will be invited back for anniversaries. Their photographs may sit in buildings that do not yet exist. Their careers will be treated as evidence about the school.

That attention can distort admissions.

The institution may be tempted to select applicants who already resemble a finished ideal: extraordinary grades, elite internships, obvious technical credentials, immaculate résumés.

A federal academy should certainly demand preparation.

It should also remember what academies are for.

They are not simply prizes for people who need the least development.

They are places where public investment turns promise into capability.

The first class should include students from across the country who have demonstrated the ability to master difficult material and the willingness to accept public obligation, not only students who found the space industry early because geography, family or school resources made it visible to them.

This is where recruiting matters as much as selection.

Space is geographically concentrated in public imagination. Houston. Cape Canaveral. Huntsville. Colorado Springs. Los Angeles. The Washington region. A handful of universities, bases, laboratories and companies dominate the story.

Talent is not concentrated so neatly.

A national academy that recruits only from communities already connected to aerospace will reproduce the existing network and call it merit.

The founders will need pathways into schools where “space career” is not an ordinary guidance-counselor category.

The first class will reveal whether they found them.

It will also reveal what the Academy means by physical discipline.

The executive order explicitly pairs technical education with leadership development and discipline. Military academies have elaborate systems of physical training, military instruction, hierarchy and conduct. Those systems are not ornamental. They are designed around the profession of arms.

A mixed Space Academy must decide which parts belong to every student.

A future Space Force officer plainly requires military formation.

A future civilian NASA engineer does not need to be trained as if preparing to command troops.

Yet an academy that separates the civilian cohort from every demanding form of shared discipline risks creating two schools occupying one address.

The common standard could be built around physical readiness, teamwork, expeditionary exercises, field problem-solving, emergency response, leadership under fatigue and responsibility for others without pretending all students have the same profession.

Space work supplies real reasons for this kind of formation.

Launch operations are unforgiving. Mission control depends on disciplined teams. Remote sites and test campaigns impose stress. Astronaut training is physically and psychologically demanding. Military space operations may occur in crisis while adversaries are actively trying to disrupt systems. Engineering failures do not care whether the person responding wears a uniform.

The Academy can demand resilience without manufacturing military sameness.

The distinction will be one of its earliest cultural tests.

Then there is secrecy.

The first class cannot be educated honestly about American space power while pretending classified work does not exist.

National-security space includes capabilities, vulnerabilities, collection systems, operational concepts and threat information that cannot all be taught in an ordinary classroom. Some students may hold clearances. Others may not. International students, if the Academy eventually admits them, would introduce additional restrictions. Civilian and military tracks may require different access.

The wrong response would be to classify the culture of the school.

The right response is harder: teach durable principles in the open, use controlled environments where genuinely necessary, and make classification itself part of the professional education.

Students should understand why governments protect information, how overclassification damages coordination, how secrecy affects scientific exchange, and how leaders make decisions when not everyone in the room can know the same facts.

That is not a side lesson.

It is one of the realities of the American space enterprise.

The first class will also tell the Academy whether faculty want to stay.

Founding a university is a labor-market problem disguised as a curriculum problem.

The institution will need mathematicians, physicists, engineers, computer scientists, political scientists, economists, historians, lawyers and leadership educators. It will need practitioners who understand mission operations, acquisition, intelligence, cyber, launch, satellite design and public administration. Some will come from federal service. Some from the military. Some from universities. Some perhaps from industry.

The strongest candidates will have alternatives.

An academic may be wary of federal personnel rules or political interference. A senior engineer may earn much more in industry. A military expert may rotate before a course sequence matures. A NASA practitioner may be superb in the field and inexperienced in teaching. A classified specialist may be unable to publish enough to fit normal academic incentives.

The first faculty model must account for those differences rather than assuming a federal name will summon a university.

A healthy Academy may need a permanent academic core surrounded by rotating practitioners, visiting scholars and joint appointments with other institutions.

That model could keep the curriculum alive.

It could also create a school where no one stays long enough to build culture.

Again, the first class provides evidence.

Which instructors become mentors?

Which courses survive their original designers?

Which practitioner rotations add urgency and which produce discontinuity?

Which partnerships broaden the school and which make students feel they are commuting among bureaucracies?

These questions cannot be answered before teaching begins.

The Academy's founders should plan to learn in public from them.

That requires a kind of institutional confidence different from promotional confidence.

Promotional confidence says the Academy will be world class.

Institutional confidence publishes the evidence that shows whether it is.

The first cohort should be measured aggressively.

Not with the crude logic that every educational outcome can be reduced to a dashboard, but with enough discipline to prevent founding mythology from substituting for performance.

How many students entered?

How many left, and why?

How strong were they in the common technical core when they arrived and when they graduated?

Did students from different service tracks actually work together?

Where did graduates serve?

Did receiving organizations consider them unusually prepared?

What did each graduate cost compared with alternatives?

How many remained in public service beyond the minimum obligation?

Which parts of the curriculum became obsolete before the class graduated?

Which experiences mattered enough to preserve?

Which could have been obtained more cheaply elsewhere?

The first class should not be protected from comparison because it is first.

It should be compared precisely because it is first.

There is another reason to begin with people rather than buildings.

The Academy's location may become the most politically visible decision the commission makes. A permanent campus can anchor jobs, contracts, research relationships and identity for generations. Cities will understandably want it.

But if the site is chosen before the educational operating model is tested, geography can lock in assumptions the pilot would have challenged.

Perhaps the school needs daily access to a NASA center.

Perhaps it needs proximity to Space Force operational units.

Perhaps the most important resource is a concentration of aerospace industry.

Perhaps it should deliberately sit apart from all three to build an independent culture.

Perhaps a distributed first phase will show that no single location can supply the experience the curriculum requires.

A site commission should know which of those statements is true before it values acreage.

The first class can help answer the question by moving through the system before the system has a permanent home.

This would make the cohort unusual in another way.

Its members would enter an institution that is still arguing about itself.

That is not necessarily a weakness.

West Point, Annapolis and the Air Force Academy now possess traditions so thick that a new cadet or midshipman enters a story already written by generations. The Space Academy will not have that luxury. Someone will have to decide which rituals matter, what the honor system means, how students address one another, which service distinctions are preserved, what symbols belong to the whole school and what traditions should never be borrowed simply because other academies have them.

The first class will participate in those decisions whether the administration intends it or not.

Culture emerges from repeated behavior faster than committees can write handbooks.

If civilian students are treated as guests in a military institution, they will notice.

If Guardians believe military requirements are diluted for the sake of a mixed identity, they will notice.

If engineers receive more prestige than operators, or astronauts become the glamorous center of a school whose graduates mostly do other work, students will learn the hierarchy.

If industry internships become more coveted than public-service assignments, the school's stated mission and revealed incentives may diverge.

The first class will learn what the Academy values from what it rewards.

That makes the founding cohort more than a student body.

It is a constitutional convention conducted through ordinary days.

Who eats together.

Who trains together.

Who studies together.

Who can enter which building.

Who is trusted with which responsibility.

Who gets selected for which summer experience.

Who receives praise when a team succeeds.

Which failures are punished and which are treated as learning.

These small decisions become institutional character before anyone writes them into history.

The founders should therefore avoid one final temptation: choosing the first class mainly for symbolic effect.

A new federal academy will inevitably be used to tell a national story. The administration will want visible proof that the project is moving. NASA will want evidence that the institution serves exploration and science. Space Force will want officers capable of warfighting. Members of Congress will want geographic representation. Host communities will want local connection. Industry will want access to talent. Universities will want partnerships.

The students cannot carry all of those political expectations at once.

Their first obligation is to learn.

The government's first obligation is to make that learning worth the service it asks in return.

If the Academy reaches the point where an entering class can make that bargain knowingly, much of the hard institutional work will already have been done.

A law will exist, or a legally durable pilot structure will.

Money will have been appropriated.

Admissions rules will be public.

Service obligations will be specific.

Courses will have instructors.

Degrees will have academic meaning.

Partner agencies will have agreed to receive students and graduates.

The distinction between military and civilian tracks will be operational rather than rhetorical.

The first class will still encounter mistakes.

Every first class does.

The relevant question is whether the institution has designed itself to correct them.

That is why the first real milestone of the Space Academy should not be a groundbreaking.

It should be the day a student can read the offer, understand the bargain and say yes.

Until then, the Academy is a proposal.

After that, it becomes responsible for a life.
