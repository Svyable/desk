# Space Cadets Before the Space Force

The Space Force had academy graduates before it had an academy.

On April 18, 2020, at a graduation ceremony altered by the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic, a group of U.S. Air Force Academy cadets received commissions into a military service that was not yet four months old. The Academy's later historical account records 86 members of the Class of 2020 entering the United States Space Force.

There was no United States Space Academy to send them there.

There did not need to be.

The Air Force Academy had been educating officers for space work for decades.

This fact is inconvenient for anyone who wants to tell the story of the new Academy as the moment the United States finally noticed that space required educated professionals. The country noticed a long time ago. It built departments, laboratories, courses, military units, graduate programs and career fields around the problem. The history of American military space is full of people who learned the domain inside institutions created for something else.

That is not evidence of failure.

It is evidence of adaptation.

The Air Force Academy's relationship with space reaches back to the early space age. Its curriculum developed astronautics as a serious field, and generations of cadets studied subjects that would become central to military space operations. Academy graduates became missile officers, engineers, space operators, acquisition leaders and astronauts. The institution that had been authorized when jet aviation and nuclear strategy dominated the imagination learned to educate officers for orbital systems too.

By the time the Space Force became independent in 2019, the educational foundation was already there.

So was the personnel pathway.

The Department of the Air Force houses both the Air Force and the Space Force. That administrative relationship matters. The Space Force did not emerge as a completely separate military society disconnected from the Air Force. It inherited organizations, personnel, bases, missions and professional practices from the service that had carried most American military space responsibilities for decades.

The Air Force Academy naturally became one of its commissioning sources.

The legal structure reflects that reality. Cadets at the Academy can be appointed upon graduation as officers of the Regular Air Force or the Space Force. The familiar service-academy bargain—a publicly funded education in exchange for an obligation of active service—can therefore send graduates directly into either service.

This is more than a bureaucratic detail.

It establishes the baseline against which the proposed United States Space Academy must be judged.

The country already has a federal academy capable of commissioning Guardians.

It already has technical programs relevant to space.

It already has cadets who enter the Space Force and then continue through the service's own professional training system.

Any new institution has to create value on top of that.

The strongest case for a Space Academy begins by conceding this completely.

USAFA is not the problem.

The question is whether the space profession has grown beyond what even an excellent shared academy can reasonably center.

To see the difference, consider what happens to a cadet's attention.

An Air Force Academy cadet lives inside an institution whose central obligation is to produce officers for the Department of the Air Force, overwhelmingly for the Air Force and in smaller numbers for the Space Force. The curriculum can include substantial space education. The cadet can major in astronautical engineering or another technical discipline. Space Force officers can mentor students. Space units and missions can appear throughout professional development.

But the institution must also prepare people for aviation, cyber, intelligence, logistics, acquisitions, engineering, special operations support and the enormous range of missions an air service performs.

Space is present.

It is not the whole organizing environment.

A dedicated Space Academy could invert that ratio.

The student interested in law would study law in the context of orbital activity. The economist would encounter launch markets, spectrum, insurance, procurement and industrial policy. The engineer would work beside students headed for operations, intelligence or civil agencies. The history curriculum could treat the development of rockets, reconnaissance, deterrence, NASA, commercial launch and international space law as central rather than elective.

That sounds attractive because specialization often creates depth.

It also raises a difficult question.

How early should a profession specialize?

An eighteen-year-old is not merely a future space professional. He or she is still discovering what kind of professional to become. A broad service academy can expose cadets to missions they did not know existed. A general university can allow a student to move from physics to computer science or from engineering to public policy. A narrow institution can create extraordinary focus while making exploration harder.

The ideal Space Academy would therefore have to be broad inside its specialization.

That sounds contradictory until one looks at what space has become.

Space is not a single technical field. It is a meeting place of many fields under unusual constraints.

A satellite depends on structures, materials, thermal engineering, power, communications, software and orbital mechanics. Its mission may depend on intelligence, weather, navigation, imaging or scientific instruments. Its operation may involve cyber defense, spectrum management, ground networks and data processing. Its acquisition may involve commercial providers, classified requirements, international partners and supply chains stretching across the world.

A student can specialize deeply and still remain inside space while changing disciplines.

This gives the proposed Academy a plausible academic breadth that a phrase like “space school” can obscure.

The harder part is professional breadth.

The executive order's list of intended roles extends beyond the military. This changes the admissions and identity problem profoundly. At USAFA, whatever a cadet's major, the institutional destination is clear: military service. The cadet is being formed as an officer. The service obligation binds the enterprise together.

At the proposed Space Academy, one student might become a Guardian and another a NASA engineer. A third might enter a civil agency. Another might become an astronaut later, after years of professional experience. The order even includes entrepreneurs in its vision of the broader professional corps, although how a federal service obligation would intersect with entrepreneurship remains something the commission must clarify.

What, then, would make all of them classmates in more than the administrative sense?

The answer cannot be “they like space.”

Institutions need stronger glue than enthusiasm.

A common obligation to public service could provide it.

So could common technical foundations, shared leadership education, ethics, law, history and repeated work on problems that require cross-disciplinary teams. But the identity would still differ from a traditional service academy. A cadet headed toward civilian service may not share every military demand placed on a future Guardian. A future military officer requires preparation for command, lawful orders, deployment, physical standards and the possibility of armed conflict. A future NASA scientist requires a different professional formation.

Trying to make both experiences identical would weaken both.

Separating them completely would weaken the reason for placing them in one academy.

This is the design challenge hiding behind the existing USAFA pipeline.

The proposed Academy is not needed to create the first space cadet.

It is being proposed to create a different kind of cohort.

The distinction becomes clearer after commissioning.

The Space Force has been building a common professional foundation for new officers regardless of how they received their commissions. A Guardian coming from the Air Force Academy, ROTC or Officer Training School enters the service with different educational and developmental experiences. The Officer Training Course created by Space Training and Readiness Command is designed to bring those new officers into a shared Space Force foundation.

The logic is powerful.

Commission broadly.

Professionalize specifically.

If that system works, the case for a separate undergraduate Academy weakens. The service can draw talent from many educational environments and then teach a common Guardian language after commissioning. Diversity of educational background becomes an asset rather than a problem.

This is the most serious institutional alternative to the Space Academy.

It deserves more attention than the simple objection that a new school costs money.

A distributed pipeline may be better suited to a fast-changing domain.

Universities compete. Students encounter different professors and research cultures. Some institutions lead in aerospace engineering; others in computer science, physics, robotics, public policy or law. ROTC and other federal programs can recruit from that diversity. The Space Force can then provide domain-specific formation after commissioning.

A single academy, by contrast, creates coherence at the cost of concentration.

Its faculty can become excellent, but excellence is housed in one institutional ecosystem. Its assumptions can spread efficiently because students share them. That is useful when the assumptions are good.

When they are wrong, coherence becomes a liability.

Military organizations know this problem well. A strong common culture allows people to coordinate under pressure. It can also make dissent harder and create shared blind spots. The service academies have always lived inside this tradeoff.

A Space Academy would too.

The question is whether the domain requires enough common formation early enough to justify the concentration.

USAFA's history provides another clue.

Its graduates have helped build the space profession without attending a space-only school because the Academy teaches something that transfers across domains: how to combine technical education with public obligation. The specific platform may change. The professional expectation survives.

This suggests that the best Space Academy curriculum may be less specialized than its name implies.

A student should know orbital mechanics, but also history.

Understand software, but also institutions.

Study engineering, but also ethics.

Know how a constellation works, but also how Congress funds it, how international law constrains it, how a commercial provider prices it and how an adversary may interpret its behavior.

The Space Academy's advantage, if it has one, would lie in integration rather than narrowness.

That brings the argument back to the people already passing through Colorado Springs.

By the Class of 2026, the Air Force Academy reported hundreds of its graduates had been commissioned into the Space Force since the service's creation. Each year added more. Some came from technical majors closely aligned with orbital systems. Others brought different academic backgrounds. Once in the service, they joined Guardians commissioned elsewhere and entered a common professional system still being built.

This is not a placeholder waiting for the real academy.

It is a functioning model.

A new Academy should not destroy it.

The United States may need both.

USAFA could continue commissioning Guardians, preserving institutional connection between air and space officers and providing the Space Force with graduates shaped by a broader Department of the Air Force environment. ROTC could continue drawing from universities across the country. Officer Training School could continue providing another route. Direct and specialized hiring could bring in experienced technical talent where appropriate. STARCOM could continue building common Guardian formation.

The Space Academy could then occupy a role none of them is designed to fill: deliberately mixing future military and civilian space professionals before their careers diverge.

That would make it less like a replacement for USAFA and more like a federal institution at the boundary between a service academy and a technical university.

Whether that hybrid is stable remains uncertain.

Institutional boundaries exist for reasons. Military students and civilian students have different obligations. Classified and open research have different norms. Federal agencies have different personnel systems. NASA and the Space Force answer to different chains of authority. A school designed to bridge all of them could become a place where boundaries are understood—or a place where no constituency feels fully served.

The first Guardians commissioned from USAFA did not have to solve this problem.

Their institutional identity was clear before their service identity changed.

They were Air Force Academy cadets becoming Space Force officers.

The proposed Space Academy reverses the sequence.

It asks the country to define a space-centered institutional identity before it has decided exactly which services and agencies its students will join.

That may be its greatest opportunity.

It may be its greatest vulnerability.

The difference depends on whether the commission treats existing pipelines as rivals or as evidence.

USAFA demonstrates that the United States can educate excellent Guardians without a separate Space Academy.

That fact should not kill the proposal.

It should discipline it.

A new Academy must answer the question USAFA's success makes unavoidable:

What can only happen—or can happen substantially better—when space becomes the center of the institution rather than one destination within it?

If the answer is merely more orbital-mechanics courses, the country already knows how to provide them.

If the answer is a new professional network spanning military, civil, scientific and commercial space, the institution has a more interesting reason to exist.

The first Space Force cadets came through an academy built for another service and proved that institutions can adapt.

The Space Academy will have to prove something harder.

That adaptation is no longer enough.
