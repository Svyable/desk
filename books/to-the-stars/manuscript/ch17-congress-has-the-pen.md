# Congress Has the Pen

The President could sign the order alone.

He cannot build the institution alone.

That distinction is visible inside Executive Order 14423 itself. The commission is told to recommend legislative actions necessary to implement the Space Academy. NASA is directed to carry out approved recommendations only after presidential approval and any necessary legislative action. The entire order is subject to applicable law and the availability of appropriations.

The language is bureaucratic because constitutional boundaries often are.

It means Congress is not a spectator.

A presidential commission can study, coordinate, pilot and recommend. Executive agencies can sometimes reorganize programs or use existing authorities. But a durable federal academy with degrees, service obligations, admissions rules, a permanent campus, recurring appropriations and possibly new statutory authorities will eventually encounter Congress in several forms.

Authorization.

Appropriation.

Oversight.

Appointments and nominations, depending on design.

The Space Academy may be announced in Houston.

Its long-term life will be written in Washington.

This is one reason the 120-day commission clock should not be confused with an opening date.

The report due in December 2026 can propose an institution. It can recommend which agency should control it, how graduates should serve, what legislation Congress should pass and how pilot programs could begin under existing authority.

A recommendation is not a statute.

It is a negotiating position.

Congress may accept it.

Congress may revise it.

Congress may fund only part of it.

Congress may attach conditions.

Congress may decide the Academy should be located or governed differently.

Congress may do nothing.

The possibility of inaction is not dramatic, but it is one of the most powerful forces in American institutional history. The federal government contains many ideas that received announcements, reports, commissions and endorsements without becoming permanent organizations. Durable institutions require coalitions capable of surviving the moment that created them.

The existing service academies have those coalitions.

Members of Congress nominate applicants, visit ceremonies, protect appropriations and follow the performance of institutions whose graduates enter public service. The academies also have host-state delegations, alumni networks, service leadership and local communities invested in their continuation.

A new Space Academy starts without those defenses.

Its first congressional challenge will be definitional.

What is Congress being asked to authorize?

If the commission recommends a traditional military service academy, lawmakers can draw on a familiar statutory structure. Congress has long legislated admissions, appointments, cadet status, service commitments and governance for military academies. The debate would still be difficult, but the category would be known.

If the commission recommends a NASA-led mixed civil-military academy, Congress may need to create a new category.

That could involve civilian student status alongside military cadets, multiple forms of service obligation, agency-specific hiring authorities, accreditation provisions, appropriations across departments and governance rules designed to preserve both NASA's civilian mission and Space Force's military requirements.

The novelty is not a reason to reject the idea.

It is a reason to write the law carefully.

Congress will also ask the question executive-branch commissions are often least eager to answer: compared with what?

A new federal academy will have opportunity costs.

Every dollar for land, construction, faculty and student support is a dollar unavailable for some other NASA, Space Force, defense or education priority unless Congress increases total spending. The comparison should not be framed only against doing nothing.

Compare the Academy with expanding Space Force pathways at the Air Force Academy.

Compare it with ROTC scholarships at technical universities.

Compare it with a federal scholarship-for-service program across dozens of campuses.

Compare it with a graduate institution or mid-career space college.

Compare it with expanded NASA internships and fellowships.

Compare it with partnerships that use existing universities and federal centers rather than a new campus.

A strong Academy proposal should survive those comparisons on purpose, not prestige.

This is where Senator John Hickenlooper's criticism becomes an early preview of congressional debate. His argument was blunt: the Air Force Academy already trains Space Force leaders, so a new academy risks duplication and cost. Colorado has institutional interests in that position. The substance will still appeal to legislators worried about federal spending or skeptical of creating a new bureaucracy.

Supporters need an answer measurable enough for appropriators.

What workforce gap exists?

How many graduates are needed each year?

What skills are existing programs failing to produce?

Why must those skills be created in one residential academy?

What is the expected cost per graduate?

What share will enter military service, NASA and other federal roles?

How long will they serve?

How many remain after the obligation ends?

What existing programs will be consolidated, expanded or left untouched?

A commission report that answers only with vision will make the congressional case harder.

The second challenge is money.

Academies have high fixed costs because they combine university, residential community, leadership-development system and, for military students, training infrastructure. Technical laboratories can add more. Secure facilities can add more. A permanent campus can turn land and architecture into a multibillion-dollar commitment before the institution has graduated anyone.

Congress can change the project's character through appropriations alone.

Fund a pilot cohort but not a campus, and the Academy begins as a partnership.

Fund planning money but delay construction, and the commission's design remains provisional.

Fund a permanent site aggressively, and the institution becomes much harder to reverse.

Fund NASA but not Space Force participation, or vice versa, and the balance of the school changes.

The budget is governance expressed numerically.

This is why “subject to the availability of appropriations” is not boilerplate the book can skim past.

It is the mechanism by which the legislative branch decides how much of the executive vision becomes physical.

The third challenge is committee jurisdiction.

A NASA-led academy touches civil space.

A Space Force pathway touches armed-services policy.

Federal education and accreditation raise other questions.

Merchant-marine-style service obligations could draw comparisons with transportation statutes.

Construction, public lands or regional development may engage still more interests.

The exact legislative path will depend on the commission's architecture. A simple military academy might fit existing armed-services structures more naturally. A genuinely cross-government school may require coordination among committees whose institutional perspectives mirror the silos the Academy is supposed to bridge.

Congress can make integration difficult because Congress itself is organized by jurisdiction.

That is not a flaw unique to this project. It is how specialization works in legislatures.

The Academy will need sponsors capable of translating across those jurisdictions.

The fourth challenge is accreditation.

The executive order explicitly asks the commission to recommend appropriate accreditations. Federal ownership does not eliminate academic standards. Existing service academies are accredited institutions whose engineering and other professional programs may also have discipline-specific accreditation.

A new school must decide whether it awards its own bachelor's degrees from the start or begins through partnerships with accredited universities while its institutional systems mature.

Congress may not dictate accreditation details, but legislators will care whether students enter a school whose degrees are recognized and whose academic governance can withstand political turnover.

This matters because the Academy will be born under a President whose personal support is central to the announcement.

A serious institution must be able to survive a future President who dislikes it.

Statutory clarity and independent accreditation can help separate the school from the administration that created it.

That is one of the paradoxes of presidential institution-building.

The founder needs enough control to begin.

The institution needs enough independence to outlive the founder.

Congress is often where that transition occurs.

Bipartisan durability matters for another reason.

A four-year academy cannot operate intelligently if every presidential election threatens to reverse its mission, governance or service tracks. Students need to know the bargain they accepted will still exist when they graduate. Faculty need confidence that programs can survive long enough to build. Accreditation requires institutional continuity. Agencies need to plan positions for cohorts years ahead.

That does not mean the Academy should be insulated from democratic control.

It means Congress should distinguish policy choices that can change with administrations from structural promises made to students.

Service obligations, degree authority, student status, governance and basic funding mechanisms should not depend entirely on executive preference once people have enrolled under them.

The fifth challenge is service obligation.

A military obligation can be written into appointment and commissioning law. A civilian federal obligation may require Congress to define qualifying service, enforcement, waivers, repayment, graduate education and what happens when appropriate jobs are unavailable.

If private-sector service ever counts, the law will need to explain why taxpayers are subsidizing graduates whose immediate employer is not the government.

Kings Point has decades of statutory experience with a domain-wide obligation. Space policymakers should study both what works and what enforcement problems have appeared there rather than assuming the phrase “service commitment” writes itself.

The sixth challenge is admissions.

Congressional nominations are not merely academy customs. They are embedded in the statutory machinery of several federal academies and create a direct political relationship between the institution and every state and district.

Lawmakers may want the Space Academy to use a similar system because it distributes appointments and gives Congress a role in access.

They may also decide a mixed civilian institution requires a different model.

The result will affect the Academy's political durability. Members of Congress tend to care about institutions their constituents can enter through visible local pathways.

A purely national admissions system may be academically simpler and politically weaker.

The seventh challenge is oversight.

New institutions make promises before they have data.

Congress should decide in advance what data it wants.

Cost per student.

Applicant pool.

Graduation rate.

Service placement.

Retention after obligation.

Geographic reach.

Faculty recruitment and turnover.

Partnership use.

Research output.

Space Force and NASA satisfaction with graduates.

Comparison with existing pathways.

The Academy should expect to justify itself through evidence rather than founding rhetoric.

That oversight should include an explicit counterfactual.

Congress should not ask only whether the Academy's graduates are good.

Elite institutions can produce impressive graduates while still being an inefficient way to create them.

Lawmakers should ask whether comparable students supported through USAFA, ROTC, civilian scholarships, NASA internships or university partnerships achieve similar outcomes at lower cost or greater scale.

The Academy's claim is not that excellent students become excellent professionals.

Its claim must be that the institution adds something distinctive enough to justify its fixed costs.

That comparison can be uncomfortable because it threatens prestige.

It is also the comparison responsible government owes the public.

One useful approach would be staged authorization.

Congress could authorize a pilot phase with a limited cohort, partnerships and temporary facilities, require independent evaluation, then decide whether to fund a permanent campus at scale.

This would not be a vote of no confidence.

It would reflect the way the Space Force says it wants to develop capabilities: test assumptions before committing to an architecture.

The Academy itself should welcome that discipline.

If a pilot reveals that mixed civil-military cohorts create unsolved governance problems, fix them before building dormitories around the model.

If students can obtain most of the promised benefit through partnerships with existing universities, redesign the permanent institution accordingly.

If the Academy produces extraordinary results, the evidence strengthens the case for expansion.

Pilot-first legislation would also reduce the political temptation to treat site selection as the first act.

A permanent campus creates facts on the ground.

A temporary academy creates facts about education.

Congress should prefer the second kind first.

There is a risk that staging becomes a method of killing the institution slowly. Temporary programs can be starved, leadership can change and opponents can point to small scale as evidence of irrelevance.

That is why milestones need to be explicit.

The Academy should know what outcomes trigger expansion and what failures trigger redesign.

Congress could require a sequence rather than a vague promise.

Authorize a founding cohort.

Require an implementation report before enrollment.

Require independent review after the first two academic years.

Set a ceiling on permanent-construction obligations until defined milestones are met.

Require agencies to document actual workforce demand before class size expands.

Require a later reauthorization point at which Congress considers performance data rather than founding projections.

A reauthorization requirement need not be a threat to abolish the school.

It can be a forcing function that prevents the experimental phase from becoming permanent by inertia.

The oversight body should also be chosen deliberately.

The Academy will produce its own assessments, and sponsoring agencies will have reasons to describe the project as successful. Congress may want independent analysis from organizations such as GAO, inspectors general, accreditation bodies or specially commissioned external reviewers, depending on the question.

No evaluator is perfectly neutral.

The value of outside review is that the people measuring success are not the same people whose careers depend on declaring success.

This matters most around cost.

Construction projects can make early estimates obsolete. Shared facilities can become dedicated facilities. Security requirements can expand. Faculty recruiting can require higher compensation or partnership costs than planners expected. A small pilot can hide expenses that appear only at scale.

Congress should require lifecycle estimates, not merely opening-day budgets.

The Academy should know what a graduate is expected to cost after construction, operations, faculty, student support and partner programs are included.

Then Congress can compare that number with alternatives.

Institution-building benefits from commitment.

It also benefits from exit ramps.

The commission's December report will therefore need two kinds of writing.

One kind will describe the Academy America wants.

The other will translate that desire into clauses Congress can enact and appropriators can fund.

Governance.

Appointments.

Service obligations.

Authorities.

Budget.

Land.

Accreditation.

Oversight.

Evaluation.

Reauthorization.

The second kind is less inspiring.

It is where institutions become real.

The President's signature created urgency.

Congress will decide durability.

That does not mean Congress will own the Academy's vision. It means a national institution must pass through a branch designed to represent competing constituencies, control public money and make laws that survive presidents.

If the Space Academy cannot survive that process, it was probably never an academy.

It was an initiative.
