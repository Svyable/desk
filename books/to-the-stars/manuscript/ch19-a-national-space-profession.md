# A National Space Profession

The United States has never lacked people who work in space.

It has lacked a single answer to what kind of profession they belong to.

An astronaut is easy to name.

A Guardian is easy to name.

An aerospace engineer is easy to name.

So is a launch director, satellite operator, intelligence analyst, procurement officer, planetary scientist, flight surgeon, software engineer, lawyer, regulator, program manager, entrepreneur or civil servant.

The difficulty begins when these people have to solve the same problem.

Modern space systems do not respect organizational charts.

A launch can involve a commercial provider, a federal range, military safety authorities, civil regulators, intelligence considerations, weather support, communications networks, contractors, insurers and a customer whose spacecraft may serve scientific, commercial or national-security purposes. A satellite can be designed by a private company, bought by the government, operated by military personnel, dependent on commercial ground infrastructure and useful to civilian users. A technical decision about resilience can become an acquisition decision, a budget decision, a diplomatic decision and, in crisis, a military decision.

The people involved may all be competent.

They may still speak different professional languages.

That is the strongest version of the case for a United States Space Academy.

Not that America has no universities capable of teaching orbital mechanics.

Not that the Space Force has no way to train Guardians.

Not that NASA cannot hire engineers.

The case is that the country may need a place designed around the interfaces.

Service academies create more than graduates. They create networks of people who share formative experiences, common references and assumptions about public duty. Professional schools do something similar. Law schools create a language for legal argument. Medical schools teach a way of seeing patients and evidence. Engineering programs create habits of approximation, verification and design. Military institutions create an understanding of command, responsibility and the legitimate use of force.

Space has all of those professions inside it.

What it does not yet clearly have is a broad professional formation that says: before you become a specialist in one part of this system, you should understand the obligations and constraints of the others.

That proposition sounds modest.

It is institutionally ambitious.

A profession is not created by declaring one.

It emerges when people develop standards for competence, shared ethical obligations, recognizable pathways into the work, methods for transmitting knowledge and a sense that membership carries responsibilities beyond employment.

The American space enterprise has pieces of that already.

NASA has a powerful institutional culture built around engineering discipline, mission assurance, scientific purpose and accumulated experience. The Space Force is intentionally building a Guardian identity and a profession of arms specific to the space domain. Commercial space companies have created their own cultures around speed, iteration, manufacturing, launch cadence and market competition. Universities train the technical disciplines on which all of them depend.

Those cultures overlap.

They are not interchangeable.

The Academy would fail if it tried to make them interchangeable.

The point of a national space profession would not be to teach a NASA engineer to think like a military officer or a Guardian to think like a venture-backed founder. It would be to make each capable of understanding why the others behave as they do, where their authorities begin and end, and what assumptions they bring to a shared mission.

That distinction matters because integration can become homogenization very quickly.

The civilian-military boundary is one example.

NASA's civilian character is not a historical inconvenience to be educated away. Space Force's military role is not simply another technical specialty. A profession that treats both as parts of one ecosystem must preserve the legal and ethical differences between them.

A common education should make those differences clearer.

A future NASA leader should understand why a military commander may need speed, secrecy and operational control under conditions that scientific institutions would find uncomfortable. A future Guardian should understand why civilian agencies protect openness, international scientific exchange and nonmilitary purposes. Both should understand the statutory boundaries that prevent coordination from turning into mission confusion.

That is professional literacy.

It is more useful than institutional imitation.

The same is true of government and industry.

The American space economy increasingly depends on companies that do not behave like traditional government programs. They can raise private capital, accept technical and financial risks differently, change designs quickly, sell the same capability to multiple customers and fail in ways a federal agency cannot.

Government has powers companies do not.

It can regulate, appropriate, classify, command military forces, negotiate treaties and spend for objectives that may never produce commercial returns.

A national space profession should teach both systems without romanticizing either.

The government is not inherently slow because public servants lack imagination.

Industry is not inherently efficient because markets exist.

Each contains incentives that produce characteristic strengths and failures.

A technically gifted graduate who understands only one set of incentives will make expensive mistakes at the boundary.

An acquisition officer may write requirements that accidentally destroy the advantages of a commercial product.

A company executive may misunderstand why a national-security customer cannot accept a normal commercial risk.

A civil servant may treat market concentration as somebody else's problem until a critical capability depends on one supplier.

A military operator may assume a service contract will remain available in conflict because it is available in peacetime.

An engineer may optimize a subsystem without understanding the legal or budget process that determines whether it can ever be fielded.

These are not failures of intelligence.

They are failures of professional context.

The Academy's opportunity would be to make context part of the core curriculum rather than something learned painfully after promotion.

That requires a different theory of undergraduate education.

Traditional universities are organized largely around disciplines. That structure has enormous value because deep knowledge requires concentration. The danger is that a student can graduate highly competent in one field and almost innocent of the institutions that turn technical work into national capability.

A Space Academy could require every graduate to understand a common set of problems before specialization.

How objects move in orbit.

How spacecraft are designed and tested.

How software controls physical systems.

How networks fail.

How uncertainty is measured.

How public money becomes programs.

How contracts distribute risk.

How military command differs from civilian administration.

How international law and treaties shape behavior in a global domain.

How scientific evidence is evaluated.

How intelligence is used without confusing probability with certainty.

How organizations learn after failure.

How leaders make decisions when technical experts disagree.

The list is too large for mastery.

That is precisely the point.

A common professional core should not pretend to create experts in everything. It should create people who know when they are not experts, know enough to ask the next good question and know which kind of expert must be in the room.

This may be the most important competence in a domain built from tightly coupled systems.

The Space Force's own training evolution points in this direction. Its Officer Training Course was designed to give newly commissioned Guardians exposure across operational, intelligence, cyber and acquisition functions before deeper specialization. The logic is visible in the phrase the service has used to describe its educational philosophy: Guardian first, specialist second.

A national academy would broaden the experiment.

Space professional first, perhaps, then Guardian, engineer, scientist, operator, acquirer, administrator or another specialty.

The phrase is appealing.

The implementation is dangerous.

“Generalist” can become a flattering word for insufficient depth.

Space systems are too unforgiving for shallow expertise. A person who knows a little orbital mechanics, a little software, a little strategy and a little acquisition is not automatically more useful than a first-rate engineer. The country does not need a new class of professional talkers who can translate across disciplines but cannot do difficult work inside any of them.

The Academy must therefore protect specialization fiercely.

A common core should be the floor.

A demanding major should remain the column.

The cross-domain experiences should connect them.

The professional identity should never become an excuse to reduce technical rigor.

This is one place where the first decades of West Point offer a useful historical echo without becoming a template. The academy produced Army officers, but its engineering education also helped supply a young country with technically trained people who built infrastructure and carried engineering habits into civilian life. The military mission and broader state capacity were connected because the nation had few institutions capable of producing that expertise at scale.

The United States of 2026 has no comparable shortage of universities.

It has the opposite condition: enormous educational capacity distributed across institutions that specialize well.

A Space Academy must therefore justify itself not by replacing that capacity but by connecting it to public purpose.

This suggests the institution should be unusually porous.

Students should not spend four years inside a federal enclave and emerge to discover the space enterprise they have been studying.

They should move through it.

A NASA-bound student should spend time with military operators and commercial teams.

A Guardian should encounter civilian science and commercial production, not as field trips but as serious work.

An engineering student should see acquisition decisions being made.

A policy student should work close enough to hardware to learn what an engineering margin feels like when it disappears.

All should experience operations where a mistake has consequences beyond a grade.

The Academy's campus, wherever it eventually stands, should function as a hub rather than a container.

This is one reason an argument over location can become too narrow.

No American city contains the whole space enterprise.

Houston has human-spaceflight history and Johnson Space Center.

Florida has launch infrastructure.

Colorado has a dense military-space community and the Air Force Academy.

Alabama has Marshall and deep engineering history.

California contains major aerospace companies, universities and government facilities.

The Washington region contains policy, budgeting, intelligence and acquisition institutions.

New Mexico, Virginia, Maryland and other states contain important pieces.

A school trying to create a national profession should exploit the geography rather than pretending one winning city possesses the domain.

The academic calendar could be designed around movement.

Semesters on campus.

Summer placements.

Operational exercises.

Research at federal laboratories.

Temporary assignments with partner agencies.

Industry work under carefully written public-purpose rules.

Joint projects with existing universities.

The logistical complexity would be considerable.

The educational return could be the Academy's reason to exist.

Networks matter too.

The graduates of service academies often carry durable relationships across decades. Those relationships can accelerate trust and coordination. They can also create insider cultures, alumni favoritism and institutional conformity.

A Space Academy would have to cultivate the first effect without pretending the second cannot happen.

A national space profession should broaden the network, not close it.

This matters especially because the American space workforce is much larger than any academy could educate.

Even a substantial graduating class would represent a small fraction of engineers, operators, scientists, technicians, military personnel and commercial workers in the field. If the Academy becomes a prestige gate that implies its graduates are the authentic national space elite, it will weaken the ecosystem it claims to strengthen.

Its graduates should be connectors, not aristocrats.

They should enter organizations understanding that most of the expertise they need will belong to people who attended other schools, entered through other pathways, enlisted rather than commissioned, learned in industry, arrived from allied countries or spent years accumulating experience no undergraduate program can manufacture.

The Academy's professional identity must therefore include epistemic modesty.

Know what you know.

Know what the system knows.

Know who knows what you do not.

This is not a slogan for a crest.

It is a survival habit in complex organizations.

The issue of enlisted Guardians makes the point sharply.

If the Academy is built mainly around undergraduate officer and civilian pathways, it will educate only one slice of the people who make space operations work. Enlisted Guardians can possess deep operational and technical expertise. Civilian technicians and long-serving federal employees often carry institutional memory that rotates past military officers. Contractor workforces may understand systems at a level program managers do not.

A national profession cannot define itself around bachelor's degrees alone.

The Academy could still be undergraduate at its core while serving the broader profession through mid-career courses, short technical programs, fellowships, joint exercises, research partnerships and continuing education. It could become a place where people return, not simply a place nineteen-year-olds enter.

That possibility may prove more important than the initial class size.

Professional institutions gain influence when they become sites of recurring conversation.

Military colleges bring officers back at different career stages. Medical institutions combine education, practice and research. Universities host conferences and laboratories that influence people who never enroll in a degree program.

A Space Academy designed only as a four-year pipeline could miss the faster-moving problem.

Space technology and doctrine will change after graduation.

The professional community needs places to relearn.

The Academy could help provide them.

That would also make it less vulnerable to the accusation that it duplicates the Air Force Academy. USAFA's primary mission is to educate and commission officers for the Air Force and Space Force. A Space Academy that simply commissions another set of Guardians would compete directly with an institution already doing the work.

A broader national institution could complement it.

USAFA could remain one of the strongest sources of Space Force officers. ROTC and OTS could continue. STARCOM could remain responsible for service-specific training and readiness. Universities could continue producing specialists. NASA could continue its internships and workforce programs.

The Space Academy would have to make the links among those systems more valuable than they are today.

That is a much harder mission to measure.

It is also the only mission broad enough to justify the order as written.

The commission's language about a professional corps is revealing. Corps can mean a formal body with defined membership. It can also mean a community organized around shared service.

The Academy should be careful with the first meaning.

Creating a formal national space corps with legal status would raise questions far beyond education. Who belongs? Who commands it? Does industry count? Does a NASA scientist share professional obligations with a military operator? What happens to the identities of existing agencies and services?

The second meaning is more plausible.

A corps of people whose different jobs share a common grounding in public purpose, technical seriousness and an understanding of the space domain.

That is less dramatic than a new uniform.

It may be more durable.

Professions often become visible when they develop ethical boundaries.

Space will need them.

The domain is becoming commercially valuable and strategically contested at the same time. Decisions about debris, interference, proximity operations, spectrum, surveillance, dual-use technology, autonomous systems, cyber operations and the use of force may carry consequences far beyond the organization making them.

Technical capability will often outrun settled norms.

A national space professional should be educated to recognize that “can” and “should” are different questions.

That does not mean teaching a single approved morality for every future dispute.

It means teaching students how to identify competing obligations.

Duty to mission.

Duty to law.

Duty to public safety.

Duty to scientific truth.

Duty to protect classified information.

Duty to disclose risk.

Duty to allies and partners.

Duty to subordinates.

Duty to the taxpayer.

Duty, in military service, to lawful command.

These obligations can conflict.

Professional education should prepare graduates for the conflict rather than pretending values arrive in perfect alignment.

The Academy's most important classroom may therefore be one where no technical answer resolves the case.

A spacecraft can be saved only by consuming a resource another mission needs.

A commercial partner has data the government wants but did not contract to receive.

An operational advantage depends on a capability whose use may establish a norm the United States later regrets.

A program is technically feasible but budget assumptions are implausible.

A safety concern is serious but uncertain and schedule pressure is intense.

An intelligence assessment is alarming but incomplete.

Students should practice making decisions where every option has a cost.

That is what professionals are ultimately paid to do.

The Academy will know whether it has created a national profession only years after graduation.

The evidence will not be a motto.

It will appear in organizations.

Do graduates translate effectively across civil and military institutions?

Do they make acquisition programs more realistic?

Do commanders trust technically trained officers without discovering that they lack operational judgment?

Do NASA managers value graduates' understanding of security without seeing the agency's civilian culture eroded?

Do industry partners find them capable of working at commercial pace without treating public obligations as friction to be routed around?

Do graduates remain in public service?

Do they recruit others?

Do they develop new practices that spread beyond their alumni network?

Do people who never attended the Academy still benefit from its research, courses and professional standards?

If the answer becomes yes, the Academy may matter far beyond the number of diplomas it awards.

If the answer becomes no, the school may still be excellent in ordinary academic terms and fail its national purpose.

This distinction protects the argument from prestige.

America does not need another excellent university simply because space is important.

It has excellent universities.

It does not need another military school simply because the Space Force is new.

It has military schools.

It needs a new academy only if a new kind of professional formation is necessary.

That proposition remains unproved.

The commission has been given the chance to test it.

The first students, if they come, will begin the longer test.

And the nation should judge the institution not by whether its graduates become a recognizable elite, but by whether people in different parts of the American space enterprise become better at working together without forgetting why their differences matter.
