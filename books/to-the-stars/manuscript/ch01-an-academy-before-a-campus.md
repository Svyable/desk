# An Academy Before a Campus

The first thing to understand about the United States Space Academy is that, on September 3, 2026, it was not yet an academy.

It had no cadets.

It had no professors.

It had no admissions office, dormitories, classrooms, honor code, football team, accreditation, commencement ceremony or permanent address.

It had an executive order and a commission.

This may sound like a lawyer's distinction, the kind of technical caveat attached to a government announcement after the interesting part is over. It is actually the interesting part.

The United States had decided to design an institution before deciding what the institution would be. That is not unusual in government. Congress and presidents frequently establish commissions precisely because an idea is politically mature enough to deserve action but institutionally immature enough that the details still matter. What made the Space Academy unusual was the size of the implied ambition.

A federal academy is difficult to improvise.

It must decide whom it serves before it can decide whom it admits. It must know what kind of graduate it is trying to produce before it can build a curriculum. If graduates owe the government service, somebody has to define the obligation and the consequences of failing to complete it. If students will become military officers, the institution must connect education to commissioning authority, medical standards, military training and the personnel systems of an armed service. If students will become civilian federal employees, a different set of statutes, hiring systems and career paths applies.

Then there are the ordinary things that stop being ordinary at federal scale.

Land.

Buildings.

Faculty.

Laboratories.

Security.

Classified instruction.

Student housing.

Athletics, if there are athletics. Flight or simulator facilities, if those become part of the model. Partnerships with NASA centers, military bases, commercial companies and universities. Accreditation. Research rules. Intellectual property. Procurement. Cybersecurity. Medical care. Student discipline. A library that knows what to do with both open scholarship and restricted information.

The executive order signed on August 28 did not pretend those questions had been answered. It turned them into an assignment.

The Presidential Commission on the United States Space Academy was instructed to submit recommendations within 120 days. The NASA Administrator was made chair. The Assistant to the President for Science and Technology and the Assistant to the President for Economic Policy were made vice chairs. The NASA Deputy Administrator became executive director. Other members were drawn from departments and offices whose presence said something about the problem before the commission wrote a single page.

Defense mattered.

Education mattered.

Labor mattered.

Economic policy mattered.

Science and technology mattered.

NASA mattered enough to sit at the center.

The proposed academy was therefore born with a broader constituency than a traditional military service school. The order described a professional corps that could include astronauts, scientists, engineers, operators, entrepreneurs, civil servants and warfighters. It contemplated service obligations in the Armed Forces or civilian federal service. It asked the commission to consider whether the Academy should be established within NASA, not simply transferred to the Department of the Air Force as an obvious new commissioning source for the Space Force.

That architecture makes the name slightly deceptive.

“United States Space Academy” sounds like a finished category. The words arrive already dressed in tradition. United States Military Academy. United States Naval Academy. United States Air Force Academy. Add space to the sequence and the mind supplies the rest: cadets in formation, severe architecture, technical degrees, military uniforms, graduates receiving commissions.

Perhaps some of that will happen.

But the order leaves room for something more hybrid.

That matters because the problem the institution is supposed to solve is itself hybrid.

The United States does not conduct space activity through one organization. NASA explores and conducts science. The Department of Defense uses space for military missions. The intelligence community operates systems whose details are often classified. The Department of Commerce has growing responsibilities in civil space traffic coordination. The Federal Aviation Administration regulates commercial launch and reentry. The Federal Communications Commission allocates spectrum and licenses satellite systems. NOAA operates environmental satellites. Private companies launch rockets, manufacture spacecraft, sell imagery, operate communications networks and increasingly provide services directly to the government.

No single career pipeline naturally covers all of that.

Neither does a single degree.

An electrical engineer can understand a communications payload and know little about orbital law. A political scientist can understand deterrence and know little about thermal limits. An acquisitions officer can understand contracts and still be unable to distinguish a technically credible schedule from an optimistic one. A brilliant software engineer can write flight code without understanding how a military commander will use the system. A space operator can know the mechanics of a mission and still misunderstand the incentives of the commercial company supplying part of it.

The problem is not ignorance.

The problem is interfaces.

Modern institutions fail at interfaces all the time. The people on each side can be competent while the system between them is brittle. Engineers optimize for technical performance while budget officials optimize for cost. Operators demand resilience while acquisition teams inherit a schedule built years earlier. Civilian regulators try to preserve safety and access while companies race to launch. Military planners want assured capabilities while commercial providers want customers across markets and countries.

The Space Academy proposal can be read as an attempt to create people who understand more than one side of those interfaces.

That is an attractive idea.

It is also where the danger begins.

Every educational institution that tries to teach everything eventually discovers that time remains finite. Four years is four years. A student cannot become a first-rate orbital dynamicist, cyber operator, intelligence analyst, acquisition professional, military leader, space lawyer and entrepreneur simultaneously. Broad education can create fluency. It cannot repeal specialization.

So the commission's curriculum question is really a question about what graduates should share before they divide into specialties.

That is the same question every service academy has faced in a different form.

West Point did not need every graduate to become the same kind of officer. Annapolis did not assume every midshipman would spend a career doing identical work. The Air Force Academy did not produce only pilots. Their institutional value came partly from a common formation laid beneath later specialization: a shared intellectual base, shared obligations, shared professional standards and a shared story about service.

The Space Academy would have to identify its equivalent.

Orbital mechanics is an obvious candidate, but perhaps too narrow. So is engineering. Space is increasingly a software domain. It is an intelligence domain. It is a legal domain, an economic domain and a domain of public policy. It is also physical in unforgiving ways. A software update cannot change the fact that an object in orbit has momentum, heat, power limits, communication geometry and a finite ability to maneuver.

A useful common education would have to teach both kinds of reality.

Code and physics.

Markets and missions.

Law and power.

Technology and institutions.

And then, because this is an academy tied to public service, judgment.

Judgment is harder to put in a catalog.

A student can pass a course in space law and still make a poor decision under uncertainty. A cadet can memorize doctrine and fail to recognize when a commercial service changes the operational picture. A scientist can know a field deeply and underestimate how political pressure affects a program. A military officer can understand a threat and overestimate the wisdom of reacting to it.

Professional schools exist partly because knowledge alone does not create a profession.

Repeated practice under standards does.

That is why the proposed service obligation matters so much. If the Academy becomes merely a prestigious federal technical university whose graduates are free to leave immediately for the highest-paying private employer, the public investment would have a different character. If every graduate owes a fixed military term, the school becomes much closer to a conventional service academy and may narrow its civilian purpose. If obligations vary by track, the institution must find a way to preserve common identity across students headed toward very different forms of service.

The executive order leaves this open.

It asks the commission to recommend the obligations.

This is a small phrase with enormous consequences.

The traditional service-academy bargain is understandable. The public pays for an expensive education and military formation. The graduate accepts years of service. The exchange is explicit enough that applicants can decide whether they want it.

A hybrid Space Academy complicates the bargain.

What is four years of NASA engineering service equivalent to in relation to four years of active-duty military service? Should a graduate joining a civilian agency owe the same number of years as a Guardian? What happens if the government has no suitable position available when the student graduates? Can a graduate serve at the Department of Commerce, NOAA, the FAA or another agency? Does working for a federal laboratory count? What about a company performing national-security space work under contract?

The order points toward federal service, not merely participation in the broader economy. But the edges will matter.

So will admissions.

The existing service academies use nomination systems rooted in American political history. Members of Congress, the Vice President and certain military authorities can nominate candidates. Applicants pass through academic, medical and physical standards. The system is selective, geographically distributed and deeply familiar.

A NASA-led academy could copy much of it.

It could also decide that copying it would defeat the purpose.

Perhaps the country needs an admissions system optimized for rare technical talent. Perhaps it needs older students, transfer students or people who have already demonstrated ability in laboratories and competitions. Perhaps it needs a national scholarship network rather than one residential campus. Perhaps future astronauts and future acquisition officers should not enter through the same door at eighteen.

These are not implementation details. They determine who the institution will become.

The location question works the same way.

A campus near Johnson Space Center would carry obvious symbolic and practical advantages. So would proximity to Space Force installations, launch ranges, research universities or commercial clusters elsewhere. The Air Force Academy's own history shows how quickly the selection of a national academy becomes a geographic competition. A permanent site can shape faculty recruitment, partnerships, institutional identity and political support for generations.

A commission asked to recommend a selection process is therefore being asked to design politics as well as real estate.

Then there is Congress.

Executive orders can organize the executive branch and direct planning within presidential authority. A durable federal academy with appropriations, service obligations, commissioning arrangements, property, governance and perhaps new statutory authorities is likely to require legislation. The order itself recognizes that implementation may require legislative action and must depend on available appropriations.

This is another reason to resist speaking about the Academy as though opening day is simply waiting on a construction schedule.

The commission's report is a beginning.

Congress can agree, disagree, redesign, delay, fund partially, attach conditions or decline to act. Agencies can fight over jurisdiction. Existing academies can argue that expansion is more efficient than duplication. States can compete for the campus. Universities can propose partnerships. Industry can offer facilities and training. Budget officials can ask what problem the institution solves that existing programs do not.

A serious proposal has to survive all of them.

This is where the comparison with the Space Force becomes useful.

The service itself took years of debate before it became law. The strategic concern preceded the institution. Military space organizations already existed inside the Air Force and elsewhere. The argument was not whether the United States did military things in space. It plainly did. The argument was whether those missions had become important and distinct enough to deserve their own armed service.

The Space Academy begins with the same kind of institutional question.

The United States already educates people for space.

It does so very well in many places.

The question is whether those places add up to what the country now needs.

The answer cannot be found by counting aerospace degrees alone.

The GAO workforce findings provide one reason for urgency but also a warning against easy solutions. A service that has filled about 75 percent of the positions it says it needs plainly has a personnel problem. Yet GAO also found weaknesses in the service's own workforce planning and requirements process. If an organization is unsure that its stated requirements are current and well founded, increasing the supply of new entrants may solve the wrong equation.

The Academy must therefore justify itself against alternatives.

Expand the Air Force Academy's space programs.

Increase Space Force commissioning from USAFA, ROTC and Officer Training School.

Fund more scholarships at universities with strong aerospace, computer science, physics and engineering programs.

Create a federal space scholarship with a civilian service obligation.

Build a graduate institute instead of an undergraduate academy.

Use existing NASA centers as a distributed educational network.

Expand STARCOM.

Create a consortium among universities, service academies and industry.

Recruit experienced technical professionals later in life rather than trying to predict them at seventeen.

A new Academy is not serious until it can explain why these approaches are insufficient or why it complements them rather than replacing them.

That is the healthy effect of designing an institution before pouring concrete.

For a brief period, architecture remains metaphorical.

You can still ask what the building is for.

The danger comes when a proposal develops momentum faster than its purpose develops precision. Once a city expects jobs, a state expects investment, an agency expects authority and contractors expect construction, the institution acquires defenders before it acquires evidence. The campus can become the objective.

The Space Academy has an opportunity to reverse that sequence.

Start with the profession.

Decide which public problems require people trained differently from the people existing systems already produce.

Decide which parts of their formation must happen together.

Decide what they owe the public in return.

Decide which existing institutions should keep doing what they already do well.

Only then decide what kind of academy follows.

The executive order, perhaps unintentionally, created exactly that window. Its nine recommendation areas force the commission to confront the structure before the structure hardens. Governance, curriculum, obligations, prerequisites, location, administrative action, legislation, coordination and implementation are separate questions, but they are not independent.

Change one and the others move.

Put the school inside NASA and military commissioning becomes a governance problem.

Make it a Space Force academy and the civilian mission becomes harder to preserve.

Require every student to hold or qualify for a security clearance and the admissions pool changes.

Build the curriculum around classified missions and academic openness changes.

Make service obligations flexible and institutional cohesion may become harder.

Choose a remote campus and industry access changes.

Choose a major commercial hub and military culture changes.

Make the school small and elite and it may shape culture without solving workforce volume.

Make it large and it may create graduates faster than agencies can absorb them.

Institutional design is the art of discovering that every clean answer has a second-order effect.

The United States had 120 days to begin discovering them.

The calendar made the task sound like a deadline. It was really an invitation to define the problem correctly.

The most important product of the commission would not be a map with a star on it.

It would be a sentence.

A sentence precise enough to explain why this Academy should exist when so many institutions already teach Americans how to reach, use and defend space.

Without that sentence, a campus would be expensive scenery.

With it, the Academy might become something rarer: an institution designed around the world that arrived before the institution did.
