# The Academy Without Walls

The permanent location may become the easiest part of the Space Academy to understand.

A place can be pointed to on a map.

A building can be photographed.

A congressional delegation can announce jobs.

A student can imagine arriving there.

The educational problem the Academy is supposed to solve is not located in one place.

The American space enterprise is distributed by design and by history.

NASA's centers developed around different missions and technical capabilities. Military space organizations occupy bases and commands shaped by operational needs. Launch ranges sit where geography permits launch. Universities have built specialized departments and laboratories over decades. Commercial companies cluster around talent, capital, customers and infrastructure. Policy and budgeting concentrate in Washington while spacecraft are built and flown elsewhere.

No permanent campus can contain the domain.

The Academy can respond in two ways.

It can treat the outside world as a set of destinations students visit from the real school.

Or it can treat the network as part of the school.

The executive order quietly leaves room for the second possibility. The commission is directed to consider pilot programs, partnerships, preparatory initiatives and coordination with existing federal education programs. Those provisions are easy to read as temporary mechanisms used until a campus is complete.

They may deserve to become permanent architecture.

A networked academy would not mean a weak institution assembled from borrowed courses.

It would mean deciding deliberately which capabilities must be owned and which should be accessed.

Universities make this decision constantly. No institution builds every research instrument its faculty may need. Scientists use observatories, supercomputers, ships, accelerators, archives and field sites far from their home campus. Medical education depends on hospitals and clinics because patients do not belong inside lecture halls. Military education uses ranges, units and exercises because war cannot be understood through seminars alone.

Space education should be similarly honest about where the work lives.

A student studying launch operations can learn equations anywhere.

Understanding a launch range requires seeing how weather, scheduling, safety, airspace, maritime activity, telemetry, public authorities, commercial customers and physical infrastructure converge around a narrow window in time.

A student studying human spaceflight can read about mission control.

Understanding its culture means seeing teams rehearse, hand over shifts, manage anomalies and distinguish confidence from evidence.

A student studying military space can learn doctrine on campus.

Understanding operational readiness means observing how units train, qualify, exercise and respond to adversary behavior.

A student studying acquisition can read the Federal Acquisition Regulation.

Understanding acquisition means watching a requirement become a solicitation, a negotiation, a contract, a program and eventually hardware or software that operators must live with.

The Academy's distinctive curriculum could therefore be built partly from movement.

Not travel for its own sake.

Sequenced exposure.

Learn a principle.

See it in an institution.

Return to analyze the gap between theory and practice.

Apply it in a project.

Move to a different institution where the same principle appears under different constraints.

This would make the United States itself part of the laboratory.

It would also reduce one of the largest risks in a new academy: building expensive facilities before knowing which facilities are educationally essential.

A propulsion laboratory may be central to one degree program and unnecessary for another. Secure spaces may be needed for some national-security instruction but not for most of the curriculum. High-end test facilities may already exist at federal centers, military ranges and partner universities. Duplicating them on campus could consume money better spent on access, faculty and students.

Prestige pushes the other direction.

Institutions like to own visible capability.

A named laboratory signals seriousness. A new building creates a constituency. Dedicated equipment is easier to schedule than shared equipment. Donors and appropriators can see what they bought.

A networked Academy would have to defend the proposition that access can sometimes be more valuable than ownership.

That is a healthy discipline for students who may later manage public programs.

The same logic applies to faculty.

A new university cannot instantly recreate the concentration of expertise that mature institutions have built over generations. It can recruit aggressively, but every professor hired away from another public institution or university may simply move national capacity rather than create it.

Joint appointments could change the arithmetic.

A NASA engineer might teach one course while remaining connected to a center.

A Space Force practitioner could rotate through the Academy without leaving operational work permanently.

A university professor could supervise research across institutions.

A commercial expert could teach under conflict-of-interest rules.

Students would encounter people whose authority comes from current work rather than only academic title.

This model creates problems of its own.

Faculty need time to prepare, mentor and build coherent courses. Practitioners with demanding jobs can become unreliable instructors. Joint appointments can divide loyalty and attention. A school with too many visitors can lack a stable intellectual center.

The Academy would still need a permanent core.

The network should expand a university, not prevent one from forming.

That distinction is important because culture cannot be outsourced easily.

Students need a place where the common professional language is reinforced every day. They need faculty who know them across years. They need peers from different service tracks living through the same academic sequence. They need an institution capable of saying no when partner agencies or companies want the curriculum bent toward immediate workforce needs.

A campus can protect those functions.

It does not need to perform every function itself.

The Air Force Academy's temporary beginning at Lowry offers one historical reminder. A permanent institution can begin before the final architecture is finished. The Space Academy's circumstances invite a more radical question: what if some temporary features turn out to be better than the planned permanent ones?

Suppose a pilot cohort takes accredited foundational courses through a partner university, spends technical rotations at NASA centers, trains with Space Force organizations, completes industry placements and returns to a small federal residential hub for common seminars and leadership development.

If that model produces the integration the Academy is supposed to create, the commission should not abandon it merely because the permanent campus opens.

The permanent school could be designed around mobility from the beginning.

This would also protect against regional capture.

Every campus creates a local ecosystem. That can be productive, but it can also narrow perspective. A Space Academy in Houston might naturally orient toward human spaceflight and the institutions nearby. A Colorado location might absorb the culture of military space and USAFA. A Florida location would live close to launch. Huntsville would bring deep engineering and program history. California would bring a different commercial and technical network.

There is no neutral location.

A networked curriculum can make location less destiny.

Students should learn the whole system even if they sleep most nights in one state.

The Academy could make geographic rotation a requirement rather than an enrichment option.

Every student might experience a launch range.

A NASA center.

A military operational environment, with appropriate access.

A commercial production or operations site.

A policy or acquisition organization.

A research university or federal laboratory outside the home region.

The details would depend on service track and major, but the principle would be common: no graduate should mistake the campus for the domain.

This approach would also broaden recruiting.

Partnerships with universities, community colleges, technical schools and federal programs could create entry ramps for students who are not ready for direct admission at eighteen or who discover space careers later. Summer programs could identify talent. Transfer agreements could create alternative routes. Scholarships could support students at partner institutions before they join Academy programs.

A national academy should not confuse exclusivity with reach.

The wider network could become one of its most important public goods.

That raises a governance problem.

Networks are easy to praise and hard to control.

Who owns the curriculum when courses are taught by multiple institutions?

Who evaluates faculty?

Who handles student discipline during a rotation?

Who owns intellectual property from joint research?

Which cybersecurity rules apply when students move among systems?

How are clearances recognized?

What happens when a partner institution changes leadership or budget priorities?

Can a commercial partner terminate an internship pipeline abruptly?

What standards determine whether a partnership continues?

A networked Academy would need unusually strong agreements and a strong academic center capable of maintaining coherence.

This is not bureaucracy to be wished away.

It is the cost of sharing capability.

The Academy should teach students to see that tradeoff too.

Interoperability is never free.

Shared systems require standards, governance and negotiation. The education system itself can model the kind of integration graduates will later manage.

There is another institutional advantage to a network.

It is easier to change.

If a technology or mission area becomes more important, the Academy can add a partnership faster than it can construct a new building. If a program becomes obsolete, it can end a rotation without leaving an empty facility. If a region develops a new concentration of expertise, students can go there.

The network becomes a hedge against technological obsolescence.

This matters because the Academy will face pressure to become physically complete.

Founders understandably want to finish things. A university with temporary facilities and distributed programs can feel provisional. Political leaders prefer ribbon cuttings to memoranda of agreement. Students may prefer a campus with every resource immediately available.

But space itself is becoming more networked.

Satellite constellations distribute capability across many vehicles. Military planners emphasize resilience through architectures that do not depend on a single exquisite node. Commercial companies use distributed supply chains and ground infrastructure. Scientific collaborations share instruments and data.

The Academy does not need to imitate an orbital architecture as metaphor.

It can learn the underlying institutional lesson.

Concentration creates efficiency and identity.

Distribution creates reach and resilience.

A strong design uses both deliberately.

The permanent campus should contain what gives the Academy its soul.

The shared core.

The residential community.

The faculty center.

The places where students from different tracks encounter one another repeatedly.

The laboratories and secure facilities that genuinely need to belong to the school.

The archive of institutional memory.

The rest can remain connected to the places that already do the work best.

This would make the Academy harder to describe in a rendering.

It might make it easier to justify in evidence.

The commission has been asked to recommend a permanent physical location.

It should.

A school needs an address.

It should also decide how much of the Academy is supposed to stay away from it.
