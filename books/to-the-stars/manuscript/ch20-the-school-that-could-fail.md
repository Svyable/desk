# The School That Could Fail

The United States Space Academy could fail.

That sentence should remain true even if the school opens, receives a handsome campus, attracts exceptional students and survives long enough to develop traditions.

Institutions can persist after their purpose has become unclear.

They can produce competent graduates while solving the wrong problem.

They can become politically durable before they become demonstrably useful.

They can acquire buildings, alumni, appropriations and constituencies that make the original question difficult to ask.

Was this the best way to strengthen the American space profession?

A serious founding argument has to leave room for the answer to be no.

The Space Academy begins with advantages that can disguise weakness.

Space is popular.

National-security concerns are real.

NASA carries extraordinary symbolic power.

The Space Force is young enough that institutional experimentation can sound like destiny.

Commercial space has produced visible technical achievement and large private fortunes. Human exploration still supplies some of the strongest images the federal government can put on a screen. Politicians understand the attraction of a school whose name contains both *United States* and *Space*.

Those advantages can help a worthy institution begin.

They can also help a mediocre institution avoid scrutiny.

The first failure mode is the simplest.

Duplication.

The Air Force Academy already commissions Guardians. ROTC and Officer Training School already provide additional commissioning paths. STARCOM already trains newly commissioned Guardian officers and develops service-specific education, doctrine, exercises and readiness. NASA already recruits scientists and engineers from universities across the country. Federal scholarship and fellowship programs already connect education to public service. American universities already contain world-class programs in aerospace engineering, computer science, physics, robotics, remote sensing, public policy and other fields the Academy is likely to teach.

If the Space Academy reproduces those functions under a new seal, it will have failed even if the reproduction is competent.

Duplication is not proved by the existence of overlap.

Every institution overlaps with others.

The question is whether the new institution creates enough additional value to justify its fixed costs and the opportunity costs imposed on programs that already work.

The Academy should therefore be forced to answer a counterfactual throughout its life.

What would happen if this school did not exist?

Would the Space Force simply commission the same officers through USAFA, ROTC and OTS?

Would NASA hire the same engineers from existing universities?

Would partnerships among agencies and companies continue to form through internships, fellowships and ordinary employment?

Could the government produce the same cross-domain literacy by paying for scholarships at a network of universities and bringing students together for summers, exercises and federal rotations?

Could an existing service academy create a Space Academy program inside itself at lower cost?

Could a graduate school or mid-career institution solve the coordination problem more effectively than a four-year undergraduate academy?

The institution should never be allowed to compare itself only with absence.

It must compare itself with alternatives.

The second failure mode is mission confusion.

The executive order's breadth is intellectually attractive. It is also dangerous.

Astronauts.

Scientists.

Engineers.

Operators.

Entrepreneurs.

Civil servants.

Warfighters.

A school that tries to form all of them can become a school designed clearly for none of them.

The professions have different obligations.

A military officer must be prepared for lawful command, combat, hierarchy and the use of force.

A scientist must be prepared to challenge assumptions, expose uncertainty and sometimes reject the premise of the institution funding the work.

An engineer must be prepared to say a system is unsafe or infeasible even when leaders want the opposite answer.

A civil servant operates inside statutory authority and administrative processes that survive individual administrations.

An entrepreneur is rewarded for identifying market opportunity, accepting risk and moving resources toward it.

These roles can learn from one another.

They cannot be collapsed into one model of leadership.

If the Space Academy creates a generic culture of “space leadership” that smooths away those differences, it will graduate people less prepared for the actual professions they enter.

The civil-military boundary makes this especially important.

A NASA-led Academy could build valuable understanding between civilian and military space. It could also allow military assumptions to migrate into a civilian institution or encourage civilian language to obscure the distinctive obligations of military service.

Success would mean students understand the boundary better.

Failure would mean they stop noticing it.

The third failure mode is prestige capture.

Federal academies possess symbolic power because admission is selective, the public pays for education and graduates are tied to service. A Space Academy would acquire additional glamour from the domain itself.

This can become self-justifying.

Selective students produce impressive statistics.

Impressive statistics strengthen the school's reputation.

The reputation attracts more selective students.

Graduates obtain influential jobs partly because they are talented and partly because the institution becomes a credentialing signal.

Those jobs are then cited as proof that the Academy produces leaders.

The loop can continue without demonstrating that the education caused the outcome.

This is not unique to the Space Academy. Elite institutions everywhere face the selection-versus-formation problem.

A public institution has a special responsibility to confront it.

The Academy should measure what it adds.

Did graduates become more technically capable than comparable students entering through other routes?

Did the cross-domain curriculum improve performance?

Did public-service retention increase?

Did receiving organizations observe differences that matter?

Did the Academy widen access to space careers or mainly concentrate opportunity among applicants already positioned to succeed?

If the main measurable result is that highly talented students attended a highly selective school and later obtained impressive jobs, the institution may be confusing intake with impact.

The fourth failure mode is academic shallowness.

The space domain is broad enough to generate an irresistible curriculum.

Orbital mechanics.

Propulsion.

Structures.

Power systems.

Remote sensing.

Cybersecurity.

Artificial intelligence.

Software engineering.

Electronic warfare.

Intelligence.

Space law.

Economics.

Acquisition.

Strategy.

History.

Leadership.

Ethics.

Physical training.

Military studies for uniformed students.

The result can become a buffet of seriousness.

Students touch everything and master nothing.

An Academy intended to bridge disciplines must be more protective of depth than an ordinary university because its founding story will constantly tempt administrators to add one more required subject.

Every new national concern can become a course requirement.

Every agency on the governing board can argue that students need more exposure to its mission.

Every technical trend can demand curriculum space.

A school can drown in relevance.

The defense is not minimalism.

It is a hierarchy of learning.

Some principles are durable and foundational. Mathematics. Physics. Engineering reasoning. Software fundamentals. Probability. Systems thinking. Writing. History. Constitutional government. Leadership. Ethical judgment.

Some knowledge is professionally necessary but changes more quickly. Acquisition rules. Threat systems. commercial architectures. Current doctrine. Particular software tools. Regulatory regimes.

Some knowledge should be encountered through exercises, internships or later service rather than packed into undergraduate coursework.

A successful Academy will know which category a subject belongs to.

A failed Academy will update course titles while leaving the intellectual structure confused.

The fifth failure mode is technological obsolescence.

Four years is a short time in a life and a long time in a technology cycle.

A student can enter under one launch market and graduate under another. Satellite architectures can move toward different levels of proliferation. Software practices can change. Artificial intelligence tools can alter design, operations and analysis. Threat assessments can change. Commercial providers can merge, fail or become dominant. New missions can emerge while old ones become routine.

The Academy cannot win by chasing every change.

It will always be late.

It can win by teaching students how to learn under change.

If it instead builds its identity around particular technologies, contractors, platforms or doctrines, the institution can become a museum remarkably quickly.

Prestige architecture makes this risk worse because buildings encourage curricular commitment.

A laboratory built around one technical stack creates pressure to keep using the stack. A secure facility designed for one training model can become expensive inertia. Faculty hiring in narrow specialties can make adaptation institutionally painful.

The physical campus must not become a hardware lock-in mechanism for the curriculum.

The sixth failure mode is faculty weakness.

An academy is not a collection of course descriptions.

It is people capable of teaching them.

The Space Academy will compete for faculty with universities, companies, federal laboratories, NASA centers, defense organizations and other institutions that value the same expertise. Many candidates will earn more elsewhere. Some will want academic independence and publication freedom that classified or politically sensitive work complicates. Some outstanding practitioners will not be outstanding teachers. Some outstanding scholars will have limited experience with operational realities.

The founding faculty will shape the school more deeply than the founding architecture.

If recruitment is treated as a staffing exercise after the institution's design has been announced, the Academy may discover that the curriculum exists only on paper.

It needs enough permanent faculty to preserve academic standards and institutional memory.

It also needs rotating practitioners to keep the work connected to reality.

Too much permanence can produce isolation.

Too much rotation can produce incoherence.

The balance will require active management for decades.

The seventh failure mode is politicization.

Every federal institution is political in the broad sense that Congress creates authorities, presidents appoint leaders and public money reflects national choices. That is not the same as partisan capture.

A new academy born from a presidential initiative is particularly vulnerable to being treated as an extension of the administration that announced it.

The risk runs in both directions.

Supporters may define loyalty to the Academy as loyalty to the President who created it.

Opponents may define the school as illegitimate because of its political origin.

Both reactions would damage the institution.

A durable academy needs academic standards, governance rules and public-service commitments strong enough to survive changes in party control.

Its students should not be educated into a presidential faction.

Its faculty should not fear that technical or historical conclusions will be evaluated for political usefulness.

Its leadership should be accountable to elected government without turning curriculum into a campaign platform.

This is not solved by claiming the school is apolitical.

No institution concerned with national security, budgets, law and public service can avoid politics.

It is solved by building rules for legitimate political authority and professional independence.

The existing academies have accumulated such rules over generations, sometimes after painful controversies.

The Space Academy will begin without that accumulated restraint.

The eighth failure mode is service-obligation mismatch.

The promise of a federally funded education paired with public service can be powerful. The wrong obligation can repel precisely the students the Academy hopes to recruit.

A military commitment has a clear institutional destination.

A civilian federal commitment is harder when specialized jobs are distributed across agencies, locations and hiring systems. If students owe years of service but cannot obtain positions matching their training, the bargain becomes punitive. If waivers are too easy, the public investment becomes difficult to defend. If the obligation is too long, top candidates may choose universities that leave their careers open. If it is too short, the government may subsidize expensive education only to lose graduates once they become most valuable.

The Academy should track not only whether graduates complete required service but whether they remain afterward.

A school that requires five years and sees most graduates leave government in year six has produced compliance, not necessarily commitment.

That result would deserve study rather than moralizing.

Perhaps compensation is uncompetitive.

Perhaps federal personnel systems frustrate technically ambitious people.

Perhaps the curriculum prepares students better for industry than for public organizations.

Perhaps the Academy itself teaches them to value a national space enterprise wider than government, making departure predictable.

The data should be used to improve the bargain.

The ninth failure mode is scale.

A school can be too small to matter and too large to remain distinctive.

The American space workforce is vast and distributed. Even several hundred graduates a year would represent only a small share. If the Academy's goal is to transform the national profession, it will need effects beyond its degree population: continuing education, research, doctrine, fellowships, convening, partnerships and standards that spread into other institutions.

If it remains a tiny prestigious pipeline, its national claims may exceed its reach.

Expansion creates another danger.

A large Academy needs more faculty, more facilities, more appropriations and more students capable of thriving under its demands. Growth can dilute the very integration and mentoring that justified the school.

The institution should define the scale at which its marginal graduate still adds value compared with funding another route.

That number should not be assumed to rise forever.

The tenth failure mode is regional capture.

Wherever the permanent campus goes, a host community will become an ally. That is normal and can be valuable. Local institutions can build research partnerships, housing, infrastructure and an ecosystem around the school.

The danger is that the Academy gradually becomes identified with one regional cluster rather than the national space enterprise.

Admissions may tilt toward the networks nearby. Faculty may be drawn disproportionately from local organizations. Internships may become convenient rather than nationally representative. Political defense of the institution may become inseparable from local economic interests.

A national academy should be rooted somewhere without becoming provincial.

Its students should leave the campus repeatedly.

Its partnerships should remain geographically broad.

Its admissions outreach should reach communities far from traditional aerospace centers.

Its governing structure should make national breadth visible.

The eleventh failure mode is industry capture.

Commercial involvement is unavoidable if the Academy is serious about contemporary space. Companies design and launch spacecraft, build components, operate constellations, supply software, provide communications and remote-sensing services, and employ enormous numbers of the people the school may call part of the national space profession.

Students should learn from that world.

They should not be trained for a preferred company.

Industry funding, equipment donations, endowed programs, internships and visiting instructors can enrich education while also shaping incentives. A company with a dominant platform may prefer curriculum that produces graduates already familiar with its tools. Students may favor internships at famous firms over less glamorous public assignments. Faculty research can drift toward sponsor interests.

The Academy will need conflict-of-interest rules strong enough to make commercial participation credible.

Public service requires the ability to tell an important supplier no.

Students should learn that skill before their careers depend on the supplier's approval.

The twelfth failure mode is bureaucratic layering.

The Academy is being imagined partly because the national space enterprise is fragmented.

Creating another organization can increase fragmentation.

A NASA-led school coordinating with the Department of the Air Force, Space Force, other federal agencies, universities and industry could become a masterpiece of interagency paperwork. Each partner may retain approval rights. Each may protect its programs. Security rules can differ. Personnel systems can conflict. Budget lines can create separate masters.

The Academy could spend extraordinary effort coordinating itself before it coordinates the profession.

This is why governance matters more than organizational elegance.

Someone must have authority to decide.

Agencies must know which responsibilities they have ceded and which they retain.

Students should not experience interagency collaboration mainly as delayed paperwork.

If the Academy's administrative complexity becomes its defining feature, the school will teach exactly the wrong lesson about integration.

The thirteenth failure mode is success becoming indistinguishable from expansion.

Institutions naturally seek more.

More students.

More programs.

More faculty.

More buildings.

More research centers.

More budget.

Growth is easy to report and politically useful. It is not the same as impact.

A healthy Academy should be willing to close programs that no longer work, reduce requirements that do not add value, partner rather than own, and send resources elsewhere when another institution can perform a function better.

That kind of restraint is difficult because every program eventually has defenders.

The founders can help by writing evaluation and sunset mechanisms before constituencies harden.

Pilot programs can expire unless renewed.

Degree programs can face periodic external review.

Partnerships can be compared on outcomes.

The permanent campus can be phased rather than assumed.

The Academy itself can be reviewed against alternatives on a regular statutory schedule.

A public institution should not fear a test it expects to pass.

The final failure mode is moral vanity.

Space invites grandeur.

The language comes naturally: frontier, destiny, superiority, exploration, leadership, future.

An academy can surround students with those words until public service begins to feel like membership in a chosen class.

That would be a profound failure.

The space enterprise is important because ordinary life depends on it, national security depends on it, scientific knowledge grows through it and economic activity increasingly uses it. The work remains full of maintenance, testing, procurement, software debugging, budgeting, documentation, safety review, shift work and decisions made far from cameras.

A useful professional culture respects that ordinary competence.

It does not need every graduate to imagine becoming an astronaut, general, founder or agency administrator.

Most national capacity is created by people who become very good at work that never appears in a presidential announcement.

The Academy should graduate leaders who understand that.

The school's strongest evidence of success may be graduates who make systems more reliable, contracts more intelligent, teams more honest about risk, operations more disciplined and organizations better at learning from mistakes.

These achievements do not fit easily on monuments.

They are what institutions are for.

A school that could fail should know in advance what failure looks like.

By ten years after the first graduation, the government should be able to answer questions that the commission cannot answer today.

Did the Academy produce capabilities existing pathways did not?

Did graduates perform differently enough to justify the cost?

Did the school strengthen, rather than blur, civilian and military professionalism?

Did it improve coordination with industry without being captured by it?

Did it recruit talent the existing system overlooked?

Did graduates remain in public service?

Did the curriculum stay technically serious?

Did partner organizations change because of what graduates brought with them?

Did people outside the Academy benefit from its research and professional education?

Did the school become less dependent on the politics of its founding administration?

Could the same outcomes have been achieved more cheaply another way?

Some answers may be mixed.

That is normal.

The Academy does not need perfection to justify itself.

It needs evidence that the institution is more than a name attached to ambitions America already had.

The founding generation should make that judgment possible.

If they build only the arguments for permanence, they will have built an advocacy organization.

If they build mechanisms that can expose weakness, force comparison and permit correction, they may build a school capable of becoming a profession's conscience.

The order signed in Houston asked the commission how to establish an Academy.

The harder question is how to establish one that can discover it should change.

That is the test of whether the project is truly about education.

Students are supposed to learn.

Institutions should have to learn too.
