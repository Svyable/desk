# Colorado Springs Learns Space

The strongest competitor to the United States Space Academy already has cadets.

It has classrooms, laboratories, military training, a national admissions system, an astronautics tradition and a view of the Rocky Mountains.

It also has the wrong name.

The United States Air Force Academy was never supposed to become a museum of atmospheric flight. Almost as soon as the permanent campus opened, space entered the curriculum.

In 1958, the year NASA was created, the Academy activated an Astronautics Department. The chronology is almost too neat. The school that had been authorized to form officers for the new age of airpower was still establishing itself when the new age of space arrived and challenged the distinction.

The Academy adapted rather than protested.

By the mid-1960s it offered astronautical engineering. Cadets studied the mechanics of vehicles and trajectories beyond the atmosphere. Academy graduates entered a military and civilian space ecosystem growing around missiles, satellites, launch systems and NASA.

In 1983, Karol Bobko, a member of the Academy's first graduating class, became its first graduate to fly in space when he piloted the Space Shuttle *Challenger*.

The symbolism is useful because Bobko belonged to a generation admitted before human spaceflight had become an American profession.

He entered an Air Force Academy and eventually flew for NASA.

Institutions often prove their flexibility through graduates who cross the boundaries founders assumed would remain fixed.

Colorado Springs kept learning space.

Military space organizations accumulated along the Front Range. Air Force Space Command eventually became a major center of gravity for national-security space. The Academy's location, originally selected for a service defined by aircraft, became increasingly valuable to a service whose missions extended far above them.

Then, in December 2019, the institutional map changed again.

The United States Space Force was created from a large portion of the Air Force's military space structure. The Air Force Academy suddenly faced a question its founders could not have imagined: could an academy belonging to one service form officers for another service that had just separated from it?

The answer came quickly.

In 2020, 86 cadets commissioned into the Space Force.

The Academy became the first service academy to send a cohort of officers directly into the new branch. Those graduates entered a Space Force still building its culture, terminology and training systems. They carried with them four years of Air Force Academy formation and then joined an institution trying to explain how a Guardian should differ from an Airman.

This is the institutional fact that any argument for a separate Space Academy must confront.

The United States did not create a Space Force and discover that it had nowhere to educate academy officers.

It already had an academy with deep space expertise.

The Air Force Academy now explicitly describes itself as the premier commissioning path for officers who want to join the Space Force. Its space programs reach beyond a single academic major. Cadets can encounter space through engineering, operations, research, military training and programs designed to expose them to Space Force units and industry. The Academy says roughly ten percent of each graduating class may commission as Guardians.

A hundred officers a year is not a trivial contribution to a deliberately small service.

The duplication argument begins here.

If the Academy is already capable of producing Guardians, why build another federal undergraduate institution?

A serious answer cannot say merely that space is different from air.

The Space Force already decided that at the service level.

The educational question is whether the difference requires four undergraduate years inside a separate institution or whether Space Force identity can be layered onto a common Department of the Air Force foundation.

The Marine Corps analogy is difficult for Space Academy advocates.

Marines are a distinct armed service with a distinct culture, doctrine, uniform and warfighting identity. They do not have a Marine Corps Academy. The Naval Academy commissions both Navy and Marine Corps officers. Shared undergraduate formation has not prevented the Marine Corps from developing one of the strongest service identities in the American military.

Space Force and Air Force live inside a similar departmental structure.

That suggests a plausible model: keep USAFA as the shared academy, deepen Space Force pathways inside it and allow service-specific formation to occur through later training and assignments.

The Space Force's own Officer Training Course strengthens this argument. Regardless of whether a new officer comes from USAFA, ROTC or Officer Training School, the service can bring those officers together after commissioning and teach a common baseline.

Why move that work four years earlier?

One answer is culture.

A Guardian at the Air Force Academy remains a cadet in an institution whose primary identity is the Air Force. Space programs can be excellent while the academy's ceremonies, history, leadership and institutional imagination remain shaped by airpower.

That matters if undergraduate formation is supposed to do more than teach technical material.

A separate Space Academy could make space the default rather than the specialty.

Every cadet, whether studying engineering, policy, computer science or economics, would inhabit an institution organized around the space domain. Guest speakers, exercises, research projects, internships and peer culture would all begin from the assumption that space is the central professional environment.

This is exactly the argument Air Force Academy advocates once made about West Point.

The problem is that history has already demonstrated USAFA's capacity to adapt.

The Academy adopted astronautics before NASA had completed its first year. It produced astronauts. It incorporated cyber. It created space programs. It began commissioning Guardians months after Space Force was formed.

Adaptability is not institutional weakness.

It may be the strongest reason not to duplicate.

The new Academy proposal becomes more defensible only when it stops competing with USAFA on USAFA's terms.

A Space Force-only academy asks the federal government to create an expensive new institution to do something an existing academy demonstrably does.

A broader Space Academy asks a different question.

Could the country create an institution that USAFA cannot become without ceasing to be the Air Force Academy?

NASA leadership makes that possibility real.

The Air Force Academy is a military service academy. Its students enter a military system. Its cadet culture, chain of command and commissioning mission give the institution coherence.

It cannot simply add large numbers of future NASA civil servants and commercial-space professionals without changing its basic nature.

A NASA-led federal Space Academy could make civil and military service pathways part of its founding design rather than additions to a military school.

This distinction turns Colorado Springs from an opponent into a potential partner.

If policymakers treat the new Academy as a replacement for USAFA's space role, institutional conflict is almost guaranteed and intellectually unnecessary.

If the Space Academy has a distinct cross-domain mission, USAFA can continue doing what it already does well: form military officers inside the Department of the Air Force, including Guardians.

The new school would need to produce something else.

That something else should be defined in outputs rather than adjectives.

Suppose the commission says the new Academy will be interdisciplinary, innovative and world-class. None of those words distinguishes it from what an existing university or academy would say about itself.

The useful questions are harder.

Will the Academy produce officers who require less remedial cross-domain training after commissioning?

Will NASA receive engineers who understand operational constraints and public acquisition better than comparable recruits from existing universities?

Will federal space organizations retain graduates longer because service commitment and professional identity begin together?

Will graduates be more effective at moving among engineering, operations, policy and acquisition without creating new bureaucratic friction?

Will the school expand the population willing to enter public space service rather than simply redistribute applicants who would have gone to USAFA or a civilian aerospace program anyway?

Those are differences an appropriator can eventually test.

They also reveal why a new academy cannot be justified by symbolism alone.

This raises a hard admissions question.

The United States has a finite supply of students with the preparation and desire to handle rigorous technical education tied to public service. A Space Academy will recruit from the same population courted by USAFA, MIT, Caltech, Georgia Tech, Purdue, Stanford, state engineering schools and commercial employers promising extraordinary opportunities.

New capacity does not automatically create new talent.

It can redistribute it.

If Space Academy simply pulls the strongest space-oriented cadets away from USAFA, the federal government may spend billions to move students from one public institution to another.

The Academy must therefore expand the pipeline, not merely rearrange it.

That could involve students who want NASA or civilian federal service but would never apply to a military academy.

It could involve technical specialties underserved by current commissioning models.

It could create scholarship and preparatory pathways reaching communities that do not currently see a route into federal space work.

It could partner with existing universities rather than attempting to own every faculty specialty.

It could use NASA centers, Space Force units and industry as an experiential network no single university can reproduce.

Again, these are possibilities the commission must evaluate, not facts about the final design.

The point is that the Academy has to add people or capability somewhere.

Otherwise Colorado Springs already has the answer.

There is another way the Air Force Academy could shape the new school: as a warning about institutional time.

USAFA's space capacity did not appear because a committee declared it. It accumulated.

Faculty built programs.

Cadets conducted research.

Graduates returned with operational experience.

Space organizations around Colorado Springs grew. Academic departments matured. Relationships with the service deepened. The Academy learned what kinds of technical education remained useful after technology changed.

A new Space Academy will not possess that accumulated judgment on opening day.

It may recruit brilliant faculty and experienced leaders. It may build exceptional laboratories. It may borrow curriculum and partnerships. But it cannot purchase alumni memory in the first appropriation.

This is why proposals to make a new academy immediately “the best” should be treated with caution.

Prestige is an output.

The government can fund facilities. Students decide whether to apply. Faculty decide whether to stay. Employers decide whether graduates are useful. Alumni decide whether the institution deserves loyalty. Accreditation bodies decide whether academic processes meet standards. Time decides whether the place becomes a tradition or a project.

The Air Force Academy had to survive those tests.

So will the Space Academy.

Colorado Springs also demonstrates how geography can create professional concentration.

Space Force units, commands and associated defense organizations around the region give cadets access to operational problems that would be difficult to simulate entirely on campus. A student can encounter the service not as an abstraction but as nearby institutions with real missions and people.

This is one reason Colorado politicians can argue sincerely, in addition to politically, that a new Space Academy belongs there if it is created at all.

Houston can make the same argument around NASA.

Florida can make it around launch and training.

The location debate will therefore become a debate over which part of the national space enterprise should become the Academy's home culture.

That question may be more important than weather or acreage.

An institution learns from its neighbors.

Put the Academy beside NASA human-spaceflight operations and it may grow toward exploration and engineering.

Put it inside the military space cluster and warfighting may dominate.

Put it near launch infrastructure and industrial operations may become central.

Put it beside a major research university and academic partnerships may define it.

There is no neutral site.

This is also why a network model deserves serious consideration before the government treats the permanent campus as the institution itself.

A cohort could study in one place and rotate through others.

A common academic core could be paired with semester or summer experiences at NASA centers, operational Space Force units, launch ranges, research laboratories and commercial facilities. Faculty appointments could include practitioners from multiple agencies. Capstone projects could be sponsored by real public missions rather than invented classroom exercises.

Such a model would be administratively harder than a self-contained campus.

It might also be more faithful to the actual space enterprise, which is distributed by nature.

The comparison with USAFA makes that design choice sharper. Colorado Springs already demonstrates the value of proximity to a dense military space ecosystem. A new academy could either reproduce one cluster or deliberately teach students to move among several.

If the academy's thesis is cross-domain literacy, mobility may be part of the curriculum.

If its thesis is concentrated military formation, Colorado Springs already has unusually strong infrastructure.

The location choice therefore cannot be separated from the mission choice.

The Air Force Academy became a space institution partly because its institutional environment changed around it.

What was once a school for a new air service became a school capable of producing officers for a space service.

That is a remarkable success.

It is also the obstacle the new Academy must clear.

The case for the United States Space Academy cannot depend on saying America lacks a place where future Guardians learn space.

Colorado Springs makes that false.

The case must be that the country now needs a place where future Guardians learn beside people who will serve different parts of space—and where those other people receive an equally serious formation in public responsibility.

That is a narrower argument than “Space Force deserves its own West Point.”

It is also more ambitious.

The Air Force Academy learned space by expanding the meaning of airpower education.

The Space Academy will need to justify itself by learning something the old academy cannot teach without becoming a different institution.

If it cannot, the mountains already have a school.
