# The Campus War

The United States Space Academy does not have a home.

It already has suitors.

President Trump acknowledged the pressure at the announcement itself. Speaking at Johnson Space Center in Houston on August 28, he said the location had not been decided and joked about the lobbying surrounding the choice. Texas had an obvious claim: the President was standing at the center that had directed American human spaceflight for generations, and Senator Ted Cruz was among the political figures with strong opinions about where a new national space institution should sit.

Within days, Houston boosters were publicly making the case.

Nothing about that should be surprising.

A federal academy is not merely a collection of classrooms. It is a permanent stream of students, faculty, staff, contracts, construction, visitors, research partnerships, federal attention and symbolic status. Once built, it becomes extremely difficult to move. A city that wins the Academy does not win a four-year grant.

It enters the national map.

The Air Force Academy made this lesson plain in the 1950s. Hundreds of communities wanted the new institution. The site-selection commission traveled thousands of miles and considered proposals from nearly every state. Colorado Springs prevailed, and seven decades later it is difficult to imagine the Academy anywhere else.

That feeling is what every contender wants.

A Space Academy campus would create its own inevitability after the fact.

The danger is that location politics can answer an academic question before the academic question has been settled.

The executive order gets the sequence right on paper. It asks the commission to recommend governance, curriculum, service obligations and applicant prerequisites, and it calls for a process to select a permanent physical location appropriate for the chosen governance framework.

Appropriate for the governance framework.

Those words matter.

A military academy centered on Space Force would have one set of location needs.

A NASA-operated federal university with a large civilian student population would have another.

A hybrid institution dependent on partnerships with existing universities, laboratories and agencies might value proximity differently again.

Choose the location first and the institution will begin adapting itself to the winner.

That may happen anyway because politics rarely follows the clean order of an executive document.

Houston's argument begins with Johnson Space Center.

The institution there carries a concentration of human-spaceflight expertise difficult to reproduce. Mission Control, astronaut training, engineering, flight operations and the history of Apollo and Artemis make the region an intuitive home for a school that NASA is chairing. Students could encounter NASA not as a logo but as a working organization. A curriculum emphasizing human exploration, systems engineering and civil space would have nearby laboratories, practitioners and mission operations.

Houston also has political symbolism.

The Academy was announced there.

Founding stories have momentum.

A school placed near the site where the executive order was signed could describe itself as an extension of that moment for generations.

The weakness of the Houston case is the strength of its NASA identity.

If the Academy is supposed to form a truly mixed national space profession, placing it inside the gravitational field of a major civil-space center could make military and other federal missions feel like visitors. Johnson is important to American space. It is not all of American space.

Colorado Springs makes the mirror-image argument.

The Air Force Academy already sits there. So do decades of military-space heritage and a dense network of national-security space organizations in the region. A Space Academy located nearby would have immediate access to Guardians, operational problems and an established ecosystem of defense-space expertise.

Colorado also possesses the strongest institutional critique of a new Academy: maybe the country should invest in the Air Force Academy it already has.

Senator John Hickenlooper made that argument immediately, calling the proposal duplicative and costly. His position cannot be separated from Colorado's long interest in preserving its place in national-security space, but local interest does not invalidate the substantive question.

If the final Space Academy looks primarily military, Colorado Springs becomes both the most obvious site and the strongest argument against building a second school at all.

Why construct a new campus in the same professional ecosystem when an existing federal academy is already commissioning roughly ninety Space Force officers a year?

The commission would need a clear answer.

Florida has a different kind of claim even before a formal bid is documented.

The Space Coast makes launch physical. Kennedy Space Center, Cape Canaveral and the surrounding commercial launch industry expose students to a part of the space enterprise that no classroom can reproduce. Launch cadence, range operations, spacecraft processing, commercial providers and federal missions meet there in public view.

A school near launch infrastructure would absorb an industrial rhythm rather than a headquarters rhythm.

That could be valuable for an Academy trying to connect engineering, operations and commercial space.

It could also overidentify the profession with launch, one dramatic phase of systems that spend most of their lives somewhere else.

Other regions can make equally serious cases.

California contains major aerospace companies, research universities, military space institutions and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory and Ames Research Center. Alabama has Marshall Space Flight Center and a deep rocket and defense engineering tradition. New Mexico offers ranges, laboratories and a long history of military technology. The Washington region concentrates federal policy, acquisition and national-security leadership. Ohio, Maryland, Virginia and other states can point to aerospace research, intelligence, cyber or government institutions.

The point is not to predict the shortlist before the commission publishes one.

It is to recognize that every credible location embodies a theory of what the Academy should value.

The commission should therefore publish criteria before it falls in love with sites.

The first criterion should be mission access.

Students need repeated exposure to real space organizations. A campus that requires expensive travel for every serious field experience wastes one of the advantages of a federal academy. Proximity to NASA, Space Force, laboratories, launch operations, research institutions or multiple categories of partner should count heavily.

The second should be academic ecosystem.

The Academy will not be able to hire permanent world-class faculty in every specialty immediately. Nearby research universities can provide joint appointments, laboratory access, graduate pathways and intellectual competition. A city with strong engineering, computer science, physics, policy and medical institutions offers a deeper bench than a place chosen mainly for cheap land.

The third should be industrial diversity.

Space manufacturing, software, launch, remote sensing, communications and services do not all live in the same city. The best site would give students access to more than one business model and more than one company. An academy too close to a dominant contractor risks confusing one firm's methods with the industry.

The fourth should be military access without military capture.

Future Guardians need contact with operational units and service culture. Civilian students need to understand national-security space. But if the Academy's broader mission survives, the campus should not become functionally a Space Force base with civilian degree tracks attached.

The fifth should be civil-space access without NASA capture.

The same warning applies in reverse.

The sixth should be cost.

Prestige projects become dangerous when advocates treat money as evidence of seriousness. Land, housing, utilities, transportation, laboratories, secure facilities and construction will impose large fixed costs. A location able to use existing federal property or facilities could lower the price and accelerate a pilot class.

The executive order explicitly tells the commission to consider existing authorities, partnerships and preparatory initiatives. That should make adaptive reuse a serious option rather than an embarrassment.

The first Space Academy does not need to look like a monument.

The seventh should be resilience and physical suitability.

A school expected to teach national infrastructure should think seriously about its own dependencies: power, communications, water, transportation, extreme weather and access. A campus does not need to survive every catastrophe independently. It should at least understand the risks it is teaching students to analyze elsewhere.

The eighth should be national accessibility.

Federal academies recruit nationally. Students and families need reasonable transportation. Faculty recruiting matters too. A spectacularly isolated site may provide inspiring photographs and make it harder to hire the people who create the education.

The ninth should be expansion potential.

The Academy may begin small and change substantially. A permanent site should allow laboratories, secure spaces, research facilities and housing to grow without forcing the institution to predict its mature form in year one.

The tenth should be culture.

This is the hardest criterion to score and perhaps the most important.

What will students absorb from the place without anyone assigning it?

At West Point, terrain, history and the Army's presence are inseparable.

At Annapolis, the Navy and the Chesapeake shape the institution.

At Colorado Springs, the mountains, Air Force history and military-space community surround the cadet experience.

The Space Academy's setting will teach a hidden curriculum.

A launch town teaches cadence and spectacle.

A research city teaches intellectual competition.

A military cluster teaches mission and hierarchy.

A NASA center teaches engineering culture and exploration.

A federal capital teaches policy and bureaucracy.

No choice is neutral.

This is why the site process should not become an auction of political favors.

That outcome is difficult to prevent because elected officials are supposed to advocate for their states. Governors will offer land and infrastructure. Universities will promise partnerships. Companies will describe workforce synergies. Members of Congress will discover that the national interest aligns remarkably well with local economic development.

The commission cannot remove politics.

It can make politics legible.

Publish criteria.

Score proposals.

Disclose major assumptions.

Explain tradeoffs.

Separate donated land from lifetime operating cost.

Distinguish an existing “space cluster” from a cluster likely to serve the Academy's actual academic mission.

Make every finalist answer the same questions.

Transparency will not eliminate lobbying. It will make lobbying compete with evidence.

There is also a case for delaying the permanent decision.

The Air Force Academy's first class entered at Lowry Air Force Base while its permanent campus was being built. A Space Academy could begin with a pilot cohort in existing facilities and use early experience to learn what the permanent campus actually requires.

That approach has political disadvantages.

Temporary institutions feel less triumphant.

A groundbreaking is easier to celebrate than a borrowed classroom.

But a pilot campus could answer questions worth billions of dollars.

How much secure instruction is genuinely necessary?

How many laboratories need to be owned rather than shared?

Do civilian and military students require separate residential structures?

How much time do students spend at external partner sites?

Which academic programs attract faculty?

Does a mixed-service model work socially?

What class size makes interdisciplinary education possible?

A country that teaches test and evaluation to Space Force should test and evaluate its academy before pouring concrete around assumptions.

There is one more reason to resist haste.

Location can determine institutional coalition.

Once a city wins, its congressional delegation becomes a natural defender. Local contractors and universities become stakeholders. Alumni eventually settle nearby. The Academy accumulates people whose livelihoods depend on its continued expansion.

This is normal.

It is also the moment when closing or redesigning the institution becomes politically difficult even if the original mission changes.

The permanent site therefore converts an educational hypothesis into a durable constituency.

That is why the choice deserves more seriousness than a beauty contest among space cities.

The Academy needs a home that reinforces its purpose without defining it too narrowly.

As of September 2026, Texas has the momentum of the announcement and visible lobbying. Colorado has the strongest existing military-academy and space infrastructure—and one of the loudest skeptical voices. Other regions have substantial claims that may become formal as the commission develops its process.

The winner is unknown.

That uncertainty is healthy for the book and useful for the institution.

For a few months, America can still ask what the campus needs before asking where politicians want it.

Eventually a place will be chosen, if the Academy survives the design process.

The map will acquire a star.

Students will learn its weather.

Traditions will attach themselves to local roads, buildings and horizons.

A decision made through memos and lobbying will begin to look inevitable.

Before that happens, the commission should remember what the site is supposed to be.

Not the prize.

The instrument.
