# The Order Signed in Houston

The United States named its next academy before it knew where to put it.

On August 28, 2026, at Johnson Space Center in Houston, President Donald Trump signed an executive order establishing a Presidential Commission on the United States Space Academy. The setting carried its own history. Johnson was where NASA had trained astronauts, run Mission Control and turned the abstractions of national space policy into checklists, simulations and decisions made by people sitting at consoles. The announcement came during a ceremony connected to the Artemis II crew, the next American crew assigned to travel around the Moon.

Then the ceremony widened.

The order did not open a campus. It did not admit a cadet. It did not create a degree program, hire a faculty, choose a permanent home or settle how many years a graduate would owe the government. What it did was more preliminary and, in one sense, more revealing. It declared that the United States had reached the point where an academy devoted to space was worth designing.

That distinction matters.

Institutions often appear inevitable after they exist. West Point looks natural from two centuries away. Annapolis looks like the obvious place to train naval officers. The Air Force Academy, with its sharp modernist campus against the Colorado foothills, can seem as though the country simply built a school when it created an independent air service.

None of them began as inevitabilities.

They began as arguments.

Arguments about what kind of people a republic needed. Arguments about whether existing institutions could train them. Arguments about money, geography, politics, professional identity and the danger that a school created to solve one problem would create another. An academy is never merely a cluster of classrooms. It is a machine for deciding which knowledge matters, which habits deserve repetition and what kind of person the state is willing to spend years trying to produce.

The proposed United States Space Academy arrived inside the same problem, except the domain it was meant to serve was more institutionally tangled than land, sea or air had been when their academies took shape.

Space was already civilian and military. It was already governmental and commercial. It belonged to NASA astronauts and Space Force Guardians, but also to weather forecasters, intelligence analysts, engineers, acquisition officers, launch companies, satellite operators, scientists, regulators, software teams and thousands of people whose daily work could affect an orbital system without requiring them ever to see a rocket.

The executive order reflected that mixture. It described a proposed NASA-led federal academy intended to educate and prepare a professional corps for the space enterprise. The list of possible graduates crossed familiar bureaucratic borders: astronauts, scientists, engineers, operators, entrepreneurs, civil servants and warfighters. The order contemplated service in the Armed Forces, but it also contemplated civilian federal service.

This was not, at least on paper, simply West Point with rockets.

Nor was it a NASA university with uniforms added later.

The ambiguity was the point.

By 2026, the United States had spent more than six decades learning how to send people into space, more than half a century operating military satellites, decades building commercial launch and communications industries, and nearly seven years operating an independent Space Force. The country already possessed excellent universities, military academies, Reserve Officer Training Corps programs, Officer Training School, graduate programs, NASA internships and fellowships, specialized military schools and a rapidly growing Space Training and Readiness Command.

The shortage was not that America had forgotten how to teach orbital mechanics.

The harder question was whether the existing pieces added up to the professional system the next era required.

That question had become less academic as the domain changed.

For most of the twentieth century, space was expensive enough and difficult enough that a relatively small number of institutions could dominate it. Governments launched reconnaissance satellites. NASA flew exploration missions. Large aerospace contractors built exquisite machines in small numbers. Commercial communications satellites created important businesses, but the infrastructure of orbit still felt remote from ordinary economic life.

Then the rate of change accelerated.

Launch became more frequent. Rockets became reusable. Constellations turned hundreds or thousands of satellites into networked systems. Private companies began operating capabilities that governments once would have considered strategic infrastructure. Military planners depended on space for communications, navigation, missile warning, intelligence and the synchronization of forces across Earth. Civilian life depended on many of the same orbital layers without thinking much about them.

A satellite stopped being merely a machine in the sky.

It became part of an economy.

It became part of a battlefield.

It became part of the electrical nervous system of modern life.

That changed the human problem.

The Space Force, created in December 2019, inherited experienced people and mature missions, but a new service does not inherit centuries of culture by changing the name over a door. It had to define what a Guardian was, how Guardians should be trained, which skills belonged together and what professional military education meant in a service where a young officer might spend a career thinking about orbital regimes, cyber vulnerabilities, acquisition timelines, intelligence indicators and commercial service contracts rather than commanding formations in the traditional sense.

By 2025 and 2026, Space Training and Readiness Command had begun to make that professional architecture more concrete. Newly commissioned Guardian officers from service academies, ROTC and Officer Training School entered a common initial training course. The curriculum deliberately crossed career fields that older military organizations often kept in separate lanes: space operations, cyber, intelligence and acquisition. Other courses trained Guardians for component staff work and joint planning. Doctrine expanded. Wargaming expanded. The service was trying to teach itself how to be a service.

At the same time, the Government Accountability Office found a more basic problem. In fiscal year 2025, the Space Force had filled only about three quarters of the positions it had identified as necessary for its missions. GAO also found weaknesses in the way the service determined personnel requirements and said it lacked a strategic workforce plan that fully addressed the challenge.

A school cannot solve a workforce system by itself.

That fact sits at the center of this story.

It would be easy to write the Space Academy as a simple answer to a simple shortage: America needs more people for space, therefore America should build a school for space. But institutions rarely behave so neatly. If the underlying problem is retention, a four-year academy does not fix it. If the problem is outdated staffing requirements, producing more graduates may simply pour people into the wrong billets. If the problem is security-clearance delay, federal hiring friction, compensation or the competition for experienced technical talent, a new campus addresses the problem only indirectly.

The case for the Academy has to be stronger than head count.

It has to be about formation.

That is why the executive order is more interesting than the slogan around it.

The commission was instructed to recommend a governing structure, academic and leadership curriculum, service obligations, applicant prerequisites, a site-selection process, administrative actions, legislation, coordination with existing programs and an implementation strategy. In other words, it was asked to answer nearly every question that separates a ceremonial announcement from a functioning institution.

The chair was the NASA Administrator.

That choice placed the proposed academy inside a productive tension from the beginning. NASA is a civilian agency. The Space Force is an armed service. NASA's public mythology is built around exploration, scientific discovery and human spaceflight. The Space Force's mission includes protecting national interests and preparing for conflict in, from and to space. The commercial sector moves according to yet another rhythm, one shaped by financing, engineering cadence, customers and competition.

An academy designed for the space age would have to teach people to cross those cultures without pretending they are the same.

A Guardian should understand why a satellite operator worries about latency, resilience and adversary action. An engineer should understand why an acquisition decision can matter as much as a technical one. A civil servant should understand the commercial incentives inside a procurement strategy. A warfighter should understand the law governing the domain and the risks of escalation. A scientist should be able to work inside institutions that classify information and make decisions under strategic pressure. An entrepreneur taking public money should understand the obligations that arrive with a government customer.

That is an unusually ambitious educational project.

It is also unusually easy to get wrong.

A federal academy can become rigid. It can confuse exclusivity with excellence. It can reproduce the bureaucracy it was created to outgrow. It can pull talent away from universities rather than enlarge the country's talent pool. It can turn a national workforce problem into a site-selection contest among states. It can be so military that NASA's civilian mission becomes decorative, or so civilian that the Space Force receives little it could not obtain elsewhere. It can build a curriculum around today's technology and graduate its first class into a domain that has already changed.

The strongest argument against the Space Academy is not that space is unimportant.

It is that space may be too important to entrust to one new institution.

The strongest argument for it begins in the same place.

When a domain becomes central enough, the United States has repeatedly discovered that distributed competence is not always the same as a profession. Universities can teach aerospace engineering. Companies can teach a particular satellite architecture. Military schools can teach operational practice. NASA can teach human spaceflight and mission discipline. But a profession also needs a common language, a shared memory, an ethical framework and a place where people learn the boundaries of their own expertise.

The argument is over whether space has reached that point.

On August 28, 2026, the federal government said the question deserved an institution-sized answer.

The commission received 120 days to begin designing one.

That is a very short time in the life of an academy.

It is a very long time in politics.

By the end of it, the commissioners would have to recommend whether the proposed school should sit inside NASA or somewhere else, how it should coordinate with the existing service academies, what its graduates should owe the country, who should be allowed to enter, what they should study and where a permanent physical institution should eventually stand.

The easy version of the Space Academy already existed on the day of the signing.

It was a name.

The difficult version would have to survive contact with law, budgets, existing institutions, service cultures, educational reality and the uncomfortable fact that the people it sought to prepare would spend their careers in a domain changing faster than the government could build campuses.

That is the story of *To the Stars*.

Not the story of a school after it became famous.

The story of a country deciding, in public and in real time, whether it needed the school at all.
