# The People Who Keep It Running

An academy naturally points attention upward.

Officers.

Engineers.

Scientists.

Astronauts.

Future agency leaders.

The people most likely to appear in brochures are the people whose jobs already carry titles that sound like leadership.

Space does not run on titles.

It runs on practiced competence distributed through teams.

The Space Force's own workforce makes the point. Enlisted Guardians can spend long periods in technical training before becoming fully useful in operational assignments. In 2026 the service introduced an enlistment-bonus pilot tied to unusually long commitments because, as its personnel leaders explained, the service invests heavily in training and needs enough time afterward for Guardians to build deep operational mastery. The service has also been redesigning enlisted education around the idea that technical expertise, teaching, mentorship and later institutional leadership belong to one career rather than separate categories.

This matters to the Space Academy because a school can accidentally teach the wrong theory of expertise.

If the Academy educates mostly officers and degree-holding civilians, its students may leave with a network concentrated among people who make plans while knowing too little about the people who keep systems working.

That would be a serious professional defect.

Space operations contain work learned through repetition as much as coursework.

A console operator develops judgment about telemetry that cannot be downloaded from a textbook. A technician learns what a connector, cable, procedure or test setup looks like when something is subtly wrong. An enlisted Guardian may know a mission system more intimately than a newly arrived officer who holds formal authority over the unit. A contractor who has supported several program generations may remember why an awkward procedure exists after the people who wrote the original requirement have retired. A civilian employee may provide continuity across military rotations.

Organizations become dangerous when formal rank and educational prestige are mistaken for exclusive possession of knowledge.

A national space profession should teach the opposite.

Authority and expertise often overlap.

They are not identical.

The military already knows this in practice. Officers command, but competent officers learn quickly that command does not make them the best technician in the room. Noncommissioned officers and experienced enlisted specialists carry standards, practical knowledge and the credibility that comes from having performed the work under real conditions.

A Space Academy that commissions Guardians would need to make that relationship part of formation rather than a lesson discovered after graduation.

The temptation will be especially strong because the domain is so technical.

A student who has spent four years mastering hard mathematics may reasonably feel accomplished. The degree may be difficult. The admissions process may have been selective. The graduate may understand orbital mechanics at a level many operators never need.

None of that means the graduate knows how a particular mission fails at three in the morning.

The Academy should create experiences where students depend on people with different kinds of expertise.

Not staged demonstrations in which cadets observe technicians performing tasks.

Actual teams in which success requires listening to them.

A future acquisition officer should spend enough time near maintenance and operations to understand how contractual language becomes somebody else's repeated burden. A future engineer should see how design decisions affect the people who troubleshoot systems after launch. A future military officer should learn from enlisted Guardians who possess years of specialty knowledge. A future civil servant should understand how continuity is often preserved by career employees far from headquarters.

This is also a question about who gets invited into the Academy itself.

The executive order's proposed institution is not yet defined as a conventional officer-only service academy. That leaves room for the commission to think beyond the simplest undergraduate model.

Could enlisted Guardians attend selected courses?

Could experienced technicians teach?

Could the Academy operate advanced certificates or mid-career programs that do not require a four-year degree pathway?

Could enlisted personnel earn credits toward degrees without leaving their careers for an all-or-nothing residential program?

Could the school become a place where civilian technicians, military specialists, engineers and managers encounter one another at multiple career stages?

Those questions do not require the Academy to abandon academic rigor.

They require it to distinguish education from credentialism.

A bachelor's degree is valuable for many space professions because the work genuinely requires advanced theory. It is not the universal unit of competence.

The United States has repeatedly damaged technical workforces by constructing career ladders that force excellent practitioners to become managers if they want status and pay. The same mistake can occur inside a national space profession if prestige flows overwhelmingly toward degrees, commissions and administrative authority.

The Space Force's evolving enlisted framework points toward a different model. Early career is built around foundational mastery. Noncommissioned officers add team leadership and mentorship. Senior enlisted leaders eventually carry broader institutional responsibilities. Technical depth is not treated as something to escape on the way to leadership.

The Academy should learn from that structure.

Its public image will probably be dominated by undergraduates if a four-year school becomes the centerpiece. Its actual contribution could be wider if it treats learning as a career-long system.

This would help with another problem: speed.

The space domain changes too rapidly for any undergraduate education to remain sufficient. A graduate who leaves the Academy in one technological environment may need substantial retraining ten years later. Enlisted Guardians, civil servants and technical specialists face the same challenge.

A national institution could provide recurring education around new architectures, doctrine, law, cyber threats, acquisition methods and operational lessons.

Those programs could bring back graduates and people who never attended the undergraduate Academy.

That would prevent the school from becoming a closed alumni club.

The people who keep systems running would have a door into the institution.

They should also shape its research.

Universities often organize research around questions scholars and sponsors identify. Operational organizations generate another category: problems visible only after a system meets daily use.

A procedure takes too long.

A software interface creates avoidable error.

A training sequence assumes knowledge new operators do not have.

A maintenance burden is growing.

A data format prevents information from moving between organizations.

A security rule protects one risk while creating another.

These are not always glamorous research topics.

They can have enormous operational value.

The Academy could build mechanisms for enlisted Guardians and technicians to bring such problems into classrooms and laboratories. Student teams could work on them under proper security constraints. Faculty could learn which theoretical questions matter in practice. The people who identified the problem could remain part of the solution rather than serving merely as sources of anecdotes.

That is what respect for expertise looks like institutionally.

There is a cultural danger here too.

Service academies produce strong identities partly because students endure difficult shared experiences. A new Space Academy may develop its own sense of distinction quickly. Its graduates will be firsts. Politicians will celebrate them. Employers may seek them. Alumni networks will form.

The school should deliberately inoculate students against believing that distinction makes them superior to the workforce they join.

One method is simple.

Make dependence visible.

Put students in situations where the mission cannot succeed without enlisted operators, technicians, maintainers, security professionals, contracting specialists, range personnel, software teams and experienced civil servants.

Then refuse to let the curriculum describe those people as “support” when the system collapses without them.

The word *support* can hide architecture.

Positioning, navigation and timing support other forces until it disappears and those forces discover how deeply it shaped their operations. Ground systems support satellites until a ground failure makes the spacecraft inaccessible. Technicians support engineering until a design reaches hardware and the technician discovers the assembly cannot be performed as planned.

The people who keep things running often appear peripheral only because success makes their work quiet.

The Academy's students should be taught to look for quiet dependencies.

That habit would improve officers.

It would improve engineers.

It would improve administrators.

It would also make the Academy more credible to a workforce that may reasonably wonder whether the new institution is being built for them or merely above them.

The United States Space Academy does not need to admit every category of space worker into the same degree program.

It does need a theory of the whole workforce.

Otherwise a school created to integrate the national space profession could reproduce one of the oldest divisions inside technical organizations: the people who are educated to decide and the people who are expected to make the decision work.

Space is too unforgiving for that division to become contempt.

The machine will eventually reveal who understood it.
