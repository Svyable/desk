# The Missing People

A shortage is an answer to the wrong question until you know what is missing.

In July 2026, the Government Accountability Office published a report on the Space Force workforce. The headline number was difficult to ignore. In fiscal year 2025, the service had filled about 75 percent of the positions it had identified as necessary to carry out its missions.

One quarter of the stated need was unfilled.

For a service responsible for capabilities woven through modern military operations, that sounds like the kind of statistic that could justify an academy by itself.

It does not.

The same GAO report explained why.

The Space Force had not only struggled to recruit, train and retain enough qualified personnel. It also had weaknesses in the way it determined how many people its organizations required. Some personnel requirements were outdated. Analysis was inconsistent. The service lacked a strategic workforce plan that fully addressed the challenges GAO identified.

This creates an uncomfortable sequence.

Before producing more people, know what people you need.

Before building a pipeline, know where it ends.

Before creating an academy, know which shortage the academy can actually solve.

The Space Force's workforce problem is real. The service had about 15,400 total military and civilian personnel in fiscal year 2025 according to GAO. That is small enough to fit inside a large university and small enough that every specialized shortage can become visible quickly. It is also a misleadingly simple number because the Space Force's mission depends on people outside its direct head count.

Contractors build and sustain systems.

The intelligence community contributes people and capabilities.

Other military services supply support.

Commercial firms operate satellites, launch vehicles, networks and data services.

Federal civilians hold technical and institutional knowledge that uniformed personnel rotate past.

A Space Force billet chart therefore describes only part of the human architecture supporting military space.

The proposed Space Academy is broader still. Its stated purpose reaches beyond the Space Force into NASA and civilian federal service. The workforce case has to be built around an ecosystem rather than a single vacancy rate.

This is where Washington frequently gets human-capital policy wrong.

A labor problem is described as a pipeline problem because pipelines are visible and politically satisfying. Create a scholarship. Create a fellowship. Create a school. Recruit more students. Announce a target.

Sometimes that works.

Sometimes the real problem begins after the student arrives.

Consider retention.

A technical professional with skills valuable to the government may also have skills valuable to private industry. Compensation is only one part of the decision. The person may care about location, flexibility, access to modern tools, speed of decision-making, quality of management and whether bureaucracy allows meaningful work to get done. A new academy can produce a brilliant engineer and still lose that engineer five years later if the institution receiving her makes expertise frustrating to use.

Consider security clearances.

A mission may need people who can access classified information. The government can recruit them, but clearance processes can create delay and uncertainty. A student who spends years preparing for a role may discover that administrative eligibility becomes a bottleneck entirely separate from education.

Consider assignments.

A service can have enough people in total and still have the wrong mix of experience at a specific unit. Entry-level graduates do not replace senior engineers. A new lieutenant does not substitute for a civilian with fifteen years of knowledge about a ground system. Workforce shortages are often shortages of accumulated experience rather than bodies.

Consider requirements themselves.

If an organization has not updated its assumptions about how many people a mission requires, the vacancy rate can produce false precision. Automation may reduce some needs. New missions may increase others. Commercial services may replace government ownership in one area while creating new integration work in another. A unit may carry billets based on an old architecture even as the technical system changes beneath it.

GAO's criticism is important because it refuses to let the number end the inquiry.

The Space Academy should do the same.

The strongest workforce case for the institution has three layers.

The first is quantity.

The national space enterprise is growing and needs more technically capable people. Military missions are expanding. NASA is pursuing ambitious human and robotic exploration. Commercial space activity has increased launch cadence, satellite manufacturing, communications, imagery and other services. Government agencies need people who can regulate, buy, integrate and understand these systems.

The second is quality.

The work requires combinations of skills that ordinary pipelines may not produce deliberately. A space acquisition officer who understands software and market structure can make different decisions from one trained mainly in process. A civil servant who understands orbital mechanics and international law can reason across questions that otherwise require translation among specialists. A Guardian who has worked alongside future NASA and commercial professionals may understand interdependence earlier.

The third is continuity.

Institutions need people who stay long enough to develop memory. A new academy can create service obligations that retain graduates for a minimum period. Traditional service academies do this explicitly. The proposed Space Academy could extend the concept to civilian federal service, creating a pool of graduates who owe the public meaningful years before private employers can recruit them away.

This third layer may be the most powerful.

A scholarship can pay tuition.

A service obligation shapes the first years of a career.

If designed well, that obligation could send technically trained graduates into federal organizations that struggle to compete for talent. The government would be buying time as much as education.

But compulsory service is not the same as retention.

A person can remain employed while becoming disengaged. A graduate can count the months until an obligation ends. An agency can technically retain talent while wasting it on work that does not require the talent's skills.

The Academy therefore cannot be separated from the quality of the jobs waiting at graduation.

This is where the Space Force workforce report becomes a governance document for the school.

If the service says it needs more people, the Academy should ask for evidence about roles, skills and projected demand. If NASA expects a particular technical shortage, the curriculum and class size should be connected to real workforce planning rather than general anxiety about STEM. If civilian agencies are included in service obligations, they should commit to pathways where graduates can use the education the public paid for.

Otherwise the Academy risks becoming a producer without a customer map.

Traditional service academies avoid some of this uncertainty because the military controls both school and destination. Cadets know the broad bargain. The service knows a graduating class is coming. Personnel systems assign new officers into defined career fields.

The proposed Space Academy may be more complicated.

If graduates can enter multiple agencies, somebody has to coordinate demand across organizations with different budgets and hiring systems. A class admitted in one political administration may graduate in another. Mission priorities may change during the four years. Congress may fund fewer positions than agencies predicted. Some students may discover they are qualified for fields where the government has limited openings.

A cross-agency academy therefore needs workforce planning stronger than the systems it is trying to fix.

That is a high bar.

It is also an opportunity.

A small central institution can make fragmentation visible. If NASA, the Space Force, Commerce and other agencies must collectively forecast the number and type of graduates they expect to absorb, they are forced to state assumptions that usually live in separate personnel systems.

The Academy could become a forcing function for national space-workforce planning.

That may ultimately be more valuable than the number of degrees it awards.

The danger is that the school becomes the plan.

Politicians can point to a campus as proof of commitment while harder personnel reforms remain unfinished. Recruitment is visible. Workforce design is not. A ribbon cutting photographs better than an updated methodology for calculating manpower requirements.

The GAO report warns against confusing the two.

Suppose the Space Force fills its entry-level officer pipeline successfully but continues to lose experienced civilian software engineers. The Academy does little.

Suppose the real bottleneck is classified test infrastructure or outdated ground systems that make technical work miserable. The Academy does little.

Suppose commercial services replace some government-operated functions. The service may need fewer operators and more people skilled in integration, contract management, cyber assurance and vendor risk. An academy designed around the old workforce model could make the mismatch worse.

The correct question is not “How many graduates?”

It is “What work will the graduates make possible?”

This changes how success should be measured.

An academy will naturally publish admissions numbers, retention rates, majors, graduation rates and commissioning statistics. Those are useful. They are also proximal measures. The country should eventually ask whether graduates fill persistent skill gaps, remain in public service, improve acquisition outcomes, strengthen mission readiness, create technical leadership and move effectively across institutional boundaries.

Those outcomes take years.

A school can look successful long before anyone knows whether it is useful.

The workforce case also has a geographic dimension.

Space expertise is clustered. Colorado, California, Texas, Florida, Alabama, New Mexico, Virginia and other states contain concentrations of military units, NASA centers, laboratories, universities and companies. Federal employees and military members move, but technical ecosystems have gravity. A spouse's career matters. A research partner matters. A supplier base matters. A community of people who can leave one company and join another without moving across the country matters.

A single Academy campus could draw from these clusters and then redistribute graduates.

It could also become another cluster competing for the same people.

Faculty recruitment will make this obvious immediately. A first-rate professor in autonomy, propulsion, cyber, machine learning or remote sensing can work at universities, laboratories or companies. The Academy will need a value proposition for faculty just as agencies need one for graduates.

Prestige helps.

Mission helps.

Access to students and facilities helps.

But hiring rules, compensation and academic freedom will matter too.

A federal institution cannot assume people will accept bureaucratic friction simply because space is exciting.

The private sector makes the competition sharper.

Commercial space companies can offer technical professionals the experience of building and flying systems at high cadence. Software companies can offer compensation beyond federal scales. Startups can offer ownership and responsibility early. Government offers a different proposition: public mission, continuity, scale and the ability to work on problems private firms do not own.

The Academy should make that proposition explicit.

Public service should not be sold as private-sector employment with worse pay.

It is a different kind of work.

The student should know what is being exchanged. The public pays for education. The graduate accepts constraints and obligations. In return, the graduate gains access to missions, authority and responsibility that exist only because a democratic government has entrusted them to public institutions.

That bargain can be powerful for the right person.

The wrong person will experience it as captivity.

Admissions therefore becomes part of workforce retention.

If the Academy selects students mainly for technical scores and prestige, it may admit people who never wanted federal service deeply enough to thrive in it. If it selects mainly for demonstrated service orientation, it may miss technical talent that would flourish once exposed to the mission.

Selection should search for both capability and informed consent.

Applicants need to understand the obligation before the Academy benefits from the seduction of the mission.

This is another area where the astronaut comparison is useful.

NASA can attract thousands of highly qualified applicants to a handful of astronaut positions because the mission has extraordinary symbolic power. The Space Academy may enjoy a similar initial surge. The first classes could be selected from unusually deep applicant pools.

That is not the same as solving a national workforce problem.

Prestige concentrates applicants.

It does not necessarily create them.

If the Academy simply pulls the best aerospace students away from MIT, Georgia Tech, Purdue, Colorado, Stanford or other universities, the national talent pool may not grow. The government will have rearranged where people study.

The more ambitious objective is to enlarge the pool.

Recruit students who would not otherwise consider space.

Find talented programmers who think the only serious careers are in consumer technology or artificial intelligence.

Find mechanical builders who have never seen themselves inside a federal institution.

Find students from communities with little direct connection to the aerospace economy.

Find people interested in law, economics and public policy who can become technically fluent enough to work at the domain's edges.

A national academy can use prestige to change what young people imagine as possible.

That is a legitimate workforce function.

It is also difficult to measure.

The Academy would need to know whether applicants are new entrants to the space pipeline or simply the same students moving among prestigious options.

This is where partnerships matter.

A single campus cannot be the national workforce strategy. The Academy could build programs with universities, community colleges, high schools, NASA centers, military installations and industry. It could make course materials available more broadly where security permits. Faculty exchanges could move knowledge in both directions. Scholarships could complement residential admissions. Summer programs could expose students who never become Academy cadets or scholars but later enter the space workforce elsewhere.

The institution becomes more useful when its influence exceeds its enrollment.

That is how West Point and the Air Force Academy matter today. They do not commission every officer. Their standards, research, alumni and professional cultures influence services much larger than their graduating classes.

A Space Academy should be judged by the same leverage.

The workforce numbers make urgency tempting.

About 15,400 people in a service.

About three quarters of stated positions filled.

A national domain expanding.

A new academy seems like arithmetic.

But the report underneath the arithmetic tells a more important story. The service needs not simply more people but better workforce reasoning.

The Academy should be one instrument inside that reasoning.

If it becomes a substitute for it, the school will produce graduates into the same ambiguity GAO found.

If it becomes a catalyst for it, the Academy could force agencies to articulate which skills matter, which careers need continuity and what public-service experience they are willing to guarantee in return for a student's obligation.

A shortage is not defined by an empty chair.

It is defined by work that cannot be done well because the right capability is missing.

The Space Academy's first duty to the workforce problem is therefore not to fill seats.

It is to make the country more precise about which people are actually missing.
