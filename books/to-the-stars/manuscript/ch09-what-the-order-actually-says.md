# What the Order Actually Says

Government documents often become more interesting when read after the speeches stop.

The executive order establishing the Presidential Commission on the United States Space Academy is short enough that its omissions are as important as its declarations.

It does not say, in finished detail, what the Academy is.

It says what must be decided.

This is a very different document from a charter that creates a functioning service academy with settled authorities. The order creates a commission and gives it a design brief. The commission is expected to recommend how a United States Space Academy should be established and structured, then deliver that work within 120 days.

The political announcement gave the institution a name.

The legal text gave the uncertainty a list.

There are nine principal areas the commission must address. Read together, they form a kind of X-ray of the proposal. They reveal every place where the Academy's identity remains unfixed.

The first is governance.

Who owns the institution?

This is not the same as asking who chairs the commission. The NASA Administrator leads the design process, but the commission must recommend the Academy's governing structure and specifically consider whether it should be established within NASA.

That single choice could shape everything downstream.

An academy inside NASA would begin from a civilian agency's legal and cultural framework. NASA possesses deep technical expertise, a national network of centers and a long history of training people for high-consequence spaceflight. It also does not operate as an armed service academy. If students are to become commissioned officers, military authorities, personnel systems and standards must be integrated deliberately.

Put the Academy somewhere else and a different set of problems appears.

Place it under the Department of the Air Force and the military pathways become clearer, but the civilian mission may feel appended. Create an independent federal entity and neutrality improves while overhead grows. Build a consortium and flexibility improves while institutional authority becomes more complicated.

Governance is not an org-chart exercise.

It is a decision about which culture gets to define normal.

The second area is academic and leadership curriculum.

This sounds like the heart of any school, but the commission cannot answer it until the mission is narrow enough to make tradeoffs. A Space Force-only academy would build one curriculum. A NASA-led mixed academy would build another. A school intended primarily to create technical officers might emphasize engineering, physics, computing and military studies. A school serving astronauts, scientists, civil servants and warfighters would need a wider common foundation and differentiated professional tracks.

The phrase “leadership curriculum” is equally important.

It signals that the institution is not envisioned merely as a federal science university. The Academy is supposed to form people who will assume responsibility inside public institutions.

Leadership education is where the difference between knowing and being entrusted becomes visible.

A technically gifted student can calculate an orbit.

A leader may have to decide whether a system should be maneuvered at all, who must be consulted, which risks are acceptable and what consequences follow for other missions.

The curriculum must prepare for those questions without pretending undergraduate education can simulate a career.

The third area is service obligation.

Here the proposed Academy approaches the traditional federal-academy bargain. Public money pays for education. Graduates provide public service in return.

The order contemplates obligations in the Armed Forces or civilian federal service. It does not specify the exact length, track structure, assignment mechanism or remedies if a graduate fails to complete the obligation.

Those details determine whether the Academy becomes a serious workforce instrument.

A short obligation may provide weak return on a costly education. A long obligation may deter talented applicants who would otherwise consider public service. Different obligations for military and civilian tracks may be justified by the nature of the careers but complicate institutional cohesion. An obligation that begins before the government can guarantee a suitable position creates unfairness and administrative risk.

A slogan about free tuition can conceal a contract.

The commission has to write the contract.

The fourth area is applicant prerequisites.

The order specifically points toward questions such as citizenship, security-clearance eligibility, government employment and military status.

This is unusually revealing.

It suggests the Academy may not simply copy the admissions model of existing undergraduate service academies. If current government employment or military status is seriously considered, the institution could include pathways for people who have already begun careers. If security clearances become central, the Academy must decide when and how eligibility is assessed. If citizenship requirements are strict across all programs, that may be necessary for some missions and unnecessarily limiting for others.

Admissions becomes national-security architecture.

The fifth area is a process for selecting a permanent physical location.

Not a location.

A process.

That wording matters because the political competition began almost as soon as the Academy was announced. A federal institution with prestige, jobs and long-term investment naturally attracts local advocacy. Houston's connection to the announcement and Johnson Space Center gives it an obvious claim. Other states can point to military space organizations, launch centers, research universities, aerospace industries and laboratories.

The commission's duty is not to reward enthusiasm.

It is to define criteria before the criteria are reverse-engineered around a favored site.

Mission access, security, infrastructure, academic partnerships, faculty recruitment, cost, climate resilience, transportation, housing and expansion capacity could all matter. So could proximity to NASA centers or Space Force installations. The relative weighting of those factors will shape the outcome.

A transparent process cannot eliminate politics.

It can make politics explain itself.

The sixth area is administrative action.

What can the executive branch do using authority it already possesses?

This is where a commission report can become operational before Congress enacts a full statutory framework. Agencies may be able to establish pilot programs, partnerships, scholarships, temporary offices, curriculum-development efforts or other preparatory work under existing authorities.

But administrative action has limits.

A durable academy should not be built on legal ambiguity. If service obligations, commissioning powers, appropriations or governance require Congress, trying to improvise around those requirements can make the institution fragile.

The order itself recognizes this by separating administrative actions from legislative actions.

The seventh area is legislation.

This is the point where aspiration meets Article I.

Congress controls appropriations and writes laws establishing many of the durable structures federal academies depend upon. Members will have questions about cost, need, governance, duplication, admissions, military authorities, location and oversight. Committees with different jurisdictions may have interests in the same proposal. A NASA-led academy that also produces military officers could cross institutional lines familiar to the executive branch but awkward in congressional committee structure.

Legislation forces specificity.

How much will it cost?

How many students?

Which department owns the property?

Who appoints the superintendent or president?

Who awards degrees?

Who commissions military graduates?

What happens if a student leaves?

Which agency receives civilian graduates?

What authorities exist for faculty hiring and research?

A commission can recommend principles. Statutory language must eventually decide who can do what.

The eighth area is coordination with existing programs.

This may be the most important line in the order.

It prevents the Academy from pretending the country is starting with nothing.

The Air Force Academy already commissions Guardians. ROTC and Officer Training School already produce Space Force officers. STARCOM already trains them. NASA already operates education, internship, fellowship and workforce programs. Universities already teach aerospace, physics, computer science and the other disciplines the Academy will need. Federal laboratories and companies already train specialists.

Coordination can be bureaucratic code for “do not duplicate.”

It should be stronger than that.

The Academy should be designed around what the existing ecosystem does well.

If USAFA already provides excellent foundational education for some Guardians, preserve it. If STARCOM already teaches service-specific skills effectively after commissioning, do not reproduce them in the freshman year. If universities lead the world in certain technical fields, partner rather than assume a federal campus must dominate every specialty. If NASA centers already have unique facilities, use them.

A new institution earns legitimacy by knowing where it is unnecessary.

The ninth area is implementation strategy.

This sounds like the last step because it appears at the end of the list.

It reaches backward into all the others.

A strategy must sequence decisions. The government could attempt to settle everything before admitting students: law, site, campus, faculty, accreditation, curriculum and service agreements. That would produce a more complete institution at opening and could take years.

Or it could begin smaller.

Temporary facilities.

A pilot cohort.

Partnerships with existing universities and academies.

A core faculty with visiting instructors from NASA, the Space Force and industry.

A limited number of service tracks until placement systems are proven.

This approach would resemble the early Air Force Academy in one respect: the institution could begin before the permanent campus is finished.

It would differ in another. The modern federal education ecosystem makes distributed beginnings far more practical than they were in 1955.

The implementation question therefore contains a deeper choice.

Is the Academy primarily a place or a system?

Traditional academies are both. The residential campus is central to their culture because education, military training, physical development and peer life occur together. The intensity is intentional. Students do not merely attend classes. They live inside the institution.

A hybrid Space Academy might decide that this residential model is indispensable for common identity.

It might also decide that a networked institution connected to NASA centers, military bases and universities better reflects the domain it serves.

The executive order does not decide.

This is the recurring pattern of the document.

Its language is confident about the need to design and cautious about the design itself.

That is appropriate at this stage.

The danger comes from public conversation outrunning the legal text.

Announcements compress uncertainty because institutions are easier to explain as nouns. “The United States Space Academy” sounds more tangible than “a presidential commission evaluating the governance, curriculum, service obligations, applicant criteria, location process, administrative and legislative requirements, program coordination and implementation strategy for a proposed academy.”

The noun wins.

Then expectations harden.

A city begins campaigning for a campus.

Students ask how to apply.

Observers assume graduates will all join the Space Force.

Supporters describe the institution as established.

Critics attack a fully formed version that nobody has actually proposed.

The order itself provides the antidote.

Read the verbs.

Establish a commission.

Evaluate.

Develop recommendations.

Consider.

Report.

Then, subject to approval, legislation, law and appropriations, implement.

The Academy is at the beginning of that chain.

This should not diminish the significance of the announcement.

Government often changes reality by changing which questions receive organized attention. A presidential commission with named leadership, a deadline and a formal mandate creates momentum. Agencies must assign people. Staff work begins. Existing programs are inventoried. Legal authorities are examined. Site advocates organize. Members of Congress receive proposals. Journalists ask questions that were hypothetical a week earlier.

The order creates a process capable of producing an institution.

That is real.

It is simply not the same thing as the institution itself.

The commission's composition reinforces the breadth of the task. Science and technology policy sits beside economic policy. Defense sits beside civilian space. Education and labor matter because the problem is human capital. NASA leads because the proposal reaches beyond military training.

This is a coalition design problem.

Coalitions can produce durable institutions because multiple constituencies become invested.

They can also produce institutions with too many missions.

The Academy could become the answer to every concern anyone has about the space workforce: STEM education, military readiness, astronaut development, NASA recruitment, commercialization, international competition, regional economic development, technological leadership and national prestige.

No school can carry all of that without losing clarity.

The order's nine tasks should therefore be read as opportunities to narrow, not expand, the mission.

Governance asks who owns the problem.

Curriculum asks what graduates must know.

Service obligation asks what the public receives.

Prerequisites ask who the students are.

Location asks what physical presence is required.

Administrative action asks what can begin now.

Legislation asks what requires democratic authorization.

Coordination asks what should remain elsewhere.

Implementation asks what sequence keeps mistakes reversible.

Each question should eliminate possibilities.

By the time the commission finishes, the Academy should be less vague than it was on August 28.

If the report simply adds aspirations, it will have failed.

The hardest recommendation may be coordination because it requires humility before existing institutions.

It is easier to design a new school when the old schools are treated as insufficient by definition. It is harder to say precisely why the Air Force Academy, ROTC, OTS, STARCOM, NASA programs and universities together leave a gap that only a new Academy can fill.

That gap is the whole case.

The order implicitly recognizes it by requiring coordination rather than replacement.

The Academy is not being asked to become the American space-education system.

It is being asked to find a place inside one.

That should also shape legislation. A statute can encode boundaries. It can require partnerships. It can preserve multiple commissioning sources. It can cap enrollment while the model is tested. It can require periodic review. It can separate authorities that should not be concentrated.

Federal institutions often become permanent because permanence is assumed at birth.

A more adaptive approach would treat some founding choices as experiments.

The curriculum can be reviewed frequently.

Service-track demand can be adjusted.

Partnerships can expand or contract.

Admissions criteria can be tested against outcomes.

The permanent location, once built, is harder to change.

The legal charter should distinguish between the parts that need stability and the parts that need learning.

This may sound administrative rather than visionary.

It is where vision survives.

The history of American spaceflight is full of ambitious goals made real by people who converted them into systems, responsibilities, interfaces, schedules and tests. Apollo did not reach the Moon because the national objective was emotionally powerful. It reached the Moon because organizations learned to translate an objective into millions of disciplined decisions.

An academy requires the same conversion at smaller physical scale and longer human timescale.

The executive order is the mission statement before the engineering review.

The commission has to turn it into architecture.

And the most important thing the architecture can preserve is the honesty already visible in the order's structure.

Almost everything important remains a choice.
