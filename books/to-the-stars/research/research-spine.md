## A. The August 28, 2026 order

### Established

President Donald J. Trump signed Executive Order 14423, *Establishing the United States Space Academy*, on August 28, 2026.

The order establishes a **Presidential Commission on the United States Space Academy**. It does not itself create a functioning degree-granting academy with an enrolled student body.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman is named chair. The Assistant to the President for Science and Technology and the Assistant to the President for Economic Policy are vice chairs. The NASA Deputy Administrator is executive director. The commission also includes the Secretary of War, White House Chief of Staff, OMB Director, National Security Advisor, Secretary of the Air Force, and other invited federal officials.

The order describes the proposed Space Academy as a **NASA-led Federal academy** combining rigorous technical education, leadership development, discipline, and a durable commitment to public service.

The purpose section names astronauts, scientists, engineers, operators, entrepreneurs, civil servants, and warfighters as categories the nation needs to recruit, train, and retain.

The commission has 120 days from August 28 to submit recommendations. Calendar arithmetic places that date on December 26, 2026.

Required topics include:

- governance and organizational structure;
- authorities and accreditation;
- relationships among agencies;
- academic and leadership curriculum;
- degree, experiential, and development programs;
- service obligations, including Armed Forces and civilian Federal service;
- applicant prerequisites, including citizenship, clearances, employment, or military status;
- a process for choosing a permanent physical location;
- pilot programs, partnerships, and preparatory initiatives;
- legislation;
- coordination with existing federal education programs;
- implementation sequencing.

Implementation is conditioned on presidential approval, necessary legislative action, applicable law, and available fiscal resources.

## B. What the Space Force already has

### Established

The U.S. Space Force was created on December 20, 2019 through the FY2020 National Defense Authorization Act. It is a separate armed service organized within the Department of the Air Force.

The U.S. Air Force Academy commissioned 86 officers into the Space Force in 2020, the first academy cohort to enter the new service.

The Air Force Academy describes itself as the premier commissioning path for officers wishing to serve in the Space Force. Its current space-program page says roughly ten percent of each graduating class—approximately 100 cadets—will commission into the Space Force.

New Space Force officers also commission through Reserve Officer Training Corps and Officer Training School.

STARCOM's Officer Training Course is the Space Force's initial skills training program for newly commissioned officers. STARCOM reported in June 2026 that more than 350 Guardians had graduated since the course's inception in August 2025. The course gives students instruction across space operations, cyberspace warfare, intelligence, and acquisitions before a capstone exercise.

STARCOM also operates or supports professional military education, advanced training, doctrine, testing, wargaming, and readiness programs. In 2026 it graduated the first class of a Space Component Staff Course designed to standardize joint command-and-control preparation.

### Interpretation

A new academy cannot be justified factually by saying the Space Force currently lacks a way to commission or train officers. It plainly has multiple pathways.

The stronger pro-academy claim is different: existing pipelines are distributed across institutions created for other purposes. A new academy might provide a dedicated undergraduate culture and a shared civil-military-technical formation. Whether that benefit justifies duplication and cost is an open question.

## C. The closest historical precedent: U.S. Air Force Academy

### Established

The Air Force became a separate service in 1947. Advocates had discussed a dedicated air academy for years before independence.

Congress authorized the U.S. Air Force Academy in 1954.

A site commission considered hundreds of proposals. The Academy's own history says the commission traveled 21,000 miles and considered 580 sites in 45 states before narrowing the field and selecting Colorado Springs.

The first class of 306 men entered in 1955 at temporary facilities at Lowry Air Force Base while the permanent campus was being built.

Cadets moved to the permanent site in 1958 and the first class graduated in 1959.

The Academy activated an Astronautics Department in 1958 and later offered an astronautical engineering major. Long before the Space Force existed, space expertise had become part of Air Force Academy education.

In 2020, 86 Academy graduates commissioned into Space Force.

### Interpretation

The Air Force Academy offers both sides of the Space Academy argument.

Pro: a new service can eventually require a school built around its distinctive domain, culture, technology, and mission.

Skeptical: the institution created for airpower successfully expanded into astronautics and now commissions Guardians, showing that a service academy can adapt without duplicating itself.

This ambiguity should drive Chapters 2, 7, and 8.

## D. NASA's civilian foundation

### Established

President Dwight D. Eisenhower asked Congress in 1958 to create a civilian space agency. Congress passed the National Aeronautics and Space Act, and Eisenhower signed it July 29, 1958. NASA began operations October 1.

The original act declared a national policy that space activities should be devoted to peaceful purposes for the benefit of all mankind and assigned U.S.-sponsored nonmilitary aeronautical and space activity to a civilian agency, while activities primarily associated with weapons, military operations, or national defense remained the responsibility of the Department of Defense.

The act also explicitly contemplated exchange of discoveries and information between NASA and defense agencies.

### Primary sources

NASA, National Aeronautics and Space Act of 1958 (historical text):  
https://www.nasa.gov/history/national-aeronautics-and-space-act-of-1958-unamended/

NASA history of the Act:  
https://www.nasa.gov/history/65-years-ago-the-national-aeronautics-and-space-act-of-1958-creates-nasa/

### Interpretation

The civil-military boundary in U.S. space policy was never a wall. NASA and defense institutions have exchanged technology, people, launches, research, and operational experience for decades. But the distinction is foundational enough that a NASA-led academy designed in part for future warfighters raises a legitimate governance question.

Do not overstate this as a legal contradiction. The order asks a commission to propose a structure; it does not merge NASA and Space Force or assign NASA responsibility for military operations.

## E. Contemporary criticism and support

### Administration case

The White House and NASA frame the academy as a workforce and leadership institution for expanding national ambitions in security, exploration, science, and commerce. President Trump linked the proposal to growth in the Space Force and commercial space industry. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman described future needs spanning engineers, scientists, technicians, operators, and astronauts.

Primary sources: White House fact sheet and NASA release above.

### Duplication critique

Senator John Hickenlooper of Colorado publicly called the plan duplicative and costly on August 28, arguing that the Air Force Academy already trains future Space Force leaders.

Source:  
https://www.hickenlooper.senate.gov/press_releases/hickenlooper-questions-trumps-space-academy-announcement/

This is a politically interested source—Colorado has a direct stake in existing space institutions—but the underlying duplication question is factual and must be examined on its merits.

### Civil-military concern

Contemporary reporting has quoted former NASA leadership warning that a military-academy model could blur NASA's civilian mission. Use such commentary only with attribution and alongside the statutory history of NASA's civilian role.

Secondary source example: Ars Technica, August 28, 2026, reporting comments by former NASA Deputy Administrator Lori Garver:  
https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/08/trump-calls-for-creation-of-a-space-academy-to-train-future-nasa-leaders/

### Implementation skepticism

Reuters and other outlets note that a permanent academy will require money, administrative design, and likely congressional action beyond the executive order.

Secondary source: Reuters, August 28, 2026, *Trump signs order to create a US Space Academy*.

## F. Terms the manuscript must use carefully

### “Soldiers”

Do not call Space Force personnel soldiers. Uniformed members of the U.S. Space Force are Guardians. “Soldier” ordinarily refers to Army personnel. The academy proposal may produce warfighters or service members, but its graduates are not yet defined.

### “Space Force Academy”

Use only when describing the concept informally, quoting a source, discussing prior proposals, or making a comparison. The official 2026 name is **United States Space Academy**. The executive order describes it as a proposed NASA-led Federal academy, not formally as the U.S. Space Force Academy.

### “Established”

The administration and NASA use language saying the President signed an order “to establish” the academy, while the order itself establishes a commission and directs it to propose key details for the academy. In narrative prose, distinguish political establishment of the project from operational establishment of a school.

### “Service academy”

Do not assume the final institution will have the same statutory status, admissions rules, commissioning requirements, or governance as West Point, Annapolis, USAFA, Coast Guard Academy, or Merchant Marine Academy until legislation or official recommendations establish that.

## H. Questions for later reporting

These are reporting prompts, not assumed facts.

1. Why was NASA selected to chair rather than the Department of the Air Force or Space Force?
2. What workforce shortage is the academy intended to solve quantitatively?
3. How many graduates per year would meaningfully affect that shortage?
4. Which existing programs are judged insufficient, and by what evidence?
5. Will the academy commission Space Force officers directly?
6. Can civilians and military cadets share one undergraduate institution under different service obligations?
7. Will applicants need congressional nominations?
8. What is the proposed cost per graduate?
9. Which degrees require dedicated laboratories or secure facilities?
10. Can existing NASA centers or military installations host a temporary first class?
11. How will accreditation work if the institution begins through executive authorities before comprehensive legislation?
12. What role will industry play without turning public education into company-specific training?
13. What rights and obligations will students have if they leave before graduation?
14. How will the academy preserve NASA's civilian identity while educating warfighters?
15. How will it avoid draining faculty and students from USAFA or existing aerospace programs?
16. Will enlisted Guardians have pathways into or through the academy?
17. Will international allied students be admitted eventually?
18. What parts of the curriculum will be classified?
19. What does “entrepreneur” mean in an academy tied to public-service obligations?
20. What outcome five or ten years after opening would prove the academy was worth creating?
