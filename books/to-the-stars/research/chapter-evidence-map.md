# Chapter evidence map — To The Stars

Baseline: September 3, 2026.

This file is the chapter-by-chapter audit trail for the complete first draft. It is not a bibliography pasted into prose. It identifies the source families supporting concrete claims, the interpretive work each chapter performs, and the facts that would require revision as the United States Space Academy proposal develops.

The general rule is simple: later primary documents supersede the September 3 baseline. Interpretations may survive; factual descriptions do not get to remain frozen when the institution changes.

## Front matter

Evidence role: identify the work as a nonfiction narrative in progress and state the September 3, 2026 fact boundary.

Sources: A-D.

Revise if: the Academy becomes operational or the book's edition date moves beyond the baseline without updating the description.

## Prologue — The Order at Johnson

Sources: A-D; contemporary reporting only for reaction or setting not established in the primary releases.

Established claims: August 28 signing; Johnson Space Center; commission rather than enrolled university; NASA chair; 120-day design clock; breadth of target professions.

Interpretation: the location and governance make the proposal more complicated than a simple “West Point for Space Force.”

Revise if: later official records correct the event setting or define the institution more narrowly/broadly.

## Chapter 1 — The Sixth Service Has No School of Its Own

Sources: E-H, L, A.

Established claims: Space Force is a separate armed service; USAFA already commissions Guardians; multiple commissioning and training pathways exist; STARCOM provides common post-commissioning training.

Interpretation: absence of a dedicated service academy is not the same as absence of an officer pipeline.

Falsification trigger: a later statutory Space Force service academy or major change to commissioning pathways.

## Chapter 2 — The Academy Question

Sources: F, A.

Established claims: Air Force independence preceded its academy; Congress authorized USAFA in 1954; the first class began at temporary Lowry facilities while the permanent campus was built.

Interpretation: new-domain institutional independence does not automatically imply immediate educational independence.

## Chapter 3 — Why NASA Is in the Chair

Sources: A-C, K.

Established claims: NASA was intentionally established as a civilian agency for nonmilitary aeronautical and space activity while defense-related functions remained with DoD; the 2026 commission is chaired by NASA's Administrator.

Interpretation: NASA leadership is logical for a broad space-workforce academy and simultaneously creates a real civil-military governance question.

Guardrail: do not describe the proposal as a legal merger of NASA and Space Force.

## Chapter 4 — A Profession Bigger Than a Branch

Sources: A-C, E-J.

Established claims: the order explicitly names multiple professions; current U.S. space capability is produced across military, civil and commercial institutions.

Interpretation: the strongest pro-Academy theory is shared professional formation across interfaces, not simply additional technical degree capacity.

## Chapter 5 — West Point and the Engineering Republic

Source families: U.S. Military Academy official history and established histories of early federal engineering education; the manuscript uses the example to show how professional schools can create capacity beyond their immediate commissioning mission.

Audit rule: numerical or biographical claims beyond the broad institutional history must be verified against USMA/Army or high-quality historical sources before publication.

Interpretation: historical precedent, not proof that a Space Academy should exist.

## Chapter 6 — Annapolis and the Sea

Source families: U.S. Naval Academy official history, curriculum and professional-development material.

Audit rule: preserve the difference between analogy and identity. Maritime and space domains share systems, navigation, logistics, law and commons problems, but one is not a historical template for the other.

## Chapter 7 — The Air Academy Fight

Sources: F and congressional/history material cited in the book bible.

Established claims: long pre-1954 debate, authorization, national site search, Lowry temporary start and later move to Colorado Springs.

Interpretation: purpose, cost and location were contested in the closest service-creation precedent.

## Chapter 8 — Colorado Springs Learns Space

Sources: F-G.

Established claims: USAFA developed astronautics education before Space Force existed and now commissions Guardians.

Interpretation: existing institutional adaptation is both an argument for Space Academy skeptics and a baseline the new school must outperform or complement.

## Interlude — The University Question

Sources: A, F-J, and public documentation from existing university/federal partnership programs as later reporting deepens.

Established claim kept narrow: the United States already has mature civilian universities teaching the technical disciplines the Academy is likely to require, and current federal/service education already uses partnerships.

Interpretation: the Academy must be compared with distributed scholarship, ROTC, fellowship and partnership alternatives rather than with “doing nothing.”

Publication audit: add named university-program examples only after checking their current status and avoiding unsupported rankings.

## Chapter 9 — Guardians Are Born in Borrowed Institutions

Sources: E-H, F-G.

Established claims: the new service inherited commissioning pathways from Department of the Air Force structures; USAFA, ROTC and OTS can produce Space Force officers.

Interpretation: inherited institutions can provide capability while a new service develops culture of its own.

## Chapter 10 — STARCOM and the Making of a Space Warfighter

Sources: H-J.

Established claims: STARCOM exists to develop training/readiness/doctrine/test functions; OTC gives newly commissioned officers a multidisciplinary baseline; Space Force PME already uses external partners.

Guardrail: the Academy should not be described as replacing STARCOM absent official policy.

## Chapter 11 — The Operator, the Engineer, the Acquirer, the Coder

Sources: H-J and official Space Force workforce/career material.

Established claims: Space Force officer formation explicitly crosses operations, cyber, intelligence and acquisition areas.

Interpretation: breadth is necessary but can become shallowness; Academy curriculum must protect deep specialty.

## Interlude — The People Who Keep It Running

Sources: P-Q plus Space Force enlisted career/training material.

Established claims: enlisted Guardians receive substantial technical training; the service's 2026 framework emphasizes tactical expertise, later leadership and career-long development; service leaders explicitly connect lengthy training investment to retention and operational mastery.

Interpretation: formal authority and technical expertise are not identical; a national space profession cannot be defined only by officer commissions and bachelor's degrees.

Guardrail: do not infer that enlisted Guardians will be admitted to the Academy; the interlude frames that as an open design question.

## Chapter 12 — The Civilian-Military Boundary

Sources: K, A-C, E.

Established claims: the 1958 Space Act distinguishes civilian/nonmilitary NASA functions from defense responsibilities while allowing exchange and cooperation; Space Force has a military mission.

Interpretation: effective integration should make roles clearer rather than erase the boundary.

## Interlude — Allies in the Room

Sources: M-O, N, G for international-cadet precedent at USAFA.

Established claims: USSF strategy treats allies and partners as integral to force design/development/employment; coalition tactics activity in 2026 included Five Eyes partners; the service operates deliberate international-affairs education.

Open question: whether the Space Academy will admit international students.

Guardrail: never write an allied Academy cohort as fact until admissions policy exists.

## Chapter 13 — Who Gets In

Sources: A plus admissions statutes/policies of existing federal academies used as comparisons.

Established claim: the order instructs the commission to consider applicant prerequisites including citizenship, clearances, employment or military status.

Open: nomination system, age limits, military/civilian categories, international admission, preparatory school, transfer rules, exact standards.

## Chapter 14 — What They Must Learn

Sources: A; USAFA curricula; STARCOM training sources H-J; university accreditation/curriculum comparisons where named.

Established claim: the commission must recommend academic, leadership, degree, experiential and development programs.

Everything resembling a proposed course list in the chapter is analytical design work unless tied to an official recommendation.

## Chapter 15 — The Service Obligation

Sources: A; current federal-academy/service-obligation statutes and official policies; U.S. Merchant Marine Academy model used as an analogy where applicable.

Established claim: the order explicitly requires recommendations for Armed Forces and civilian Federal service obligations.

Open: duration, enforcement, graduate-school deferrals, civilian job matching, repayment, private-sector eligibility.

Guardrail: the order does not establish an entrepreneurship/private-sector service pathway.

## Interlude — The Entrepreneur Problem

Sources: A-B; Space Force commercial-space strategy/current acquisition statements used for ecosystem context.

Established claim: “entrepreneurs” appear in the order's workforce framing while the service-obligation clause explicitly names Armed Forces and civilian Federal service.

Interpretation: the Academy must teach commercial incentives without automatically equating private employment with taxpayer-repaying public service.

Falsification trigger: commission or Congress creates a qualifying private-sector service pathway.

## Chapter 16 — The Campus War

Sources: A; F for USAFA site precedent; public statements from actual state/local bidders only when documented.

Established claim: the commission must recommend a process for selecting a permanent physical location; no permanent location was selected by September 3.

Guardrail: lobbying is not site selection.

## Chapter 17 — Congress Has the Pen

Sources: A-D; constitutional/statutory framework; existing academy statutes; congressional statements including L.

Established claims: implementation is subject to law and appropriations; the commission must recommend needed legislation.

Interpretation: durable degree authority, obligations, appropriations and a permanent campus are likely to require congressional participation, with exact needs depending on final architecture.

Guardrail: do not claim every possible design element necessarily requires a new statute if existing authority could lawfully support a pilot.

## Interlude — The Academy Without Walls

Sources: A (pilot/partnership/preparatory and permanent-location requirements), F (Lowry precedent), existing federal/university partnership sources.

Interpretation: the commission should test which capabilities the Academy must own and which can remain networked; this is a design argument, not an announced distributed-campus policy.

## Chapter 18 — The First Class

Sources: A, F, federal accreditation/admissions/service precedents.

Opening fact: as of September 3 there is no first class and no announced admissions process.

The entire chapter is intentionally written from that absence. It describes decisions that must be made before students can knowingly accept an offer.

Falsification/update trigger: first-class date, admissions rules, pilot cohort, faculty model, temporary facilities, accreditation or service commitments become official. When they do, replace scenarios with the actual record.

## Chapter 19 — A National Space Profession

Sources: A-B, H-J, M-Q.

Interpretation: the Academy earns its cost only if it improves interfaces among institutions while preserving distinct civil, military, scientific and commercial responsibilities.

This is the book's constructive thesis, not an established government finding.

Future test: graduate/agency outcomes, cross-institution collaboration, public-service retention, program cost and effects beyond the alumni body.

## Chapter 20 — The School That Could Fail

Sources: all above plus future GAO, congressional oversight, accreditation and outcome data.

This chapter is deliberately falsifiable. It names failure modes before outcome data exist: duplication, mission confusion, prestige capture, weak depth, obsolescence, faculty weakness, politicization, service-obligation mismatch, scale, regional/industry capture, bureaucratic layering and growth without impact.

Future evidence must be allowed to show either success or failure. Do not rewrite the chapter later merely to protect the founding thesis.

## Epilogue — To the Stars

Sources: the factual baseline throughout the research spine; no invented future events.

Interpretation: education and institutional stewardship are part of space infrastructure even when machines receive more public attention.

Guardrail: the unidentified possible future student is framed explicitly as a possibility, not a sourced person.

## Back matter

Purpose: disclose timing, source method, terminology and revision triggers.

No later edition should preserve the September 3 unknowns as if they remain unknown. Revise the body when reality resolves them.

## Live falsification list

The following facts were **not decided** at the baseline and must be checked before any new edition or substantive revision:

- permanent site;
- legal/statutory status;
- governance;
- relationship to NASA, Department of the Air Force, Space Force and other agencies;
- whether and how military commissions occur;
- civilian service categories;
- any private-sector qualifying service;
- length and enforcement of obligations;
- first-class date and size;
- admissions and nomination systems;
- international admission;
- enlisted pathways;
- degrees and curriculum;
- faculty model;
- accreditation;
- temporary facilities or pilot cohort;
- construction and operating budget;
- congressional authorization/appropriation;
- official partnerships;
- whether the project is delayed, substantially redesigned or canceled.

A future source that resolves one of these items is not an “update” to append casually. It is a reason to revise every manuscript passage whose uncertainty has been superseded.
