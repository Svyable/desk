# Book bible — To The Stars

## Working title

**To The Stars: The Story of the United States Space Academy**

Author: Sven Hardy Benson  
Format: narrative nonfiction / institutional history / current affairs  
Target: 20 numbered chapters plus prologue and epilogue  
Target manuscript length: 75,000–90,000 words  
Draft status at launch: the institution itself is still being designed

## The book in one sentence

The United States has decided that space may require not only a military service, a civilian exploration agency, and a commercial industry, but a new federal academy designed to produce people capable of moving among all three—and the fight over that school reveals what America thinks space is becoming.

## The opening fact

On August 28, 2026, at NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, President Donald J. Trump signed Executive Order 14423, *Establishing the United States Space Academy*. The order created a Presidential Commission chaired by NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman and instructed it to recommend the academy's governance, curriculum, service obligations, admissions prerequisites, permanent location, legal authorities, and implementation strategy within 120 days.

The order does **not** by itself create a functioning four-year university, admit a class, appropriate construction money, select a campus, establish a curriculum, or determine that every graduate will enter the U.S. Space Force. It begins a design and implementation process. The commission's report is due 120 days after August 28, 2026—December 26, 2026—after which presidential approval, congressional action where necessary, appropriations, accreditation, administration, construction or reuse of facilities, and recruiting could still stand between the proposal and an entering class.

That distinction is essential to the book. The story begins at the moment an idea becomes official enough to reorganize political expectations but not yet concrete enough to know what it will become.

## The central surprise

The easy description is “West Point for the Space Force.” The executive order is more interesting than that.

It describes a **NASA-led Federal academy** intended to prepare astronauts, scientists, engineers, operators, entrepreneurs, civil servants, and warfighters. It asks the commission to consider both Armed Forces service and civilian Federal service obligations. The White House fact sheet says graduates could support the military, civil, and broader space enterprise.

So the core institutional question is not simply whether the Space Force deserves its own service academy. It is whether the United States wants to create a professional school for a national space corps broader than any single agency or branch.

That makes the book about a boundary.

In 1958, after Sputnik, the United States deliberately created NASA as a civilian agency for nonmilitary space activity while leaving military space work to the Department of Defense. In 2019 it created the Space Force as a separate armed service under the Department of the Air Force. In 2026 it proposed a federal academy chaired by NASA that may educate people whose service obligations could lead to either side of that line.

A school sounds smaller than an agency or a military branch. Institutions that educate future leaders can outlast both administrations and doctrines. The academy question is therefore a question about what kind of profession the United States believes space work is becoming.

## Narrative promise

The reader should finish the book understanding five things:

1. What the August 28, 2026 order actually does and does not do.
2. Why the United States already has a substantial pipeline for Space Force officers and space professionals—and therefore why the new academy is not merely filling a blank.
3. How previous service academies were created when new domains of warfare and state capacity became institutionally important.
4. Why NASA's role in the proposal is both logical and constitutionally/culturally provocative.
5. What choices about curriculum, service, location, governance, and culture will determine whether the Space Academy becomes a consequential national institution, a duplicative prestige project, or something in between.

## Voice and stance

The voice should be calm, concrete, curious, and unsentimental. The book is neither an administration brochure nor an opposition brief.

The academy is too new for retrospective triumphalism. The manuscript must preserve uncertainty. When the commission has not decided something, say so. When a critic raises a real duplication problem, carry the criticism fairly. When advocates point to real workforce, readiness, or cross-domain coordination problems, let those problems survive without turning them into proof that one specific institutional solution must work.

Avoid future-history narration. Do not write scenes from a campus that has not been selected. Do not invent cadets, dialogue, traditions, uniforms, mascots, graduation ceremonies, or internal commission debates. Future-facing chapters may describe design choices and plausible consequences, but they must be labeled as choices or scenarios, not facts.

## The protagonist

There is no single human protagonist.

The protagonist is the American space profession trying to become legible to itself.

Recurring human figures will include presidents, NASA administrators, Space Force leaders, academy founders, cadets and Guardians where sourced, engineers, astronauts, educators, legislators, and critics. But the narrative follows an institution coming into focus.

## The antagonists

No political faction should be cast as the villain. The recurring antagonists are structural:

- duplication;
- bureaucratic boundary problems;
- the difficulty of educating for a domain where technology changes faster than universities;
- scarcity of technically deep people who also understand command, public service, law, acquisition, and operations;
- rivalry among cities and agencies;
- the mismatch between four-year educational cycles and rapid technical change;
- the danger that prestige architecture outruns institutional purpose;
- the danger that an academy designed for national integration instead hardens silos;
- the tension between NASA's civilian identity and the military mission of the Space Force;
- the possibility that existing institutions can solve the same problem more cheaply.

## Timeline spine

### Before the Space Force

- 1802: U.S. Military Academy established at West Point.
- 1845: U.S. Naval Academy established at Annapolis.
- 1947: U.S. Air Force becomes an independent service.
- 1954: Congress authorizes the U.S. Air Force Academy after years of debate over whether the new service needs its own school.
- 1955: first Air Force Academy class enters at temporary facilities at Lowry Air Force Base.
- 1958: Congress creates NASA as a civilian agency for nonmilitary space activities while defense-related space remains a Department of Defense responsibility.
- 1958: the Air Force Academy activates an Astronautics Department.
- 1965: the Academy offers an astronautical engineering major.
- 1982: Air Force Space Command is established, helping consolidate military space expertise within the Air Force.

### The Space Force era

- December 20, 2019: the U.S. Space Force is established as the sixth branch of the armed forces and the first new branch since 1947.
- 2020: 86 U.S. Air Force Academy cadets commission into the new Space Force, creating its first academy cohort.
- August 2021: Space Training and Readiness Command (STARCOM) becomes a Space Force field command focused on education, training, doctrine, test, and readiness.
- 2023: Space Force launches officer intermediate and senior-level professional military education in partnership with Johns Hopkins SAIS.
- 2024: STARCOM begins the Space Force Officer Training Course, designed to create a common multidisciplinary baseline for newly commissioned officers.
- 2026: STARCOM reports more than 350 Guardians have graduated from OTC since the program's inception; it also creates a standardized Space Component Staff Course for joint command-and-control work.

### The Space Academy proposal

- August 28, 2026: Executive Order 14423 creates the Presidential Commission on the United States Space Academy.
- September 3, 2026: the executive order is published in the Federal Register as the manuscript research begins.
- December 26, 2026: 120-day commission report deadline implied by the order's date.

Later milestones must be added only after they actually occur.

## Structural architecture

### Prologue — The Order at Johnson

Open at Johnson Space Center on August 28, 2026. The setting matters because the announcement is made at a NASA center during an event honoring Artemis II astronauts, not at the Pentagon or at an existing service academy. Establish the order, the 120-day clock, and the immediate ambiguity: a school compared publicly to West Point but chaired by NASA and written to serve military and civilian space.

The prologue ends with the first governing question: what problem is this academy supposed to solve?

### Part I — A New Academy

#### Chapter 1 — The Sixth Service Has No School of Its Own

Explain the intuitive case. Space Force is a separate military branch but does not have a separate service academy. Then complicate it: the Air Force Academy is already the premier service-academy commissioning path for Guardians, roughly ten percent of a graduating class may commission into Space Force, and ROTC and OTS also produce officers. Every new Guardian officer attends a common Space Force Officer Training Course. The institution does not begin from zero.

Narrative question: if a pipeline exists, what is missing?

#### Chapter 2 — The Academy Question

Use the creation of the Air Force Academy as the closest historical precedent. Airpower leaders argued for decades that a separate domain needed a distinct professional school. Congress did not simply mirror West Point immediately after 1947. It debated purpose, cost, curriculum, and location. The first class trained in temporary quarters while the permanent campus was still being built.

Narrative question: when does a new branch need a school rather than access to somebody else's school?

#### Chapter 3 — Why NASA Is in the Chair

Go back to 1958 and the civilian/military split in U.S. space governance. Explain NASA's workforce and technical role, the academy order's broader list of intended professionals, and why NASA leadership makes sense for an institution that is not formally described as a Space Force academy. Then carry the tension: NASA's identity was deliberately civilian; a school serving future warfighters tests the boundary in a new way.

Narrative question: can one academy strengthen coordination without blurring missions that were intentionally separated?

#### Chapter 4 — A Profession Bigger Than a Branch

Introduce the idea that American space power is produced by overlapping communities: military operators, acquisition professionals, intelligence analysts, scientists, civil servants, launch providers, spacecraft manufacturers, software engineers, researchers, and astronauts. The academy proposal can be read as an attempt to create a common professional culture among people who currently enter through different pipelines.

Narrative question: is “space professional” becoming a national category comparable to officer, engineer, or diplomat?

### Part II — How America Learned to Train for New Domains

#### Chapter 5 — West Point and the Engineering Republic

Service academies do more than commission officers. West Point helped create an early national engineering elite. Use it to show how the state can use a military school to build civilian technical capacity indirectly.

#### Chapter 6 — Annapolis and the Sea

A maritime profession requires technical knowledge, command culture, navigation, logistics, law, and a relationship with a global commons. Use the Naval Academy to examine how an academy embodies a domain.

#### Chapter 7 — The Air Academy Fight

Deep history of the long push for an air academy, the Stearns-Eisenhower Board, 1954 authorization, site competition, temporary Lowry start, and the challenge of designing an institution for a technology that was still changing quickly.

#### Chapter 8 — Colorado Springs Learns Space

Trace how the Air Force Academy became space-capable long before Space Force existed: astronautics, graduates in space, space programs, and later commissioning of Guardians. Establish the strongest empirical argument against duplication: an existing academy has already adapted.

### Part III — The Force Before the Academy

#### Chapter 9 — Guardians Are Born in Borrowed Institutions

Map commissioning pathways and early Space Force identity. Follow what it means for a new service to inherit education from the Department of the Air Force while trying to create a distinct culture.

#### Chapter 10 — STARCOM and the Making of a Space Warfighter

Follow STARCOM, Officer Training Course, component staff training, professional military education, doctrine, and the effort to create a common Guardian baseline after commissioning. Show the difference between undergraduate education and service-specific formation.

#### Chapter 11 — The Operator, the Engineer, the Acquirer, the Coder

Space operations mix technical specialties that military organizations often separate. Explore whether an academy should create broad generalists first or early specialists, and what the Space Force has learned from multidisciplinary training.

#### Chapter 12 — The Civilian-Military Boundary

Return to the 1958 Space Act. Explore NASA, DoD, intelligence, NOAA, FAA, Commerce, and industry as distinct space institutions. This chapter should carry the strongest version of the concern that a NASA-led academy serving warfighters could confuse civil and military roles.

### Part IV — Designing the Space Academy

These chapters must be updated as the commission releases real recommendations.

#### Chapter 13 — Who Gets In

Admissions, citizenship, clearances, military status, nomination systems, access, geography, technical prerequisites, and whether the academy should resemble service academies or a federal technical university.

#### Chapter 14 — What They Must Learn

Curriculum choices: orbital mechanics, astronautics, propulsion, remote sensing, cyber, AI, software, systems engineering, acquisition, strategy, law, economics, ethics, leadership, physical training, operational exercises, internships, and research. Ask what must be taught in-house and what can be obtained from existing universities.

#### Chapter 15 — The Service Obligation

The order explicitly asks for recommendations on Armed Forces and civilian Federal service obligations. This chapter examines how service obligations create a professional corps, change the economics of tuition, shape recruiting, and constrain graduates.

#### Chapter 16 — The Campus War

Site selection is political because academies create identity, jobs, infrastructure, and prestige. Track actual bids and political coalitions only as they become public. Compare with the Air Force Academy's historic national site competition.

#### Chapter 17 — Congress Has the Pen

An executive order can create a commission. Durable federal institutions often need legislation and appropriations. Follow authorization, appropriations, hearings, accreditation, and statutory questions. Keep separate what the President can direct administratively from what Congress must create or fund.

### Part V — What the Academy Could Become

#### Chapter 18 — The First Class

Do not draft as future history before admissions are real. When a real first class exists, this chapter should follow actual recruiting, selection, temporary or permanent facilities, faculty hiring, curriculum, and culture. If the academy is delayed or canceled, the chapter becomes the story of the class that did not arrive.

#### Chapter 19 — A National Space Profession

Assess whether the academy has created common language and networks across civil, military, and commercial space or merely added another credentialing silo. Compare with professional communities created around service academies, engineering schools, and public-service programs.

#### Chapter 20 — The School That Could Fail

Failure modes: duplication, politicization, weak faculty recruitment, outdated curriculum, too-small scale, mission confusion, excessive cost, rivalry with existing institutions, service obligations that repel top students, or an institution built faster than its purpose is defined. The chapter should not assume failure; it should make success falsifiable.

### Epilogue — To the Stars

Return to the academy not as a campus but as a bet. Machines get most of the attention in space history. The deeper infrastructure is people trained to make decisions when the machines fail, adversaries adapt, budgets tighten, and missions change.

End without claiming the academy is destined to succeed. Its importance lies in the question it forces: what institutions does a spacefaring country need before space stops feeling exceptional and becomes part of ordinary national life?

## Recurring tensions

### 1. New institution versus existing capacity

Strongest pro-academy argument: the space domain now combines technical, military, civil, and commercial demands in ways no existing school was designed to integrate.

Strongest skeptical argument: the Air Force Academy, ROTC, OTS, STARCOM, NASA internships, existing universities, federal scholarship programs, and industry already provide most of the components. A new academy may be an expensive solution in search of an institutional gap.

The book must make both cases concrete.

### 2. Service academy versus federal technical academy

If the academy commissions primarily Space Force officers, the relevant comparison is West Point/Annapolis/USAFA. If it educates future NASA engineers, astronauts, civilian operators, and civil servants under service commitments, the Merchant Marine Academy and federal scholarship-for-service programs may be closer analogues. If it also claims to strengthen commercial space, public-private technical institutes become relevant.

Do not force the institution into a category before the commission does.

### 3. Civilian versus military space

NASA was created partly to preserve a civilian channel for American space activity. Space Force exists precisely because national-security space required a distinct military service. A NASA-led academy serving both communities may create valuable integration or unhealthy ambiguity. This is a genuine design problem, not a rhetorical talking point.

### 4. Generalist versus specialist

The domain demands deep expertise but strategic leaders cannot understand only one subsystem. The academy must decide how much common core a propulsion engineer, cyber operator, acquisition officer, intelligence analyst, and future astronaut should share.

### 5. Timeless institution versus fast technology

A four-year academy is slow by software standards. Space systems can also remain operational for decades. The curriculum must teach durable principles without graduating students trained for last year's stack.

## Evidence standard

Every concrete claim should be traceable to a primary or high-quality secondary source. Preference order:

1. Executive orders, statutes, Federal Register, official commission material.
2. NASA, Space Force, Department of the Air Force, service academies, GAO, CRS, congressional hearings and reports.
3. Named interviews, speeches, official transcripts, accreditation and curriculum documents.
4. Reuters, AP, major national newspapers, specialist defense/space outlets for contemporary reaction and reporting.
5. Think-tank or advocacy analysis only when clearly labeled and used for argument rather than baseline fact.

Do not use anonymous social-media posts as factual foundations. Do not write from unsourced recollection when a primary document is available.

## Falsification notes

The book's central thesis would need revision if the commission recommends a conventional Space Force-only service academy with little or no NASA/civilian component. The current thesis is based on the August 28 executive order's text describing a NASA-led Federal academy, its broad list of intended professions, and explicit consideration of both Armed Forces and civilian Federal service obligations.

The “profession bigger than a branch” thesis would also weaken if final legislation restricts admissions and service pathways almost entirely to uniformed Space Force officers.

Conversely, the thesis strengthens if the final design creates mixed cohorts, cross-agency service obligations, shared civil/military curriculum, or formal industry and university partnerships.

The duplication critique would strengthen if the academy largely reproduces USAFA astronautics/space education and STARCOM formation without a distinct cross-domain mission. It would weaken if the academy demonstrably serves workforce categories that existing commissioning and federal education pipelines cannot supply at needed scale.

## Live research rule

This book is being written while its subject is changing. Every drafting pass after September 3, 2026 should begin by checking for:

- commission membership or working-group announcements;
- public meetings, requests for information, or stakeholder consultations;
- campus/location proposals;
- draft or final commission recommendations;
- White House response to the commission report;
- NASA or Space Force pilot programs;
- authorizing or appropriations legislation;
- congressional hearings;
- accreditation steps;
- recruiting and admissions announcements;
- leadership/faculty appointments;
- changes to existing USAFA or STARCOM pipelines made in response.

New reporting should change the book when the facts change. The manuscript is not allowed to preserve a dramatic prediction after reality has disproved it.
