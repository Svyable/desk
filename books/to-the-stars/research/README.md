# Research Brief

## Subject

*To the Stars* is a narrative nonfiction book about the creation process for the proposed United States Space Academy announced on August 28, 2026.

The central factual constraint is simple and must never be blurred for drama: as of this writing, the United States does not yet have an operating Space Academy with a campus, entering class, final curriculum, settled admissions rules, or enacted statutory charter. Executive Order 14423 created a Presidential Commission charged with proposing the details of such an academy. The Commission is to report within 120 days of the order. Implementation is then subject to presidential approval, necessary legislative action, applicable law, and available appropriations.

The book therefore tells two stories at once. The first is history: the American habit of building institutions when a new strategic domain becomes important enough to demand its own professional culture. The second is history being made in real time: the attempt to decide whether space now merits an institution that combines the traditions of a federal academy, NASA's technical and exploratory culture, the Space Force's warfighting profession, and the commercial sector's pace of innovation.

## Thesis under test

The strongest version of the thesis is not that a Space Academy is inevitable or necessarily wise. It is:

> The proposal for a United States Space Academy is the institutional consequence of a deeper transition. Space has become too economically important, technically complex, strategically contested, and organizationally hybrid to rely indefinitely on personnel systems designed when space was either a specialized military mission or a small civilian exploration program. The Academy proposal is an attempt to build a professional identity for a domain that now crosses those boundaries.

That thesis can fail. Existing universities, the U.S. Air Force Academy, ROTC, Officer Training School, STARCOM, NASA's own workforce pipelines, graduate programs, internships, and private-sector training may be able to supply the talent the nation needs more cheaply and flexibly. A new academy can also create bureaucracy, duplicate programs, politicize site selection, narrow the talent pool, or institutionalize assumptions that become obsolete before its first class graduates.

The manuscript should keep both possibilities alive.

## Non-negotiable factual distinctions

1. **Proposed academy, not completed institution.** Executive Order 14423 establishes the Presidential Commission on the United States Space Academy and directs it to recommend how the Academy should be established. It does not by itself settle every legal and operational detail.
2. **NASA-led, not Space Force-only.** The order describes a proposed NASA-led federal academy. Its intended professional corps spans astronauts, scientists, engineers, operators, entrepreneurs, civil servants, and warfighters. Service obligations may include the Armed Forces or civilian Federal service.
3. **Guardians, not soldiers.** Members of the U.S. Space Force are called Guardians. Army members are Soldiers. The manuscript may use “warfighter” where the broader military role is intended.
4. **Training already exists.** The Space Force already commissions officers through existing sources and operates a growing training architecture. STARCOM's Officer Training Course, begun in 2025, provides initial skills training to newly commissioned Guardian officers regardless of commissioning source. The proposed Academy would enter an ecosystem rather than replace a vacuum.
5. **The Air Force Academy already commissions Guardians.** Since 2020, graduates of the U.S. Air Force Academy have commissioned directly into the Space Force. Any argument for a separate Space Academy must therefore explain the marginal value of a new institution rather than pretending no commissioning pipeline exists.
6. **International law does not make all military activity in space illegal.** The Outer Space Treaty prohibits placing nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit and reserves the Moon and other celestial bodies for peaceful purposes, including a ban on military bases, weapons testing, and maneuvers there. It does not prohibit military personnel from peaceful scientific activity and does not erase the military use of Earth orbit.
7. **Location is unresolved.** Political advocacy or speculation about Houston, Texas, Colorado, Florida, Alabama, or other states must not be written as a decision. The executive order specifically asks the Commission to recommend a process for selecting a permanent physical location.
8. **Service obligation is unresolved.** Existing federal service academies provide useful analogies, but the Space Academy's precise obligation has not been fixed. The Commission must recommend it.
9. **Workforce pressure is real but must be described precisely.** GAO reported in July 2026 that the Space Force filled about 75 percent of the positions it identified as necessary for its missions in FY2025 and found that the service lacked a strategic workforce plan. This supports a workforce problem; it does not prove that a new academy is the only or best remedy.
10. **Strategic competition is evidence, not a permission slip.** China and Russia are expanding military space and counterspace capabilities. The book should use that competition to explain urgency without converting every educational question into a threat slogan.

## Narrative architecture

### Prologue — The Order Signed in Houston

Open at Johnson Space Center on August 28, 2026. The event combined the Artemis II medal ceremony, presidential space policy, and the announcement of the Academy. The scene matters because the proposal was born inside an institution associated with civilian human spaceflight, even though its mandate reaches directly into national security.

### Chapter 1 — An Academy Before a Campus

Explain what has and has not been created. Treat the Commission's 120-day assignment as an institutional design problem and establish the book's governing tension: America has named a school before deciding exactly what kind of school it will be.

### Chapter 2 — The Long Line

Trace West Point and the Naval Academy as responses to professional problems. Focus on why republics build durable officer-education institutions despite recurring fears of hierarchy, bureaucracy, and elite isolation.

### Chapter 3 — When the Air Force Needed Its Own School

Use the founding of the U.S. Air Force Academy as the closest historical analogy: a new military service, years of debate, site competition, a temporary campus, and an institution whose curriculum had to define a new professional identity.

### Chapter 4 — Space Cadets Before the Space Force

Follow astronautics and space education at the Air Force Academy, culminating in the first commissioning of Guardians in 2020 and the fact that the Academy now serves both the Air Force and Space Force.

### Chapter 5 — The Astronaut Problem

Trace NASA's astronaut-selection history from the Mercury Seven to a multidisciplinary astronaut corps. Show how NASA repeatedly changed the definition of the person it needed as missions changed.

### Chapter 6 — The Service Born in Orbit

Explain the December 20, 2019 creation of the Space Force, its inheritance from Air Force Space Command and other organizations, and the challenge of building a service culture without a centuries-old institutional base.

### Chapter 7 — Guardians Need a Profession

Describe STARCOM, the Officer Training Course, component-staff education, wargaming, doctrine, and the movement toward a common Guardian professional foundation across operations, cyber, intelligence, engineering, and acquisitions.

### Chapter 8 — The Missing People

Use GAO's 2026 workforce findings to examine recruiting, training, retention, civilian expertise, contractor dependence, mission growth, and the problem of determining how many people the service actually needs.

### Chapter 9 — What the Order Actually Says

Close-read Executive Order 14423. The Commission's nine required recommendation areas become a list of institutional choices rather than bureaucratic boilerplate.

### Chapter 10 — Why NASA Is in the Chair

Analyze the decision to make the NASA Administrator the Commission chair and the option of placing the Academy within NASA. Explore the advantages and tensions of a NASA-led institution that also produces military officers and national-security professionals.

### Chapter 11 — Who Gets In

Treat admissions as mission design. Compare service-academy nomination systems, NASA selection, security-clearance requirements, citizenship, military status, technical preparation, and the risk of confusing selectivity with talent.

### Chapter 12 — What They Must Learn

Construct the intellectual problem without pretending the curriculum is settled: orbital mechanics, engineering, computer science, cyber, intelligence, acquisition, space law, history, economics, leadership, ethics, human spaceflight, commercial systems, wargaming, and the ability to communicate across professional tribes.

### Chapter 13 — Warfighters in a Peaceful Domain

Explain the legal and ethical landscape. Distinguish peaceful-use principles from the reality that satellites enable military operations and can themselves be contested. The purpose is to show why the Academy would need serious education in law, escalation, deterrence, and judgment.

### Chapter 14 — The Commercial Classroom

Use the Space Force Commercial Space Strategy and NASA's procurement history to show why future public servants must understand companies, supply chains, markets, dual-use technology, and service procurement rather than treating industry as a vendor outside the profession.

### Chapter 15 — The Campus Question

Use the Air Force Academy's site-selection history to show why location becomes more than real estate. The Academy's eventual site will influence partnerships, faculty markets, launch and test access, political identity, and the daily culture of the institution.

### Chapter 16 — The Price of a Free Education

Examine the exchange at the heart of federal academies: public financing in return for public service. Use the Air Force Academy's statutory five-year active-duty baseline as analogy while preserving that the Space Academy's service model remains undecided and may include civilian federal service.

### Chapter 17 — The Case Against Another Academy

Give the skeptical argument its strongest form: duplication, cost, rigidity, small-service scale, existing commissioning pipelines, opportunity cost, geographic politics, and the possibility that the best space workforce may come from a distributed network rather than a new campus.

### Chapter 18 — The China Clock

Explain why strategic competition creates pressure for speed, resilience, technical depth, and professional judgment. Use official U.S. assessments of Chinese and Russian space and counterspace capabilities while avoiding simplistic claims that an American undergraduate academy is a direct answer to any single foreign program.

### Chapter 19 — A Culture Not Yet Written

Ask what rituals, language, honor systems, traditions, symbols, and informal norms do inside academies. The point is not ceremony for its own sake. Culture is a compression mechanism for judgment when detailed rules run out.

### Chapter 20 — The First Class

Write the future conditionally. What would have to happen before students arrive? Commission report, presidential decisions, legislation, appropriations, accreditation, leadership, temporary facilities if necessary, admissions, faculty, curriculum, service agreements, and integration with existing agencies and services.

### Epilogue — To the Stars

Return to the fact that institutions outlive announcements. The test of the Academy will not be the attractiveness of its campus or the rhetoric of its founding but whether its graduates become the people the nation actually needs.

## Primary source hierarchy

1. Executive orders, statutes, U.S. Code, Federal Register records, congressional materials.
2. NASA, U.S. Space Force, STARCOM, Department of Defense, GAO, service-academy records.
3. Contemporaneous major-news reporting for reaction, political context, and statements not preserved in primary records.
4. Scholarly and historical secondary sources where the primary record needs interpretation.

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every major chapter should preserve a live question titled, in effect, **What would make this false?**

- If existing commissioning and education pipelines can meet military and civilian space-workforce needs at lower cost, the necessity argument weakens.
- If the Space Force's personnel shortfalls are primarily caused by authorization, retention, assignment, or workforce-planning problems, an undergraduate academy may address only a small part of the constraint.
- If NASA's civilian culture and military commissioning requirements create governance conflicts, NASA leadership may be a liability rather than a unifying advantage.
- If commercial technology cycles move faster than a four-year curriculum and federal hiring system can adapt, a campus-centered institution may become obsolete at the edges.
- If the Academy draws talent away from universities and ROTC rather than enlarging the national talent pool, it may redistribute scarce people without creating more of them.
- If congressional support fails to materialize, the proposal may remain a commission report, pilot program, or set of partnerships rather than a standalone academy.
- If a smaller model—a consortium, scholarship program, honors college, federal institute, or networked academy—meets the policy goal, the physical-campus assumption should be reconsidered.
- If foreign competition de-escalates, the Academy must still justify itself through scientific, economic, civil, and enduring national-service value.

## Source discipline

- Never write a proposed detail as settled fact.
- Separate the date of an event from the publication date of a source.
- Prefer exact statutory or executive-order language over paraphrases when the legal distinction matters, but avoid long quotation.
- Do not invent dialogue, private thoughts, room details, weather, audience reaction, or motives.
- Distinguish Space Force personnel, Air Force personnel, NASA civil servants, contractors, commercial employees, and students.
- Use “Guardian” for Space Force members when service identity matters.
- When describing threats, attribute assessments to the agency making them.
- Treat official threat assessments as evidence with institutional provenance, not neutral omniscience.
- Treat political advocacy about the Academy's location as advocacy until a lawful selection occurs.
- Record the manuscript's knowledge date. Claims about the Academy after September 3, 2026 require a new research pass.
