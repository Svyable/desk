# MAKE A BELT

## How America Secretly Changed the Space Around Earth

Sven Hardy Benson

### Evidence key

**[1] Documented:** primary/declassified/official record or strong peer-reviewed measurement.  
**[2] Credible incomplete:** serious testimony/reporting with unresolved primary reconstruction.  
**[3] Disputed:** allegation without adequate independent confirmation.  
**[4] Conjecture:** synthesis proposed here and explicitly open to defeat.

# 1. Five Months

There are secret programs whose secrecy is the interesting thing. ARGUS is not one of them.

The interesting thing is that it worked.

In April 1958 the United States decided to test an idea that sounds, stripped of its institutional nouns, like something from a particularly confident pulp magazine: explode nuclear weapons above the atmosphere and use the Earth's own magnetic field to manufacture a belt of trapped radiation around the planet. The belt might interfere with radio and radar. It might hurt the electronics of an incoming ballistic missile. It might threaten people or machines passing through it. The proposal came from Nicholas Christofilos at Lawrence Livermore. The new Advanced Research Projects Agency helped drive the experiment. The Navy assembled Task Force 88. Nine ships and roughly 4,500 people went south. Explorer IV went up. More than forty ground stations watched. Three warheads followed.

Five months from decision to first shot.

That number is worth sitting with.

Not because it proves that every rumored black program is possible. It proves almost the opposite. ARGUS gives us an actual specimen. We do not have to imagine what an extraordinary secret capability looks like while it is being made. We can inspect one after the secret opens.

On 27 August, 30 August, and 6 September 1958, missiles launched from USS *Norton Sound* carried low-yield nuclear devices into the upper atmosphere over the South Atlantic. The resulting charged particles became trapped by the geomagnetic field. Explorer IV, sounding rockets, ships, radars, and stations around the world measured the artificial electron population. Livermore's later official history says the observations confirmed the phenomenon Christofilos had predicted. [1]

The United States had made a radiation belt.

Not a metaphorical belt. Not a patent. Not a proposal. Not a witness saying that somebody in a room had once described a belt. A measurable alteration of the near-Earth radiation environment.

This book begins there because modern arguments about classified science usually start in the wrong direction. We ask whether a government *could* hide an extraordinary technology. ARGUS asks a harder question: when a government really does an extraordinary thing to the physical world, what refuses to stay hidden?

The answer is not simply "everything."

The operation itself was successfully concealed during execution. The ships sailed. The weapons launched. The measurements happened. Most of the public did not know. That matters. Anyone claiming that large technical operations cannot be kept secret has already lost the argument.

But secrecy did not repeal physics. The experiment needed a launch ship, missiles, nuclear devices, telemetry, satellites, sounding rockets, radars, scientists, crews, logistics, safety procedures, predicted conjugate effects, and a world-sized measuring problem. Its result entered an environment other people could observe. The secret had a perimeter. The phenomenon did not.

That distinction—between hiding an operation and hiding its interaction with reality—is the subject of this book.

ARGUS is especially useful because the documentary record is imperfect. The Defense Nuclear Agency's retrospective says planning was unusually informal because of secrecy and speed; Task Force 88 dissolved after the operation and its records dispersed, with some later lost or destroyed. [1] That is not a footnote to the epistemology. It is the epistemology. A real secret program can leave missing records *and* abundant physical evidence. Missing paperwork is therefore neither proof of fantasy nor proof of concealment. The world supplies a second archive.

Four years later, STARFISH PRIME made the lesson less elegant. A much larger high-altitude detonation generated trapped radiation that damaged satellites. The experiment had consequences for machines whose owners had not volunteered them for the experiment. [1] The environment had become part of the apparatus.

Here is the strange inversion. The more a secret technology depends on changing a shared physical medium, the less meaningful organizational secrecy becomes as the sole measure of detectability. You can classify the meeting. You can classify the payload. You can classify the objective. You cannot classify an electron out of somebody else's detector.

That does not make every anomaly evidence of a hidden program. It gives us a way to reject that lazy move too. A strange signal becomes evidence only after we specify what competing models predict: spatial distribution, spectrum, duration, energy, geometry, timing, instrument response, and base rate. "The government did secret things" is not a likelihood function.

ARGUS therefore becomes a control group for claims about ionospheric manipulation, electromagnetic warfare, secret propulsion, directed energy, orbital interference, weather modification, and other technologies whose alleged effects escape the fence. Some claims will become more plausible when compared with what was genuinely achieved. Others will become much less so.

The question is not whether the state can keep a secret.

It can.

The question is whether the state can keep a secret from nature.

That is a different problem.

# Working defeat condition

The book's central `shared-medium leakage` thesis is weakened if well-documented, physically large interventions can be shown to operate repeatedly in shared environments while producing no externally measurable consequences beyond information already controlled by the program. It is also weakened if the observables proposed here are so nonspecific that conventional activity produces them at comparable rates. The framework must predict discriminating traces, not merely retrospective atmosphere.
