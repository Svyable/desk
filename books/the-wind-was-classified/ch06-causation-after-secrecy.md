# Causation After Secrecy

Secrecy ages badly because it changes the burden of proof.

While a program is classified, officials can say very little. When the program is later disclosed, citizens discover that an exposure occurred decades earlier. If they suspect harm, they are then asked to prove causation using records they had no reason to collect at the time.

This is not unique to biological testing. Similar structures appear in radiation exposure, chemical contamination, intelligence experiments, occupational disease, and environmental releases. The institution controls the event record. The exposed population controls only ordinary life records. When the secret is revealed, those archives are radically unequal.

The Sea-Spray litigation made the problem concrete.

Edward Nevin's family sued the federal government, arguing that the 1950 release caused his fatal infection. The courts did not accept the causal case. The lack of proof was not irrational. Tort law requires more than temporal coincidence, and the available record could not establish that the infecting organism in Nevin's body was the same strain the Navy had released.

A court's inability to find legal causation is not equivalent to a scientific finding of no effect.

This distinction is often mangled in political retellings. Activists sometimes cite the lawsuit as though the test had been judicially proven to kill Nevin. It was not. Defenders sometimes cite the government's victory as though a court had scientifically demonstrated the release was harmless. It had not.

The litigation answered a narrower question under legal standards using incomplete historical evidence.

Causation after secrecy is difficult for at least five reasons.

First, exposure is poorly individualized. Military samplers describe an environmental field, not a person's inhaled dose.

Second, baseline disease rates may be missing. If no one knew to monitor *Serratia* infections across Bay Area hospitals before and after the release, the denominator is reconstructed from imperfect clinical literature.

Third, biological material may not be preserved. Without matched isolates, species-level coincidence remains weaker than strain-level identity.

Fourth, confounding sources exist. Hospitals themselves can generate *Serratia* outbreaks through contaminated equipment, solutions, sinks, hands, and devices.

Fifth, latency in disclosure alters testimony. Memories harden, disappear, or become contaminated by later reporting.

None of those problems favors only one side. They make both accusation and exoneration harder.

This is why the epistemology belongs inside the story rather than in a methodological appendix. The program's secrecy policy altered the evidence that later generations could use to judge the program.

A classified action can create what might be called an evidentiary debt. The institution gains immediate secrecy by postponing public scrutiny. Interest accumulates. When disclosure arrives, records are missing, people are dead, samples are gone, and uncertainty has become permanent.

Permanent uncertainty is politically useful to everyone.

A believer can say the missing evidence was destroyed or concealed. A defender can say no proof exists. Both statements may be impossible to falsify after enough time. The result is an ecology in which suspicion thrives because the decisive experiment can no longer be run.

That is one reason secret state programs generate durable mythology even when the underlying event is well documented. The secret does not need to be fantastical. Ordinary secrecy can manufacture extraordinary ambiguity.

The ethical cost is therefore larger than undisclosed risk at the moment of exposure. It includes damage to later public knowledge.

A better standard for evaluating historical secret tests should ask two separate questions. Was the risk considered reasonable given knowledge available at the time? And did the governance preserve enough independent evidence to evaluate that judgment later?

The second question is rarely asked.

If a government believes an open-air release is safe, it should be willing to build an evidentiary architecture capable of proving that claim after declassification. That means retaining batch records, strains, dose estimates, sampling maps, adverse-event criteria, medical surveillance where appropriate, and an independent review trail.

The Cold War program often retained excellent operational records and weak public-health records. That asymmetry was not random. Operational performance was the mission.

The result is a historical argument that can never quite close.

The wind carried the material away. Secrecy carried away some of the evidence.
