# Causation After Secrecy

Secrecy ages badly because it changes the burden of proof.

While a program is classified, officials can say very little. When the program is later disclosed, citizens discover that an exposure occurred decades earlier. If they suspect harm, they are then asked to prove causation using records they had no reason to collect at the time.

The Sea-Spray litigation makes this problem unusually clean because the legal record itself contains two different questions that later retellings often collapse into one.

The first is medical: did the Army's released *Serratia marcescens* cause Edward Nevin's fatal infection?

The second is jurisdictional: even if plaintiffs alleged negligence, had the United States waived sovereign immunity for the planning decision that put that organism into a secret biological-warfare vulnerability test?

Those are not the same question.

In **Nevin v. United States, 696 F.2d 1229 (9th Cir. 1983)**, the appellate majority resolved the case through the second one. The Ninth Circuit held that the relevant government decision fell within the Federal Tort Claims Act's discretionary-function exception. It therefore vacated the district court's judgment and ordered dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

That procedural posture matters.

The majority did not write a scientific appellate finding that Sea-Spray could not have caused Nevin's death. It held that the government could not be sued on that claim under the FTCA because the challenged decision was protected as a planning-level discretionary function.

The underlying trial record still contained a causation finding. Judge William Norris's dissent is useful precisely because it separates the two issues. He would have held that the court *did* have jurisdiction over the bacterial-strain decision, but he nevertheless would have affirmed the judgment because, in his view, the district court's finding that the Army's bacteria did not cause Nevin's death was not clearly erroneous.

That is much narrower than either popular slogan.

“The court proved the Army killed Nevin” is false.

“The court proved Sea-Spray was medically harmless” is also false.

The actual record is messier: the district court rejected the causal claim on the evidence before it; the appellate majority disposed of the case on sovereign-immunity grounds; a dissent would have reached the merits and accepted the district court's no-causation finding.

That distinction is exactly why historical legal outcomes have to be read at the level of what question was decided.

The case also preserves a fact that makes the governance issue harder, not easier. The Ninth Circuit record states that General Anthony McAuliffe personally approved San Francisco as the site and approved the particular *Serratia* strain after scientific and medical advice and his own evaluation of safety. The same record notes that he rejected a proposed New York subway test using that strain.

So “nobody considered risk” is not supported by the appellate record.

People did consider risk.

The harder question is whether the safety model they used was good enough, and whether the test architecture was capable of discovering that it was wrong.

That is where secrecy matters.

The 1951 Stanford clinical paper by Wheat, Zuckerman and Rantz reported eleven *Serratia* infections. Modern review of the cases notes that all eleven involved adults with significant underlying illness and urinary-tract instrumentation or related risk factors. Nevin had urinary retention, catheterization and prostate surgery before developing a urinary infection; the organism was later recovered from blood, and he died with endocarditis. Those details make hospital acquisition medically plausible independent of Sea-Spray.

They do not erase the coincidence that makes the episode impossible to ignore.

The Navy had aerosolized *Serratia* in the Bay Area shortly before a hospital that reportedly had not previously isolated the organism documented a cluster. The released strain and the clinical isolates were never preserved in a way that allows modern whole-genome comparison. The environmental test was secret, so the clinicians did not know to treat an unusual cluster as a possible exposure investigation.

That missing linkage is the center of the problem.

A modern investigation would want at least four things.

First, the exact release culture: strain identity, passage history, phenotype, batch record and genomic sequence.

Second, the environmental field: time-resolved sampler locations, recoveries, meteorology, aerosol particle-size distribution and a model translating those measurements into plausible human exposure.

Third, the clinical isolates: every patient strain preserved, sequenced and compared against the release strain and against hospital/background *Serratia* diversity.

Fourth, the epidemiology: baseline incidence, hospital-specific risk factors, patient locations, instrument exposures, contemporaneous cases outside Stanford and a predefined adverse-event window.

Sea-Spray did not leave that evidentiary architecture behind.

Some of the absence is technological. Whole-genome sequencing did not exist in 1950. Some is ordinary historical loss. But some is structural. The release was designed as a military dispersion experiment, not as a public-health trial. Its measurement system cared intensely about where the simulant traveled. The nearby hospitals did not know they were part of the causal environment being created.

This is the sense in which secrecy can create an **evidentiary debt**.

The institution receives an immediate benefit: realism, surprise, fewer observers, less chance that public behavior changes the dispersion experiment.

The debt is paid later. If an adverse signal appears, the clinical side may lack the exposure map and the operational side may lack the medical specimens. By the time disclosure arrives, samples are gone, people are dead, microbial populations have changed and memories have been rewritten by decades of publicity.

The result is not proof of hidden harm.

It is a permanently widened uncertainty interval.

That point cuts both directions. People convinced Sea-Spray caused the Stanford cases can point to the missing strain comparison and say the decisive evidence was never collected. Defenders can point to the same absence and say no causal proof exists. Both statements are compatible with the surviving record.

The epistemic mistake is to turn missing evidence into evidence with a preferred sign.

A court loss is not a negative bacterial culture.

A classified test is not a positive genomic match.

Temporal proximity is not strain identity.

Government safety review is not proof that the review was correct.

The modern *Serratia* literature adds one more uncomfortable constraint. *Serratia marcescens* is now a well-established opportunistic and nosocomial pathogen. Hospital outbreaks involving urinary catheters, contaminated solutions, sinks, equipment and vulnerable patients are entirely ordinary explanations in the clinical sense. That makes the Stanford cluster less uniquely diagnostic of a citywide aerosol source than it appeared in some later popular retellings.

At the same time, that modern knowledge makes the original choice to call the organism effectively harmless look historically contingent. A simulant is never “safe” in the abstract. It is safe enough relative to a particular host population, exposure route, dose, detection system and state of medical knowledge.

This is why the policy lesson is not that secret field testing can never occur, nor that every later illness must be attributed to it.

The lesson is that a government conducting a classified environmental release should be judged partly by whether it preserves a future path to causal adjudication.

That means retaining batch and strain records. Preserving enough biological material for later reanalysis. Maintaining exposure maps. Predefining adverse-event triggers. Creating some independent medical-surveillance channel proportionate to risk. Establishing who can stop the experiment when safety assumptions fail. Planning declassification with the knowledge that tomorrow's analytical tools may answer questions today's tools cannot.

The Cold War program often kept excellent records for the mission it was trying to accomplish: dispersion, coverage, sampler recovery, operational feasibility.

The public-health side was thinner because public health was not the mission.

That asymmetry is what later generations inherited.

The legal record should therefore be used with precision. **Nevin v. United States** establishes the existence of the wrongful-death litigation, the government's planning structure, its safety deliberation, and the appellate court's discretionary-function holding. It does not magically resolve the missing microbiology.
