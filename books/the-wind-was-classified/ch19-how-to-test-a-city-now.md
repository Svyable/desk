# How to Test a City Now

A city still needs to know how dangerous material moves through it.

The requirement did not expire with the Cold War.

Anthrax letters in 2001 demonstrated that small quantities of spores could disrupt buildings and postal systems. The Tokyo subway attack had already shown the vulnerability of transit networks to chemical agents. COVID-19 forced schools, hospitals, offices, aircraft, and public agencies to relearn aerosol transmission at civic scale. Wildfire smoke routinely turns atmospheric dispersion into a public-health emergency.

The old question survives: where does the air go?

What should change is how we answer it.

A modern urban-dispersion program can begin with an explicit hierarchy of surrogates. Use computational models first where they are adequate. Validate with inert or exceptionally low-risk tracers. Escalate to biological surrogates only when the biological property is essential to the question and when risk can be bounded. Separate transport validation from pathogenicity assumptions.

The second requirement is independent safety review with access to the operational details needed to challenge the test. Classification can protect tactical specifics without eliminating external scrutiny entirely.

Third, predefine the evidence that would stop the experiment. An adverse-event plan written after something goes wrong is reputation management. A plan written before release is science.

Fourth, preserve material and metadata. If a living surrogate is used, retain reference samples so unexpected clinical isolates can be compared. Preserve batch identity, genomic sequence, release quantities, particle distributions, weather, sampling results, and exposure maps.

Fifth, distinguish public notification from full operational disclosure. A community can be told that an approved low-risk environmental tracer study will occur within a broad window without publishing exact release points or security hypotheses. In some cases even that level of notice may compromise the test, but secrecy should be justified as a specific variable, not inherited automatically from the sponsor.

Sixth, build independent health surveillance when the exposure warrants it. If risk is truly negligible, explain why. If uncertainty is meaningful, collect data capable of testing the safety claim.

Seventh, commit to a declassification clock. Secrets without expiration become folklore factories.

These principles sound expensive compared with dropping a bulb on a subway platform.

They are.

Ethics often appears as friction because its purpose is to prevent organizations from optimizing a single objective. The Cold War program optimized realism, secrecy, and military relevance. Modern governance adds legitimacy, auditability, and reversibility.

The interesting question is whether those additions destroy the science.

Not necessarily.

Large urban tracer studies have been conducted openly or semi-openly using safe materials and sophisticated sensor networks. Emergency agencies run exercises. Building scientists release tracers. Epidemiologists use natural experiments. Digital twins and computational fluid dynamics can narrow where physical validation is actually needed.

No model reproduces reality perfectly. But perfect realism is not a moral entitlement.

There are experiments society chooses not to run even when the data would be excellent.

The proper legacy of Sea-Spray is not fear of measurement. It is better measurement under conditions that preserve the public as citizens rather than background terrain.
