# Front Matter

In September 1950, a Navy vessel moved outside San Francisco and released organisms into the coastal air. The people on shore were not told. That fact is now old enough to have acquired the hard gloss of trivia: one more Cold War story told with a half-smile about the things governments used to do.

The half-smile is a mistake.

The experiment was not interesting because it was primitive. It was interesting because it was competent. Someone had a question about biological attack that could not be answered in a flask: if a cloud containing microbial particles were released outside a coastal city, how efficiently would weather and urban air move it through an inhabited landscape? The answer required wind. It required streets and buildings. It required distance. It required samplers placed where actual people lived. The city was not incidental to the apparatus. The city was the apparatus.

That is the uncomfortable thing about the American open-air biological vulnerability program. Its worst feature and its scientific value were entangled.

The easy version of this history says the military sprayed unsuspecting citizens with germs. True, in a broad sense. Too broad to be useful. It collapses several different substances, purposes, risks, periods, and programs into a single moral photograph. The equally easy official version said that the organisms were simulants, selected because they were believed harmless, and that the work was necessary to understand vulnerability. Also true, in a narrower sense. Too narrow to be sufficient.

A simulant is not merely a substance. It is an argument about similarity. It says: this particle behaves enough like the thing we fear to stand in for it, but differs in the dangerous property we do not want. Every field test therefore carries two experiments. One asks whether the surrogate travels like the weapon. The other, usually less visible, asks whether the surrogate is safe enough to release at scale. The first can succeed while the second turns out to have been badly specified.

This book follows that second experiment.

It begins with Operation Sea-Spray because San Francisco supplies nearly all the parts of the problem in one place: a secret release, a bacterium chosen under an older medical understanding, a striking hospital cluster soon afterward, decades of uncertainty about causation, and records disclosed only after the people who inhaled the material had lost any chance to create a contemporaneous exposure history of their own. From there the trail moves underground into the New York subway, across the country with fluorescent particles, through mosquito-release tests, into Fort Detrick's giant aerosol sphere, onto Project SHAD ships, and finally into the congressional rooms where pieces of the program became public.

The book does not claim that every illness reported after every test was caused by the test. That would be a second abuse of the evidence. In the San Francisco case, the temporal proximity between the 1950 release and a cluster of *Serratia marcescens* infections is real. So is the absence of the strain-level molecular evidence we would now want. So are alternative routes of hospital acquisition. So is the historical fact that *Serratia* was once treated far more casually than modern clinical microbiology permits. The causal question remains bounded by records that were not collected because the exposed public did not know there was a causal question to investigate.

Secrecy does something peculiar to epidemiology. It does not merely hide an answer. It can prevent the evidence required for an answer from ever existing.

That is the thesis beneath the history. Secret testing does not just create an information asymmetry between the state and the citizen. It can create a permanent evidentiary asymmetry between later claim and later denial. The institution knows that an exposure occurred. The public does not know to collect baseline samples, preserve isolates, record symptoms against a release clock, challenge the chosen dose, or ask whether a supposedly harmless organism remains harmless when aerosolized across a metropolis. Decades later, everyone is left arguing over fragments.

The Cold War is full of secret programs whose files eventually emerged. This one is different because its archive was partly written into moving air.
