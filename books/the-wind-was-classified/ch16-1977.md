# 1977

Twenty-seven years after Sea-Spray, the public learned enough of the story to become angry in the present tense.

In March 1977 Senate hearings examined biological testing by the Department of Defense. The Army supplied a two-volume historical account of its biological warfare program. Officials disclosed that 239 open-air tests had been conducted between 1949 and 1969 in the broader program, including tests in populated areas using biological simulants and other tracers.

The number was made for headlines.

Two hundred thirty-nine secret open-air germ-warfare tests. It compressed an entire research architecture into an indictment. Newspaper accounts named San Francisco, New York, Washington, Key West, Panama City, military installations, and other sites. Legislators questioned whether organisms described as harmless really were.

The hearings are important less because they revealed a perfect archive than because they show the state trying to narrate a secret technical program after its governing assumptions had changed.

By 1977, research ethics had changed. Public trust after Vietnam and Watergate had changed. The Tuskegee syphilis scandal had become public. Congressional investigations had exposed CIA and military behavior once protected by classification. The language of informed consent had more political force.

The Army's old explanation—that simulants were selected as harmless and tests were necessary to determine vulnerability—landed in a different moral climate.

Officials were not wrong that planners had safety procedures. The historical report described scientific review, meteorological controls, and surrogate selection. That complicates any claim that the releases were casual or sadistic.

But procedural care inside a secret system did not answer the senators' core objection: the people exposed had no voice.

The hearings also illustrate how retrospective disclosure creates category confusion. Senators and reporters had to separate public simulant releases from volunteer studies, occupational infections, actual-agent tests on military ranges, CIA activities, and other programs. The institutional boundary lines that made sense inside the bureaucracy were almost invisible from outside.

This is a recurring feature of classified scandals. The public receives the archive all at once and assembles a single story from programs that were administratively separate.

Sometimes the synthesis reveals a real system-level pattern. Sometimes it creates false continuity.

The correct response is not to accept the bureaucracy's compartments as morally decisive. Agencies can fragment responsibility in ways that hide the whole. Nor should historians erase every boundary. Evidence has to remain attached to the program that produced it.

The 1977 Army history is therefore both source and subject. It documents the program and demonstrates how the institution wanted the program understood.

It emphasized necessity, safety judgments, and scientific oversight. Critics emphasized nonconsent, secrecy, and emerging evidence that some simulants were not as benign as assumed. Both narratives selected real features.

The hearings did something else: they created a searchable public object. Once code names, dates, organisms, and locations entered the record, journalists, scholars, litigants, veterans, and families could connect documents that had previously been isolated.

Disclosure changed the research ecosystem.

The Stanford *Serratia* paper could now be read beside Sea-Spray records. Veterans could ask about test participation. Local histories could search for release sites. FOIA requests could use program names.

This is why transparency has compounding effects. One disclosed noun unlocks other archives.

It is also why incomplete disclosure is so corrosive. When Project SHAD emerged later, the public learned that even the 1977 map had blank spaces.

The hearings did not close the history. They made it investigable.
