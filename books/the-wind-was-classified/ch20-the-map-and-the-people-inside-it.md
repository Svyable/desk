# The Map and the People Inside It

A military map is good at removing the people who make it matter.

Put a release point offshore. Draw the wind. Mark sampling stations. Add recovery counts. Plot concentration contours over San Francisco. The city becomes legible in precisely the way the experiment requires.

Then look again.

Inside the contour are hospital patients, children, people with lung disease, night-shift workers, sailors, commuters, scientists, janitors, physicians, and one retired pipefitter named Edward Nevin. The map has not changed. The meaning has.

This book began with a claim that the ethical breach and the scientific value of the Cold War vulnerability tests came from the same design choice. Real cities supplied realism. People made them real.

That does not mean every test was equally dangerous or every later illness was caused by a release. The evidence refuses that simplification.

The documented record is substantial. The United States operated an offensive biological warfare program until 1969. It conducted open-air vulnerability tests. It released microbial simulants such as *Serratia marcescens* and *Bacillus globigii* in populated environments and used fluorescent particles in large dispersion studies. It tested transit systems and military facilities. It investigated vectors. It used volunteers under separate controlled programs. Congress publicly examined much of this history in 1977. Additional compartments, including Project SHAD, became public later.

The credible but incomplete record is also substantial. Some tests generated exposure fields broad enough that large populations likely encountered the surrogate. Contemporary safety judgments were sincere but sometimes narrower than later medical understanding. Secrecy impaired contemporaneous epidemiological integration and made later health attribution difficult.

The disputed record should remain disputed. Sea-Spray has not been proven by modern strain-level evidence to have caused the Stanford cluster or Edward Nevin's death. Claims that every Cold War tracer was knowingly poisonous flatten toxicology and history. Claims that covert field tests caused large unrecognized epidemics require evidence stronger than temporal coincidence.

Then there is conjecture: permanent microbial changes, hidden casualty counts, undisclosed programs inferred solely from known secrecy. Some conjectures may eventually acquire evidence. Until then they belong in the fourth drawer.

Keeping the drawers separate does not make the story less unsettling. It makes the documented part harder to dismiss.

The United States really did turn parts of its own inhabited landscape into a biological warfare measurement system.

Why?

Because the threat was difficult to model. Because atmospheric transport matters. Because defense planners feared surprise. Because simulants seemed to offer a safe compromise. Because classification culture rewarded controlled information. Because the institutional machinery for collective environmental consent was weak. Because the Cold War made many extraordinary things feel provisional until they became routine.

The tests also succeeded at something more subtle than dispersion science. They demonstrated how easily an organization can redefine people as environment when the variable of interest sits outside the body.

That habit remains available.

Sensors measure crowds. Algorithms experiment on traffic. platforms run behavioral tests at population scale. public-health agencies model movement. cities become data-producing systems because they are too complex to understand any other way.

The analogy should not be stretched into equivalence. A software experiment is not a bacterial aerosol. The useful inheritance is the question: when does studying a system become experimenting on the people whose ordinary lives make the system realistic?

There is no single threshold.

Risk matters. Consent matters. identifiability matters. reversibility matters. governance matters. whether the public can contest the experiment matters.

The Cold War cases sit on the far side of several of those lines because secrecy removed contestability by design.

That is why the phrase harmless simulant never quite settles the argument. Even if every disputed medical claim vanished tomorrow, the political fact would remain: officials made a population-level risk decision for people who were deliberately prevented from knowing the decision existed.

Perhaps the risk was tiny. Perhaps the military benefit was real. Those possibilities do not cancel the question. They sharpen it.

Who gets to decide when everyone becomes the control group?

The wind never answered. It only carried the material where the map said it might go.
