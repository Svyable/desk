# SHAD and the Missing Ledger

The 1977 disclosures did not disclose everything.

That fact became painfully clear decades later when veterans learned about Project SHAD—Shipboard Hazard and Defense—and related tests conducted under the broader Project 112. Some SHAD tests involved actual chemical or biological agents in military test settings; others used simulants. The program's purpose was to evaluate vulnerability and protective measures for ships and forces.

SHAD belongs beside the city tests not because the exposures were identical but because disclosure was incomplete.

When the Army presented a broad history of its biological warfare activities in 1977, the public could reasonably believe it was receiving the major ledger. Later revelations showed that significant compartments had remained outside that account.

This changes how historians should treat official completeness claims.

An official report can be accurate within the files its authors were allowed to see and still be incomplete at the system level. Compartmentation produces bureaucratic ignorance deliberately. People with a need to know one piece may not know adjacent pieces. Years later, an investigator can search sincerely and miss a program stored under another organization, code name, or classification channel.

That structure is central to intelligence and special-access work. It is also a machine for generating future suspicion.

Once a major undisclosed program emerges after an earlier disclosure, every gap in the archive becomes easier to interpret as concealment. Sometimes it is. Sometimes records were destroyed, misfiled, or never centralized. Sometimes a later allegation attaches itself to a real history of secrecy without independent evidence.

Project SHAD therefore teaches a methodological rule: prior concealment raises the rational prior for additional missing information, but it does not validate any particular new claim.

The rule matters far beyond biological warfare.

Veterans' health concerns sharpened the issue. Participants wanted to know what they had been exposed to, at what dose, with what protective gear, and whether later illnesses could be linked. The Department of Defense undertook identification and disclosure efforts, and the Department of Veterans Affairs developed responses for potentially exposed veterans.

Again, causation after secrecy proved difficult.

Decades separated exposure from many health claims. Records varied in quality. Agent lists and test plans did not necessarily equal personal dose. Epidemiological studies faced small groups, multiple exposures, aging populations, and incomplete controls.

The same evidentiary debt appeared, now with military personnel rather than an ambient public.

The lesson is not that secrecy makes epidemiology impossible. It makes good recordkeeping more important. A classified program can preserve personnel rosters, exposure estimates, agent lots, protective status, medical baselines, and adverse-event logs under seal. Classification need not mean amnesia.

But secrecy creates incentives against the very records later needed for accountability. Lists of exposed personnel can themselves be sensitive. Operational planners focus on mission variables. Health surveillance can be expensive and can create discoverable evidence of risk.

A program optimized only for secrecy and operational learning will tend to underproduce accountability data.

SHAD also prevents this book from ending the story in 1969. Nixon's renunciation changed U.S. policy dramatically, but defensive testing, legacy exposures, treaty verification, and military biodefense continued. The boundary between offense and defense became legally and politically more important, not less.

A ship tested against a simulated attack may be defensive. The data about how the attack penetrates defenses can also reveal offensive performance.

Dual use survived the policy change.

So did the archive problem.

The missing ledger is not proof of an infinite hidden program. It is evidence that disclosure itself must be audited.
