# The Harmless Organism

Before *Serratia marcescens* became a hospital organism people feared, it was the sort of bacterium a classroom could love. Some strains produce a conspicuous red pigment. The color makes colonies easy to see. Long before the Cold War, the organism appeared in demonstrations because an invisible biological process could announce itself in red.

Visibility is useful in a simulant.

The military needed microorganisms that could be produced in quantity, dispersed as aerosols, distinguished from background organisms, and recovered after traveling through real air. A lethal pathogen would answer the dispersion question at an unacceptable human cost. A tracer that died immediately would answer the wrong question. The ideal organism would behave enough like a dangerous agent to make the test meaningful while remaining benign enough to make the test permissible.

There is no such thing as a perfect surrogate. There are only tradeoffs that look acceptable at a particular moment in scientific history.

In the middle of the twentieth century, *Serratia marcescens* often occupied a category that later became difficult to defend: a convenient, readily identifiable bacterium with little reputation for causing disease in healthy people. That did not mean the organism was literally incapable of infection. It meant the clinical literature had not yet made it a routine symbol of opportunistic hospital disease.

This distinction matters because retrospective moral judgment can become lazy. It is easy to write as though military scientists in 1950 secretly knew *Serratia* was a dangerous pathogen and sprayed it anyway. The documentary record supports a more complicated and, in some ways, more instructive failure. Researchers used an organism under a safety judgment that reflected contemporary knowledge and institutional incentives. When evidence accumulated that the organism could cause disease, particularly in vulnerable patients, the old category became untenable.

A safety claim can be sincere and still be wrong.

That is more disturbing than simple villainy because it generalizes.

The phrase harmless simulant hides several variables. Harmless to whom? At what dose? By what route? Under what physiological conditions? A bacterium that rarely harms an immunocompetent adult after ordinary environmental contact may behave differently when aerosolized, delivered at a high concentration, introduced into a hospital population, inhaled by people with underlying disease, or deposited on devices and surfaces that create unusual routes into the body.

Scale also changes the meaning of a small probability. If an organism has a very low chance of producing serious disease in any one exposed person, releasing it over hundreds of thousands of people is not equivalent to exposing a handful of healthy laboratory staff. A rare event becomes less rare as the denominator grows.

The Cold War vulnerability program frequently treated simulant safety as a property of the material. Modern risk assessment is more likely to treat risk as an interaction among material, route, dose, host, and context. Those are different mental models.

The case of *Bacillus globigii* has a similar shape. The organism—associated in later taxonomy with *Bacillus atrophaeus*—was used extensively as a stand-in for hardy bacterial spores. Spores are attractive to a military aerosol program because they persist. Persistence is also the reason the word harmless needs qualification. Members of the broader *Bacillus* group occupy an enormous biological range, from common environmental organisms to serious pathogens. A particular surrogate can be low-risk while still requiring evidence about inhalation, dose, contamination, and susceptible hosts.

The scientific problem is not that simulation is illegitimate. Simulation is how dangerous systems are studied without reproducing the danger itself. Crash dummies, harmless tracers, attenuated organisms, inert particles, test doubles, and computer models all exist because direct testing would be intolerable.

The problem begins when the surrogate becomes rhetorically safer than the evidence.

A simulant is selected because of similarity in some dimensions and difference in others. For biological aerosol work, planners might care about aerodynamic diameter, viability after release, sensitivity to ultraviolet light, ease of culture, and detectability. Yet pathogenicity is not a single switch. An organism can be nonpathogenic in one population and opportunistic in another. It can be safe on skin and unsafe in blood. It can be low-risk in tiny doses and different at industrial scale.

There is another complication. The closer a surrogate comes to the feared agent's environmental behavior, the more likely it may be to retain biological properties that make a field test informative. If investigators choose something too inert, the data may not transfer. If they choose something too biologically realistic, they recover the very hazards simulation was meant to avoid.

This was not just an American problem. Biological defense programs in many countries wrestled with surrogate selection, and modern biodefense still does. The important historical lesson is narrower: the 1950s and 1960s U.S. program often made surrogate judgments inside a classified system while exposing people outside that system. Those people could not scrutinize the assumptions or contribute information about vulnerabilities the planners had not considered.

The asymmetry mattered after the fact. Once *Serratia* became recognized as a significant opportunistic pathogen, the old tests looked different. The government could accurately say that the organism had been considered safe when used. Citizens could accurately answer that they had never been asked whether they were willing to be subjects in a safety judgment that might later change.

Both statements can be true.

The interesting question is what follows when they are.
