# Spores in the Subway

A subway is a biological distribution machine that nobody designed as one.

Trains displace air. Pistons of steel enter tunnels and push pressure ahead of them. Braking heats equipment. Doors open into crowded stations. Passengers carry material on clothing and shoes. Vent shafts connect underground space to the city above. Multiple lines intersect. Air moves according to a geometry far more complicated than a simple pipe.

For a biological-warfare planner, the New York subway posed an obvious question: if material were introduced at one point, would the system spread it?

In 1966 the U.S. Army tested that question using *Bacillus globigii*, a spore-forming simulant. Accounts of the test describe light bulbs containing the organism being dropped or shattered in subway stations or from moving trains, with samplers positioned elsewhere in the network. The method sounds almost childish. The physics was not.

A small source can become a large exposure if the transport network does the work.

That is the strategic attraction of infrastructure. An attacker does not necessarily need to fill every station. It may be enough to exploit a system already optimized to move people and air.

The Army's later descriptions of the subway work emphasized the speed and breadth of dissemination. Material released at one location could be recovered at distant stations. The system behaved less like a set of isolated rooms than a connected airflow network.

That result has aged into something more familiar after decades of ventilation modeling, smoke-control engineering, and post-9/11 transit-security research. Today, transit agencies and national laboratories model how contaminants propagate through stations and tunnels. The extraordinary part is not that the 1966 experiment asked a sensible question. It is that the answer was obtained by releasing a live microbial surrogate into a public system without informing passengers.

Again the ethics and the realism are entangled.

If officials had announced the trial, normal passenger density might collapse. Station staff might alter ventilation. Cleaning crews might respond differently. The resulting data would describe an alerted subway rather than an ordinary one.

But public ignorance also meant no one could avoid exposure.

The simulant choice was meant to solve that moral problem technically: use an organism believed safe enough that consent is unnecessary. This is the same logic visible in San Francisco, but the subway sharpens it because the release is intentionally placed inside a dense transport network.

The question becomes: how safe must a surrogate be before a population-scale release can reasonably be treated like a smoke test rather than human experimentation?

There is no universal answer. Carbon dioxide, sulfur hexafluoride, harmless fluorescent tracers, DNA-tagged particles, and other modern surrogates can be used in controlled environmental studies with very low risk. Governance matters as much as chemistry. Notification may be broad without revealing exact security-sensitive details. Independent safety review can occur. Emergency plans can exist.

The 1966 program lacked much of that public accountability.

The subway test also offers a useful correction to one persistent biological-warfare fantasy. The recovery of spores throughout a transit system does not mean a lethal pathogen would produce identical casualties. Different organisms have different survival, infectious dose, aerosol stability, environmental persistence, and susceptibility to treatment. The simulant validates aspects of transport, not the entire weapon.

This is the central discipline of surrogate science: never let similarity in one dimension become identity in all dimensions.

Military rhetoric sometimes drifted toward casualty estimates based on recovered simulant concentrations. Such estimates can be useful planning exercises, but they stack assumptions. The aerosol must behave similarly. The pathogen must remain viable. The inhaled dose distribution must be estimated. The infectious-dose curve must be known. Population susceptibility must be modeled. Detection and medical response must be assumed.

Each layer can multiply uncertainty.

The subway result was still strategically important because it demonstrated a vulnerability in principle. A network designed for mobility can also distribute contamination.

The troubling lesson is broader. Modern cities are full of systems whose efficiency is dual-use. Ventilation moves clean air and contaminants. Water networks deliver water and whatever enters them. Logistics systems deliver medicine and contraband. Communication networks spread warnings and rumors.

The attacker looks for infrastructure that will volunteer as an accomplice.

In 1966, the Army used the subway to prove the point.
