# The City Becomes an Instrument

A laboratory usually has walls. That is partly how we know who is inside the experiment.

The vulnerability program erased that convenience.

A city offered things no chamber could reproduce at full scale: street canyons, transit tunnels, rooftop turbulence, variable heating, traffic wakes, open windows, ventilation systems, commuters, coastlines, parks, bridges, industrial plumes, and the thousand accidental geometries created by buildings erected for reasons having nothing to do with aerosol physics. The mess was the point.

Biological attack planners faced a model-validation problem. They could calculate dispersion from meteorological theory, but a weapon does not care whether the equation is elegant. It cares whether material remains airborne and viable long enough to reach people. The real environment adds loss mechanisms and transport pathways that small experiments miss.

The open-air program therefore converted inhabited places into test ranges.

This conversion was not merely geographic. It changed the status of ordinary infrastructure. A subway became a mixing chamber. A bus terminal became a crowd-and-airflow experiment. A highway became a transect. An airport became a vulnerability surface. A coastal city became a receptor field for an offshore release. The public moved through these systems while technicians placed samplers and recovered organisms or particles.

Military science has always borrowed landscapes. Artillery ranges, proving grounds, deserts, islands, and oceans become measurement spaces because they provide scale. The difference here was that many of the landscapes were already occupied by people who did not know they had been placed inside a measurement system.

One reason secrecy appealed to planners was operational realism. If a city knows a tracer release is coming, behavior changes. Doors close. transit agencies alter ventilation. reporters arrive. hospitals prepare. people avoid stations. An enemy would prefer surprise, so a realistic vulnerability study would prefer normality.

That logic contains a trap. The more valuable ignorance becomes to experimental validity, the more the population's lack of consent becomes a feature rather than an accidental omission.

It is useful to distinguish three types of subjects in these tests.

The first were intentional research personnel: technicians, scientists, military staff, sometimes volunteers. They could be briefed, monitored, medically screened, or protected to varying degrees.

The second were operational participants who knew something was being tested but not necessarily every detail: crews, installation staff, local officials in selected cases.

The third were ambient subjects—the people whose presence made the city a city. They were not recruited as human subjects in the ordinary experimental sense. Planners often framed the releases as environmental tests using materials judged safe, rather than biomedical experiments on people. Yet people were part of the exposure field by design.

This category problem survives in modern research ethics. If a researcher releases a nonhazardous tracer into public air to study ventilation, is every person who passes through the plume a human subject? Usually not, if the tracer is demonstrably safe and no identifiable private data are collected. But the answer depends on the risk profile, the purpose, the ability to opt out, and the governance regime.

The Cold War cases are difficult because the safety evidence was partly classified, the exposure scale was large, and some surrogates later acquired a less benign medical reputation.

The city also introduced a denominator problem. When a release covers a population of hundreds of thousands, every illness afterward can appear suspicious. Some of those illnesses would have occurred anyway. Without a prospective surveillance design, background rates, matched controls, and strain identification, temporal association can become narratively powerful and scientifically weak.

The government created part of this problem by keeping the experiment secret. If you do not tell clinicians that an unusual organism has been released, they cannot design surveillance to separate signal from background. If you do not preserve a complete chain of custody for environmental and clinical isolates, later investigators cannot match them. If you do not disclose the dose field, later epidemiologists must reconstruct it from incomplete military sampling.

A classified experiment can therefore be exquisitely measured on the variable the sponsor cares about and almost unmeasured on the variable the public later cares about.

In Sea-Spray, the sponsor cared about penetration and dispersion. In New York, it cared about how material moved through a subway network. In continental fluorescent-particle tests, it cared about long-range transport. The public later asked about health.

Those are not the same experiment.

It is easy to say the military should have studied health too. Perhaps. But that would have changed the program from a vulnerability test into something closer to population exposure research, creating ethical requirements the institution was trying not to invoke. Calling the material harmless made health follow-up seem unnecessary. The absence of follow-up then reinforced the harmless framing because little systematic evidence of harm was gathered.

That is a closed loop worth noticing.

An assumption about safety can determine what data are collected. The resulting lack of adverse-event data can then be cited as evidence that the assumption was sound.

A real city can validate a dispersion model. It cannot validate its own safety without a different measurement system.
