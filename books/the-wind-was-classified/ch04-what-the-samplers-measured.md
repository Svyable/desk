# What the Samplers Measured

The most important objects in the vulnerability program were not the sprayers. They were the samplers.

A release without recovery tells you almost nothing. The military did not need a theatrical cloud. It needed numbers: concentration at a location, viable particles recovered after a known interval, decay with distance, effects of weather, differences between open terrain and built environments. The sprayer created the problem. The sampler converted it into data.

This distinction matters because popular accounts tend to make the release itself the story. A ship sprays bacteria toward San Francisco. Light bulbs shatter in the subway. Cars emit fluorescent particles. Those are vivid scenes. The scientific work begins afterward.

A biological aerosol is unstable in several ways at once. Particles settle. Wind shears and dilutes the cloud. Sunlight damages organisms. Humidity changes survival. Surfaces capture material. Air enters and exits buildings. Different particle sizes follow different trajectories and penetrate different parts of the respiratory tract. A useful field test therefore needs not just a yes-or-no detector but a network.

The 1950 San Francisco work used multiple sampling locations to reconstruct how the released organisms moved through the city. Later tests expanded this logic. The point was often to compare observed recovery with predictions. If the predicted cloud missed the measured one, planners learned that their model or assumptions needed work.

This is ordinary science conducted under extraordinary governance.

There is a tendency, when an experiment is ethically compromised, to assume its technical data must also be worthless. That is a mistake. Bad ethics do not automatically produce bad aerobiology. In fact, the more useful the data were, the more important it becomes to understand what institutional bargain produced them.

The inverse mistake is just as common: if the data were useful, the test must have been justified. Scientific value is not a moral solvent.

Sampling also reveals the narrowness of the original question. Investigators could know a great deal about where the surrogate went while knowing little about what happened inside the bodies it encountered. An environmental sampler might recover colonies. It would not tell you which passerby inhaled them, whether a susceptible patient received a larger dose, or whether an organism lodged in a hospital reservoir.

Individual dose reconstruction is especially difficult after the fact. People move. They spend different amounts of time outdoors. Buildings exchange air at different rates. Exposure does not map cleanly from an outdoor concentration contour to a personal inhaled dose. When later accounts state that every resident received a particular number of organisms, those statements usually compress a model into a bodily fact.

The better claim is often probabilistic: the measured dispersion indicated citywide penetration at concentrations sufficient to make broad exposure plausible or likely.

That may sound cautious. It is actually more powerful because it does not ask the evidence to do work it cannot do.

The field program also depended heavily on meteorology. Release timing mattered. A failed test could result from the wrong wind rather than the wrong weapon. Contemporary reports emphasized meteorological monitoring because an uncontrolled atmosphere can erase an experiment quickly. Temperature inversions, sea breezes, rainfall, solar radiation, and boundary-layer structure can all change outcomes.

To a military planner, this variability was not noise to be eliminated. It was part of the weapon system. Biological warfare outsourced part of its delivery chain to weather.

That is why some of the grander Cold War claims about biological attack deserve skepticism. A map showing successful dispersion under selected conditions does not guarantee an enemy can reproduce those conditions on command. Biological weapons promise scale but inherit uncertainty from atmosphere and organism alike.

The vulnerability program therefore answered two opposing questions at once. It demonstrated that aerosols could spread frighteningly far. It also demonstrated how much performance depended on conditions.

The sampler makes both visible.

A strong reconstruction of these tests should therefore resist the temptation to count only releases. The meaningful unit is release plus weather plus sampling geometry plus recovery method plus model. One test might produce many individual releases. One release might be operationally useless because wind shifted. A positive recovery far downwind might be scientifically important even if concentrations were too low to produce a military effect.

The official 1977 history eventually counted 239 open-air tests in the broader program. That number became a headline because it sounded enormous. Yet the number alone conceals the experimental structure. Some events used biological simulants. Others used chemical or particulate tracers. Some involved populated places; others occurred on military ranges. Some programs later disclosed under Project SHAD involved actual agents under controlled military conditions rather than the same public-city simulant model.

Counting everything together produces outrage more efficiently than understanding.

The samplers force discipline. What substance? What route? What concentration? What recovery? What weather? What question?

Those are the boring questions that keep a secret history from becoming folklore.
