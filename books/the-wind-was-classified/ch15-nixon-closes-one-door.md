# Nixon Closes One Door

On November 25, 1969, President Richard Nixon renounced U.S. use of lethal biological agents and weapons and committed the country to destroying existing biological warfare stocks. In 1970 he extended the policy to toxins. The decision helped clear a path toward the Biological Weapons Convention, opened for signature in 1972.

The renunciation is one of the strangest pivots in Cold War weapons history because it did not follow a triumphant technological climax. It followed doubt.

Biological weapons promised enormous area effects at relatively low production cost. They also promised operational uncertainty, retaliation risks, contamination, strategic instability, and a poor fit with a nuclear superpower already possessing other means of mass destruction.

The United States concluded that the category was not worth maintaining as an offensive arsenal.

That policy change did not make the preceding science disappear. Dispersion data, aerosol technology, agent knowledge, protective research, detection methods, and vaccine work remained relevant to defense.

The same expertise could move across the line because the line was purpose.

This is the enduring treaty problem. The Biological Weapons Convention bans development, production, acquisition, transfer, and stockpiling of biological weapons while allowing prophylactic, protective, and peaceful work. A fermenter can serve medicine or weapons production. Aerosol science can defend cities or design attacks. Genomics can identify pathogens or modify them.

The law therefore cannot classify capability by hardware alone.

The Nixon decision also changes how we should read the earlier open-air tests. It is tempting to assume that because the offensive program ended, the tests had failed scientifically. That is not necessarily true. A technology can work and still be rejected strategically.

Nuclear-powered aircraft were abandoned for reasons beyond whether reactors could produce heat. Chemical weapons can be militarily effective and still be prohibited. A state can renounce a capability because its strategic costs exceed its value.

Biological warfare's particular problem was controllability. The atmosphere that gave it reach also denied the attacker precision. Disease can spread beyond a target. Incubation creates delay. Weather changes. Detection may come too late. Retaliation attribution can be uncertain. A weapon attractive on a map may be awkward in command-and-control reality.

The vulnerability tests made some of those ambiguities visible. They showed transport could be impressive. They also showed transport depended on conditions.

After 1969, defensive research still required understanding the threat. This is why the history cannot be divided into an evil offensive era and a clean defensive era. Defensive competence requires intimate knowledge of offensive possibilities.

The ethical standard has to rest on governance, not on labels.

A defensive program can conduct irresponsible research. An offensive-era experiment can produce knowledge later used for protection. Intent matters legally and morally, but it does not settle the quality of the method or the legitimacy of exposing people.

Nixon closed one door. The corridor behind it remained full of instruments.
