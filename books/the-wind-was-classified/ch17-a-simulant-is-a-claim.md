# A Simulant Is a Claim

A simulant looks like a noun. It is really a sentence.

This behaves enough like that for the purpose we care about.

Every word in the sentence can fail.

'Behaves' may refer to particle size, settling velocity, ultraviolet survival, heat resistance, persistence on surfaces, ability to penetrate filters, ease of detection, or dozens of other properties. 'Enough' is a tolerance. 'Like' identifies a target analogue. 'Purpose' limits transfer.

The Cold War vulnerability program became controversial partly because this grammar was hidden. The public heard harmless organism. The research program meant useful surrogate under specified conditions.

Those are not equivalent.

A good simulant for aerosol transport can be a bad simulant for infectivity. A good surrogate for a bacterial spore can differ radically from a virus. An inert fluorescent particle can reproduce aerodynamics while failing to reproduce biological decay. A live bacterium can reproduce viability while carrying an opportunistic risk absent from an inert tracer.

The discipline of simulation requires stating which dimensions transfer and which do not.

This is surprisingly hard because successful models invite promotion. Once a surrogate predicts one outcome, users begin treating it as validated more broadly. A wind-tunnel model becomes an aircraft claim. A mouse result becomes a human claim. A benchmark becomes intelligence. A language model test becomes a deployment claim.

The pattern is not specific to biological warfare.

Sea-Spray is useful precisely because the error is so physical. *Serratia* was chosen partly because it was easy to detect and considered low-risk. Those properties made it scientifically convenient. Later medicine changed the risk classification. The surrogate did not become malicious. The model boundary moved.

A mature safety system should expect model boundaries to move.

That means reversible experiments, conservative exposure, preserved records, and monitoring designed to detect when the supposedly irrelevant dimension turns out to matter.

The old open-air program often had the opposite structure. The release was irreversible. Exposure could not be withdrawn. Public monitoring was minimal because safety was assumed. Records were classified. By the time the model boundary moved, the experiment was history.

This is why the ethical issue cannot be reduced to whether scientists were reasonable by 1950 standards. Even a reasonable judgment can deserve additional safeguards when the action is irreversible and population-scale.

The concept resembles engineering margin. A bridge is not built to the exact predicted breaking load because models are incomplete. Safety factors acknowledge uncertainty.

What is the biological equivalent of a safety factor in a city-scale tracer test?

Use a nonliving tracer where possible. Reduce quantity. Exclude populations with unusual vulnerability if exposure cannot be bounded. Conduct staged scaling. Maintain independent review. Preserve samples. Predefine stop criteria. Monitor for unexpected outcomes. Disclose after the operational need for secrecy expires.

None of these guarantees zero risk. They make uncertainty governable.

The most useful lesson from the Cold War tests is therefore not 'never simulate dangerous things.' Modern society could not function under that rule. We simulate pandemics, reactor accidents, cyberattacks, chemical releases, fires, and military threats because preparation requires contact with uncomfortable possibilities.

The lesson is that surrogate selection is itself a hypothesis and should be treated with the humility we demand from any other hypothesis.

Harmless is not a material category.

It is a conclusion with error bars.
