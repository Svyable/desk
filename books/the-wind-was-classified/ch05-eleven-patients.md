# Eleven Patients

The most consequential paper in the Sea-Spray story was written by people who did not know Sea-Spray existed.

In 1951 physicians reported a cluster of *Serratia marcescens* infections at Stanford University Hospital. Eleven patients were described. The organism was unusual enough in human infection to deserve publication. One of the patients, Edward J. Nevin, died.

The paper's ignorance is part of its evidentiary value. It was not written decades later by activists searching the medical literature for something to connect to a government test. The clinicians observed a cluster and documented it because it was clinically strange on its own terms.

But contemporaneity is not causation.

The dates sit close enough to make any careful reader uncomfortable. The Navy released *Serratia* into the San Francisco Bay Area in September 1950. The hospital cluster emerged in the same general period. If we were investigating today, a known release of an unusual organism followed by infections involving the same species would trigger an aggressive epidemiological response.

Today we would want the isolates.

Whole-genome sequencing can distinguish strains that older methods could not. We would compare the environmental release strain with every clinical isolate. We would construct phylogenies, examine resistance markers, reconstruct patient movements, sample sinks and equipment, and search for environmental reservoirs. We would ask whether the patients were plausibly in the exposure field. We would compare incidence before and after release. We would look for cases at other hospitals. We would distinguish community acquisition from nosocomial spread.

Almost none of that can be done cleanly now.

The military did not notify local physicians. Stanford did not know to preserve evidence for a future government-exposure investigation. Molecular tools capable of resolving the question did not exist. Medical records and environmental samples were not created with this hypothesis in mind.

Later litigants would inherit a causation problem with missing pieces that secrecy itself had helped remove.

This does not mean the government caused the infections. It means the government made later causal resolution harder.

*Serratia marcescens* eventually became well recognized as an opportunistic pathogen, particularly in hospital settings. It can cause urinary tract infections, pneumonia, bloodstream infections, wound infections, and outbreaks associated with contaminated equipment or solutions. Modern infection-control literature contains many examples in which *Serratia* spreads through reservoirs that have nothing to do with military aerosol testing.

That background matters. A hospital cluster has plausible endogenous explanations.

The strongest case for a Sea-Spray connection rests on timing, unusual organism, and exposure plausibility. The strongest case against certainty rests on the absence of strain matching, the possibility of hospital transmission, incomplete exposure reconstruction, and the broader fact that *Serratia* exists in the environment independently of military use.

One can hold all those facts at once without collapsing into agnosticism about everything.

The release happened. The hospital cluster happened. *Serratia* can cause disease. The public was not informed. A definitive strain-level causal link has not been established.

The death of Edward Nevin later became the emotional center of the story because death gives abstraction a name. That is understandable and dangerous. A named death can pull an uncertain causal chain toward certainty through sheer narrative gravity.

Nevin deserves the opposite treatment: enough respect not to use him as evidence stronger than the record.

The important question is not whether a historian can produce a courtroom-standard causal finding seventy-five years later. It is why the test was structured so that a plausible adverse signal could occur in the same region without the investigators or clinicians being able to connect their data streams.

Imagine the alternative design. The government tells a small network of public-health officials under confidentiality. Hospitals preserve suspicious isolates. Baseline *Serratia* incidence is established. A prewritten adverse-event protocol defines what would trigger investigation. Exposure contours are retained. Independent physicians can halt the program if unusual disease appears.

That design would have reduced uncertainty.

It also would have increased the number of people who knew about the secret test and made public discovery more likely. National-security secrecy and epidemiological quality were in tension.

The program chose secrecy.

That choice is the durable fact. It is why the eleven patients remain both important and unresolved.
