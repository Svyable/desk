# The Lawsuit Problem

Courts like facts that arrive on time.

A secret exposure disclosed decades later arrives carrying absences.

Nevin v. United States forced the Sea-Spray story into the grammar of tort law. The family of Edward Nevin argued that the government's release of *Serratia marcescens* caused the infection that contributed to his death. The government disputed causation. The plaintiffs did not prevail.

The outcome is often recruited into two incompatible myths.

In one, the lawsuit proves that Sea-Spray killed Nevin and the government escaped on a technicality. In the other, the verdict proves that the military release caused no harm.

Neither is a fair description of what courts can establish from this record.

A tort plaintiff generally must show duty, breach, causation, and damages under applicable law. Causation requires connecting a specific act to a specific injury with sufficient evidence. Species identity alone is rarely enough. If the organism exists naturally and in hospitals, the plaintiff needs more.

Modern microbial forensics might have supplied it. The 1950s could not.

The case therefore exposes a mismatch between the precision law demands and the precision secret historical science preserved.

This mismatch can create perverse incentives. An institution that fails to collect individual exposure data can later benefit from the absence of individual exposure proof. A program that does not monitor health can later point to the lack of documented adverse events. This does not mean records were intentionally omitted to defeat litigation. It means accountability architecture matters before anyone imagines a lawsuit.

There is a symmetrical danger for plaintiffs. Missing evidence can become a canvas. If the state behaved secretly, every uncertainty can be filled with the most damaging possibility. The absence of a strain match becomes evidence the match was concealed. The absence of medical surveillance becomes evidence illnesses were ignored. The logic becomes impossible to falsify.

A serious history has to resist both asymmetries.

Classify the claims.

Documented: the release occurred; *Serratia* was used; a hospital cluster was reported; Nevin died; the family sued; the causal case did not succeed.

Credible but incomplete: the timing and geography make a connection worthy of investigation; broad population exposure was plausible; secrecy prevented contemporaneous integration of military and clinical data.

Disputed: that Sea-Spray caused the Stanford cluster or Nevin's death.

Speculative: that the release permanently altered Bay Area microbial ecology in clinically important ways, absent a lineage reconstruction capable of demonstrating persistence from the released strain.

This four-part sorting is not bureaucratic caution. It is how a strange history remains strange without becoming fictional.

The lawsuit also asks a deeper question about state responsibility. Suppose a government conducts a secret experiment reasonably believed to be low-risk, and decades later new science shows the material had a hazard no one understood. Is the state morally responsible even if legal negligence is difficult to establish?

There is no simple answer.

Foreseeability matters in law. Consent matters in ethics. Public authority matters in politics. A risk can be unforeseeable and still feel illegitimate when imposed secretly.

That is why Sea-Spray continues to bother people who know the causation case is unresolved.

The moral injury does not depend entirely on proving the medical injury.
