# The Model Gets a Vote

The Federal Trade Commission's complaint against Rite Aid described a facial-recognition system that sometimes did something machines are uniquely good at doing badly: it repeated a mistake at scale.

According to the Commission, the retailer had used facial-recognition technology in hundreds of stores in an effort to identify people suspected of shoplifting or other problematic behavior. Employees could receive an alert when the system reported a match between a customer and an image in a database. The system produced thousands of false positives, the FTC said, including instances in which people were matched with images associated with locations thousands of miles away. In one pattern cited by the Commission, the same person could be flagged at many different stores across the country.

The machine had not discovered that a customer possessed some hidden criminal identity. It had made a comparison. A face-recognition system does not output guilt; it outputs a similarity result under a particular algorithm, threshold, image quality, reference database, and use case. The institution decides what that result will mean.

That distance between comparison and consequence is where artificial identity becomes politically interesting. Before the alert, the model was a technical process. After the alert, an employee might look at the customer differently. The model had acquired a vote in what happened next.

Not the only vote, and not necessarily a decisive one. But once an institution relies on a model, the representation is no longer sitting quietly in storage. It has entered the chain of action.

We often compress that chain into phrases such as *the fraud system blocked the card* or *the model denied the loan*, as though the output arrived with its own instructions. It did not. Somebody designed the pipeline, chose the threshold, decided whether a human would review the result, assigned costs to false positives and false negatives, and determined how easily the person could contest what followed. The artificial identity is partly technical and partly institutional because the system does not merely estimate; an institution decides what an estimate is allowed to do.

NIST's work on face recognition makes the distinction concrete. A one-to-one verification task asks whether two images are likely to belong to the same person, as when a live image is compared with the enrolled image associated with a claimed identity. A one-to-many identification task asks whether one image resembles any image in a larger gallery. The underlying concept of similarity may be related, but the human consequences are not. A false negative in verification may force the legitimate user to try again or use another method. A false positive in a one-to-many search may put the wrong person on a candidate list for additional scrutiny.

This is why saying *the AI made a mistake* is often too vague to help. The useful questions are about the task, the threshold, the kind of error, who bore its cost, and what happened after the output reached the rest of the institution. Those details reveal the hidden chain between representation and treatment.

Credit makes the same point in a different register. A lender cannot wait years to discover whether every applicant would have repaid a loan, so underwriting necessarily turns current evidence into a judgment about future behavior. Modern models can make that judgment through complex combinations of features, but complexity does not dissolve the institution around the model. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has clarified that, where Regulation B requires specific principal reasons for adverse action, using an opaque or sophisticated predictive model does not excuse a creditor from giving them.

That requirement is revealing. The creditor cannot answer a person's question with a shrug and the phrase **the model decided** because the institution made the decision using the model. Machines acquire social authority when people and institutions choose to rely on their outputs.

This also explains why the language of personal data is sometimes too narrow. A raw fact can be accurate while the operational identity built from it produces a disputed result. You may truly have made a purchase in a certain city, visited a particular website, or used a device associated with a certain neighborhood. The argument may not be about any one fact. It may be about what the system inferred from the combination and what the institution allowed that inference to change.

Fact correction is comparatively legible. If a credit file says you have an account you never opened, the dispute has an object. A probability of default is different. You cannot produce a certificate proving that you would definitely have repaid a loan whose future never occurred. The claim has to be governed through procedure: which data were relevant, whether their use was lawful, whether the model was validated for the task, whether required reasons were supplied, whether prohibited factors or proxies carried weight, and whether a person has a meaningful way to challenge an error.

Artificial identity therefore moves some disputes from correcting a record toward governing a model. That is harder because the representation can be wrong without containing a single false sentence.

The model gets a vote in retail as well, though the stakes may look smaller. In 2025, Federal Trade Commission staff published initial findings from a study of what it called surveillance pricing. The staff examined intermediary companies offering tools that could use information such as location, demographics, browsing behavior, shopping history, and other signals to help retailers tailor prices, promotions, or product presentation. The findings did not establish that every consumer was routinely receiving a unique secret price. They showed that companies had built systems capable of using granular observed and inferred information about consumers inside pricing and promotion.

This is not entirely new. Merchants have always treated customers differently through negotiation, coupons, loyalty programs, student discounts, targeted direct mail, and hotel revenue management. The newer problem is not differentiation by itself but the granularity and invisibility of the classification. A student discount is legible: the category is stated and the rule can be understood. A promotion produced from browsing history, location, inferred segment, time of day, and purchase behavior may be almost impossible for the customer to reconstruct. The model votes on the commercial context without showing its ballot.

Recommendation systems turn that vote into a continuous process. A ranked feed is not usually described as a decision about a person's rights, and often it is simply useful. A good recommender can save attention and surface music, articles, businesses, jobs, products, or people the user would never have found through a generic list. But the feed also shows how an artificial identity can become entangled with the behavior it is supposed to predict.

The system estimates that you prefer a kind of material and shows you more of it. You interact with what is available, and those interactions become evidence for the next estimate. The same loop can appear in commerce, where premium products shown to a customer generate purchases that later look like proof of premium preference, or in fraud detection, where unusual transactions that are blocked disappear from the surviving history. The model is not merely observing a person from outside. It can help shape the environment in which the next observation occurs.

That does not make prediction meaningless. It means evaluation has to include intervention. A model operating inside a feedback loop changes some of the evidence it later learns from, so the question is not only whether the prediction matched behavior but how the surrounding system helped produce the behavior being measured.

Law has begun approaching pieces of this problem without using the phrase *artificial identity*. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation includes protections concerning certain decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, when those decisions produce legal or similarly significant effects. The rule has exceptions and detailed conditions; it is not a universal ban on automation or a magical right to a simple explanation of every model. The EU AI Act draws other boundaries, including restrictions around certain uses of biometric categorization and rules for higher-risk applications. Again, the details matter more than the slogan.

What those legal systems recognize is that a machine-generated representation can stop being mere back-office computation once enough consequence is attached to it. Governance has to follow the representation into the decision.

That is difficult because consequential systems often need classification. Fraud detection is useful because institutions do not treat every transaction identically. Lenders distinguish risk. Security systems sometimes deny access. Platforms moderate and rank. Insurers pool and price risk. Hiring is selective by definition. A world without models would not be a world without classification or power; people would simply make more of those classifications manually, sometimes with less consistency and less evidence.

The question is therefore not whether the model gets a vote. It is what kind of vote the institution gives it. Weight, evidence, audit, stakes, visibility, retention, update rate, and avenues for challenge all matter because the same model output can be harmless in one response regime and severe in another. A moderately accurate fraud score may be useful if it requests another authenticator. The same score would be much harder to defend if it permanently froze an account with no practical recourse. A highly accurate estimate that a person is vulnerable to a sales tactic can still be used abusively. Accuracy matters, but it cannot tell us what an institution should do with what it knows.

The most revealing moment comes when probability reaches the edge of the system. Inside the machine, the output may be a similarity score or a probability distribution. The person does not experience that uncertainty directly. A card is declined. An account is challenged. A loan is denied. A promotion appears. A face is flagged. A feed changes. Somewhere between model and interface, a number becomes a branch and an estimate becomes a verb.

That conversion deserves more attention than it usually gets. The artificial identity may be probabilistic, but the surrounding world often has to do something categorical enough to matter. The person discovers the machine-made version of themselves through the action it produces, sometimes without ever seeing the representation that preceded it.

Transparency helps, but it is not the same as contestability. A list of hundreds of features may be technically transparent and practically useless. A risk score may be understandable while the underlying data are wrong. An explanation may describe the current decision without revealing how years of behavior produced the profile. A system may be fully documented and still use a category society decides should not carry weight.

Contestability asks a more practical question: can the person meaningfully challenge what the machine-made identity is doing? The answer will differ by domain. Sometimes correction is enough. Sometimes the system needs another authentication path or a human review. Sometimes law should prevent a category from being used at all. Sometimes the model is functioning properly and the institution is entitled to rely on it, even if the individual dislikes the outcome. A real appeal process does not guarantee victory; it prevents an estimate from masquerading as unquestionable fact.

That distinction becomes more important as representations grow more fluent. A primitive score looks like a tool. A conversational model can explain a recommendation in ordinary language, even when the explanation is incomplete or reconstructed. A persistent assistant can speak with the confidence of something that seems to remember the user. Once an agent receives tools and credentials, the representation no longer influences only what happens to the person. It may receive permission to do something for the person.

That is the next threshold.

The artificial identity becomes executable.
