# The Profile Learns to Predict

In 2013, three researchers published a result that became famous partly because the raw material seemed so trivial.

Facebook Likes.

Not medical records. Not tax returns. Not a long psychiatric interview. Not secret files obtained by an intelligence service.

Likes.

Michal Kosinski, David Stillwell, and Thore Graepel analyzed digital records from tens of thousands of volunteers and showed that the pattern of pages a person had liked could support predictions about a range of personal attributes. Some were obvious enough: age and gender. Others were more sensitive, including political and religious views, personality-related measures, and several characteristics that users might not have expected a list of clicked buttons to reveal.

The result did not mean that every prediction was correct.

It did not mean Facebook Likes contained a hidden essence of the person.

It did not mean a machine could look at a few scattered clicks and know everything worth knowing.

What it demonstrated was more important than omniscience and much more plausible.

Behavior leaves structure.

Once enough behavior is recorded across enough people, a model can discover relationships that no single act announces by itself.

A Like is an event.

A pattern of Likes becomes evidence.

That change from event to evidence is where the modern profile began learning to predict.

Two years later, a related study by Wu Youyou, Kosinski, and Stillwell made the point harder to dismiss. They compared computer-based personality judgments derived from Facebook Likes with personality judgments made by humans who knew the participants in different relationships. On the study's Big Five measures, the computer judgments became increasingly accurate as the number of Likes rose and, at sufficient levels of observed behavior, could outperform several categories of human acquaintance.

The headline version of that research quickly became something like: computers know you better than your friends.

That is too broad.

The system was not tested on whether it could comfort you after a funeral, detect a joke from your childhood, recognize that you were pretending to enjoy a party, or know which promise you would keep under pressure. It performed a defined prediction task using a defined behavioral trace and a defined personality measure.

The narrower result is enough.

Machines do not need to know you in the human sense in order to know something useful about you that you did not explicitly tell them.

That is the breakthrough.

A traditional profile is declarative.

Name: provided.

Address: provided.

Date of birth: provided.

Occupation: provided.

A predictive profile is inferential.

Likely to respond to this offer.

Likely to enjoy this song.

Likely to churn.

Likely to default.

Likely to be the legitimate account holder.

Likely to prefer premium service.

Likely to click.

Likely to ignore.

Likely to buy if the discount arrives this afternoon instead of tomorrow morning.

The machine-made identity becomes less like a form and more like a weather forecast.

That comparison helps because weather forecasts are obviously probabilistic without being useless. Nobody argues that a seventy-percent chance of rain is a secret fact about the sky waiting to be uncovered. It is an estimate generated from observations and a model.

Person models work similarly, with one crucial difference.

The weather does not read its forecast.

People live inside environments changed by predictions about them.

That is where inference becomes socially complicated.

Suppose a music service predicts that you prefer a certain kind of song. It shows you more songs like that. You listen to some because they are available. The service observes the listening and strengthens the prediction.

Did it discover your taste or help form it?

The answer can be both.

Suppose a retailer predicts that you are price sensitive and emphasizes discounts. You buy discounted products. The behavior confirms the segment.

Did the system detect frugality or create a shopping environment in which frugality became easier to express?

Again, both can be true.

Predictive systems are often trained on behavior produced under earlier predictive systems.

This recursive quality matters because the artificial identity can become self-reinforcing without ever becoming perfectly accurate.

A model only needs to be useful enough that the surrounding system keeps consulting it.

The data-broker industry made a business out of this long before generative AI entered ordinary life.

The Federal Trade Commission's 2014 report on data brokers described an ecosystem largely invisible to consumers. The companies it studied assembled information from many sources and sold products for marketing, risk mitigation, people search, and other purposes. Consumers often had little direct relationship with the broker building the profile.

This was not simply a larger address book.

The commercial value came from categorization.

Raw transactions and records could be turned into audiences, segments, and predictions that were easier for clients to use.

The original data might say that a household bought certain products, lived in a certain area, used a particular device, or visited certain kinds of websites.

The product sold to another company could be a compressed proposition about what kind of consumer the household probably was.

Compression is the key economic act.

No advertiser wants to manually inspect the life history of ten million people.

No recommender wants a human editor to reason from every prior click.

No fraud team wants an analyst to read every transaction in sequence before approving a card payment at a grocery store.

A model turns high-dimensional history into a decision-relevant representation.

This is useful precisely because it throws information away.

The artificial identity is therefore not merely incomplete by accident.

Incompleteness is part of the design.

A credit-risk model ignores your favorite color because it does not improve the task.

A recommender may ignore your salary if it does not help ranking.

A biometric verifier ignores your biography and concentrates on measurable features useful for comparison.

A fraud model may care about location, transaction amount, device, timing, merchant, and behavioral sequence while knowing nothing about why you are buying the item.

The person disappears so the signal can become manageable.

That sounds dehumanizing, but it is worth noticing that humans do something similar.

A mechanic does not need your political philosophy to diagnose an engine noise.

A dentist does not need your investment portfolio to fill a cavity.

A chess opponent does not need your childhood history to punish a weak move.

Selective attention is often a condition of competence.

The difference is that computational selection can happen without the person knowing which details became relevant.

A form shows you its questions.

An inference engine can generate questions you never saw.

This changes the old idea of disclosure.

Privacy law and ordinary social norms often assume that information moves outward from a person. You tell the doctor. You fill out the application. You post the photograph. You authorize the account. You volunteer the answer.

Predictive systems reverse part of that flow.

The person discloses behavior.

The system supplies meaning.

That meaning can be surprisingly remote from the original act.

A shopping pattern can suggest a life stage.

A movement pattern can suggest routine.

A typing rhythm can support continuous authentication.

A collection of media choices can support a personality estimate.

A short block of natural language can now support inferences that once required a dedicated questionnaire.

In 2026, researchers reported in *Nature Human Behaviour* that widely available large language models could estimate personality traits from brief open-ended narratives with useful agreement to self-reports and predictive validity for other measured outcomes.

Again, the result should not be inflated.

A personality estimate is not a diagnosis.

A narrative is not a complete life.

A model that performs well on a research measure has not discovered an immutable inner self.

But the input is important.

Facebook Likes were machine-native behavioral traces. Open-ended narrative is ordinary human expression.

The inference surface is expanding.

A person no longer needs to click a labeled preference for a system to form a view about preference.

Language itself becomes evidence.

This is a profound change because language is how people experiment with identity.

We exaggerate, speculate, joke, rehearse, vent, wonder, contradict ourselves, try on positions, and say things in one context that we would never want preserved as a permanent statement of self.

A model trained to extract stable traits from language has an incentive to ignore some of that ambiguity.

Stability is useful.

People are not always stable.

The artificial identity therefore faces a problem that becomes more serious as inference improves: when does a pattern become permission to generalize?

Imagine a person who spends three months researching a disease because a relative has been diagnosed.

A system observes unusual reading, purchases, and travel. Depending on the objective, those behaviors might be useful for inferring concern, caregiving, health interest, or future spending.

The person may never have the disease.

The model does not need to claim otherwise in order to alter the person's environment.

It only needs to associate the behavior with a category relevant to the next decision.

This is why inferred identity can be more difficult to correct than factual identity.

If a file lists the wrong middle name, you can provide a document.

If a model infers that you are a high-value customer, what exactly would count as proof that you are not?

You might be one for this transaction and not the next.

You might want premium travel and discount groceries.

You might be wealthy and intensely price sensitive.

You might have clicked expensive products because you were writing a report.

You might visit luxury stores because you work nearby.

You might read extremist material because you study extremism.

The inference can be statistically reasonable and individually absurd.

That is not an edge condition. It is what statistical inference means.

A model maps individual observations through relationships learned from populations.

Sometimes the individual resembles the learned pattern.

Sometimes the individual is the exception the average cannot see.

The risk becomes larger when the category feels semantically rich.

A label such as **frequent traveler** is easy to treat as a convenience.

A label such as **financially vulnerable**, **likely parent**, **high risk**, **politically persuadable**, or **health conscious** can sound more like a judgment about the person.

The language tempts us to forget that the category exists because somebody defined an objective, chose data, selected training examples, and built a system to make a certain distinction useful.

There may be no stable thing in the world called a **highly persuadable consumer** waiting to be measured like blood pressure.

The system may be constructing a market category whose meaning comes from the use it serves.

This is an important limit on the artificial-identity metaphor.

The machine-made self is not simply a hidden portrait assembled from discovered facts.

It is often a working theory.

The theory can be revised.

It can be wrong.

It can be locally useful.

It can conflict with another theory built for another purpose.

And it can change the evidence that arrives next.

The Federal Trade Commission's 2016 big-data report described how statistical models could use past purchases, searches, shopping habits, and location to predict consumer behavior at different prices. The report was careful to discuss both benefits and risks. Big-data techniques can help institutions recognize opportunity in people ignored by older rules. They can also create exclusion, discrimination, or opaque segmentation.

This dual character has persisted.

A predictive identity can widen access because the model notices evidence that a crude legacy category missed.

It can narrow access because the model discovers a proxy for disadvantage.

It can protect an account by recognizing genuine behavior.

It can lock out the real owner because today's legitimate behavior looks unfamiliar.

It can reduce irrelevant advertising.

It can create a sealed informational environment that overlearns yesterday's attention.

It can remember accessibility needs.

It can remember a temporary condition long after the person wants it forgotten.

The quality of the inference does not settle the ethics.

A perfectly accurate prediction can still be used for a purpose a person would reject.

An inaccurate prediction can still be harmless if the stakes are low and correction is easy.

The decisive questions are purpose, consequence, transparency, and control.

That is why arguments about whether algorithms are “really accurate” often miss the institutional point.

Accuracy matters, but accuracy relative to what?

A music recommender can tolerate error because the cost of one bad song is low.

A fraud system may prefer a certain balance of false positives and false negatives because one missed theft and one blocked legitimate purchase carry different costs.

A lender operates under legal requirements that do not disappear because a model is complex.

A facial-identification system used in a security context creates a different burden because a false positive can attach suspicion to the wrong person.

Artificial identity is therefore always attached to a decision regime.

The same quality of prediction can be acceptable in one regime and reckless in another.

A model that is good enough to recommend a movie is not automatically good enough to recommend a medical intervention.

A model that predicts a broad population trend is not automatically good enough to classify an individual.

A model that estimates personality in a research sample is not automatically good enough to decide who should be hired.

These statements sound obvious when written plainly.

Automation has a way of hiding obvious things behind scale.

Once a prediction is built into a pipeline, the surrounding institution begins treating the output as ordinary input.

The model returns a score.

The score triggers a threshold.

The threshold routes the person.

Nobody needs to announce that a theory about the person has become operational identity.

It simply happens one conditional statement at a time.

This is where the profile's evolution matters.

The old profile answered: what facts are on file?

The predictive profile answers: what should the system expect?

Expectation is more powerful because it points forward.

The machine-made identity is no longer only a record of what you did.

It becomes a wager about what you will do next.

And once institutions begin staking money, access, security, attention, and opportunity on that wager, the model acquires something it did not have while sitting quietly in storage.

A vote.
