# Chapter 2 — Zero-shot personality inference boundary

## Primary study

Aidan G. C. Wright, Whitney R. Ringwald, Colin E. Vize, Johannes C. Eichstaedt, Mike Angstadt, Aman Taxali, and Chandra Sripada, **“Assessing personality using zero-shot generative AI scoring of brief open-ended text,”** *Nature Human Behaviour* 10 (2026), 541–555. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02389-x. Published January 30, 2026.

This is the primary article behind the Chapter 2 sentence currently supported by the adjacent *Nature Human Behaviour* research briefing.

Across two samples using different forms of open-ended narrative — spontaneous streams of thought and daily video diaries — the authors asked seven commercially available generative LLMs to score Big Five personality traits without task-specific model training. The resulting scores converged with self-report measures, and the study reported predictive validity for measured daily behavior and mental-health outcomes. Averaging scores across models produced the strongest agreement with self-report.

## What the chapter may safely claim

The study supports the bounded proposition that ordinary open-ended language can contain enough statistical structure for current general-purpose LLMs to estimate some measured personality traits under research conditions. This extends the chapter’s inference argument beyond machine-native traces such as Likes: the input can be ordinary narrative expression.

It does **not** establish that an LLM has discovered a person’s stable essence, that Big Five scores exhaust personality, that one model is uniformly reliable, or that research-level trait scoring is appropriate for hiring, insurance, clinical diagnosis, credit, security, or other consequential classification.

## Counterevidence and limits

The paper itself supplies useful friction against a triumphalist reading. Results varied across the seven LLMs, and the ensemble average performed best, so the evidence is not “any LLM can infer personality accurately.” The principal comparison remains convergence with self-report and prediction of selected measured outcomes; neither is a ground-truth assay of the whole person.

The two narrative settings also matter. A spontaneous stream of thought and a daily video diary are richer than a single stray sentence. The chapter should therefore resist converting “brief open-ended narratives” into a claim that any isolated message reliably reveals stable traits.

There is a second conceptual limit important to *Artificial Identity*: even an accurate trait estimate is still a task-specific representation created for a purpose. Its predictive validity does not make it the person, and accuracy alone does not authorize consequential use.

## Editorial consequence

No manuscript revision is necessary for this pass. Chapter 2 already contains the right cautions: a personality estimate is not a diagnosis, narrative is not a complete life, and research performance does not establish an immutable inner self. The high-value correction is evidentiary: cite the original peer-reviewed study as the primary source and retain the research briefing only as secondary context.
