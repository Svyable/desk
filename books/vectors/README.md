How Direction Became the Language of Intelligence.

The working thesis is that vectors are becoming one of the hidden primitives of modern life. They began as a way to describe quantities with direction—velocity, force, displacement—but today machines use high-dimensional vectors to represent words, images, products, people, memories, preferences and goals.

This book follows the arrow from geometry and physics into embeddings, semantic search, recommendation systems, multimodal models, vector databases, retrieval, model steering and autonomous agents. It asks what becomes possible when meaning can be placed in a space, compared by distance, combined by direction and acted on by machines.

The argument is deliberately practical and political as well as technical. A vector space is not reality. Coordinates are chosen, training data leaves fingerprints, similarity encodes assumptions, averages can erase minorities, and a direction optimized at planetary scale becomes a form of power. The central question is not merely how vectors work. It is who chooses the space and what the machines learn to move toward.
