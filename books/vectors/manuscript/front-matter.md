# Vectors

**Sven Hardy Benson**

An arrow is one of humanity's oldest abstractions.

It says two things at once.

How much.

And which way.

For most of history, that was enough to make vectors feel like the property of mathematicians, navigators, physicists and engineers. Velocity has a direction. Force has a direction. A ship can be ten miles from shore and still be in trouble if it is moving the wrong way.

Then computers began giving directions to things that do not seem to have directions at all.

Words.

Pictures.

Songs.

Customers.

Memories.

Ideas.

Modern machine-learning systems routinely turn these things into long lists of numbers called vectors. The lists may have hundreds or thousands of dimensions. No person can look at them and see what they mean. Yet inside that strange geometry, machines can discover that a sentence resembles another sentence, that an image corresponds to a description, that a customer may like a product, that a document could answer a question, or that one concept lies closer to another than a third.

Meaning becomes location.

Similarity becomes distance.

Preference becomes direction.

This transformation is easy to underestimate because the underlying mathematics is old. The consequences are not.

A keyword search asks whether the same symbols appear.

A vector search asks whether two things point into nearby regions of meaning.

A recommendation engine can treat your history as a direction through a space of possible choices.

A language model can retrieve memories by geometry rather than by filename. A multimodal model can place text and images near one another in a shared representational space. An agent can be steered toward one behavior and away from another by altering the direction of its internal state.

The vector has escaped the blackboard.

It now helps decide what we see, what machines remember, what they associate, what they recommend, and increasingly what they do.

That makes vectors a technical subject with human consequences.

Every vector space has a design history. Somebody chose the training data. Somebody chose the objective. Somebody chose what counts as similar enough. Some distinctions become geometrically obvious. Others disappear. Averages pull toward majorities. Sparse regions are easier to ignore. A system optimized to move toward engagement, conversion, relevance or compliance can become very effective at heading in exactly the wrong human direction.

This book is therefore about two kinds of orientation.

The first is mathematical: how vectors let machines represent and navigate complicated spaces.

The second is civilizational: what happens when more of the world is translated into spaces that machines can navigate for us.

We will begin with arrows and coordinates, move through embeddings and semantic search, then follow vectors into recommendation systems, memory, model steering, agents, markets and institutions.

The mathematics will remain simple enough to see through.

The consequences will not.

A number can tell you how much progress you made.

A vector asks the more dangerous question.

Toward what?
