How AI agents that shop, match, and choose on our behalf are rewriting the psychology of wanting.

The working thesis is that the twentieth century built an enormous machinery for influencing what people want — advertising, database marketing, recommendation engines, choice architecture — but always left a human hand on the final decision. That hand is now optional. By the mid-2020s, AI agents had moved from suggesting products to completing purchases, screening romantic matches, and executing financial decisions with only a standing instruction from their owner. The book asks what happens to taste, identity, and markets once the last click stops being required.

It is not a technology panic. Recommendation systems, defaults, and algorithmic pricing have a long, well-studied history, and the record on their harms is more mixed than the popular version of "filter bubbles" and "manipulation" usually admits. The book tries to hold both things at once: the tools that shape preference are older and better-researched than most readers assume, and something genuinely new happens when a system stops nudging a choice and starts making it.
