# Chapter briefs

Working beat sheet for each chapter. Facts and citations get filled in from
the source ledger as research lands; this file tracks the argument each
chapter is responsible for so the book adds up to one continuous case rather
than twenty loosely related essays.

## Thesis

The tools for shaping what people want are old and well studied: advertising,
database marketing, choice architecture, recommendation engines. What is new,
starting in the mid-2020s, is that the systems doing the shaping have started
completing the transaction themselves, with only a standing instruction from
a human owner. The book's question is not whether influence is new — it
isn't — but what changes when the final click stops requiring a person.

The book commits to counter-evidence throughout: choice-overload research is
more mixed than the "jam study" made it sound; the "filter bubble" thesis has
real empirical pushback; ego depletion did not replicate cleanly; decision
fatigue research has a contested flagship study. A book that only stacked
alarming anecdotes would be easier to write and less honest.

## Part I — How wanting got built (Ch 1-7)

**Ch 1 — The Last Free Choice.** Open on a concrete, dated scene of agentic
checkout (drawn from real reporting on OpenAI Instant Checkout / Perplexity
shopping / similar, not invented dialogue). State the thesis. Preview the
book's shape without a bullet list.

**Ch 2 — A Short History of Being Sold To.** Department stores, mail-order
catalogs, motivational research (Ernest Dichter), Vance Packard's *The Hidden
Persuaders* (1957) and its reception, the rise of database marketing. Ground
the reader: influence-over-choice is at least a century old.

**Ch 3 — The Machine Learns to Recommend.** Amazon item-to-item collaborative
filtering (Linden/Smith/York 2003), the Netflix Prize and Netflix's own
account of why the winning algorithm was never fully shipped, Spotify
Discover Weekly, YouTube's watch-time-optimized deep learning system
(Covington et al. 2016) and the Tufekci "radicalizer" debate, TikTok's For
You algorithm.

**Ch 4 — The Jam Problem.** Iyengar & Lepper's 2000 jam study *and* the
Scheibehenne/Greifeneder/Todd 2010 meta-analysis that found close to a null
average effect. Teach the reader to distrust a single famous study.

**Ch 5 — The Architecture of Nudges.** Thaler & Sunstein, Madrian & Shea's
401(k) default-enrollment study, the coining of "dark patterns" (Harry
Brignull), the FTC's 2022 dark-patterns report, the FTC v. Amazon Prime
cancellation suit and its resolution.

**Ch 6 — Anchors, Decoys, and the Price That Isn't Real.** Tversky & Kahneman
wheel-of-fortune anchoring, Ariely/Loewenstein/Prelec arbitrary coherence,
Huber/Payne/Puto decoy effect, the *Economist* subscription anecdote traced
to its actual source.

**Ch 7 — The Filter Bubble That Mostly Wasn't.** Eli Pariser's 2011 thesis,
then Flaxman/Goel/Rao 2016 and Bakshy/Messing/Adamic 2015, and the later
Meta-university 2023 Science/Nature election-study collaboration. Land on a
calibrated view: algorithms shape exposure at the margin; users' own choices
usually do more of the sorting than the popular narrative admits.

## Part II — From suggestion to delegation (Ch 8-11)

**Ch 8 — From Suggestion to Delegation.** The 2024-2026 turn: OpenAI Instant
Checkout, Perplexity agentic shopping, Google Project Mariner, Anthropic
Computer Use, Visa Trusted Agent Protocol / Mastercard Agent Pay, Amazon
Rufus. Be honest about what is live and used versus announced-but-thin.

**Ch 9 — Who Is the Customer Now?** Retailers optimizing product feeds for
agents instead of people (AEO/GEO), what changes when the reader of your
product page is a language model.

**Ch 10 — The Taste of a Machine.** Does an agent have taste, or an average?
Algorithmic monoculture, the RealPage/YieldStar DOJ suit as a case where
independent "optimal" decisions produced coordinated outcomes without
communication, Calvano et al.'s reinforcement-learning collusion research.

**Ch 11 — The Ease of Yes.** One-click buying and its patent history,
subscriptions, buy-now-pay-later data, what gets easier to buy when a human
no longer has to re-authorize it.

## Part III — What delegation touches (Ch 12-16)

**Ch 12 — Loneliness, Love, and the Matching Engines.** Tinder's Elo history
and Hinge's Gale-Shapley-derived matching, AI companion apps (Replika,
Character.AI), the Character.AI wrongful-death lawsuit, handled carefully
and factually.

**Ch 13 — Attention Merchants Become Decision Merchants.** Tim Wu's
"Attention Merchants" thesis updated: the business model shift from selling
attention to selling completed decisions; affiliate and agent-commission
economics.

**Ch 14 — Whose Agent Is It?** Conflicts of interest built into "free"
shopping agents, price discrimination history (Orbitz/Staples WSJ reporting,
Uber surge pricing research), the FTC's 2024 surveillance-pricing 6(b)
study, the case for a fiduciary standard for purchasing agents.

**Ch 15 — Children of the Recommendation Engine.** What it means to grow up
inside curation from early childhood; treat sparingly and evidence-led, no
moral panic.

**Ch 16 — The Economics of Everyone Getting the Same Answer.** Market
concentration when many agents converge on one "best" option; ties back to
Ch 10's algorithmic-monoculture material with a market-structure lens.

## Part IV — What's left to choose (Ch 17-20)

**Ch 17 — Building an Honest Agent.** Real design alternatives: EU AI Act
Article 5 manipulative-practice provisions, EU DSA Article 25 dark-patterns
provisions, proposals for agent fiduciary duty (Hartzog/Richards "loyalty"
line of work), companies positioning themselves as on-the-user's-side.

**Ch 18 — What Effort Was For.** IKEA effect, self-determination theory, the
ego-depletion replication failure (Hagger et al. 2016 RRR), the
Danziger/Levav/Avnaim-Pesso parole study and its later critiques — what
survives of "decision fatigue" once the flagship evidence is re-examined.

**Ch 19 — Teaching Taste.** Practical, non-preachy chapter on cultivating
deliberate choice-making amid delegation — for individuals, families,
institutions.

**Ch 20 — The Last Free Choice, Revisited.** Return to the Chapter 1 scene.
Synthesize without a checklist. Leave the reader with a way of noticing when
a choice has quietly become someone — or something — else's.
