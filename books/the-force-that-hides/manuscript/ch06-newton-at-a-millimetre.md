# 6. Newton at a Millimetre

Newton wrote one law. Experimentalists inherited many distance regimes.

At planetary scales, orbital dynamics checks gravity. At astronomical scales, binaries and lensing take over. At laboratory scales, source masses can be moved deliberately. Below a millimeter, the experiment enters a hostile region where gravitational force falls rapidly while electrostatic, magnetic, Casimir, and surface effects become comparatively enormous.

This short-range frontier became important for reasons that had little to do with the 1986 fifth force. Models with extra dimensions suggested that gravity might deviate from inverse-square behavior below the compactification scale. Light scalar fields suggested additional Yukawa interactions. Dark-sector models generated new mediators.

The experiments kept finding Newton.

A 2007 HUST torsion-pendulum test reported no new gravitational-strength Yukawa interaction in its tested submillimeter range. Eöt-Wash experiments pushed comparable ideas with rotating attractors and exquisitely engineered null geometries.

The null result is easiest to misunderstand as boredom. It is actually a measurement of how little room new physics has to occupy if it behaves linearly.

The word linear matters. In a linear Yukawa picture, double the source and the field doubles. Put shielding around it and the new interaction passes through ordinary matter according to its coupling and range. In a nonlinear screened theory, the source can saturate. Adding more mass may mostly add screened interior. The surface region can matter disproportionately.

That is why short-range gravity became one of the places where screening theory had to become experimentally literate. It was no longer enough to write a cosmological scalar potential. One had to solve the field around plates, spheres, chamber walls, atoms, and pendula.

The fifth force had entered engineering.
