# 13. MICROSCOPE Takes the Argument to Orbit

On Earth, equivalence tests fight the Earth.

In orbit, MICROSCOPE made the Earth the attractor and the spacecraft the laboratory.

The mission carried differential electrostatic accelerometers containing test masses of different composition. If titanium and platinum fell differently toward Earth, the electrostatic forces needed to keep them centered would contain a composition-dependent signal.

After two and a half years of operation and months of science data, the final result found no violation. The reported Eötvös parameter for the titanium–platinum pair was consistent with zero at the level of a few parts in 10^15.

This is one of the strongest direct tests of the weak equivalence principle ever performed.

The result severely constrains new composition-dependent interactions. But screened dark-energy models force another question: what does “orbit” do to the field?

The environment around a satellite is vastly less dense than a terrestrial laboratory, but the Earth itself is a large dense source. The spacecraft and proof masses have their own screening properties. A model must be solved in this geometry, not merely compared to the number 10^-15.

In March 2026, Feleppa, de Graaf, Brax, and Lambiase published a Physical Review Letters analysis of screened dark energy using near-Earth space measurements. They derived constraints from Gravity Probe B geodetic precession and LAGEOS-2 pericenter advance, and studied a prospective orbital Sagnac configuration. In their models, LAGEOS-2 supplies strong Earth-orbit limits on symmetron and dilaton parameter space, while future clock-scale Sagnac precision could become especially powerful for chameleons.

The broader lesson is almost geological: experiments at different densities and gravitational potentials are not redundant. They are cross-sections through a nonlinear theory.
