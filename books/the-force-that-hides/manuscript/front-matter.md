# The Force That Hides

## Chameleons, Fifth Forces, and the Experiment Gravity Keeps Escaping

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a childish version of experimental physics in which nature puts the answer on the apparatus if the apparatus is good enough. Better clock, better balance, better vacuum, better answer.

Gravity has been less cooperative.

For a century, physicists have suspended objects from fibers, dropped them in towers, bounced lasers from the Moon, flown accelerometers in orbit, split atoms into spatial superpositions, levitated microscopic sensors, and asked some version of the same question: does all matter really fall the same way, and does Newton's inverse-square law remain exact once we look closely enough?

The answer has been an extraordinary sequence of nulls. Those nulls deserve respect. They are some of the hardest-won facts in physics.

But there is a class of theories that turns the experimental instinct against itself. In those theories, the hypothetical field changes behavior with its environment. Put it near a dense object and it may acquire a large effective mass. Put it inside ordinary laboratory walls and only a thin layer of matter may source it. Move the same experiment into a better vacuum, or into orbit, and the effective problem can change.

This is not a loophole to be worshipped. A theory that survives only by becoming invisible wherever somebody looks is not automatically profound. It may simply be evasive mathematics waiting for its parameter space to die.

Still, screening is a real piece of field theory, and it creates a rare experimental situation: the apparatus does not merely measure the field. The apparatus helps set the field's local equations of motion.

That is worth following carefully.

The central rule of this book is simple. A null result excludes what it actually tested. A positive result must survive everything the apparatus can fake. Between those two rules lies a surprisingly large piece of modern fundamental physics.
