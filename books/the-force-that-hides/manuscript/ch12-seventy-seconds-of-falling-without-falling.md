# 12. Seventy Seconds of Falling Without Falling

Seventy seconds is an absurdly long time for a matter-wave superposition in a gravity experiment.

The 2024 Berkeley lattice interferometer did not make an atom immune to gravity. It used optical potentials to support the wave packets while preserving phase coherence between spatially separated components. The trick converts the usual fountain limit—how long the atom can fly before it hits something—into a coherence and control problem.

That is important for more than fifth forces. It opens routes to precision gravimetry, measurements of Newton's constant, gravitational Aharonov–Bohm-like effects, and tests of whether gravity can entangle or phase quantum matter in predicted ways.

For screened fields, the attraction is practical. A static source mass can remain beside the atoms for a long interrogation. The source can be moved or modulated between runs. Different atom species can test composition dependence. Different chamber geometries can probe environmental response.

The 2026 long-baseline proposal by Banks and collaborators takes this logic further. It considers a roughly ten-meter atom interferometer equipped with an annular planar source mass inside the vacuum chamber. The authors introduce a Q-flip sequence to alternate interferometer configurations, creating a controllable time signature that helps separate a static fifth-force acceleration from drifting backgrounds. They also propose in-situ calibration of the source's Newtonian gravity.

The projected reach improves existing chameleon and symmetron constraints by roughly one to one-and-a-half orders of magnitude in targeted parameter regions.

This is theory doing what it should do after years of nulls: not asking for faith, but designing a place where the surviving model is forced to produce a measurable pattern.
