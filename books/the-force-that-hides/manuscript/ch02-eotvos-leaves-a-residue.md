# 2. Eötvös Leaves a Residue

The Eötvös experiment became famous for saying no.

That is why the 1986 reanalysis was so disruptive. Ephraim Fischbach and colleagues went back to the early twentieth-century Eötvös–Pekár–Fekete torsion-balance data and argued that the old results were not simply null. They reported an apparent composition dependence correlated with baryon number per unit mass and proposed an intermediate-range interaction.

The historical experiment deserves to be seen as hardware before it becomes mythology. A torsion balance is a suspended object that wants to rotate if different horizontal forces act on its test masses. If inertial mass and gravitational mass are exactly equivalent, test bodies of different composition should respond to Earth's gravity in the same way, after known disturbances are removed. If some new interaction couples differently to different materials, the balance can twist.

The difficulty is that the world contains many ways to twist a balance.

Gravity gradients matter. Temperature gradients matter. Magnetic properties matter. Suspension fiber behavior matters. Geometry matters. Nearby masses matter. Even the material labels used in a retrospective analysis matter, because a proposed coupling may depend on nuclear composition at a precision the original experiment never needed to characterize.

The 1986 paper made a strong claim from old data: what generations had read as experimental scatter might contain a composition-correlated pattern. That was enough to ignite a search program.

It was not enough to establish a force.

This distinction is the first recurring problem in the book. Reanalysis can reveal structure that the original experiment was not designed to test. It can also overfit structure that arises from the original apparatus, metadata, or environmental systematics. The cure is prospective prediction. If the proposed force is real, a new balance with chosen materials, controlled geometry, and predetermined analysis should reproduce it.

That is what later experiments largely failed to do for the particular intermediate-range force suggested in the 1980s.

The episode therefore matters less as an unsolved anomaly than as a clean case study in how precision gravity creates new physics and then disciplines it. The old balance did not prove a fifth force. The reanalysis created a target. New instruments attacked the target.

The target mostly disappeared.

But the method survived, and the next generation of theories learned from the execution.
