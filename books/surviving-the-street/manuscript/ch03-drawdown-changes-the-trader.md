# Drawdown Changes the Trader

A losing position does not sit quietly in an account.

It begins to rewrite the story.

At first the investor may still see the original thesis clearly. Revenue growth. Balance-sheet improvement. A valuation gap. A catalyst. A statistical signal. A change in policy. Whatever justified the position remains available as an argument.

Then the price falls.

The first decline can feel like opportunity. Nothing important has changed, only the quote. The investor may buy more.

The second decline requires explanation.

The third can become personal.

Now the investor is no longer asking only whether the asset is attractive. The investor is asking whether selling would admit a mistake, whether the loss will come back, whether the market is being irrational, whether somebody else knows something, whether buying more would lower the average cost, whether checking the price again might somehow produce a different answer.

The position has acquired a history.

That history matters because humans do not experience a portfolio as a sequence of independent decisions. Each new choice arrives carrying the emotional balance sheet of the choices before it.

Behavioral finance has names for pieces of this. One of the oldest and most durable is the disposition effect: investors tend to realize gains more readily than losses. Terrance Odean's 1998 study examined trading records from ten thousand discount-brokerage accounts from 1987 through 1993. The investors in the sample showed a strong preference for selling winners rather than losers, and the pattern was not explained away by rebalancing, higher trading costs in low-priced stocks, or superior subsequent performance of the losers. The [published abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00072) is almost brutally concise.

The finding does not mean every investor always holds losers too long. It does not mean every losing position should be sold. A stock that falls can become more attractive if the thesis remains intact and the price is the thing that changed.

The useful point is narrower.

The purchase price becomes a reference point even when the market has no obligation to care about it.

If you bought a stock at sixty dollars and it trades at forty-two, sixty can begin to feel like a destination. Getting back to even becomes emotionally different from earning the best available return from forty-two dollars today.

Those are not the same problem.

Imagine that the position vanished overnight and forty-two dollars per share appeared in cash. Would you recreate the position at the same size?

That question is uncomfortable because it removes the history.

It also exposes how much of the current decision belongs to the asset and how much belongs to the investor's relationship with the original price.

A drawdown can distort decisions in the opposite direction too. Some investors do not hold losers forever. They abandon risk entirely.

A person who believed they could tolerate a thirty-percent decline during a questionnaire may discover at twenty-two percent that the number was fictional. The loss is happening alongside a job, a mortgage, a family, a news cycle, and the possibility that this time really is different.

The questionnaire measured an opinion about a hypothetical person.

The drawdown measures the person who actually showed up.

There is evidence that risk preference itself can move with circumstances rather than behaving like a fixed personality trait. In a 2014 double-blind crossover experiment, researchers raised cortisol levels in thirty-six healthy volunteers over eight days to levels they said were comparable to increases previously observed in traders under market uncertainty. Under the sustained treatment, participants became more risk-averse. The study was small, experimental, and not a license to reduce markets to hormones. But its central challenge is useful: the [researchers explicitly tested whether financial risk preferences were stable](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3948282/) and found evidence that they were not.

Earlier work from some of the same researchers had measured traders in London under real working conditions and found cortisol rising with market volatility and the variance of trading results. Again, this does not prove a simple biological story in which a hormone explains crashes. The [study itself treated that as a possibility requiring caution](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2329689/).

What it does challenge is the fantasy of the investor as a fixed decision engine.

The market changes.

The investor changes while observing it.

That creates a nasty feedback loop.

A calm investor takes a position sized for a calm investor.

The position loses money.

The investor becomes less calm.

The position is now being managed by someone with a different appetite for risk than the person who opened it.

This is one reason risk management cannot be postponed until after the trade.

Position size is partly a bet on the future investor.

How large can the loss become before the person managing it starts needing the loss to disappear?

That is not a philosophical question. Needing a position to recover changes the quality of thought around it.

Information gets sorted differently.

A negative development becomes temporary.

A positive development becomes proof.

An analyst downgrade is dismissed as backward-looking.

An analyst upgrade is treated as confirmation.

A competitor's strength is irrational pricing.

A competitor's weakness validates the thesis.

The investor can still produce reasons. The problem is that the reasons are no longer being admitted through the same door.

This is confirmation bias in ordinary language: the research process turns into a legal defense.

The most dangerous version is sophisticated.

A novice may simply refuse to look at contrary evidence. An experienced investor can do something more impressive: find contrary evidence, understand it, and explain why it does not count.

Expertise supplies more tools for self-deception as well as more tools for analysis.

That is why a useful investment process needs decisions that were made before the position became emotionally expensive.

Write down what would make the thesis weaker.

Not what would make the stock fall. Price can fall for many reasons.

What fact about the business, security, market structure, or model would cause the original reasoning to deserve less weight?

The answer should exist before the fact arrives.

This sounds bureaucratic until the first real drawdown.

Then the document becomes a message from a version of yourself who was not yet negotiating with the loss.

The message does not have to be obeyed mechanically. New information can make an old invalidation rule irrelevant. But changing the rule should itself require an argument.

Otherwise every stop becomes movable.

Every deadline becomes extendable.

Every thesis becomes immortal.

There is a similar problem on the upside.

A large gain changes the trader too.

Winning can make risk feel cheaper. A position that doubles creates capital, but it can also create identity. The investor begins to believe they understand the company unusually well. Attention paid to the winner produces more information about it, which produces more confidence, which can justify letting the position become a larger share of the portfolio.

Sometimes that is correct. Selling a great business merely because it appreciated can destroy long-term compounding.

But the decision has to be made again at the new size.

A five-percent position that becomes fifteen percent is not the same portfolio decision it was at five.

The company's fundamentals may be identical.

The portfolio's dependence on being right has changed.

This is where the disposition effect and concentration can collide. An investor may sell a winner too early because the gain feels real and hold a loser too long because the loss feels reversible. Or the investor may do the opposite: keep an enormous winner because the winner has become part of personal mythology, while cutting every new idea quickly because nothing else has earned the same trust.

Behavior does not arrive in one recognizable costume.

The better question is what pressure the portfolio is creating.

Does the investor check one position ten times more often than the others?

Does new research begin with the conclusion already known?

Has the language changed from "the company" to "my company"?

Is the investor calculating what the price needs to do to get back to even?

Has the time horizon expanded only because the short-term thesis failed?

Has a long-term thesis suddenly become a short-term trade because the stock rose faster than expected?

These are not automatic sell signals.

They are evidence that the decision environment has changed.

A drawdown also changes opportunity cost.

Capital tied to a losing position is unavailable elsewhere unless the investor adds new money or uses leverage. The psychological focus on recovery can obscure this. The investor compares the position with the original purchase price when the relevant comparison is the set of opportunities available now.

The question is not, can this get back to sixty?

The question is, from forty-two, is this still the best use of forty-two?

That shift sounds small. It is one of the hardest habits in investing.

The original price contains biography.

The current opportunity set does not care.

Professionals face another layer. Their own capital may not be the only capital at risk.

Clients redeem.

Investment committees ask questions.

Risk officers reduce limits.

A fund with a long-term thesis can have short-term liabilities in the form of impatient capital. A portfolio manager may personally tolerate a drawdown that the business around the portfolio cannot tolerate.

This is why "time horizon" is not a single number.

The asset has a horizon.

The thesis has a horizon.

The financing has a horizon.

The client has a horizon.

The person has a horizon.

The shortest one can end the trade.

Individual investors often imagine that freedom from clients solves this problem. In one sense it does. Nobody redeems from your personal account except you.

But you are also the client.

You can fire the portfolio manager at two in the morning by changing the strategy.

This is where the distinction between strategy risk and behavior risk becomes useful.

A strategy can fail because its underlying edge disappears.

It can also fail because the investor stops following it after losses.

From the account statement, both failures may look identical.

Suppose a diversified strategy has a historically normal drawdown of twenty-five percent. The investor allocates half of retirement savings to it after reading about the long-term return. At minus eighteen percent, the loss feels intolerable and the strategy is abandoned. Six months later it recovers.

It is tempting to say the investor lacked discipline.

That may be true.

It may also be true that the allocation was wrong from the beginning.

A strategy whose normal bad period exceeds the investor's real capacity for loss is not a suitable strategy for that investor at that size, no matter how attractive the long-run statistics look.

The behavioral failure was embedded in the portfolio before the drawdown began.

This is a harsher standard than "know your risk tolerance."

It asks the investor to design around a future self who may be frightened, sleep-deprived, embarrassed, overconfident, newly unemployed, newly wealthy, or simply tired of being wrong.

You do not need to predict which version will appear.

You need enough margin that none of them can destroy the process in one decision.

That margin can come from smaller positions.

It can come from diversification.

It can come from holding assets whose liquidity matches the need for cash.

It can come from separating long-term capital from money with near-term obligations.

It can come from written review rules, external accountability, or a deliberate delay between emotional impulse and portfolio action.

The exact mechanism matters less than the design principle.

A portfolio should not require the investor to become a better person precisely when the investor is under the most pressure.

Markets already ask enough.

The goal is to reduce the number of decisions that must be made heroically.

This is also why the phrase "diamond hands" is such a dangerous compliment. Refusing to sell can be courage. It can also be a refusal to process new information. Selling can be panic. It can also be disciplined updating.

The action does not reveal the quality of the decision.

The reasoning does.

A robust process tries to preserve access to that reasoning while the account is moving.

That is what the prewritten thesis is for.

That is what sizing is for.

That is what liquidity is for.

That is what a review cadence is for.

They are not signs that the investor lacks conviction.

They are ways of remembering that conviction is a temporary mental state attached to an uncertain claim.

The market can change the claim.

A drawdown can change the mind evaluating it.

The next problem is worse.

Sometimes the investor is right.

The thesis works.

The event arrives almost exactly as expected.

And the trade still fails because it took too long.
