# Drawdown Changes the Trader

A losing position does not sit quietly in an account. It begins to rewrite the story around itself.

At first the investor may still see the original thesis clearly: revenue growth, a balance-sheet repair, a valuation gap, a catalyst, a statistical signal. Then the price falls. The first decline can feel like opportunity; the second needs an explanation; by the third, the investor may be thinking about something that never appeared in the initial research note—whether selling would amount to admitting a mistake.

Now the decision contains history. The investor is no longer asking only whether the asset is attractive. The purchase price has become a reference point, the loss has acquired emotional weight, and getting back to even can start to feel like an objective in its own right.

Behavioral finance has a name for one common version of this: the disposition effect. Terrance Odean's 1998 study examined trading records from ten thousand discount-brokerage accounts from 1987 through 1993. The investors in the sample realized gains more readily than losses, a pattern that the paper did not explain away through rebalancing, higher trading costs in low-priced stocks, or superior subsequent performance of the losers. The [published abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00072) is admirably unromantic about the result.

The finding does not mean every losing position should be sold. A stock that falls can become more attractive if the business case survives and the price is the thing that changed. What matters here is the way the original purchase price can begin to organize the decision even though the market has no obligation to remember it.

Suppose you bought at sixty dollars and the stock now trades at forty-two. One useful test is to imagine the position vanishing overnight and the forty-two dollars per share returning as cash. Would you recreate the position at the same size? The question is uncomfortable because it strips away biography and forces the investor to compare the asset with the opportunity set that exists now.

Drawdowns can produce the opposite reaction as well. Some investors do not hold losers forever; they abandon risk entirely. A person who believed they could tolerate a thirty-percent decline while filling out a questionnaire may discover at twenty-two percent that the number described a hypothetical person who no longer exists.

The loss is happening alongside a job, a mortgage, a family, a news cycle, and the possibility that this crisis might be different from the last one. The investor who answered the questionnaire in calm conditions is not necessarily the investor making the decision after months of uncertainty.

There is experimental evidence that risk preference can move with circumstances rather than behaving like a fixed personality trait. In a 2014 double-blind crossover study, researchers raised cortisol levels in thirty-six healthy volunteers over eight days to levels they said were comparable to increases previously observed in traders under market uncertainty. Under the sustained treatment, participants became more risk-averse. The sample was small and the setting experimental, but the [researchers' direct question—whether financial risk preference is stable—is useful](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3948282/).

Earlier observational work from some of the same researchers had measured male traders in London and found cortisol varying with market volatility and the variance of trading results. The [study did not establish a simple hormone-causes-crashes story](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2329689/), nor should this book pretend it did. Its relevance is narrower: the body experiencing uncertainty is not separate from the mind taking risk.

That makes position size partly a bet on a future version of the investor. A calm person opens the trade. The position loses money. The person managing it is now sleeping differently, watching the account more often, or feeling a greater need for the loss to disappear. The asset may not have changed nearly as much as the decision environment has.

Once a position becomes threatening, information starts arriving through unequal doors. A negative development is temporary; a positive development is confirmation. An analyst downgrade is dismissed as backward-looking while an upgrade is treated as validation. A competitor's strength proves irrational pricing; a competitor's weakness proves the thesis.

A novice may simply avoid contrary evidence. Expertise permits a more sophisticated defense: find the contrary evidence, understand it, and produce a technically impressive explanation for why it does not count. The same knowledge that improves analysis can improve self-deception.

This is why useful investment processes preserve some decisions from before the position became emotionally expensive. The investor can write down what would make the thesis weaker, what would change the expected time horizon, and what evidence would justify adding or reducing size. Those conditions should concern the business, security, market structure, or model—not merely the fact that the price went down.

A prewritten rule is not sacred. New information can make an old invalidation condition irrelevant. But changing the rule after the loss has arrived should itself require an argument. Otherwise every stop becomes movable, every deadline becomes extendable, and every failed short-term thesis can be granted asylum in the long term.

The same problem appears on the upside. Winning changes the trader too.

A large gain creates capital, but it can also create identity. The investor follows the company more closely, accumulates more information about it, and may become increasingly convinced that the depth of knowledge justifies allowing the position to become a larger share of the portfolio. Sometimes that is exactly right. Selling an exceptional business merely because it appreciated can be a costly mistake.

But a five-percent position that grows into fifteen percent is no longer the same portfolio decision, even if the company's fundamentals are unchanged. The portfolio's dependence on being right has increased. The decision deserves to be made again at the new size rather than inherited from the old one.

Behavior therefore does not arrive in one recognizable costume. One investor sells winners too quickly and nurses losers. Another lets one winner become a personal mythology and cuts every new idea before it has time to work. A third alternates between the two depending on what happened most recently.

The better diagnostic is pressure. Does one position get checked ten times more often than the others? Has the research language changed from "the company" to "my company"? Is the investor calculating what price is needed to get back to even? Has the time horizon expanded only after the original catalyst failed? Has a long-term thesis become a short-term trade because the stock rose faster than expected?

None of these is an automatic sell signal. They are evidence that the decision process has acquired another variable: the investor's relationship with the position.

Drawdowns also distort opportunity cost. Capital tied up in a losing position is unavailable elsewhere unless new money is added or leverage is used. The emotional focus on recovery makes it easy to compare the current price with the purchase price when the more useful comparison is with the alternatives available today.

The question is not simply whether forty-two can get back to sixty. It is whether forty-two dollars of capital is still best employed there.

Professional investors face an additional layer because their own tolerance is not the only one that matters. Clients redeem. Investment committees ask questions. Risk officers reduce limits. A fund can hold a five-year thesis with capital that begins leaving after five bad months.

This is why "time horizon" is not one number. The asset has a horizon, the thesis has a horizon, the financing has a horizon, the client has a horizon, and the person has a horizon. The shortest one can end the trade.

Individual investors often believe freedom from clients solves the problem. In one sense it does; nobody can redeem from a personal account except the owner. But the owner is also the client and can fire the portfolio manager at two in the morning by changing the strategy.

That distinction helps explain why a good strategy can produce a bad realized result. A strategy may fail because the edge disappears. It may also fail because the investor abandons it during a historically ordinary drawdown. From the account statement, those failures can look identical.

Imagine a diversified strategy whose bad periods have historically included declines around twenty-five percent. An investor allocates half of retirement savings to it after being attracted to the long-run return. At minus eighteen percent the loss becomes intolerable and the strategy is abandoned; months later it recovers. Calling that a failure of discipline may be fair, but it misses an earlier error: the strategy was sized for a person who did not exist under stress.

A process should not require the investor to become a better human being precisely when the investor is under the most pressure. The portfolio can be designed to reduce the number of heroic decisions required: smaller positions, better diversification, liquidity matched to actual cash needs, long-term capital separated from near-term obligations, written review rules, or an intentional delay between impulse and action.

The exact mechanism matters less than the design principle. Leave enough margin for the future investor to be frightened, embarrassed, overconfident, tired, or simply different from the person who opened the trade.

This is also why slogans about "diamond hands" are useless as analysis. Refusing to sell can be courage or denial. Selling can be panic or disciplined updating. The action alone does not reveal the quality of the decision; the reasoning and the evidence do.

A robust process tries to preserve access to that reasoning while the account is moving. That is what the prewritten thesis, position size, liquidity plan, and review cadence are for. They are not substitutes for judgment. They are ways of remembering that conviction is a temporary mental state attached to an uncertain claim.

The market can change the claim, and a drawdown can change the mind evaluating it. The next problem is harder because sometimes the investor is eventually right. The thesis works, the event arrives almost as expected, and the trade still fails because the waiting period was longer and more expensive than the position could survive.
