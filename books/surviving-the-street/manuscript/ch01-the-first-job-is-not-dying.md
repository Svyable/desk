# The First Job Is Not Dying

A portfolio can recover from almost anything except zero.

That sounds childish until the losses become large enough to make the arithmetic visible. A ten-percent decline leaves ninety cents of every dollar. Recovering to a dollar requires an 11.1 percent gain. After a twenty-percent loss, the required gain is twenty-five percent. A one-third loss needs roughly fifty percent. A fifty-percent loss needs one hundred percent. A seventy-five-percent loss needs three hundred percent.

At ninety percent down, the portfolio needs to rise nine hundred percent to get back to even.

The curve is not symmetrical because money is not scored in percentage points. It compounds on what remains.

This should be the first page of every conversation about risk. Instead, risk is often introduced as a feeling. How much volatility can you tolerate? How nervous would you be if your account fell twenty percent? Choose a number between one and ten.

The questions are not useless. They are simply too polite.

A portfolio is not harmed because a red number causes distress. It is harmed because losses alter the amount of capital available to participate in the next opportunity. They can also alter the investor's behavior, borrowing capacity, time horizon, and willingness to follow the strategy that produced them. A drawdown is therefore not a mood. It is a change in the geometry of the future.

Consider a simple sequence. An investment rises fifty percent and then falls fifty percent. The two percentage moves look like opposites. They are not. One hundred dollars becomes one hundred fifty, then seventy-five. The investor is down twenty-five percent.

Reverse the order. One hundred falls to fifty and then rises fifty percent. The result is seventy-five again.

The path changed. The destination did not.

Now add leverage and the path begins to matter more. Suppose an investor uses borrowed money to increase exposure to an asset. A move that would be survivable without leverage can become existential with it. The exact outcome depends on financing terms, maintenance requirements, portfolio composition, broker rules, and what happens to collateral along the way. The important point is simpler: leverage does not merely magnify the final return. It changes the set of paths the investor is allowed to survive.

That distinction is easy to miss in a spreadsheet.

A backtest can show that a strategy recovered from a sixty-percent drawdown over the next four years. The line eventually returns to the upper right. The result may even be mathematically accurate. But the line has no rent, no margin agreement, no investor redemptions, no nervous spouse, no investment committee, no client who calls after the third bad month, and no competing need for the capital.

The line never has to sleep.

A human does.

This is why "I would have held" is one of the cheapest sentences in finance. It costs nothing when spoken after the recovery is visible. The useful question is whether the investor could have held when the recovery did not yet exist.

Could the position be financed? Would the broker allow it? Would the investor still have income? Would the capital be needed elsewhere? Would the thesis still be testable, or would each new loss be explained away as another reason the market was irrational? Would the investor have enough psychological room to distinguish a deteriorating business from a deteriorating price?

Survival lives inside those questions.

The word can sound defensive, as if the goal were merely to avoid loss. That is not what I mean. A person who refuses every risk will survive the stock market by not participating in it. Cash can avoid market volatility while losing purchasing power. A portfolio can be so diversified that every interesting view disappears. An investor can protect against every imaginable disaster and spend the entire return on insurance.

Risk cannot be removed from investing because return is partly payment for bearing uncertainty that somebody else does not want.

The job is to choose which uncertainty can be carried.

That begins with position size.

Investors love entry prices because entry prices create the feeling of precision. A stock is attractive at forty-seven but not fifty-two. A moving average crossed. A valuation multiple reached the bottom of a historical range. A catalyst is three weeks away. These may all matter, but the decision that determines whether the idea can injure the portfolio is how much capital is attached to it.

A mediocre idea at two percent can be educational.

A brilliant idea at sixty percent can become a biography.

The market is especially dangerous when concentration feels justified by intelligence. Research increases conviction. Conviction invites size. Size increases emotional involvement. Emotional involvement makes disconfirming evidence harder to process. What began as an analytical advantage can quietly convert itself into a behavioral disadvantage.

There is no universal correct position size because the relevant variables are not universal. A liquid large-cap stock is different from a thinly traded small company. An unlevered position is different from a derivative with convex exposure. A trade with a known event date is different from a five-year thesis. A portfolio of highly correlated positions is not diversified because the ticker symbols differ.

The point is not to replace judgment with a sizing formula. It is to understand that sizing is where judgment becomes survivability.

A position should be small enough that being wrong remains informative.

That sentence is more demanding than it first appears. If a loss is large enough to threaten the investor's financial security, the mind stops treating the trade as an experiment. It becomes a threat. Threats change attention. People seek reassurance, shorten time horizons, check prices more often, and become unusually creative at producing explanations.

A portfolio can therefore become less intelligent as the stakes rise.

This is one reason professional risk systems often look boring from the outside. Limits, collateral requirements, exposure caps, concentration rules, liquidity buckets, scenario tests, and stress assumptions do not predict the future. They constrain the amount of damage the future can do before humans lose the ability to make choices.

The individual investor usually has fewer formal constraints, which can feel like freedom.

It can also mean there is nobody in the room authorized to say enough.

Margin makes that problem sharper because the lender gets a vote. In an ordinary cash position, an investor may be able to wait through a severe decline if the underlying asset survives. With borrowed money, the investor's time horizon is partly owned by someone else. Regulatory requirements, house rules, collateral values, and the broker's risk management can force action at exactly the moment the investor most wants patience.

This is why a leveraged strategy cannot be evaluated only by its expected return.

You have to ask what happens on the worst plausible path before the expectation arrives.

The same logic applies without explicit borrowing. Options can embed leverage. Concentrated portfolios can create economic leverage to one theme. A business owner whose wealth, income, and retirement account all depend on the same industry may be more exposed than a brokerage statement suggests. An investor who needs to sell assets to fund a tuition payment in nine months has less capacity to wait than a person investing capital that will not be touched for twenty years.

Risk is partly a property of the asset.

Capacity for risk is a property of the investor's life.

Confusing the two is expensive.

There is another reason survival deserves to come first: the market distributes opportunity unevenly through time. Some periods offer many attractive setups. Others offer almost none. If an investor feels compelled to be fully invested, fully active, or fully certain at all times, boredom becomes a source of risk.

Waiting can be a position.

That does not mean sitting in cash whenever the world feels frightening. Fear is often highest after prices have already fallen. The point is that capital has option value. A dollar not committed to a mediocre idea remains available when a better idea appears, when a dislocation creates forced sellers, or when new information changes the odds.

The investor who survives keeps choices.

This is one reason drawdowns hurt twice. The obvious damage is the loss itself. The less visible damage is that a depleted portfolio has less capacity to exploit the environment that caused the loss.

Imagine two investors entering a crisis with the same research ability. One loses ten percent. The other loses sixty percent. Even if both identify the same extraordinary opportunity at the bottom, they do not possess the same economic ability to act. The second investor may also possess less psychological ability. Every new position is now filtered through the experience of recent pain.

The crisis has changed the investors, not just the prices.

This is where traditional discussions of "risk tolerance" often fail. Tolerance sounds stable, like height. In practice it is state-dependent. People tolerate volatility differently after a promotion than after a layoff, differently at age thirty than sixty, differently after three winning years than after three losing months. The same portfolio can feel conservative when household finances are strong and reckless when an unexpected expense arrives.

A resilient process has to assume the person running it will change.

That does not make discipline impossible. It changes what discipline means.

Discipline is often imagined as force: hold the line, trust the process, ignore the noise. Sometimes that is correct. Sometimes "discipline" is the story an investor tells while refusing to update.

A better discipline is procedural. Decide before the pressure which facts would weaken the thesis. Decide what size allows those facts to be considered without panic. Decide how often the position will be reviewed. Decide what would trigger a reduction because the risk changed, even if the investment story did not. Record the reasoning while the mind still has access to alternatives.

Then, when the market moves, compare the new world with the old decision.

This is less heroic than conviction.

It is also more reusable.

The same principle applies to strategies rather than individual positions. A strategy that produces a strong long-run return with intolerable drawdowns may be unusable even if the backtest is correct. The relevant return is not the return of the strategy in a vacuum. It is the return of the strategy after a real investor sizes it small enough to stick with it, pays its costs, survives its bad periods, and avoids changing the rules at the worst moment.

There is an important trap here. Once survival becomes the theme, it is tempting to worship low volatility. That would be another simplification.

Some risks are visible precisely because they fluctuate every day. Other risks remain hidden until they arrive all at once. A strategy that sells insurance may produce smooth returns for years and then reveal that the calm was compensation for carrying a rare loss. A company can report stable earnings while leverage quietly accumulates. An asset can look safe because it has not recently moved.

Calm is not the same as safety.

Volatility is not the same as danger.

The investor's job is to understand what kind of loss is being carried, who can force action, and how the position behaves when assumptions fail.

That requires analytics, which we will get to. It requires information, because risk often changes before a price chart explains why. It requires behavior, because a loss that looks manageable in a spreadsheet can become intolerable in the body.

But all three begin with the same question.

What would have to happen for this decision to remove my ability to keep making decisions?

If the answer is one ordinary market move, the position is too fragile.

If the answer depends on borrowing remaining available, the financing deserves as much research as the asset.

If the answer is "I would never sell," the behavioral assumption deserves suspicion.

If the answer is unknown, that is information too.

Surviving The Street is not about becoming timid. Timid investors can make terrible decisions just as easily as aggressive ones. It is about preserving the capacity to think.

Capital does that.

Liquidity does that.

A position small enough to reconsider does that.

A process written before the loss does that.

The first edge is not prediction.

It is staying alive long enough to learn whether the prediction was any good.
