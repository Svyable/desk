# The Cost of Being Early

In 2005, Michael Burry had a timing problem disguised as a housing thesis.

He had spent months reading mortgage documents, and the more he looked at the loans being packed into securities, the less interesting the broad question "is American housing expensive?" became. He was looking for something more mechanical: when would weak loans be forced to reveal themselves? His answer involved adjustable-rate mortgages, teaser periods, refinancing, and the moment borrowers could no longer escape a bad loan by selling into a rising market.

That is a better investment question than asking whether there is a bubble because bubbles do not carry clocks. Positions do.

Burry later described the work in a 2011 talk at Vanderbilt. By May 2005, he said, Scion had agreed to its first trades shorting subprime mortgages with Deutsche Bank; the first transactions were executed in early June. He worked on making the credit-default-swap language standardized enough to trade across counterparties and ultimately used multiple dealers. His [first-person account is unusually detailed](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/2011/04/13/michael-burry-transcript/), and the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission separately preserved an [interview with him in its public archive](https://fcic.law.stanford.edu/interviews/view/14).

The trade is now so famous that hindsight turns 2005 into the opening scene of a victory. The mortgages deteriorate, the crisis arrives, and Burry becomes one of the people most associated with seeing the structure early. Living through 2005 and 2006 did not come with that editing.

A credit default swap makes timing concrete because being early has an invoice. The protection buyer generally makes periodic payments to the protection seller in exchange for a contingent payment if a defined credit event occurs. The [CFTC calls those periodic payments the fee leg](https://www.cftc.gov/MarketReports/SwapsReports/DataDictionary/index.htm). If the event does not occur, the buyer can spend years paying for protection that expires unused.

Every investment has some version of this carrying cost, although it may not appear on a statement. A short seller can pay borrow fees. An options buyer gives up time value. A futures strategy rolls contracts. A bond investor forgoes the yield available elsewhere. A value investor can sit in a cheap company while a better business compounds across the street. A hedge fund can lose clients before its thesis matures, and an employee can spend career capital defending a position the committee hates.

Being early is therefore not simply waiting. It is financing the wait.

Suppose two investors reach the same conclusion: an asset is badly mispriced and will eventually fall by half. One chooses a structure that costs five percent a year to maintain, faces uncertain margin demands, and can be closed by a broker if collateral deteriorates. The other accepts a smaller theoretical payoff but builds enough time and liquidity into the position to survive several false starts. They do not own the same thesis in any practical sense.

Financing belongs inside the thesis because markets can force a correct investor out without changing the eventual destination. A margin call, redemption, premium payment, rising borrow rate, collateral haircut, covenant, or client can all turn time into a creditor. The familiar market saying about irrationality lasting longer than solvency survives because it points at this mechanism, but the slogan is less useful than the invoice.

Burry's own account makes the middle of the story vivid. He said his attempt to raise a dedicated vehicle for the mortgage short in 2005 met intense skepticism. By 2006, even as he believed the underlying mortgage evidence was worsening, marks on the credit-default-swap book became a source of conflict with dealer counterparties. He described angry investors asking for their money back, layoffs, salary cuts, threats of litigation, and the possibility of liquidating the fund at what he regarded as the worst possible time. Under pressure, he sold other credit-short positions while retaining mortgage positions he could continue to carry. Those details come from Burry's retrospective [Vanderbilt account](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/2011/04/13/michael-burry-transcript/) and should be read as his account, not as a license to romanticize every investor who refuses to change a view.

That distinction is essential. For every famous investor who was early and vindicated, there are investors who were early because their timing model was bad, investors who called a structural break that never arrived, and investors who kept extending the date of a failed thesis until the record could no longer be audited. The fact that one painful position eventually worked does not turn pain into evidence.

A useful thesis needs some kind of clock, even when the clock is wide. Burry's housing view was linked to observable mortgage structures and reset schedules. If loans had kept performing, borrowers had refinanced easily, home prices had continued absorbing weak underwriting, or the contractual protection had failed to respond to credit deterioration, the thesis would have deserved a different interpretation.

That is very different from saying a stock should eventually get back to where you bought it.

The clock does not need to be a date. It can be a sequence of observable events. A new factory should reach a certain utilization before margins improve. A drug should produce a defined result in a trial. A debt refinancing should remove a maturity wall. A pricing action should begin appearing in renewal revenue. A factor should continue to predict returns outside the sample that inspired it.

The investor does not need the market to agree immediately, but reality has to begin doing the things the thesis requires. Otherwise "long term" becomes a place failed ideas go to avoid judgment.

This matters especially in contrarian investing because contrarianism gives the investor a ready-made excuse for disagreement. If the market disagrees, that proves the view is nonconsensus. If the position loses money, the opportunity appears larger. If peers think the thesis is absurd, their skepticism can be treated as further confirmation. A view constructed this way can become impossible to falsify.

The antidote is to separate price from evidence. Price moving against you is evidence, but not always decisive evidence. The business missing the operating milestone that justified the thesis is different. The expected catalyst arriving without the expected economic effect is different. The data-generating process changing is different. The borrow disappearing is different. Each failure belongs to a different layer of the position.

That is why a position should be decomposed before it becomes painful. What has to be true about the asset? What has to be true about timing? What has to be true about financing and market structure? What has to be true about the investor's own ability to hold? The trade can fail at any one of those layers without the others changing.

Short selling makes the structure obvious. The fundamental thesis may be that a company is worth far less than the current price, but the short still has to borrow shares. The lender can recall them, the borrow cost can rise, the stock can rally sharply, and losses are not capped at the original investment because the price can keep rising. A crowded short can even create its own demand when short sellers buy shares to close positions.

A long position bought for cash looks simpler because it cannot generate a margin call by itself, but time still charges rent. An investor can wait five years for a cheap business to rerate while the business earns mediocre returns and the rest of the opportunity set compounds. The stock may eventually rise thirty percent and still have been an inferior allocation.

Absolute profit can conceal relative failure. "I made money" does not answer what else the capital could reasonably have earned, what risk was carried, how much attention the position consumed, or whether the expected return looked attractive before the ending became known. Opportunity cost is the quiet financing charge because it never arrives as a separate bill.

Institutions face a still more visible clock. A portfolio manager can believe a popular sector is dangerously overvalued and avoid it, then underperform for eighteen months while the sector keeps rising. The thesis may be correct on a three-year horizon and professionally intolerable on a one-year horizon. Pension funds answer to boards, mutual funds to shareholders, hedge funds to redemption terms, banks to capital rules and risk committees. The organization has its own survival function.

Individuals often possess an advantage here because they do not have to defend quarterly relative performance to a consultant or client. They can hold cash, buy an unloved security, or simply do nothing. But that advantage disappears if they voluntarily recreate institutional pressure by using margin, investing money needed next year, posting every trade publicly, or checking prices until a long-term position is psychologically managed minute by minute.

A person can own patient capital and manage it impatiently.

The practical question is therefore not whether a thesis is early. It is whether the position has been built to make early survivable. That can mean less leverage, more cash for collateral, smaller size, avoiding instruments with punitive decay, refusing a short whose borrow is unstable, or accepting a lower theoretical payoff in exchange for more time.

There is no universal structure because the right design depends on the edge. There is, however, a universal mistake in pretending structure sits outside analysis. It does not.

Burry's housing trade is remembered for the forecast: weak subprime mortgages, fragile securitization, and a market that priced the risk too cheaply. The trade was also a contractual and organizational problem. Which bonds? Which tranches? Which counterparties? Which swap language? Which marks? Which capital could remain in place while the thesis matured?

The [Vanderbilt transcript](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/2011/04/13/michael-burry-transcript/) is valuable because it preserves that ugly middle—the contract negotiation, investor pressure, dealer disputes, staffing cuts, and positions sold under duress. That is what being early looks like before anyone knows the ending.

A good thesis has an expected return. A usable thesis has an expected return plus a survivable path. When evaluating one, ask what time costs and who is paying for it. The answer may be capital, premium, borrow, liquidity, attention, reputation, or opportunity.

The bill may be small. It may be worth paying. It may even be the reason the edge exists, because other investors cannot or will not carry it. But it belongs in the model.

Part I began with the arithmetic of loss, moved through the gap between intelligence and outcome, and then through the way losses alter the person making decisions. Time completes the picture. A market edge that cannot survive its own waiting period is not an edge the investor can use.

That is where survival stops sounding defensive. The question is no longer merely what should happen. It is what must remain true while you wait for it to happen.
