# The Cost of Being Early

In 2005, Michael Burry had a timing problem disguised as a housing thesis.

He had spent months reading mortgage documents. The more he looked at the loans being packed into securities, the less interested he became in the broad question of whether American housing was expensive. He was looking for something more mechanical: when would weak loans be forced to reveal themselves?

The answer he settled on involved adjustable-rate mortgages, teaser periods, refinancing, and the moment borrowers could no longer escape a bad loan by selling into a rising market.

That is a much better investment question than "is there a bubble?"

A bubble can remain a bubble longer than a trade can remain funded.

Burry later described the work in a 2011 talk at Vanderbilt. By May 2005, he said, Scion had agreed to its first trades shorting subprime mortgages with Deutsche Bank; the first transactions were executed in early June. He would eventually use multiple dealer counterparties. The instrument was a credit default swap, a contract whose value could rise if the referenced mortgage securities suffered losses. His [first-person account is unusually detailed](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/2011/04/13/michael-burry-transcript/), and the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission separately preserved an [interview with Burry in its archive](https://fcic.law.stanford.edu/interviews/view/14).

The trade has become so famous that the hard part can disappear in retrospect.

We know the ending.

The mortgages deteriorated. The crisis arrived. Burry became one of the people most publicly associated with seeing the structure early.

Knowing the ending makes 2005 look like the beginning of a victory.

Living through 2005 and 2006 did not feel like that.

A credit default swap makes timing concrete because being early has a bill attached to it. The protection buyer generally makes periodic payments to the protection seller in exchange for a contingent payment if a defined credit event occurs. The [CFTC describes the periodic payments as the fee leg](https://www.cftc.gov/MarketReports/SwapsReports/DataDictionary/index.htm). If the credit event never comes, the buyer can spend years paying for protection that expires unused.

This is the carry cost of a view.

Every investment has some version of it.

A short seller may pay borrow fees.

An options buyer loses time value.

A futures position rolls from one contract into another.

A bond investor gives up the yield available elsewhere.

A value investor can sit in an apparently cheap company while a stronger business compounds across the street.

A hedge fund can suffer redemptions.

An employee can spend career capital defending a position the committee hates.

An individual investor can spend attention.

Being early is not simply waiting.

It is financing the wait.

That changes how an edge should be evaluated.

Suppose two investors reach the same conclusion. An asset is mispriced and will eventually fall by half.

The first investor is directionally right but has chosen a position that costs five percent a year to maintain, faces uncertain margin requirements, and can be closed by a broker if collateral deteriorates.

The second investor has found a structure with a smaller payoff but enough time and liquidity to survive several false starts.

They do not own the same thesis.

The financing is part of the thesis.

This is the piece that disappears when people repeat the line usually attributed to markets about staying irrational longer than you can stay solvent. The slogan survives because it is true enough to be useful. But slogans hide mechanism.

How, exactly, can a correct investor become insolvent?

The answer is usually not that the final destination was wrong.

It is that something on the path demanded cash.

A margin call.

A redemption.

A premium payment.

A rising borrow rate.

A short squeeze.

A collateral haircut.

A covenant.

A client.

A boss.

A spouse who reasonably wants to know why the money for the house is now a macroeconomic experiment.

Time has creditors.

Burry's own account makes this vivid. He said his attempt to raise a dedicated vehicle for the mortgage short in 2005 met intense skepticism. By 2006, even as he believed the mortgage evidence was worsening, marks on the credit-default-swap book became a source of conflict with dealer counterparties. He described angry investors demanding money back, layoffs, salary cuts, threats of litigation, and the possibility of liquidating the fund at what he regarded as the worst possible moment. He sold other credit-short positions under pressure while retaining the mortgage positions he could. The payoff later arrived.

The point is not to turn Burry into a patron saint of stubbornness.

That would be a dangerous reading.

For every famous investor who was early and vindicated, there are investors who were early because they were wrong about timing, investors who called a permanent structural break that never came, and investors who kept extending the date of a failed thesis until the record became impossible to audit.

The fact that one difficult position eventually worked does not make suffering a signal.

Pain is not evidence of correctness.

The useful question is what distinguished justified patience from denial.

In Burry's case, the thesis was linked to observable mortgage structures and reset schedules. He had a mechanism and a rough clock. The details mattered. If the loans had performed, if borrowers had refinanced easily, if home prices had kept absorbing weak underwriting, or if the contractual protection had failed to respond to mortgage losses, the trade would have deserved a different interpretation.

That is very different from saying, "I still think this stock should be higher."

A thesis needs a clock even when the clock is wide.

Without one, "long term" becomes a place failed ideas go to avoid judgment.

The clock does not have to be a date. It can be a sequence.

Margins should improve after the new plant reaches a certain utilization.

A drug should produce a defined result in a trial.

A debt refinancing should remove a maturity wall.

A pricing action should show up in renewal revenue.

A housing thesis should become visible when teaser-rate loans reset.

A factor should continue to predict returns outside the period that inspired it.

The investor does not need the market to agree immediately.

The investor does need reality to begin doing the things the thesis requires.

That distinction matters most in contrarian investing because contrarianism creates social permission to dismiss disagreement.

If the market disagrees, good. I am contrarian.

If the position loses money, even better. The opportunity is larger.

If peers think the thesis is absurd, that proves how nonconsensus it is.

This logic can make a thesis unfalsifiable.

Being early becomes a universal explanation for being wrong.

The cure is to separate price from evidence.

Price moving against you is evidence, but it is not always decisive evidence.

The business missing the operating milestone that justified the thesis is different.

The macro variable moving in the wrong direction is different.

The expected catalyst arriving without the expected economic effect is different.

The data-generation process changing is different.

The borrow disappearing is different.

Each failure belongs to a different layer of the trade.

That is why a position should be decomposed before it becomes painful.

What has to be true about the asset?

What has to be true about timing?

What has to be true about financing?

What has to be true about market structure?

What has to be true about the investor's ability to hold?

The market can attack any one of them.

Consider short selling.

The fundamental thesis may be that a company is worth much less than the current price. Fine.

The short still has to borrow shares.

The lender can recall them.

The borrow cost can rise.

The stock can rally sharply.

Losses on a short are not capped at the original investment because the price can keep rising.

A crowded short can become a source of demand when short sellers buy shares to close positions.

The company can issue new information, find financing, attract speculative interest, or simply remain overvalued longer than expected.

The fundamental thesis is one line in a much larger contract with time.

Long positions have their own version.

They look safer because a fully paid cash stock cannot generate a margin call by itself.

But capital still has opportunity cost.

An investor can spend five years waiting for a cheap business to rerate while the business earns mediocre returns and the rest of the market compounds. The stock may eventually rise thirty percent and still have been an inferior allocation.

Absolute profit can conceal relative failure.

This is why "I made money" is not enough to evaluate a long-duration thesis.

What else could the capital reasonably have earned?

What risk was carried?

How much attention did the position consume?

What was the probability-weighted return at the time, not after the ending became known?

Opportunity cost is the quiet financing charge.

It does not arrive as an invoice.

It arrives as a portfolio that grew less than it could have.

Career risk works the same way in institutional investing.

Imagine a manager who believes a popular sector is dangerously overvalued. The manager can avoid it.

If the sector keeps rising for another eighteen months, the manager may underperform the benchmark badly enough to lose clients or lose the job.

The thesis can be correct on a three-year horizon and unemployable on a one-year horizon.

This is not evidence that institutions are stupid.

Their capital has liabilities too.

Pension funds answer to boards.

Mutual funds answer to investors.

Hedge funds answer to redemption terms.

Banks answer to capital rules and risk committees.

Portfolio managers answer to employers.

The organization has a survival function that may be shorter than the investment thesis.

Individuals often possess an advantage here.

They can choose not to report quarterly performance to anyone.

They can hold cash without explaining the decision in a client letter.

They can buy an unloved security without worrying that a consultant will remove them from an approved list.

They can wait.

This advantage is real only if they do not voluntarily recreate institutional pressure.

Checking prices every five minutes can manufacture a short horizon.

Using margin can manufacture a lender.

Investing money needed next year can manufacture a redemption schedule.

Posting every position publicly can manufacture an audience.

A person can own patient capital and manage it impatiently.

The practical question, then, is not simply whether a thesis is early.

It is whether the position has been built to make early survivable.

That may mean using less leverage.

It may mean accepting a smaller expected payoff in exchange for more time.

It may mean avoiding instruments with decay.

It may mean keeping cash available for collateral.

It may mean refusing to short an asset whose borrow can become impossible.

It may mean making the position small enough that the rest of life does not become hostage to the clock.

There is no universal structure because the right structure depends on the edge.

But there is a universal mistake: pretending structure is separate from analysis.

It is analysis.

Burry's trade is remembered for the forecast. Housing was fragile. Subprime mortgages were deteriorating. The securities built on them were mispriced.

The trade was also a contractual engineering problem.

Which bonds?

Which tranches?

Which counterparties?

Which swap language?

Which maturity?

Which marks?

Which capital could remain in place while the thesis matured?

The [Vanderbilt transcript](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/2011/04/13/michael-burry-transcript/) is valuable because it preserves those ugly middle details. It contains skepticism, contract negotiation, investor pressure, dealer disputes, staffing cuts, and sales made under duress.

That is what being early looks like before it becomes a movie.

The same principle applies to far less dramatic investments.

A good thesis has an expected return.

A usable thesis has an expected return plus a survivable path.

When evaluating a position, ask what time costs.

If the answer is nothing, look again.

Something is paying for the wait.

Capital.

Premium.

Borrow.

Liquidity.

Attention.

Reputation.

Opportunity.

The bill may be small. It may be worth paying. It may even be the reason the edge exists, because many other investors cannot or will not carry it.

But the bill belongs in the model.

Part I began with the arithmetic of loss, moved through the difference between intelligence and outcome, then into the way losses change the investor.

The final step is time.

A market edge that cannot survive its own waiting period is not an edge the investor can use.

That is where survival stops being defensive and becomes analytical.

You are no longer asking only what should happen.

You are asking what must remain true while you wait for it to happen.
