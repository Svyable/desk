# February Had Thirty Days

For one year in Sweden, February had thirty days. The date was February 30, 1712. There is a surviving image of a Swedish almanac bearing that date, reproduced in Claus Tøndering's [historical calendar guide](https://tondering.dk/article/en/calendar/60-30feb.php). It is a useful object because it ends the familiar argument about whether February 30 is merely an error in a computer program. The date really was printed and used. Sweden was not trying to invent a longer month. It was repairing an earlier attempt to fix the calendar.

It is difficult to manufacture a fictional bureaucratic blunder more appealing than that. A government sets out to modernize its system of dates, gets partway through, becomes out of step with both the old and new systems, and eventually solves the problem by giving February an additional day it was not entitled to have under either ordinary calendar. The date existed not because astronomy required it, but because a sequence of administrative choices had accumulated a one-day discrepancy.

The relevant conflict began with the Julian and Gregorian calendars. The Julian system, introduced under Julius Caesar, treated a leap year as occurring every fourth year. That adjustment keeps a calendar much closer to the seasonal year than a system of 365 identical days, but it is not exact. Over centuries, the difference between the Julian calendar and the astronomical cycle accumulated. The Gregorian reform of 1582 adjusted leap-year rules and removed dates to bring the calendar back into closer alignment with the intended seasonal and ecclesiastical relationships.

The Gregorian rule can be written in a sentence. Years divisible by four are normally leap years; years divisible by one hundred are exceptions unless also divisible by four hundred. That means 1600 was a leap year, 1700 was not, 1800 was not, and 2000 was. The Julian calendar would have retained every fourth-year leap day. The difference became larger with time because the systems inserted different numbers of extra days over long intervals.

Religious and political divisions complicated adoption. The Gregorian reform originated in Catholic Europe, and Protestant countries did not all accept it immediately. The disagreement was not about whether the Sun had changed course. It concerned the authority of institutions, the computation of religious festivals, and the practical difficulty of coordinating records with places using different calendars. An astronomically motivated reform could be delayed for reasons having little to do with celestial mechanics.

Sweden chose an unusual compromise. Rather than jump immediately from the Julian to the Gregorian system, it planned a gradual adjustment, described in the [historical calendar analysis by Tøndering](https://tondering.dk/claus/cal/gregorian.php). Sweden would omit leap days over a series of years so its calendar moved closer to the Gregorian one in increments. The plan began in 1700, which would have been a leap year in the Julian calendar but not in the Gregorian calendar. Sweden omitted February 29 that year.

At that moment Sweden had created a third system. Its calendar was not simply Julian or Gregorian. By omitting the leap day in 1700, it moved one day ahead of the Julian count while remaining behind the Gregorian count. That may sound like a manageable difference, and it was, provided the country continued the planned changes. A gradual transition requires discipline. Every subsequent scheduled omission must be performed according to the agreed timetable.

The problem was that later leap days were not omitted as planned. In 1704 and 1708, Sweden kept the leap days the gradual plan required it to drop. The country therefore remained one day out of step with the Julian calendar and ten days behind the Gregorian calendar, according to [specialist historical calculations](https://www.hhogman.se/change-of-calendars-sweden.htm). The government had created an awkward hybrid and then stopped moving it toward the intended destination.

The episode demonstrates why a transition system can be more fragile than the system it replaces. Two parties using the Julian and Gregorian calendars could at least apply a known conversion. Sweden now had an intermediate count whose difference depended on the year and the moment of the interrupted reform. A traveler, merchant or historian comparing dates across regions could not assume that a Swedish date was identical to the Julian date used elsewhere. One had to know the local rule in that particular period.

To repair the mismatch, Swedish authorities eventually abandoned the gradual transition and decided to return to the Julian calendar. Since the Swedish count was one day ahead, it needed one extra nominal day to fall back into alignment. The solution arrived in 1712, a year that would ordinarily have included February 29 under the Julian system. The authorities added an additional leap day: February 30. The day after February 29 was not March 1. It was a second extra day.

One might say that the country was performing a subtraction by addition. To move its calendar back by one day relative to an uninterrupted sequence of physical days, it inserted a duplicate nominal interval before returning to the normal calendar count. The mechanical details of that statement matter. Adding a calendar date did not lengthen the Earth's year. It changed how the sequence of days was numbered so the Swedish dates again matched those in the Julian system.

This was not a spontaneous act by every Swedish town agreeing to call a morning February 30. It depended on central authority and on the distribution of the new calendar rule. Clergy, officials and people maintaining records had to know which date to write. A church register, tax record or letter could bear the unusual entry, and later readers need the historical rule to interpret it. The almanac is not a clever fake. It is evidence that the extra date belonged to an actual civil-calendar system.

A modern programmer could recognize the problem immediately. A date-validation routine that assumes February has twenty-eight or twenty-nine days would reject a legitimate Swedish historical date from 1712. Such a program may be perfectly suited to current Gregorian dates yet fail when a historian asks it to represent events from another system. A data model is not the world. It is an agreement about which cases it expects to describe.

The international consequence is easy to underestimate. A person writing a Swedish date in 1712 and a correspondent using the Gregorian calendar might record numerically different dates for the same day. This was already a normal problem across parts of Europe, but Sweden's intermediate system added another conversion. A modern historian working with sources from these regions cannot simply apply today's time-zone rules and assume the dates align. Calendar chronology requires knowledge of the local legal conventions.

The [genealogical calendar guide by Hans Högman](https://www.hhogman.se/change-of-calendars-sweden.htm) gives the interval for the Swedish transitional calendar as March 1, 1700 through February 30, 1712. From March 1, 1712 until February 17, 1753, Sweden again followed the Julian system. The country had spent twelve years experimenting with a third schedule and returned to the one it had meant to leave. The second attempt at adoption would be much less gradual.

In 1753 Sweden finally made the jump to the Gregorian calendar. This time it did not try to distribute the correction over decades. February 17 was followed immediately by March 1. The missing late-February dates were eliminated from the local calendar, while the physical sequence of days continued. The earlier extra day and the later omitted days were different administrative methods for reconciling the labels. The planet itself took no position.

There is an important numerical detail in this history. The difference between calendars is not always the same number of days. The Gregorian rule's treatment of century years causes it to diverge from the Julian system at particular intervals. The size of the correction needed in 1700 was different from one required in 1900 or 2100. A historian who memorizes a present-day thirteen-day difference and applies it to every earlier century will produce errors. The rules must be applied for the time and place being studied.

That difficulty explains why calendar reform is not just a matter of telling the population to write a different year. One has to consider transactions, religious observances, recurring obligations, records and existing agreements. A date may function as evidence in a legal dispute or as the condition for a payment. A system change can affect how past and future dates are compared. A government can remove or add a label, but it must still decide what obligations are preserved and how documents are interpreted.

The Swedish story contains no requirement to invent a riot over the extra day. The surviving evidence is rich enough without assigning feelings to an imaginary crowd. A policy was designed, partially implemented, neglected, reversed and eventually replaced. The record reveals institutional difficulty more clearly than a theatrical story about people insisting on an extra sunrise.

The event also shows that a government can make a formally rational choice that creates an awkward result when follow-through fails. Gradual convergence was not an inherently absurd idea. It was intended to avoid the disruption of an immediate multi-day calendar jump. But a transition whose success depends on rules being applied for decades can fail when later officials do not carry them out. The longer the implementation period, the more opportunities for instructions to be forgotten or changed.

An engineer would call it a migration problem. Sweden was operating one system, intended to arrive at another, and needed a temporary set of rules for the interval. The migration did not complete, so the temporary state became a lasting source of incompatibility. The final repair had to account for the discrepancy the migration itself had created. This language is modern, but the underlying administrative problem is the same one that confronts organizations changing units, databases or software protocols.

A calendar is particularly unforgiving because it is used by everyone. A technical standard for a specialized instrument may affect only a narrow community of experts. The numbering of days appears in contracts, official correspondence, court records, private letters and local custom. Every discrepancy creates a chance for confusion. One date may have a civil meaning in a place even if it is invalid under a widely used modern calendar.

The strange consequence of 1712 is that it supplies a perfectly correct answer to a question modern software often treats as impossible. Was there a February 30? Yes, under the Swedish civil-calendar rules of that year. Was February 30 a normal Gregorian date? No. Is it useful to reject it when processing dates in ordinary twenty-first-century business software? Usually. Is it useful to reject it when transcribing an authentic Swedish register from 1712? Certainly not.

The distinction between valid and invalid is therefore contextual. The rule under which a record was created determines what counts as a valid date for that record. This is not an argument for abandoning standards. It is an argument for knowing their scope. A present-day calendar library has no obligation to support every historical scheme unless that is part of its purpose. A historical archive does.

The surviving Swedish almanac offers a welcome dose of material evidence. A person can see a page that does not fit the expected month. The page does not mean someone invented a different February for dramatic effect. It was part of a practical attempt to get the country's official dates back into an agreed sequence. A historical date became abnormal only from the perspective of a later standardized calendar.

I would rather preserve the awkwardness than polish it away. Sweden tried to move from one calendar to another without losing several dates at once. The procedure was begun and interrupted. To undo the discrepancy, February received an extra date. Forty-one years later a more direct adjustment removed a block of dates and completed the adoption. The record is a history of institutions living with the consequences of their own transition plan.

The next time a form insists that February cannot have thirty days, it is performing a useful service within a particular standard. It is also forgetting something the Swedes once knew by law. The number of dates in a month can be a matter of administrative history. February 30 is what happens when the repair to a calendar requires a repair to the repair.
