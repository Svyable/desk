# The Last Real Kilogram

For more than a century, the kilogram was a particular object stored near Paris. The International Prototype of the Kilogram, a cylinder of platinum and iridium, defined the SI unit of mass from 1889 until May 20, 2019. If another object was to be measured in kilograms at the highest level of precision, its value ultimately depended on comparison with a standard whose exact mass was one kilogram because the international system said it was. The cylinder did not have to agree with some more fundamental definition. It was the definition.

The [International Bureau of Weights and Measures](https://www.bipm.org/en/physical-metrology-department/kilogram-realization) describes the prototype and the 2019 change. The small metal object was not a magical lump with more mass than its appearance suggested. It was approximately the size of a golf ball and kept under controlled conditions. Its importance came from an institutional agreement that made its physical mass the reference for all kilograms. A tiny cylinder could serve as the basis for a global measurement system because the world had arranged an elaborate means of comparing other masses with it.

That is both useful and uncomfortable. A standard defined by one artifact is easy to state. The object weighs exactly one kilogram, by definition. But any physical object can be affected by its environment, handling, contamination, cleaning or damage. A prototype cannot be duplicated with perfect certainty. National laboratories maintain copies and compare them using instruments whose accuracy has limits. The arrangement creates a chain of trust in which one piece of metal occupies an unusually privileged place.

The [NIST history of the kilogram](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kilogram-introduction) describes the international prototype and national standards. The United States had official copies, including K20, that were used in maintaining its mass measurements. A researcher or manufacturer rarely handled these objects. They were part of a traceability chain connecting the most precise reference to calibration laboratories, instruments and ordinary commercial scales. The kilogram on a package in a shop was not weighed directly against the international cylinder; it depended on a system of comparisons.

It is a mistake to tell this story as though the prototype physically lost a fixed, proven amount of weight every year. Comparisons among the original and its copies showed small differences over time, but determining which particular artifact changed and by exactly how much is difficult when no independent definition exists. The definition makes the original exactly one kilogram throughout its period of authority, even if its material condition changes. Other artifacts can drift relative to it. The measurement problem is to establish stability without a perfectly independent reference.

That logical problem is the heart of the 2019 reform. If the reference is an object, the system can tell whether a copy's mass has changed relative to that object. It cannot simply measure the object against itself and discover an absolute change. A definition tied to an invariant physical constant offers a different foundation. It allows qualified laboratories to realize the unit without accepting the current condition of one historical artifact as the ultimate source of truth.

The idea was not introduced casually. Metrologists spent years developing experiments capable of connecting mass measurements to physical constants with the required accuracy. The [NIST account of the 2019 transition](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kilogram/kilogram-present) describes two important approaches: Kibble balances, which compare mechanical power with electrical quantities linked to quantum standards, and measurements using nearly perfect silicon spheres and the number of atoms they contain. Both required formidable technical precision.

A Kibble balance is difficult to explain without sounding as though somebody has built a machine to weigh a concept. In simplified terms, the instrument relates the gravitational force on a mass to electromagnetic forces and electrical measurements. The electrical quantities can be connected to quantum effects whose relationships involve the Planck constant. The experiment allows mass to be realized through a chain of measured physical relationships rather than by comparison with a single special cylinder. The machine is not an ordinary bathroom scale. It is an instrument developed to make the unit independent of an artifact.

The silicon-sphere approach follows a different route. A carefully manufactured sphere made from highly purified silicon-28 can be characterized using precision measurements of its crystal lattice and volume. Researchers use those measurements to relate the sphere's mass to the number of atoms and the physical constants governing the system. The practical work includes surface chemistry, isotopic composition and the accuracy of geometric measurements. The perfect ball is not an alternative object everybody must use forever. It is a route to realizing the kilogram under a new definition.

The two methods are useful precisely because they are independent in important ways. If measurements based on a Kibble balance and silicon-sphere experiments agree within their uncertainties, confidence grows that the chosen value of a constant can support the revised definition. A standard should not depend on one laboratory's preferred apparatus. Several technically distinct approaches can help identify errors and establish reproducibility across institutions.

The [NIST museum history](https://www.nist.gov/nist-museum/defining-international-system-units-si) records the 2018 international vote to redefine the kilogram and the implementation on May 20, 2019. The date was chosen to coincide with World Metrology Day, commemorating the signing of the Meter Convention. The reform changed not only mass: the ampere, kelvin and mole were also redefined through fixed values of constants. The arrangement was a coordinated revision of the foundation of the International System of Units.

At the center of the kilogram definition is the Planck constant, conventionally written as h. In the revised SI, its numerical value is fixed exactly at 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ joule seconds. That number, combined with the definitions of the meter and second, determines the kilogram. The exactness is not evidence that experimental physicists measured the constant without uncertainty in the old system. It is the consequence of defining the unit by assigning the constant a fixed numerical value, after measurements had reached sufficient agreement.

The [BIPM explanation of kilogram realization](https://www.bipm.org/en/physical-metrology-department/kilogram-realization) makes the institutional distinction explicit. The international prototype remains a historical artifact, and comparisons with material standards continue to be important for disseminating the unit. But the physical cylinder is no longer the definition. A laboratory can realize the kilogram using an approved method tied to the constants, and the unit is no longer dependent on the condition of one piece of metal kept in one location.

A person holding a bag of rice on May 19, 2019 did not wake the following day to find it had become heavier or lighter because a conference voted. The transition was designed to preserve continuity at ordinary and high-precision scales. The practical kilogram was not intentionally changed by a detectable amount. Its source of authority changed. This is one reason standards history is hard to see: the most important achievement may be that everyday measurement continues uninterrupted while its deepest definition is rebuilt.

There is an old philosophical question about what makes an object the standard. If a ruler is defined by a particular rod, and the rod is damaged, can anyone say the ruler is no longer correct? One can compare it with copies or earlier measurements and infer a change, but the institutional definition may still privilege the original. The prototype kilogram made this problem literal. The artifact could be stored and handled under strict protocols, yet its materiality remained the limitation the system was trying to escape.

The last real kilogram in the title is deliberately provocative. There was no final day on which real mass measurements ceased. The change was the retirement of the last SI base-unit definition grounded in a unique physical artifact. The new kilogram is no less real. In practical terms it is more independently reproducible, though realizing it at extreme precision requires sophisticated instruments. The story is about a definition moving away from one object rather than about reality disappearing into abstraction.

This is the strongest rebuttal to the claim that standards are just arbitrary bureaucratic inventions. Mass is a physical property of objects and can be measured through reproducible interactions. The definition of the kilogram is a human agreement, but the agreement is constrained by the need for instruments in different places to produce matching results. One cannot simply vote to make the mass of a physical sample different while preserving the same measurement system. The definition is chosen; its usefulness is tested against the world.

The new system also redistributes potential authority. Under an artifact-based definition, the prototype and the institution holding it occupy a uniquely central position. Under a constant-based definition, any laboratory with sufficiently capable equipment and expertise can in principle realize the unit. In practice, only a limited number of institutions possess the resources required at the highest precision. The change creates a more open technical foundation without abolishing the economic and scientific inequalities involved in its implementation.

The question of trust therefore remains. Most people cannot independently confirm the exact Planck constant or operate a Kibble balance. They rely on national measurement institutions, international comparisons and chains of calibration. The authority is not the same as blind faith in a special cylinder, but it is still institutional. Precision requires procedures, accreditation, expertise and public documentation. The physical constant does not distribute itself into every shop scale without human labor.

There is a deeper reason the new definition matters for emerging technologies. As scientific instruments become more precise, the uncertainty introduced by a physical reference can become a limiting factor. Manufacturers working at microscopic scales, researchers calibrating sensitive measurements and laboratories making international comparisons benefit from a unit that can be realized through physical relationships. The change may not appear on a consumer package, but it expands the capability of institutions whose measurements have to agree more closely than before.

A caution is needed about the word exact. In the revised SI the numerical value of h is exact by definition. Measurements made with a particular Kibble balance are not exact; they still have uncertainty. The instruments can be affected by local gravity, alignment, electrical noise, temperature and other factors. Defining a unit through a constant removes one source of uncertainty without removing every source in practical measurement. A standard is a specification; making it real remains an experimental task.

The history also connects with the meter. The meter had moved from the international prototype bar to a light-based definition decades earlier. The kilogram persisted longer as an artifact-based unit because mass was technically harder to tie to constants with sufficient accuracy. The two stories are not redundant. One concerns measuring length through the speed of light; the other concerns realizing mass through quantum electrical measurements and atom counting. Their chronology shows that standards evolve at different speeds depending on what instruments can do.

The old prototype still exists. It can be preserved, studied and displayed under appropriate conditions as part of the history of science. It no longer commands the unit in the way it did before 2019. The object's retirement is not a rejection of its importance. The cylinder made an extraordinary amount of international measurement possible for more than a century. It became obsolete as the ultimate definition because the people using it developed more reproducible methods.

If I were allowed to choose an exhibit for this book, I would place an image of the cylinder beside a photograph of a Kibble balance. One is physically simple and institutionally central. The other is physically elaborate and designed to connect mass to a constant that can be realized independently. The visual contrast illustrates a change in where the certainty is supposed to come from.

The kilogram did not die. One very special kilogram-shaped object lost its legal authority to define the unit. That may be the least dramatic revolution in the history of the world, and one of the more impressive.
