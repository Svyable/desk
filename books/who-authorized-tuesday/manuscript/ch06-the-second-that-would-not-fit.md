# The Second That Would Not Fit

At the end of December 31, 2016, Coordinated Universal Time included a second labeled 23:59:60. The ordinary last second of the minute, 23:59:59, was followed by an additional second before the next day began. The extra interval was inserted to keep the atomic timescale used for civilian coordination close to time based on the Earth's rotation. The [United States Naval Observatory's leap-second record](https://maia.usno.navy.mil/products/leap-second) identifies that event as the most recent positive leap second through its 2026 notices.

Most people have never seen a clock display sixty seconds in a minute. Many digital interfaces do not show the number, even when their underlying time services account for the adjustment. That is part of the problem. The convention was designed to reconcile two ways of measuring time, but its implementation can create discontinuities that ordinary software and physical systems handle differently. A single extra second has become a serious subject for telecommunications, navigation and distributed computing.

The problem begins with the meaning of a second. Historically, people could describe it as a fraction of the day as measured by the apparent motion of the Sun. That method connects time directly with the Earth's rotation. But the rotation is not perfectly uniform. Tidal forces, motions inside the planet, changes in the atmosphere and oceans, and other processes affect the duration of a day by small amounts. Those variations are insignificant for a person arranging lunch and consequential for systems requiring extremely stable intervals.

Atomic clocks provide a different kind of reference. The modern SI second is defined through the frequency associated with a particular transition in the cesium-133 atom, fixed at 9,192,631,770 cycles for the relevant radiation. The [NIST definitions of the SI base units](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/definitions-si-base-units) specify this physical constant. Unlike a clock that follows the slightly irregular rotation of the Earth, an atomic time scale can provide highly uniform intervals based on a reproducible physical process.

Neither measurement is a hoax. The Earth's rotation is a physical process with its own variability. The cesium transition is another physical phenomenon used to define a stable unit. The difficulty appears when one calendar system must remain meaningfully connected to both. Civil life expects noon to occur near the middle of the day. Scientific and technical systems want a second that does not change length according to the planet's weather and interior. Coordinated Universal Time was designed to supply a shared time scale that could serve both needs within an agreed tolerance.

The leap-second system began in its modern form in 1972. The [NIST historical explanation](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/time-realization/leap-seconds) records the first leap second at the end of June that year. Under the established rule, an adjustment could be inserted when the difference between UTC and Earth-rotation time approached a specified limit, conventionally around nine-tenths of a second. The International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service monitors the relationship and issues bulletins announcing whether a leap second will be needed at the next opportunity.

The system is not adding a fixed second every year. The difference evolves as the Earth rotates at varying rates, and the adjustments have occurred irregularly. A leap year in the calendar and a leap second in UTC solve different problems. February 29 helps keep the calendar aligned with the seasonal year under a long-term rule. A leap second adjusts a time scale in relation to measured irregularities in rotation. A system can have a leap year without a leap second, and vice versa.

The [IERS Bulletin C 72 issued July 6, 2026](https://datacenter.iers.org/versionMetadata.php?filename=mt%2Fbulletinc-072.txt) announced that no leap second would be introduced at the end of December 2026. It also confirmed the existing difference between UTC and International Atomic Time under the current rules. The date matters because it illustrates an ongoing maintenance process. The organization does not publish one eternal table of future leap seconds. It checks observations and announces the near-term decision.

There is a particular difficulty with a clock value of 23:59:60. Some programs assume every minute contains exactly sixty seconds labeled from zero to fifty-nine. That assumption is valid for most uses and fails during an inserted leap second. A timestamp may be rejected as invalid, represented in a different way or adjusted by a service that distributes the extra interval over a longer window. Two systems can both claim to follow UTC while presenting the leap event differently to applications. The discrepancy is small enough to look trivial and large enough to upset precisely timed records.

Distributed computing relies on the order of events. A database may need to determine whether one transaction happened before another. A communications network coordinates packets and signals across equipment in different locations. An electric grid uses synchronized measurements to understand the behavior of a physical system. If a clock steps or pauses unexpectedly, a program that assumes strictly increasing timestamps may misorder events or detect an apparent anomaly. The problem is not that one second can physically destroy a network. It is that software often makes assumptions about time that an unusual interval exposes.

The [2015 NIST best-practices guidance](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/time-and-frequency-metrology/best-practices-leap-second-event) discussed procedures for preparing systems for the June 30, 2015 leap second. NIST and other agencies recognized that precise timing services and infrastructure needed consistent handling. An operator should know how equipment represents the event, whether it needs an update and what happens when two devices disagree. The very existence of a public technical guide shows that the rule required active operational management.

An [NIST study of phasor measurement units after the 2015 leap event](https://www.nist.gov/publications/investigation-pmu-response-leap-second-2015) tested eight devices used in electric-power measurement. The investigators examined their timing response during the event. A one-second adjustment may sound negligible relative to industrial machinery, but these devices compare electrical signals using high-precision timestamps. The accuracy of the comparison depends on how precisely their clocks agree. The study is a practical example of measurement infrastructure encountering a convention about the numbering of seconds.

Software engineers have sometimes used a technique called smearing to handle leap seconds. Instead of exposing a discontinuous second, a system gradually adjusts its clock rate over a defined period. That can make an application less likely to encounter an invalid timestamp, but it creates a temporary difference from a strict representation of UTC. The strategy is a compromise between continuity and exact conformity to the published time scale. It works only if users know which interpretation of time their systems share.

The debate over leap seconds became institutional. In November 2022, delegates at the General Conference on Weights and Measures adopted [Resolution 4](https://www.bipm.org/en/cgpm-2022/resolution-4), calling for a plan to increase the maximum permitted difference between Earth-rotation time and UTC by or before 2035. The resolution recognized the risk that leap-second discontinuities posed to important digital infrastructure. It did not mean the Earth would stop having irregular rotation or that leap seconds had already been removed from every system in 2022.

That distinction is easy to lose in headlines. A conference voted to move toward a change in the permitted separation between two kinds of time. The technical work still had to determine an acceptable limit, implementation schedule and methods for disseminating the difference to users who need Earth-rotation information. By 2026, international bodies continued discussing how to make UTC more continuous. A future plan is not a completed transformation.

The scientific argument has two sides. A continuous atomic-based UTC could simplify software and infrastructure by removing unpredictable discrete adjustments. But some fields need accurate information about the Earth's orientation, including astronomy, navigation and systems that depend on the relationship between coordinates and the rotating planet. Increasing the permitted offset would not eliminate those needs; it would require users to obtain the Earth-rotation correction through other information. The benefit of one clock standard may be accompanied by an obligation to publish additional data for specialized purposes.

This is a question about where complexity should live. The present system puts occasional unusual seconds into the widely used time scale so that it remains closely tied to the Earth's rotation. The proposed reform would allow the difference to grow larger, giving most users a continuous scale while leaving specialist users responsible for applying corrections when needed. Neither design removes the physical irregularity. It changes which layers of the infrastructure must handle it.

A person might ask why the definition of a second cannot simply be adjusted slightly to fit the Earth's average rotation. That would sacrifice the very stability that makes atomic time useful. A scientific unit should not fluctuate according to a changing planet if it is intended for precise measurements in laboratories around the world. One can define a time scale that follows rotation more closely, but the interval used as the SI second is anchored to a physical reference chosen for reproducibility. The two functions must be reconciled through conventions rather than by pretending one process is perfectly regular.

The issue has a deep relationship with the earlier calendar chapters. Samoa altered the date assigned to a sequence of ordinary physical days. Britain and Sweden changed the calendar rules used to count dates. Leap seconds concern a much smaller unit, but the same distinction applies between the physical system being observed and the representation used to coordinate activity. The difference is that a second is now implemented through devices operating at extraordinary precision. A convention invisible to most citizens can become technically consequential when billions of machines depend on its exact meaning.

There is also a distinction between a clock reading and the duration it measures. During a positive leap second, the label 23:59:60 identifies a real additional interval in the UTC day under the chosen system. It does not create an extra moment of physical existence. Time elapsed continuously throughout the event. The unusual number is a method of keeping the calendar clock related to an observed Earth-rotation scale. It is another way people manage discrepancies between convenient numbering and imperfectly aligned physical cycles.

The [NIST leap-second FAQ](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/leap-seconds-faqs) explains why adjustments can be positive or negative in principle, depending on the difference between the time scales. Through 2026 all inserted leap seconds had been positive. The possibility of a negative leap second, which would skip one nominal second, created particular concerns because systems might be even less prepared for a second label disappearing than for one being repeated or added. A design can be robust against a historical pattern and still vulnerable to a permitted but never previously experienced event.

There is an obvious temptation to dramatize the idea that one second could bring down civilization. The evidence supports a narrower and more useful concern: discontinuities can create faults in systems that assume continuous, monotonically increasing time. Researchers and operators have documented specific technical responses and issued preparation guidance. The risk is real enough for international bodies to consider changing the standard. It does not follow that every leap second has caused a universal disaster or that all computers fail when one arrives.

The same pressure appears in the history of other standards. A rule may have been a sensible answer to the conditions of one era. More precise instruments and more interconnected systems change the costs of maintaining it. The original leap-second design prioritized a close relationship between the UTC clock and Earth rotation. Modern computing places a higher price on discontinuities. An institution must decide whether to preserve the earlier compromise or adopt another one.

When the last positive leap second of 2016 arrived, it occupied one second in a world increasingly dependent on timestamps. The adjustment had been announced, measured and implemented, with different systems handling it in different ways. Ten years later, the world had not needed another. The question of its eventual replacement was still under international negotiation.

The second was never missing from physics. It was the numbering system that had to accommodate an irregular planet and increasingly intolerant machines. Time itself did not malfunction at 23:59:60. Some assumptions about how to represent it did.
