# The Friday They Deleted

On Thursday, December 29, 2011, Samoa was approaching the end of a perfectly ordinary Thursday. People were at work, in shops, at home, attending to the sort of business that does not usually require a discussion of the International Date Line. Under the calendar followed in the country at that moment, the next day would ordinarily have been Friday, December 30. The government had decided otherwise.

The [International Date Line Act 2011](https://www.ag.gov.ws/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/International-Date-Line-Act-2011.pdf) established a new rule for standard time. At midnight after Thursday the country advanced to Saturday, December 31. The local Friday labeled December 30 did not occur. In Australia, New Zealand, American Samoa and other places the local dates followed their own established sequences, and the planet continued rotating. A day had been taken out of a national calendar by an act of government without shortening the physical duration of any day anywhere.

The first temptation is to call this a joke about bureaucracy. I would advise reading the statute before making it. It contains something better than a punch line. Section 5 dealt with the money a worker might lose because a scheduled day had been skipped. It required employers to pay wages and associated employment benefits for the normal hours that would otherwise have been earned on the lost day. The government had changed the numbering of its dates, but it knew the alteration could cost a person a day's income. The law therefore had to recognize a date for payroll purposes while eliminating it for the civil calendar.

That is an unusually clear illustration of what a rule actually does. A day is an astronomical and social interval, but a payroll date is a convention that causes money to change hands. An ordinary worker does not care whether the line separating two consecutive dates passes east or west of a particular island until the change alters a pay slip. The calendar is infrastructure. It becomes visible when someone attempts to move it.

The [2011 Australian Broadcasting Corporation account](https://www.abc.net.au/am/archive/articles/2011/12/30/s3399768.htm) documented the planned transition and the reasons Samoan officials gave for it. The country's government wanted closer alignment with the working weeks of Australia and New Zealand, two important economic partners. Before the shift, Samoa was on the American side of the International Date Line, sharing dates more closely with the United States and American Samoa but losing a working-day overlap with partners across the Pacific. Businesses could find that their Friday was already the partners' Saturday, and their Sunday coincided with a partner's Monday. The geography of the business week was inconvenient.

There was nothing impossible about changing it. Samoa could choose its standard civil time under its own laws. The International Date Line is not a natural crack in the Pacific Ocean. It is a conventional boundary that roughly follows the opposite side of the world from the prime meridian but bends around countries and island groups as political and practical circumstances require. Countries are not required to conduct their lives on the same side of the line that the shortest path on a map might suggest.

To understand the change, it helps to separate three things that ordinary language calls time. The Earth rotates, producing local patterns of daylight and darkness. A standardized time system assigns hours to those patterns and makes clocks comparable across regions. A calendar assigns dates to sequences of days. Samoa did not stop a rotation or lose twenty-four hours of physical history. It changed the mapping between an uninterrupted sequence of moments and the local date used to identify them.

Imagine a ship operating between the two sides of the date line. The date recorded in a log may need to change when it crosses, even while the crew experiences continuous time. That convention prevents long-distance travel from producing contradictions in the bookkeeping of the calendar. A landlocked person seldom confronts this distinction because they usually remain inside one civil-time system. An island government with substantial international trade cannot treat the boundary as a curiosity for geography textbooks.

The date line itself has no one simple physical location decided by the motion of the Sun. The Earth rotates through three hundred and sixty degrees in roughly twenty-four hours relative to the Sun, which suggests a natural relationship between longitude and local solar time. But civil zones are not obliged to follow every fifteen-degree division exactly. They take account of political boundaries, transport links and practical coordination. A clock can serve economic life more conveniently by departing from the solar time at its longitude.

Samoa had once chosen the other alignment. In the nineteenth century, trade relationships with the Americas had helped shape where the islands fell in the civil-date system. By 2011 the government judged that arrangement less useful than being on the same side as important western Pacific partners. A calendar convention had become a statement about the direction of economic relationships. The crossing was not a revolt against geography; it was an adjustment to which external schedules mattered most.

The precise legal transition matters more than the popular sentence that Samoa skipped a day. Under the act, the standard-time offset changed by twenty-four hours. That means a local timestamp just before the transition and a local timestamp just after could have dates separated by two calendar labels while representing consecutive moments. To an accountant, a programmer or an airline scheduler, this is a dangerous place to assume dates always advance by one. A computer system using local dates without an up-to-date time-zone database might produce a Friday that legally never existed in that jurisdiction.

The [IANA time-zone project correspondence from 2011](https://mm.icann.org/pipermail/tz/2011-August/033745.html) recorded the text of the new rule and the planned change. The tz database, which provides information used by many computer systems to convert times for different regions, needed to reflect the legal decision. The people maintaining such data were not debating whether the Earth really had a Friday. They were ensuring software would calculate the correct local time for a place that had changed its convention.

This is a less glamorous form of global governance than an international treaty. Somebody publishes the rule. Somebody records it. Software distributed to distant machines must eventually include the new data. A calendar event can fail in practice if the authoritative decision and the systems that implement it disagree. The country's law and a computer's stored model of its time zone can temporarily become two different versions of reality, with consequences for appointments, travel, transactions and records.

The payroll clause anticipates a class of problems that cannot be solved by updating a clock. A worker who would normally earn wages on Friday might find that the legal date was skipped. The act preserved entitlements for those normal hours, including relevant contributions and benefits. An employer failing to pay could face a penalty under the statute. The government's choice thus created obligations precisely because the missing day was not imaginary in its economic effects.

It would be easy to say workers got paid for a day they did not work. That is not the full legal story. The rule dealt with normal or usual hours the employee would have earned or been paid had the day existed under the previous standard-time sequence. A worker's exact circumstances could depend on employment arrangements and the provisions of the act. The law was designed to prevent a technical change in the calendar from being used as a means of withholding compensation. The details are not incidental. They explain why the government had to legislate more than a line on a map.

There were also religious and social consequences. A church with a weekly worship schedule might worry about the relationship between its observance and the newly named dates. An airline or shipping operator had to communicate with customers in several countries. A person born on December 30 in a prior year still had a birthday associated with that date, even if 2011 lacked a local occurrence of it. A social institution can depend on calendar labels while having its own rules for what counts as a week or an anniversary.

I am careful with hypothetical consequences because the temptation to invent a Samoan wedding, a missed birthday or a bewildered tax collector is enormous. The law and contemporary accounts already provide enough detail. They tell us why the government made the change, when it happened and how wages were addressed. Individual stories require identifiable records or interviews; the existence of an unusual date does not authorize a narrator to manufacture them.

Tokelau, then administered by New Zealand, also changed its date alignment at about the same time, according to [contemporary Associated Press reporting](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/tiny-samoa-takes-big-leap-across-date-line/). The surrounding region did not move together uniformly, however. American Samoa, geographically close but politically part of the United States, remained on the other side of the date line. Islands separated by a relatively short journey could therefore carry different calendar dates at the same moment. Geography had no veto over government.

The effect is sometimes described as Samoa becoming the first country to greet the New Year. That depends on the exact definition of country and on other inhabited territories and date-line arrangements. The defensible claim is that Samoa moved from one side of the date line to the other and advanced its local civil date by twenty-four hours. The symbolic race to be first or last is less useful than the documented alteration of how a population labeled its days.

A historical account should also distinguish standard time from daylight saving time. Samoa had policies affecting seasonal clock changes, and those could complicate exact UTC offsets at particular dates. The central 2011 event was a twenty-four-hour adjustment in standard-time alignment, not a routine one-hour daylight-saving transition. Someone researching the exact timestamp must use the relevant legal and time-zone records, not simply apply a modern offset retroactively to every historical date.

The country's choice also illustrates the economic cost of misaligned weekends. Cross-border transactions often require simultaneous working hours for settlement, customer support and coordination. A time-zone difference that leaves only part of the workweek in common may be inconvenient even when electronic communication makes messages instantaneous. Digital networks do not abolish the schedules of the people, banks, ports and public agencies connected to them.

Yet the shift was not a universal victory. Closer alignment with some partners meant less calendar alignment with others. A government can optimize a convention for one pattern of relationships and make it less convenient for another. That is the ordinary condition of an administrative decision. The problem was not how to make Samoa the same date as every trading partner at once, because those partners themselves lived on different sides of the global boundary. The problem was which mismatch the country preferred to bear.

A person may ask who had the authority to remove the day. The answer is not an astronomer. It was the Samoan legislature operating within the country's system of law. Time zones are state decisions implemented through rules, publication, clocks and shared information systems. They have to be coordinated with outside institutions, but they are not controlled by a single world government that assigns everybody's Tuesday. Even the International Date Line has bends precisely because sovereignty and local practice matter.

That answer should not encourage fantasies that a country can change any fact merely by legislating it. Samoa could change its date labels. It could not change the timing of sunrise, the amount of time a ship had spent sailing or the duration of a worker's actual labor. The statute had to account for material and social facts that persisted through the change. The wage clause is the most concrete proof that conventions have limits even when they are powerful.

The episode is also a useful programming problem. Representing a date as a simple integer count may be safe for one purpose and insufficient for another. A timestamp in UTC identifies a moment under a defined time scale. A local civil date requires rules for the jurisdiction and historical period. If software assumes every civil day is always twenty-four hours or that every location follows one fixed offset, it can fail when governments alter time zones. The underlying computers may be precise; their model of the world's rules may be incomplete.

This is why maintaining public time-zone data is an ongoing job rather than a table that can be printed once and forgotten. Governments may change rules for economic, social or political reasons. The database must represent past as well as current practice because historical records still need interpretation. In one jurisdiction, December 30, 2011 exists as a date in someone else's system; in Samoa's legal local calendar it was skipped. A correct system must handle both statements without a contradiction.

The immediate story begins on a Thursday and ends on a Saturday. The deeper story concerns the authority to define a shared coordinate for social life. A calendar appears natural because it follows the Sun and seasons. Its particular labels, boundaries and offsets are selected and maintained by institutions. Those labels become obligations once employers, schools, courts, banks and families depend on them.

I keep returning to the payroll provision because it resists the easiest philosophical conclusion. It would be fashionable to say dates are just constructs, as though that rendered them unreal. Try explaining that to someone missing a day's pay. The calendar is a convention with real consequences, and a responsible government changing it has to address some of those consequences in law.

The island did not travel through time. The government changed which day it called tomorrow. The physical world continued doing the same things, but the records attached to it changed immediately. A law about a missing Friday required a system to protect wages and a world of software to update its data. That is considerably stranger than a thought experiment about time travel, because it happened.
