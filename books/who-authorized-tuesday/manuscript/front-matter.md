# WHO AUTHORIZED TUESDAY?

*Twenty True Stories Behind the Rules That Run Your Life*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

## A Short Complaint About the Calendar

There was no Friday, December 30, 2011, in Samoa.

The government did not ban Fridays. It did not lock the shops, send everyone to bed, or ask a committee of astronomers to hold the planet still. It changed its standard time by law. In Samoa, Thursday, December 29, was followed by Saturday, December 31. Elsewhere the intervening date occurred normally. The Earth's rotation continued without comment.

The law also contained a provision for workers who might otherwise lose a day's pay. Someone had to consider the wages of a date that would not exist.

I admire that detail. It is more revealing than an account of a country jumping across the International Date Line. A map can show where the line went. A payroll system has to know what a person is owed.

This book began with a small annoyance. I was looking at ordinary objects—an A4 sheet of paper, the eleven-digit identifying string on an item for sale, a train timetable, a card bearing a number—and realized that the supposed facts I was encountering were often consequences of a meeting. Some meeting was useful. A few decisions were exquisitely clever. Others were compromises or expressions of power. Even the standards that deserve to survive have histories. A calendar day is a human label attached to a real cycle. A kilogram is a unit for measuring actual mass. A category on a form may affect a person whose identity cannot be adequately represented by it. Confusing these different types of fact is how the ordinary world becomes confusing.

The tempting version of this book says everything is made up. That claim is too cheap. People did not invent the duration of the Earth's day or the chemical behavior of cesium. They invented conventions for dividing, measuring, naming and distributing those facts. The distinction is worth defending: the world has properties independently of our labels, while coordination requires labels that other people can understand.

Why should anyone care who chose the shape of a paper sheet or an area code? Because once a choice is embedded in machinery, supply chains, databases, courts, classrooms and ordinary habit, changing it can be expensive or exclusionary. The original decision starts acting like a natural law. Nobody voting in 1884 at a meridian conference expected to control every phone's map app in 2026. But an international coordinate convention is one of the ancestors of that capability, alongside many subsequent technical decisions.

This is a book of twenty true episodes, not a manifesto against standards. Each was checked against an original law, a scientific definition, an agency history, a patent, a surviving object or a documented institutional action. When folklore is more entertaining than the record, it will be left outside. There were no proved riots of ordinary Britons demanding their eleven missing calendar days back, whatever a favorite anecdote may insist. Sweden really did have February 30 in 1712. Neither fact needs a fictional mob.

By the end I want one question to become difficult to ask casually: *Who decided this?* Sometimes the answer is a legislature, sometimes an international committee, sometimes a retailer, a postal planner or an engineer solving a real operational problem. Sometimes there is no one inventor, only a long accumulation of compatible choices. The absence of a singular mastermind is important. A shared standard can be made without being a conspiracy. It can also carry costs that nobody at the original table thought important enough to count.

I have not stood at the 1884 conference or interviewed the person buying gum in Ohio in 1974. Those people and events belong to the historical record. The voice here is the author's; the acts, measurements and records belong to their sources.

A date can disappear by law. The next question is what else has been arranged so successfully that most people cannot see the arrangement.
