# The Map of Dependence

A supply chain looks different after a government learns to see it as strategy.

For decades, many corporate supply chains were optimized primarily through commercial questions. Where is production cheaper? Which supplier is reliable? How much inventory is necessary? Where should a factory sit relative to customers, ports, labor, energy, and tax? National security existed around the edges, especially in defense, energy, and regulated industries, but much ordinary trade was narrated through efficiency.

Then dependence became visible.

Semiconductors, medical supplies, critical minerals, energy, telecommunications equipment, batteries, shipping routes, cloud infrastructure, and advanced manufacturing all became objects of geopolitical attention. Governments mapped concentration. Firms mapped exposure. Investors learned new acronyms. The map changed behavior.

That last step is the important one.

A dependency can be economically benign until actors believe it may be weaponized. Once governments identify a supply chain as strategically sensitive, companies change sourcing, states subsidize domestic capacity, allies negotiate shared production, competitors accelerate substitutes, and suppliers reconsider investment. The act of declaring a dependency strategic helps make it strategic.

This is not because the original risk was imaginary. Concentration is a real property. Chokepoints exist. A region can dominate a manufacturing step. A shipping lane can be disrupted. Export controls can deny access. A foreign government can use commercial leverage politically.

But second-order geopolitics begins when everyone sees that everyone else sees the vulnerability.

A state considering an export restriction knows that the target may stockpile, redesign products, fund alternatives, or retaliate. A country considering dependence on a foreign supplier knows future leaders may use that dependence differently. Firms know governments may change trade rules. Investors know subsidies can alter industry economics. Supply-chain decisions become bets on future policy reactions.

Commercial optimization acquires a strategic discount rate.

The simplest version is inventory. Under stable conditions, lean inventory lowers carrying costs. Under uncertain conditions, inventory becomes an option. A warehouse containing extra components looks inefficient until transport fails. The value of the option depends on the probability and duration of disruption, the cost of substitute supply, and the consequences of downtime.

Geopolitics raises uncertainty not only by creating shocks but by changing the probability that others prepare for shocks.

If every major buyer begins stockpiling a critical material, prices can rise. Higher prices encourage mining and recycling. Governments may investigate concentration. Suppliers expand capacity. The original fear changes the market structure. A risk forecast produces investment that can reduce the risk over time.

This is a self-defeating prophecy in the useful sense.

The same process can overshoot. Subsidies attract too much capacity. Multiple countries build redundant plants. Prices collapse. Political support weakens. Firms exit. The system swings between shortage fear and glut.

Industrial policy therefore contains the same timing problem as market risk. Current prices summarize present scarcity, while policy acts on expected future scarcity after everyone responds to present prices.

The future includes the response to the forecast.

This is especially difficult in industries with long construction times. A fabrication plant, mine, transmission line, port, or energy facility can take years to build. By completion, the market that justified the project may have changed. Competitors may have built too. Technology may shift. Demand may disappoint. The political coalition may be different.

Long lead times amplify second-order uncertainty because more actors can respond before capacity arrives.

Nations face another problem: strategic autonomy is expensive in ways that are easy to measure, while the value of reduced dependence is conditional.

A domestic plant may cost more than imports. The subsidy is visible. The crisis it might help during is hypothetical. Voters can see the budget today and may never observe the avoided disruption. If nothing bad happens, critics can call the policy wasteful. If a disruption occurs and the plant is useful, the policy looks prescient.

This is the resilience problem at national scale.

The debate becomes ideological when it should remain partly empirical. Not every supply chain deserves redundancy. Autarky destroys the gains from specialization. Trade can deepen peace, growth, and innovation. Domestic production can be captured by incumbents, protected from competition, or sustained long after strategic need fades.

But pure efficiency can hide common-mode risk.

If the entire world concentrates production where it is cheapest, the global system may become dependent on a small number of regions or firms. Each buyer individually makes a sensible sourcing decision. Collectively, buyers create a chokepoint.

The composition problem returns.

A company diversifying suppliers pays a private cost for a public resilience benefit. If disruption is rare, the company that remains concentrated may outperform. Competitive markets can therefore underprovide redundancy when the benefit appears mainly during shared crises.

Insurance exists to price some of this risk. Government intervenes when risks are hard to insure, systemically correlated, or strategically unacceptable.

The difficulty is deciding which is which before hindsight.

Geopolitics also changes technology trajectories through anticipation. Export controls on advanced technology can slow a target’s access. They can also increase the target’s incentive to develop domestic substitutes. Sanctions can impose real costs and simultaneously accelerate efforts to reduce exposure to the sanctioning system. Control over a chokepoint is strongest before the rest of the world invests heavily in routing around it.

Power spends itself when used.

This does not mean sanctions or controls are ineffective. Effects vary enormously by target, coalition, technology, enforcement, time horizon, and alternatives. The second-order lesson is that coercive tools change the incentive to escape the tool.

The more credible the threat, the more valuable escape becomes.

Reserve currencies, payment networks, shipping insurance, semiconductor equipment, cloud services, standards bodies, energy pipelines, and trade finance all contain versions of this dynamic. A dominant infrastructure gains power because others depend on it. Using that power aggressively can increase the return to building alternatives. Restraint can preserve dependence; use can reveal the cost of dependence.

Strategic leverage is often greatest before it is demonstrated.

This creates a temptation toward ambiguity. If partners are unsure whether a dependency will be weaponized, they may continue using the efficient system. But ambiguity can also undermine trust. States and companies prefer infrastructure whose rules appear stable.

The provider faces the same credibility trade-off as a platform or manager: flexibility today can reduce trust tomorrow.

Alliances help by changing the model from independence to trusted interdependence.

No advanced economy can produce every strategically important input efficiently. Allies can specialize while reducing exposure to adversaries. Shared standards, mutual investment, common stockpiles, interoperable logistics, and coordinated controls can create resilience without full duplication.

But alliances are second-order systems too.

Each member asks whether others will remain committed during stress. Domestic politics matter. Burden-sharing debates influence future willingness. A country that underinvests may rely on allies; allies respond by demanding more investment. A country that builds too much independent capacity may weaken the economic ties that made alliance commitment valuable.

Security and dependence are not simple opposites.

Interdependence can create vulnerability and restraint. Two countries with deep trade ties have more to lose from conflict, but they also possess more channels through which pressure can be applied. The same network can deter and coerce.

This is why geopolitical models built around one variable—trade reduces war, dependence creates weakness, self-sufficiency creates security—tend to fail. The outcome depends on asymmetry, substitutability, domestic politics, military balance, geography, and beliefs about future behavior.

Networks distribute power unevenly.

A node connected to many others may be resilient because it has alternatives. A node can also become a chokepoint because many flows pass through it. Network centrality can confer bargaining power. It can also make the node a target.

Infrastructure is strategy because it shapes future options.

Ports, cables, railways, standards, payment rails, satellites, energy grids, data centers, and semiconductor fabs are not only assets. They are commitments embedded in concrete and code. Once built, they influence trade patterns, alliances, investment, and military planning.

The first move is capital expenditure. The second move is everyone else planning around the installed base.

This is why standards battles can matter as much as product battles. A technical standard adopted widely creates compatibility. Compatibility attracts investment. Investment makes the standard harder to displace. Firms and states may compete to shape the rule before network effects lock it in.

A standard is a coordination equilibrium with a history.

The danger is treating every standard as geopolitical territory. Over-politicization can fragment systems that benefit from global compatibility. Different standards increase costs, reduce scale, and make scientific and commercial exchange harder. The security gain must be weighed against the innovation lost when networks split.

Splitting itself changes incentives. If firms expect permanent blocs, they invest in duplicate supply chains. Those investments make the blocs more economically distinct, which makes political separation easier. Expectations of decoupling can produce decoupling.

Merton returns in a new costume.

The reverse can happen too. If companies invest heavily across borders and governments build institutions around interdependence, separation becomes costlier. Integration creates constituencies for integration.

Economic structure becomes political feedback.

This is why forecasting geopolitics from speeches alone is weak. Follow capital. Where are factories being built? Which suppliers are being qualified? Which inventories are rising? Which standards are being adopted? Which shipping routes are receiving investment? Which contracts are becoming longer? Material commitments reveal beliefs with costs attached.

A press statement is cheap. A ten-year factory is not.

Investors use this distinction instinctively. They look for capex, purchase agreements, order books, hiring, and policy funding. These are signals of anticipated regimes. But the investor must then take the second step: if everyone invests for the same geopolitical future, what market does that collective investment create?

A shortage thesis can produce a glut.

A security subsidy can produce commoditized capacity.

A chokepoint can produce substitutes.

A decoupling forecast can create the infrastructure of decoupling.

The map of dependence is therefore not a static risk document. It is a game board that changes when published.

Governments need to be careful about this performative effect. Label an industry strategic and capital may flood it. That may be desired. It can also invite rent-seeking. Firms learn which language unlocks subsidy. Every industry discovers a national-security paragraph for the pitch deck.

The metric problem returns as industrial policy.

A serious government needs criteria that survive lobbying: concentration, substitutability, lead times, military or essential-service relevance, allied capacity, technology trajectory, and the consequences of disruption. It also needs exit rules. Strategic programs without sunset or revalidation can turn temporary risk into permanent entitlement.

The same is true for corporate resilience. A company should not diversify every supplier equally. It should identify dependencies where failure is correlated, substitution is slow, and downtime is severe. Redundancy belongs where the return path is dangerous.

This is second-order risk management with geography.

The world economy is not becoming simple again. Even when countries localize some production, inputs cross borders. Knowledge travels. Equipment has suppliers. Energy has infrastructure. Capital has owners. Talent moves. A domestic label can conceal international dependencies several layers deep.

The dream of complete control is another abstraction.

The practical goal is optionality.

Multiple suppliers where it matters. Trusted partners. Inventories sized to plausible disruptions. Designs that can substitute inputs. Financial capacity to survive price spikes. Standards that permit interoperability. Domestic capability in a small number of genuinely critical areas. Intelligence that notices when concentration is changing.

Optionality costs money because it is the purchase of future choices.

The value appears when the other side makes a move you did not want.

Geopolitics has always been about anticipating other actors. The modern twist is the degree to which commercial networks themselves have become both the object of strategy and the medium through which strategy operates.

A nation maps a dependency.

A firm reads the map.

A competitor reads the firm’s investment.

An ally reads the competitor’s response.

Capital moves.

Factories rise.

The map is already old.
