# The Enemy Reads the Manual

Strategy becomes obsolete fastest when it works in public.

This is one reason military history is so full of temporary advantages. A new doctrine, weapon, formation, fortification, code, logistics system, or way of organizing command can create surprise. Success advertises the surprise. Opponents study wreckage, prisoners, signals, battle reports, budgets, speeches, and the shape of defeat. They adapt. The advantage becomes a lesson supplied to the other side at terrible cost.

The same pattern appears in business with lower stakes and friendlier vocabulary. A retailer invents a format. Competitors visit the stores. A manufacturer shortens setup times. Rivals hire the engineers. A software company finds a distribution channel. Everyone sees the growth. A hedge fund’s returns reveal that something unusual is happening. A startup educates customers about a new category and incumbents learn the category from the startup’s marketing.

Success is a disclosure event.

This does not mean secrecy is the foundation of strategy. Many durable advantages are visible. A trusted brand is visible. Scale is visible. A dense logistics network is visible. A culture of operational excellence can be visible without being easy to reproduce. The important distinction is between seeing a move and possessing the capabilities that make the move work.

The enemy reads the manual. The enemy may not own the factory.

This is where second-order strategy differs from cleverness. Cleverness asks, “What move will surprise them?” Strategy asks, “What happens after they understand the move?”

The distinction is easy to admire and hard to fund. Surprise produces immediate results. Capability-building can look slow. A promotional tactic can raise revenue this quarter. A supply network may take a decade. A viral campaign can be copied next week. A reputation for reliability is copied only by becoming reliable for a long time.

The more an advantage depends on other people not knowing it, the shorter its expected life once successful.

This principle links espionage, trading, marketing, and innovation without pretending they are morally equivalent. Hidden information is a wasting asset when use reveals it.

Thomas Schelling approached strategy from another direction. His work on conflict and cooperation emphasized that adversaries often share interests, that commitment can alter bargaining, and that strategic situations depend on what each side believes about the other’s choices. Nuclear strategy made these questions terrifyingly concrete. A country did not only need weapons. It needed the other side to believe retaliation would remain possible. Deterrence operated through beliefs about future behavior.

The weapon’s effect partly existed before use.

That makes credibility central to strategy. A threat that nobody believes is weak. A promise nobody believes is weak. Yet credibility cannot be increased without limit by louder communication. A threat can be too costly to carry out, making it incredible. A promise can conflict with incentives, making it fragile. A negotiator can say anything. The counterpart asks what the negotiator would actually want when the moment arrives.

Second-order strategy therefore studies incentives at the future decision point.

This sounds abstract until you watch a child negotiate bedtime. A parent says there will be no story unless pajamas are on by eight. The child knows the parent enjoys the story too. If the deadline passes, will the parent really cancel? A rule that repeatedly bends teaches the child a model of enforcement. The next evening begins from that updated model.

Credibility is the memory of past second moves.

Organizations face the same issue. A company announces that it will not discount below a certain level. Competitors and customers watch. If the company repeatedly breaks its own floor at quarter-end, the announcement loses power. A platform says violations lead to suspension but makes exceptions for high-profile users. Future users update on the exception. A regulator threatens enforcement but lacks resources or political support. Firms learn the actual boundary from action.

The formal rule and the credible rule separate.

Strategic actors search for that separation.

This is why an opponent need not know your secret plan to exploit your system. It may be enough to know what you cannot bring yourself to do.

A company may be unwilling to cannibalize its profitable product. A political coalition may be unable to accept a certain compromise. A military alliance may face slow consensus. A retailer may be structurally committed to high-margin stores. A software vendor may depend on backward compatibility. These constraints can be strengths in one context and vulnerabilities in another.

Strategy lives in commitments.

Commitments are often treated as limitations. They can also create power. A company known for refusing low-quality shortcuts may attract customers who value reliability. A central bank committed to an inflation target may shape expectations. A negotiator who cannot easily reverse a public decision may become more credible. Schelling famously explored situations in which limiting one’s own options could change bargaining power.

The paradox is that flexibility is not always strength.

If the other side believes you can cheaply reverse your position, your promise may carry little weight. If a platform can change its rules at any moment, participants have difficulty planning around them. If a company can abandon a partner without cost, the partner discounts long-term assurances. Constraint can make a future action believable.

But commitment also invites adaptation.

Once the other side understands what you cannot do, it can maneuver around the boundary. A fixed doctrine simplifies coordination inside an organization and simplifies prediction outside it. Standard operating procedures reduce internal confusion and may reveal timing or thresholds. The same regularity that makes a system reliable can make it legible.

This is not an argument against procedures. It is an argument for knowing which procedures can be safely legible.

Safety-critical systems often benefit from predictability. Air traffic rules work because pilots do not improvise secretly. Commercial contracts work because parties can anticipate enforcement. The strategic problem arises where another actor benefits from exploiting your regularity.

Competition is partly a contest over who has to reveal first.

A retailer launches a promotion. Competitors see it and decide whether to match. An airline changes a route. Rivals observe capacity. A cloud provider cuts price. Others respond. A chipmaker announces a product roadmap; customers and competitors update investment plans. Public companies disclose information required for market integrity, and rivals read the disclosures too.

Transparency creates coordination and vulnerability simultaneously.

That is why strategy cannot be reduced to openness or secrecy. Both have domains.

Open technical standards can accelerate ecosystems because participants know the interface. Proprietary implementation can preserve differentiation. Public prices reduce negotiation costs and may invite comparison. Confidential contracts permit tailoring and reduce transparency. Open-source software can build trust and adoption while business advantage moves into service, data, distribution, or execution. The boundary between visible and hidden is itself strategic.

The best strategy often places secrecy around things that decay when copied and openness around things that gain value when adopted.

This is a network logic. A payment protocol may become more valuable as others adopt it. A fraud-detection heuristic may become less valuable as adversaries understand it. A platform API benefits from developer knowledge. A spam filter does not benefit from spammers knowing its precise decision boundary.

The mistake is to use one philosophy for every layer.

Businesses sometimes become religious about moats. They ask what can never be copied. The question is too absolute. Almost everything can be copied in form. The useful question is how the return from imitation compares with the time, capital, culture, data, relationships, and complementary assets required.

A restaurant recipe can be copied more easily than a restaurant.

A lean manufacturing technique can be described in a book, but reproducing the routines, supplier relationships, worker knowledge, maintenance discipline, and management habits that make it work may take years. An airline can copy a low-cost competitor’s seat configuration without copying its labor model, fleet simplicity, airport choices, or culture. A bank can copy a product while lacking the funding base that makes the economics attractive.

Visible practice sits on invisible history.

This is why strategic imitation so often produces cargo-cult management. Firms copy the artifact rather than the system. They adopt the stand-up meeting, the innovation lab, the open office, the two-pizza team, the quality slogan, the customer metric, the venture fund. The visible move arrived after years of organizational development somewhere else. Detached from those conditions, it becomes theater.

Second-order strategy asks what competitors will copy and whether the copied surface matters.

Sometimes imitation strengthens the pioneer. If a company defines a category and competitors copy the language, the category becomes legible to customers. If an interface standard spreads, the pioneer’s ecosystem can grow. If competitors validate a market, investors and suppliers enter. The first mover may lose uniqueness and gain infrastructure.

This is why first-mover advantage is not a law. Moving first can mean learning first, building scale, setting standards, and locking relationships. It can also mean paying to educate the market, revealing the opportunity, making early technical mistakes, and showing later entrants what not to do.

The second mover gets a free case study.

The strategic value of first position therefore depends on whether learning can be captured. If the pioneer’s experience becomes proprietary capability, first movement compounds. If the experience is visible and easy to copy, later entrants can free-ride.

The same logic applies to nations and technology. A country that pioneers infrastructure can build industrial know-how. It can also expose standards and cost curves that others adopt. A military that demonstrates a technology reveals effectiveness and countermeasures. A company that deploys a new AI workflow learns operational lessons while competitors observe the category.

The first move creates both asset and intelligence for others.

This makes organizational learning speed a strategic resource.

If every visible move will eventually be understood, durable advantage can come from staying ahead of the response. The company does not need an eternal secret if it can learn faster than competitors can copy. A sports team does not need a play nobody will ever decode if it can evolve the offense before opponents fully adjust. A cybersecurity team does not expect a defense to remain untouched; it expects continual adaptation.

The advantage moves from position to rate.

This can become exhausting. “Move faster than everyone” is not a serious universal strategy. Speed has costs. Constant change can destroy reliability and institutional memory. The better idea is selective learning speed: know which parts of the environment adapt quickly and design those parts for iteration, while keeping slower capabilities stable.

A brand promise may be slow. Marketing tactics may be fast. Core safety architecture may be slow. Detection methods may be fast. Mission may be slow. Product experiments may be fast. Capital structure may be slow. Pricing can be fast until customers learn to expect constant movement.

Strategy needs multiple clocks.

The military analogy becomes dangerous when imported too eagerly into business. Customers are not enemies. Employees are not troops. Competitors can expand a market rather than merely take share. Cooperation, standards, regulation, and shared infrastructure matter. Yet the adaptive principle survives the translation: other actors learn from your behavior.

They do not need malicious intent.

A customer learns that renewal is negotiable. A supplier learns that delays are tolerated. A competitor learns which segment you will not defend. An employee learns that deadlines move. An investor learns how management responds to pressure. Every repeated decision becomes part of someone else’s strategy.

This is why character becomes strategic. Consistency creates credible expectations. If a company is known to honor contracts, partners plan differently. If a manager is known to admit mistakes, employees surface information differently. If a nation is known to uphold commitments, allies and adversaries update accordingly.

Reputation is the model others have learned about your second move.

A reputation can constrain you. The company known for premium quality may struggle to launch a cheap product. The leader known for never retreating can become trapped. The investor known for patience may be exploited by managers who assume support. Every identity is both asset and prediction.

The answer is not to become unpredictable for its own sake. Randomness destroys trust. Strategic ambiguity has a role in some conflicts, but ordinary institutions depend on legibility.

The goal is to be predictable where cooperation requires predictability and adaptable where competition punishes predictability.

That sentence is easier to write than to operationalize because the domains overlap. A company may want customers to trust prices while competitors remain uncertain about future products. Employees need stable values and changing tactics. Allies need commitment; adversaries should not receive a complete playbook.

Good strategy is partly boundary design.

What must others know to cooperate with us?

What should remain difficult to infer because disclosure destroys value?

Which visible practices sit on capabilities that are difficult to copy?

Which advantage will disappear if widely imitated?

What will competitors learn from our success?

What will we do after they learn it?

The last question is the one most strategy decks omit.

They end at the winning move.

The world keeps reading.
