# The Feed Teaches the Creator

A platform does not need to tell creators what it wants. It can show them what survives.

This is one of the strangest labor markets in modern culture. Millions of people produce videos, posts, songs, images, essays, reviews, product demonstrations, jokes, lessons, arguments, and fragments of private life. They do not receive a conventional job description from the distribution system. The system ranks their work. Distribution becomes feedback. Creators infer the rules from outcomes.

The formal algorithm may be proprietary, complex, frequently changing, and partly personalized. The practical algorithm is cultural knowledge.

Creators learn it through dashboards, experiments, consultants, peers, rumors, imitation, and observation. They learn that certain openings hold attention, certain lengths travel better, certain thumbnails attract clicks, certain topics trigger comments, certain posting rhythms keep an audience warm. Some of this knowledge is accurate. Some is superstition. The distinction does not matter as much as one might think, because even mistaken beliefs can coordinate behavior when widely shared.

A rumor about the algorithm can become culture.

Suppose creators come to believe that frequent posting is rewarded. They post more often. Audiences receive more material. The platform observes abundant fresh content and may change ranking to manage supply. Creators then experience a different distribution environment and revise their theory. No participant has to know the whole system for the system to evolve.

The feed teaches the creator; the creator supplies the next training set.

This is second-order adaptation in unusually compressed form because the observer and subject exchange information continuously. A platform measures users. Users produce content designed for the platform. The platform measures the response to that adapted content. Future models train on data generated under earlier models.

A naive analyst might call this personalization working as intended. Sometimes it is. Users receive more of what they engage with; creators learn to produce what audiences reward. A huge, chaotic cultural market becomes navigable.

But the phrase “what audiences reward” hides the platform’s role in deciding which rewards are visible.

A viewer cannot engage with a video she never sees. A creator cannot learn from an audience the platform never routes toward her. Ranking systems shape exposure, and exposure shapes the evidence used to improve ranking. Early advantage can compound. A creator who gets initial distribution receives more data, more followers, more social proof, and more opportunity to refine. A creator who gets little distribution may disappear before quality can be learned.

The exploration problem is therefore cultural as well as technical.

Recommendation systems face a familiar tension between exploiting what appears to work and exploring alternatives that might work better. Too much exploitation and the system becomes repetitive, reinforcing known tastes and established creators. Too much exploration and users receive irrelevant material. Every platform chooses some balance, explicitly or through architecture.

Creators experience that balance as fate.

The emotional consequences are easy to trivialize because “creator” sounds glamorous. In practice, many people are conducting small businesses inside an opaque distribution environment. A drop in reach can mean lost income. A format change can devalue months of learned craft. A moderation decision can remove access to an audience built over years. The platform may think in population-level metrics; the creator lives the local shock.

This asymmetry encourages ritualistic theories of control.

When outcomes are variable and the mechanism is partly hidden, humans look for patterns. Post at this hour. Avoid that word. Use this sound. Reply to comments immediately. Do not edit after posting. The folk algorithm becomes a set of charms, some grounded in evidence and some not. The behavior resembles athletes’ superstitions for the same reason: feedback is noisy, stakes matter, and the person wants agency.

The platform may publish guidance, but creators often trust observed outcomes more than documentation. They know official explanations are necessarily simplified. They also know the company’s objectives can change.

This makes platform governance a credibility problem.

If creators believe stated rules match enforcement and ranking, they can plan. If they believe the real system differs from the stated one, an interpretive economy develops. Consultants, analytics tools, newsletters, forums, and peer networks sell access to the hidden regime. Ambiguity becomes an industry.

This is not unique to digital platforms. Tax systems create accountants. Regulation creates compliance consultants. Search engines created an optimization industry. College admissions created counseling markets. Wherever a consequential evaluation rule is complex or partly hidden, people pay intermediaries to infer it.

The evaluator generates a market for reverse interpretation.

The cultural consequences go beyond business. When a distribution system rewards certain forms, artists and communicators may internalize the form as craft.

A comedian learns to reach the premise faster because viewers can swipe away. A teacher turns every lesson into a hook because retention falls in the first seconds. A musician thinks about the moment a listener might skip. A journalist writes a headline for the ranking surface. A restaurant plates food with the camera in mind because images bring customers. A politician clips a speech into fragments optimized for circulation.

The medium has always shaped form. Newspapers shaped prose; radio shaped timing; television shaped appearance; recorded music changed song structure; the three-minute single was not delivered by nature. What is different in algorithmic media is the granularity and speed of behavioral feedback.

The creator can see retention curves.

That curve is an extraordinary instrument. It can reveal where an audience loses interest. Used with judgment, it teaches craft. But an instrument changes the thing being played when it becomes the dominant teacher. If every creative decision is made to prevent local drop-off, slow build, ambiguity, silence, difficulty, and delayed payoff may become expensive.

Some works need the audience to be briefly bored.

A novel can spend pages establishing a world. A symphony can withhold resolution. A documentary can remain with an uncomfortable face. A mathematical explanation can require definitions before delight. A friendship can contain a pause. Local retention is not identical to long-run value.

This is the same problem we saw in organizational metrics, now operating at cultural scale. Fast measurable signals can crowd out slow, difficult value.

The difference is that culture reacts collectively. Once creators learn the same retention tactics, audiences become habituated. The opening that once felt urgent becomes normal. The normal becomes tiring. Creators intensify. The platform’s data then reflects an audience trained by years of intensified openings.

The demand curve has a history.

This is why arguments about whether platforms “give people what they want” often become sterile. People do want some of what they click. The choice is real. But wants are not fixed inventory stored inside the skull. Attention is trainable. Expectations adapt. Social norms change. Creators alter supply in response to ranking. The platform changes discovery. A revealed preference is revealed inside an environment that has been shaping the revealer.

None of this requires mind control. Ordinary learning is enough.

A person who watches short videos becomes skilled at evaluating short videos. She develops expectations about pacing. A person who uses recommendation feeds learns that novelty arrives without search. Search habits change. A person who receives constant personalized entertainment may find slower forms less immediately rewarding. These changes can be small and heterogeneous. Over millions of users they become a cultural variable.

The platform is not outside the preference system.

This matters for claims about optimization. If a recommendation model maximizes a short-horizon engagement measure, it can alter the future population on which that measure is evaluated. The optimization target may create users for whom the target is increasingly easy to maximize.

That is a dangerous form of success.

Imagine a system that learns that highly stimulating content keeps users engaged. It supplies more. Users become accustomed to higher stimulation, reducing response to calmer material. The next model sees stronger relative performance for stimulating content. The signal validates itself through adaptation.

The opposite can happen too. A platform can deliberately introduce diversity, friction, or quality constraints. Users may initially engage less, then develop different habits. Short-run experiments can misread long-run preference formation because the treatment period is shorter than adaptation.

The problem is not merely statistical. It is philosophical in a practical sense: what kind of user is the product trying to help exist?

Every long-lived product answers this question whether leaders admit it or not.

A fitness app trains habits. A navigation app trains spatial dependence or confidence. A financial app trains attitudes toward saving, speculation, and risk. A dating app trains expectations about choice and rejection. A workplace tool trains communication norms. A platform that says it only serves current preference is making a choice about preference formation by default.

Product design is character design at scale, lightly applied.

That sentence can be abused by paternalists, so it needs limits. Companies do not own users’ character. Adults have agency. Diverse users experience the same product differently. The fact that an environment influences behavior does not grant designers moral sovereignty over the influenced.

It does create responsibility for predictable effects.

The most responsible platform designs acknowledge three constituencies: users, creators, and the long-run ecology connecting them.

Optimizing only for users can squeeze creators into unsustainable production. Optimizing only for creators can flood users with material. Optimizing only for platform revenue can extract from both. Ecology matters because today’s optimization changes tomorrow’s supply and demand.

This is especially visible in monetization.

If advertising revenue rewards scale, creators learn to produce reliable volume. If subscriptions reward deep loyalty, smaller niches can survive. If tipping dominates, intimacy and live interaction may matter more. If brand sponsorship dominates, content becomes legible to advertisers. The revenue model becomes an aesthetic force.

Artists have always needed patrons. The patron’s form changes the art.

The Medici did not need an engagement dashboard to influence production. Newspaper advertisers influenced what newspapers could afford to publish. Record labels shaped formats and careers. The new feature is the fusion of patron, distributor, measurement system, and behavioral laboratory inside the same platform.

The platform can observe which creative choices produce commercial response at a scale older patrons could not.

This makes it powerful and vulnerable. If creators become too dependent, they seek diversification. Newsletters, direct subscriptions, merchandise, live events, memberships, and cross-platform audiences become insurance against ranking changes. The platform’s control creates incentives to route around the platform.

Power recruits counterpower.

This is a recurring second-order pattern. A system that becomes excellent at extracting value can motivate participants to reduce dependence on the system. A company centralizes procurement and suppliers merge. A regulator tightens one category and firms innovate outside it. A platform controls distribution and creators build direct channels.

The return path is strategic.

Platforms therefore face a subtle choice about predictability. If ranking is perfectly predictable, it is easier to optimize and may become stale. If ranking is too unpredictable, creators cannot build durable businesses and may leave. Some uncertainty supports exploration; too much feels arbitrary.

The same is true of moderation and monetization rules. A stable rule can be adapted to, which creates edge cases. A constantly changing rule can prevent some strategic adaptation but destroy legitimacy. Good governance cannot eliminate adaptation; it must make adaptation compatible with the system’s purpose.

That often means rewarding broad outcomes rather than narrow tactics, using multiple signals, preserving exploration, communicating changes, allowing appeals, and observing what creator behavior emerges after each intervention.

Most important, it means measuring creators as part of the product.

If a ranking change increases user minutes while forcing creators into a production pace that leads to burnout and exit, the short-run user metric is borrowing from future supply. If a monetization change raises platform revenue while making high-quality niche work impossible, the ecology narrows. If a moderation system reduces obvious violations but causes legitimate communities to self-censor because appeals are unreliable, the measured safety gain may hide a participation loss.

The product is the loop.

This idea becomes even more important as generative artificial intelligence lowers the cost of producing content. When supply becomes abundant, ranking power increases. The scarce resource is no longer content but attention, trust, provenance, and human interest. Creators may use AI to produce more. Platforms may use AI to rank more. Users may use AI to filter more. Each layer adapts to the others.

A future feed may contain machines producing for machines that are ranking for humans who are training the machines through response.

That sounds absurd only because it is stated all at once.

The structure already exists in simpler form.

A creator watches the analytics and changes the opening.

The algorithm watches the changed opening and updates the ranking.

The audience watches what survives and changes its expectations.

The creator watches the audience again.

The feed teaches the creator, and eventually the creator teaches the feed what a creator is.
