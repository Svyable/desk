# The Brand Becomes a Costume

A product can solve a problem and still be purchased for the audience.

This is not a cynical observation. Humans are social animals, and consumption has always carried signals. Clothes indicate membership, aspiration, profession, rebellion, wealth, modesty, taste, region, age, and occasion. Food can signal hospitality, discipline, cosmopolitanism, tradition, or virtue. Cars, phones, watches, books, neighborhoods, vacations, and even the absence of visible consumption can communicate something about the person doing the choosing.

The interesting loop begins when producers learn to design for the signal and consumers learn that producers are designing for the signal.

A brand starts as a mark that helps identify origin and quality. Over time it accumulates associations. Those associations attract customers who want what the product does and what possessing it says. The company notices. Marketing intensifies the symbolic meaning. More customers arrive because the meaning is legible. Eventually the brand can become a costume: a ready-made social story worn by the buyer.

Then the costume becomes crowded.

Status goods contain a built-in second-order instability. Their value can depend partly on who else has them. A scarce object can signal distinction because few people possess it. If the signal works, demand rises. Production expands or resale prices increase. The object becomes more visible. Visibility can increase desire, but widespread adoption can weaken distinction. Early adopters leave for something else.

The product succeeds at destroying one source of its own appeal.

This is the status cycle in miniature.

Thorstein Veblen wrote about conspicuous consumption in the late nineteenth century, observing how visible expenditure could communicate social position. Later sociologists and anthropologists complicated the picture. Consumption is not only a vertical race for wealth. People use goods to mark subcultures, moral identities, aesthetic taste, gender, profession, politics, and belonging. An inexpensive object can carry immense symbolic capital inside the right group. A deliberately plain object can signal confidence more effectively than an ornate one.

Once signals are social, meaning depends on interpretation by others.

A suit means something different in a courtroom, nightclub, startup office, funeral, and beach town. The object is constant; the social model changes. Brands therefore live in interpretive communities, not in packaging alone.

This is why ethnographic consumer research can reveal things a preference survey misses.

Ask someone why she bought a particular sneaker and she may say comfort, style, price, or durability. Spend time with the social world around the sneaker and additional functions appear. The shoe may connect her to a music scene, a sports identity, a period of childhood, a peer group, a desired adulthood, or a refusal of what her parents wear. None of these motives has to be conscious in the moment of purchase. They live in the network of meanings surrounding the product.

The company enters that network carefully or foolishly.

Marketing can strengthen an association by naming it. It can also ruin the association by naming it too loudly. Subcultures often value the sense that insiders recognize a signal outsiders miss. Once a corporate campaign explains the signal to everyone, some of its value evaporates.

Coolness dislikes minutes from the meeting in which coolness was approved.

This is a second-order problem because the consumer does not only observe the product. The consumer observes the brand observing the consumer. People can feel when a company has discovered their identity and is trying to sell it back to them.

Sometimes they enjoy the recognition. Sometimes they recoil.

The difference often depends on whether the brand appears to participate in the culture or merely extract its symbols. Long involvement, credible relationships, product quality, and restraint can make commercial participation feel legitimate. Sudden adoption of a cause or aesthetic after it becomes profitable can feel like costume at the corporate level too.

The brand is also being watched.

This mutual observation explains why authenticity is so commercially potent and conceptually slippery. Authenticity is supposed to mean not performing for approval. The moment it becomes a market category, people learn to perform authenticity.

Restaurants advertise rustic imperfection. Hotels design “local” experiences. clothing is pre-distressed. Founders tell origin stories. Corporate social accounts cultivate casual voices. Luxury companies sell understated products whose price is legible mainly to those who already know. The market industrializes signs of non-market sincerity.

Consumers adapt by raising the bar.

A polished story once signaled professionalism; now excessive polish can signal artificiality. A rough video once signaled spontaneity; now viewers know roughness can be carefully produced. A handwritten note once felt personal; scaled fulfillment systems can print handwriting. Each signal loses innocence as the audience learns the production method.

The semiotic arms race is not fake versus real. It is signal versus knowledge of signal production.

This resembles financial markets. An obvious arbitrage disappears when widely known. In culture, an obvious status signal can become less valuable when widely decoded. The scarce resource moves one level up: not owning the luxury item, but owning the obscure model; not visiting the famous destination, but knowing the unmarked restaurant; not displaying wealth, but displaying the confidence to ignore display.

Distinction recursively retreats from mass recognition.

Pierre Bourdieu’s work on taste and distinction emphasized that preferences are related to social position and cultural capital. One need not accept every class interpretation to recognize the recursive mechanism. Taste is partly relational. What counts as refined, vulgar, adventurous, basic, serious, or authentic changes as groups differentiate themselves from one another.

Markets chase the movement.

A niche grows. Brands enter. The niche becomes legible. Early members complain that it has been ruined. New niches form. The cycle is visible in fashion, music, food, neighborhoods, travel, fitness, technology, and language.

There is a business-history version of this pattern in countless categories. A product begins with enthusiasts. Enthusiasts tolerate inconvenience because the identity value and functional novelty are high. Mainstream adoption attracts capital. Companies standardize the experience, remove friction, professionalize design, and expand distribution. The category becomes easier to buy and less distinctive to the people who valued difficulty as evidence of commitment.

Scale changes meaning.

This is not merely snobbery. Difficulty can filter for knowledge and commitment. A hobby that requires effort creates a community of people who have invested effort. Reduce the effort and the activity becomes accessible to more people—a genuine gain—but the composition changes. Practices, norms, and status hierarchies shift.

Communities sometimes confuse the loss of exclusivity with the loss of quality. Companies sometimes confuse growth with universal acceptance. Both mistakes come from treating the population as fixed.

Growth recruits a new population.

The new population changes the product through demand. A software tool built for experts gains mainstream users who need simpler interfaces. A social network built for students gains parents, employers, politicians, brands, and journalists. The original users change behavior because the audience changed. The product did not merely acquire more users. It acquired new social consequences.

This is the context collapse problem at commercial scale.

An object that once signaled one thing to one group becomes visible to multiple audiences. A tattoo, sneaker, meme, phrase, neighborhood, or music genre can travel. Meaning fragments. Some participants celebrate diffusion; others police boundaries.

Brands become boundary managers whether they want the job or not.

Who is this for? Who is allowed to claim it? Which new customers expand the community and which make existing customers leave? How much should the company explain? What happens when the identity the brand built becomes politically charged or morally unfashionable?

These questions are hard because the brand’s own marketing changes the answers.

A company may try to broaden appeal by softening a subcultural identity. The move attracts new customers and alienates old ones. Management sees the new customers and concludes the broader identity was correct. The old customers’ departure disappears from surveys of current buyers. Selection creates apparent confirmation.

This is the consumer version of policy changing the population.

It also reveals why churn data needs narrative. A customer who leaves is not simply lost revenue. She may be evidence that the meaning of the product changed. If the company only studies retained customers, it can become increasingly confident in a strategy that is selecting the audience it prefers.

That may be acceptable. Brands need not serve everyone. The danger is confusing deliberate repositioning with neutral discovery of demand.

The market is being made, not merely measured.

Social media accelerates status cycles because signals become visible quickly. A previously local aesthetic can travel globally in days. Recommendation systems expose subcultures to mass audiences. Brands monitor the same feeds. Production follows. The distance between discovery and commercialization shrinks.

This can create opportunities for creators and communities. A small maker can reach buyers without traditional gatekeepers. A niche artist can find a global audience. A local style can generate income for people who originated it.

It can also compress the life of the niche. By the time a trend is named, optimized, manufactured, and sold back through mass channels, early participants may already be leaving.

The second move arrives before the first move feels finished.

Companies respond by trying to predict trends earlier. They analyze search, social posts, sales velocity, creator networks, and cultural signals. Predictive systems may identify emerging patterns before traditional research. Yet prediction itself can become intervention. A retailer bets on a trend, gives it shelf space and advertising, and thereby helps make it trend. A platform identifies a sound as rising and recommends it more, increasing its rise. A media outlet declares a neighborhood “the next” destination and sends visitors.

Forecasting culture can manufacture cultural evidence.

This is not proof that trends are fake. It means the distinction between discovering and amplifying becomes causal rather than semantic. A signal can be genuinely emerging and still owe part of its later scale to institutions that detected it.

The same loop operates in consumer research panels. Tell participants that a product is popular and social proof can alter evaluation. Display a bestseller badge and sales may increase. The badge is based on past sales and influences future sales. Ratings attract buyers who produce more ratings. Review counts become both information and momentum.

Reputation systems are positive-feedback machines with correction mechanisms attached.

They solve a real problem: strangers need signals of quality. But early outcomes can compound. A restaurant with strong early reviews gets more customers and therefore more chances to accumulate reviews. A comparable restaurant with weak early exposure may remain obscure. Platforms attempt to correct for fraud, recency, sample size, and bias, but the underlying path dependence remains.

Marketing’s deepest second-order question is therefore not “What does the customer want?”

It is “What kind of wanting does this market teach?”

Discount culture teaches waiting. Luxury scarcity teaches attention to drops. Subscription teaches habitual use. Influencer marketing teaches consumers to interpret friendship-like recommendation commercially. Reviews teach comparison. Return policies teach experimentation. Fast fashion teaches rapid wardrobe turnover. Repairable products teach maintenance. Every commercial system shapes routines that later appear as preferences.

The moral direction varies. Markets can teach thrift, waste, patience, status anxiety, craft, convenience, repair, novelty, loyalty, skepticism, generosity, and addiction. Most firms do not set out to teach character. They build incentives and interfaces. Character emerges downstream.

That is why consumer psychology cannot stop at persuasion.

Persuasion asks how to change a decision. A second-order consumer psychology asks how repeated persuasion changes the decision-maker.

A person who has seen ten thousand urgency banners learns a relationship to urgency. A person who receives endless personalized recommendations learns a relationship to search. A person who can return almost anything learns a relationship to commitment. A person who buys objects partly for online display learns a relationship to possession.

None of these changes is total or uniform. Humans are too stubborn and diverse for that. But markets alter the ecology of habit.

Brands that understand this have a longer strategic horizon. They know a tactic can work while degrading the category. They know scale can change meaning. They know authenticity signals decay when industrialized. They know customers model the company back.

The strongest brands often preserve some zone that is not constantly optimized for immediate conversion: a design discipline, a service promise, a product standard, a refusal to chase every trend. These constraints can look inefficient because they leave money on the table today.

They also give the customer something stable to model.

A costume changes with the audience.

A character is what remains recognizable after the audience learns the trick.
