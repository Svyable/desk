# A Canon Chasing Itself

Before feedback became a management word, it was a sound.

Stand between a microphone and a loudspeaker and move the microphone too close. The room suddenly discovers a voice of its own. A tiny signal enters the microphone, is amplified through the speaker, re-enters the microphone, is amplified again, and returns larger. The loop does not need intention. It needs a path back.

The shriek is useful because it strips away the romance. Feedback is not inherently wise. It is not “listening to customers” or “continuous improvement.” Feedback is simply output returning as input. Whether the result stabilizes, oscillates, explodes, or becomes music depends on the architecture of the loop.

Human beings learned to make art out of recurrence long before engineers formalized it. A canon begins with a line and lets another voice follow it. In the simplest round, the second voice repeats what the first voice has already sung while the first continues into new material. “Row, Row, Row Your Boat” is a child’s introduction to a deep structural pleasure: the present can harmonize with its own past. More elaborate canons invert, stretch, compress, reverse, or displace a musical line. Johann Sebastian Bach used canonic devices with a kind of practical audacity. The technique can feel like a puzzle, but a successful canon is not impressive merely because a rule closes on itself. It has to sound like music while obeying the rule.

That distinction matters outside music. A recursive system can be formally elegant and socially awful.

Consider a hiring algorithm trained on successful employees. The organization defines success using promotions and performance ratings produced by the organization’s existing managers. The model learns patterns associated with those outcomes. It then helps select new employees. Managers evaluate the new employees, perhaps with growing trust in the model’s screening. A future model is trained on this newer population. The system contains recurrence: previous institutional judgments shape new selections, which shape future judgments, which become future data. There is no guarantee the loop converges toward talent. It may converge toward whatever the institution already knows how to recognize.

The canon is chasing itself. Whether we hear harmony depends on what entered the first voice and what transformations occur on the return.

Mathematics gives us cleaner examples. A recursive definition uses earlier outputs to construct later ones. The Fibonacci sequence begins with starting values and produces each next term from previous terms. Iterated functions take a value, apply a rule, feed the result back into the same rule, and repeat. Simple rules can produce fixed points, cycles, or chaotic behavior. The fascination is not that recursion is mysterious. It is that repetition under transformation can generate structure far richer than the rule’s apparent simplicity.

Social recursion adds another ingredient: interpretation.

A thermostat does not care why the room is cold. It reads temperature and switches the furnace according to a rule. People reading a signal ask what it means, who produced it, what others will do, and how they themselves are being seen. A falling price can indicate a bargain, a warning, a margin call, or a rumor about somebody else’s information. An employee survey can be an invitation to honesty or a trap, depending on the institution’s history. A school ranking can be a description of performance or a forecast of which families will move where, which will itself alter performance. The same signal can change function as participants learn its social meaning.

This is where the formal beauty of recursion becomes dangerous as metaphor. The social scientist may be tempted to point at a spiral, a feedback diagram, or an equation and declare the human system explained. The diagram can show a return path. It cannot, by itself, tell us what people believe the returning signal signifies.

The difference is visible in one of the twentieth century’s most celebrated encounters with self-reference: mathematical logic.

At the beginning of that century, ambitious mathematicians sought rigorous foundations from which mathematical truths could be derived. The aspiration was not foolish. Formalization had delivered extraordinary power. But self-reference was already lurking in old paradoxes: statements about statements, sets that seem to contain or exclude themselves, definitions that fold back on their own conditions. In 1931 Kurt Gödel published his incompleteness results. Very roughly—and “roughly” matters here—he showed that sufficiently expressive, consistent formal systems capable of arithmetic contain true statements that cannot be proved within the system itself. His method encoded statements about provability in arithmetic, allowing the system to express claims with a self-referential structure.

The result did not prove that truth is subjective, that mathematics is broken, or that every system must contain a contradiction. Those are popular distortions. It established precise limits under precise assumptions. Yet the cultural appeal of Gödel’s work comes partly from the shock of the return path. A formal system that seemed to speak about numbers could be made, through encoding, to speak about statements in the system. The language turned inward.

Artists were drawn to similar loops for different reasons. A hand draws a hand that appears to draw the first hand. A gallery contains an image that curls back toward the gallery. A staircase seems to rise while returning to its beginning. The visual pleasure comes from violating the viewer’s expectation that representation sits safely outside what it represents. The picture catches its own frame.

The social world catches its frame constantly.

Opinion polls are published to the electorate they describe. Economic forecasts are read by firms whose investment decisions make the forecast more or less accurate. Bestseller lists influence what people buy, which determines the next bestseller list. University rankings influence applications, donations, hiring, and selectivity, which influence future rankings. Credit ratings affect borrowing costs, which affect the probability of default the ratings were meant to assess. A model of the system is released into the system.

The key fact is not that these processes are circular. Circles can be stable. The key is that the return carries force.

Engineers distinguish negative feedback from positive feedback. Negative feedback tends to counter deviations: the thermostat senses that a room is below its set point and adds heat, then stops adding heat as the room approaches the target. Positive feedback reinforces deviations: the microphone howl grows because amplified output returns as larger input. The labels are technical, not moral. Negative feedback can be oppressive if it rigidly suppresses valuable variation. Positive feedback can be productive if it helps a small innovation spread.

Human systems often mix both at once.

A market price contains negative feedback because high prices can attract supply and reduce demand, pushing price down. It can also contain positive feedback because rising prices can attract buyers who infer momentum, pushing price higher. A reputation system contains negative feedback because bad service can reduce future business. It can contain positive feedback because early ratings attract more customers, generating more ratings, making the initial advantage durable. A scientific citation network can help good work become visible and can also compound prestige independent of current quality. The same institution can stabilize along one dimension while amplifying another.

This mixed structure is why slogans about feedback are poor guides. “Create a feedback loop” is like saying “connect the microphone to the speaker.” Fine. At what gain? With what delay? What noise? What incentives? What information is lost in the sensor? Who controls the set point? Can participants manipulate the signal? Does the loop punish deviations faster than it learns from them?

Delay deserves special attention. Many disastrous loops are not disastrous because feedback is absent but because it arrives late.

A retailer raises prices. Customers gradually shift habits. The immediate margin improvement looks excellent; the loyalty damage arrives quarters later. A company cuts maintenance. Costs fall this year; failure rates rise later. A government suppresses an inconvenient indicator; the underlying problem compounds until the indicator can no longer be managed. An employee learns to optimize a quarterly target at the cost of relationships that determine next year’s sales. The system feeds back, but not on the decision-maker’s tenure.

The delay changes character as well as calculation. When consequences arrive after bonuses, elections, promotions, or exits, people may rationally behave as though the return path does not exist. Institutions then become machines for distributing causes and effects across different people.

There is a moral temptation here too: blame short-termism. Sometimes that is correct. But short horizons are often embedded in contracts. A fund manager facing monthly withdrawals cannot simply become a philosopher of twenty-year value. A public company with debt covenants cannot ignore near-term cash. A politician in a democracy should care about elections. The problem is not that humans have horizons. It is that feedback occurring outside the decision horizon is easily treated as somebody else’s problem.

Music handles delay with discipline. In a canon, the distance between entries is not an inconvenience. It is the composition. If the second voice enters at the wrong interval, harmony changes. In social systems, the equivalent timing parameter is rarely written on the score. The delay between hiring and knowing whether a hire was good may be years. The delay between a monetary policy shift and broad economic effects is variable. The delay between a platform design change and cultural adaptation may be months, while the engagement metric responds in seconds. Fast signals can crowd out slow truths.

That asymmetry explains much of modern optimization. We optimize what answers quickly.

Click-through rates answer quickly. Brand trust answers slowly. Daily active users answer quickly. A generation’s relationship with attention answers slowly. Quarterly revenue answers quickly. Organizational capability answers slowly. Test scores answer on schedule. Curiosity does not. A risk model reports today. Fragility reveals itself when conditions change.

The first move is drawn toward fast feedback because fast feedback feels scientific. Change the button, measure the clicks. Change the message, measure conversion. Change the quota, measure calls. The procedure resembles an experiment and may genuinely identify a local causal effect. The trouble begins when the fast variable is treated as a sufficient description of the system.

A musician would never say the first note is louder, therefore the piece is better.

Yet institutions make analogous errors because they need decisions before the slow effects arrive. The solution cannot be to wait forever. It is to build multiple tempos of evidence. A product team can run short experiments while tracking longer-term retention and complaint patterns. A school can use tests while examining student work, later outcomes, and evidence of curricular narrowing. A bank can use daily risk measures while running stress tests designed around conditions outside recent data. A manager can watch output while noticing whether employees are learning, hiding problems, or burning social capital.

Multiple tempos reduce the chance that one fast loop captures the organization.

There is another lesson in the canon: independent voices matter.

If every voice simply doubles the first at the same pitch and moment, the result is louder but not structurally richer. Counterpoint gains interest because lines retain some independence while relating to one another. Institutions need an equivalent. If every risk system ingests the same market prices, uses similar assumptions, and triggers similar actions, the appearance of many models can conceal one shared voice. If every newsroom watches the same social trends, every brand uses the same analytics vendor, every hiring team purchases the same assessment, and every investor benchmarks to the same index, organizational plurality can collapse into synchronized response.

Synchronized response is often efficient in normal times. It becomes dangerous when the common signal is wrong.

This is why redundancy and diversity are not synonyms. Ten identical sensors are redundant. Ten different ways of knowing may be diverse. Redundancy protects against component failure. Diversity can protect against model failure. The difference becomes critical in second-order systems because a widely shared model does more than create correlated errors. It can create correlated actions, and correlated actions change the environment.

If every bank responds to a risk threshold by selling the same class of asset, the risk threshold participates in the price decline. If every college optimizes the same ranking variables, the ranking reshapes higher education. If every creator copies the same retention tactics, those tactics reshape what viewers expect from video. If every applicant learns the same interview performance, companies select for mastery of the performance.

The echo becomes the room.

We should be careful not to romanticize independence. Standards exist because coordination is valuable. A common accounting language makes firms comparable. A shared technical protocol lets networks interoperate. A musical ensemble needs tuning. The challenge is to distinguish domains where sameness enables cooperation from domains where sameness creates a common failure mode.

This can be surprisingly subtle. Standardized financial reporting helps investors compare companies, but if management compensation becomes tightly tied to a standardized metric, the metric may attract manipulation. Standardized tests can reveal inequalities across schools, but if stakes become high enough, instruction may narrow around the tested form. Standardized safety procedures reduce avoidable variance, but a culture that punishes deviations can also suppress reports of unusual hazards. The same standard can be infrastructure at one layer and a target at another.

The concept of gain helps.

In engineering, gain describes how strongly a system amplifies a signal. Social systems have informal gain too. A ranking that nobody reads has low gain. Put scholarships, jobs, budgets, prestige, and headlines behind it and the gain rises. A customer rating that merely informs a restaurant is one thing. A rating that determines search position, revenue, worker compensation, and continued access to the platform is another. The number did not change. Its causal leverage did.

High-gain signals deserve more skepticism precisely because people have more reason to adapt to them.

This yields a principle that will recur throughout the book: the more consequential a representation becomes, the less safe it is to assume the represented behavior will remain unchanged.

There are exceptions. Some measures track constraints that are hard to game: a bridge still has a load limit; a bank still needs liquidity; a patient still has blood chemistry. Even then, the process around the measure can change. A hospital can alter which patients enter a metric. A bank can move exposures off the balance sheet. An engineer can meet a test condition that does not represent field use. Material reality disciplines the game eventually, but the game can persist for a long time before the bill arrives.

The most mature response is not suspicion of all measures. It is architectural humility.

Ask what returns. Ask how loudly. Ask how fast. Ask through whose interpretation. Ask whether many independent voices remain. Ask whether the system can hear slow consequences. Ask whether the rule rewards truthful information or cosmetic compliance. Ask whether success itself will change the behavior being modeled.

A canon is beautiful because the composer knows the line is coming back.

Most institutions are less fortunate. They write the first phrase, hear something familiar several measures later, and call it evidence that they were right.

The wiser possibility is to recognize our own melody in the answer.
