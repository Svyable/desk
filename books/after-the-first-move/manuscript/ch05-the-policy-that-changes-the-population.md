# The Policy That Changes the Population

The easiest way to make an old relationship fail is to build policy as though the relationship were a law of nature.

Suppose an economist studies years of data and finds a stable connection between two variables. When one moves, the other tends to move in a predictable way. The relationship may be useful for forecasting. It becomes more dangerous when the government uses it as an operating rule. People are not raindrops. Households and firms can learn the rule, form expectations about future policy, and alter behavior before the next observation arrives.

Robert Lucas made this problem famous in economics in 1976. His critique was technical and specific, aimed at econometric policy evaluation. But the intuition travels well. A statistical relationship estimated under one policy regime may change when the regime changes because people’s decision rules depend partly on the policy environment. You cannot safely evaluate a new rule by assuming the behavioral relationships measured under the old rule will remain fixed.

The population under policy is not necessarily the population in the historical data.

This seems obvious when stated in ordinary language. Change mortgage rules and borrowing behavior changes. Change taxes and firms reorganize transactions. Announce that a central bank will respond aggressively to inflation and wage bargaining, pricing, asset allocation, and borrowing can change in anticipation. Introduce a generous subsidy and companies enter the subsidized activity. Remove the subsidy and some leave. The policy is not pushing against a fixed behavioral surface. It is participating in the creation of the surface.

Yet organizations routinely make the Lucas mistake without using equations.

A company observes that employees stay an average of four years. It designs training economics around four-year tenure. Then it changes compensation, promotion, remote-work rules, or the employment contract in ways that alter tenure. The original average becomes less informative because the policy changed the population’s incentives.

A city observes traffic patterns and adds capacity where congestion is highest. Drivers change routes, departure times, and residential choices. The new road can generate travel that was absent from historical traffic counts. The intervention changes the demand it was meant to serve.

A university sees that students admitted with a certain credential succeed and makes the credential more important. Applicants invest more heavily in obtaining it. Coaching markets expand. The credential’s composition changes. It may still predict success, but part of what it now measures is the ability to navigate the new admissions regime.

A platform sees that users who receive certain notifications return more often and increases notifications. Users change notification settings, habits, expectations, and perhaps tolerance. A dose-response curve measured at low intensity need not survive saturation.

The general error is to treat behavior as a parameter when it is partly a strategy.

This distinction is easiest to see in games. If a basketball team studies an opponent and discovers that a particular defender tends to overcommit to the left, the team designs an attack. Once used repeatedly, the attack reveals information. The defender and coach adjust. The historical tendency may disappear. A scouting report is useful precisely because it changes play; that same usefulness can destroy its predictive edge.

Policy is often a game in this broad sense, even when nobody is an enemy. People have objectives, constraints, information, and expectations. A rule changes the payoff landscape. Participants adapt.

The word “adapt” should not be heard as “evade.” Most policy responses are legitimate. A tax credit for investment is intended to increase investment. A carbon price is intended to change energy choices. A congestion charge is intended to change driving. A safety standard is intended to change production. If nobody responded, the policy would often have failed.

The problem is that the intended response is rarely the only response.

Every intervention creates a neighborhood of adjacent incentives. A subsidy changes relative prices among technologies, organizational forms, and timing choices. A licensing rule changes the value of credentials and the number of people willing to obtain them. A capital requirement changes the relative attractiveness of assets and financing structures. A procurement rule changes which firms can profitably bid. Participants search the new landscape because that is what economic agents do.

The policymaker then sees behavior that did not exist before the policy and faces a choice: treat it as noise, treat it as abuse, or learn that the rule created a new ecology.

The third choice is hardest because it undermines the comforting distinction between policy and response. Governments often speak as though rules act on society from outside. In reality, rules become institutions only when people incorporate them into plans.

This is why credible policy can work before implementation.

If businesses believe a tax regime will change next year, investment may move this year. If markets believe a central bank will react to inflation, long-term yields can change before the policy rate moves. If households believe a subsidy will expire, purchases may be pulled forward. If firms believe a regulation will tighten, product design and lobbying begin early. The anticipated rule becomes a present variable.

Expectation is not epiphenomenal. It is part of transmission.

This can create a strange political problem. A policymaker announces a measure, behavior changes, and the realized effect differs from the forecast based on historical behavior. Critics say the forecast was wrong. It may have been. But it may also have been based on a world in which the announced measure did not yet exist.

A good model must therefore distinguish conditional prediction from unconditional prophecy. “If policy remains X, behavior is likely to follow Y” is not the same as “under a newly announced policy Z, behavior will still follow Y.” The policy regime is not scenery.

Economics has developed sophisticated methods around this insight, but the organizational lesson is simpler: before deploying a rule, ask which behavioral relationships in your model depend on people not expecting the rule.

This question is especially important for one-time incentives.

A retailer sees that a coupon reactivates dormant customers. It sends coupons repeatedly. Customers learn to wait for coupons. The reactivation effect changes because the offer changes expectations about future prices. A sales commission accelerates bookings near quarter-end. Over time customers learn that quarter-end is a good moment to negotiate. The company’s internal calendar leaks into the market.

Temporary actions can become anticipated regimes.

Once participants anticipate them, removing the action can have effects that were absent during introduction. A market trained to expect emergency support may react strongly when support is withheld. Employees accustomed to discretionary bonuses may treat their absence as a pay cut. Customers trained by constant promotions may interpret full price as temporary. The baseline itself has moved.

This is the ratchet problem of expectations. Institutions create reference points through repetition.

Behavioral economics gives this idea psychological texture. People evaluate outcomes relative to reference points, norms, and expectations rather than only absolute levels. A benefit introduced as a surprise can become an entitlement in experience. A fast delivery time can become normal. A high service level can become invisible until it disappears. The first move is judged against the old world. The second move is judged against the world the first move created.

Managers encounter this constantly when trying to motivate teams.

Offer an extraordinary reward for an extraordinary push and the reward may work. Repeat it every quarter and it becomes part of expected compensation. Publicly praise people for staying late and late work can become a status signal. Introduce a wellbeing policy while promotions still favor visible overwork and employees learn which signal is cheap and which is expensive. The explicit policy and the inferred policy diverge.

People respond more to the regime they infer than the memo they receive.

This is why culture is a policy environment. Every repeated managerial decision teaches employees a response function. Who gets promoted after a failure? What happens to the person who reports bad news early? Does an executive actually cancel a project after new evidence, or merely say that learning matters? Are customer complaints escalated or hidden? Formal values are one signal among many. Behavior under pressure is the more credible announcement.

The organization then hires and retains people who fit the inferred regime. Over time, policy changes population composition as well as individual behavior.

This is a deeper version of the Lucas problem. A firm that rewards aggressive internal competition does not merely cause current employees to compete more. It may attract people who like competition and repel people who do not. A school culture centered on a narrow form of achievement may select students and teachers aligned with it. A platform’s moderation style may influence which communities remain. The policy changes who is present.

Once composition changes, leaders may mistake the new population for evidence that the policy was always appropriate.

“We are a competitive culture because our people are competitive.” Perhaps. But years of selection may have made the statement true. The institution has endogenized its own anthropology.

This is one reason organizational turnarounds are difficult. A new leader changes a rule expecting the existing workforce to behave differently, but the workforce was partly selected by the old rule. Some employees cannot or do not want to make the change. New hiring can shift the population, but that takes time. The old culture is stored in people, routines, reputations, and networks.

Policy has memory.

Governments face the same persistence at larger scale. Tax systems create professions and software. Regulatory regimes create compliance departments, legal specialties, business models, and political constituencies. Agricultural supports shape land values and financing. Housing rules shape where people live and what gets built. Health-payment systems shape organizations that become skilled at operating under particular reimbursement categories. Changing the rule does not reset the population to a neutral state. The old regime has capitalized itself into assets and capabilities.

This makes reform path-dependent.

A designer cannot simply ask, “What is the best rule?” The practical question is, “What transition from this rule to that rule changes expectations and investments in a tolerable way?” Two countries can adopt the same formal policy and see different outcomes because they arrive with different institutions, histories, enforcement credibility, and populations of organizations built under previous rules.

The world contains fewer clean policy experiments than policy arguments pretend.

Still, the Lucas insight should not become fatalism. If every rule changes behavior, policy is not impossible; policy is powerful. The point is to model response explicitly and revise when the response changes.

Several habits help.

First, separate structural constraints from regime-dependent behavior. A household budget constraint is not the same kind of object as a historical propensity to refinance. A physical production limit is not the same as a staffing convention. The more a relationship depends on expectations and incentives, the more cautiously it should be transported across regimes.

Second, look for anticipation. If participants know the policy is coming, pre-implementation data may already be contaminated by response. The treatment can begin at announcement, not at legal effective date.

Third, look for selection. Who enters, exits, or changes category under the new rule? A response observed among survivors may not represent the original population.

Fourth, distinguish transition from steady state. A policy can look effective during adoption because behavior is adjusting, then settle into a different long-run pattern. Or the reverse: implementation can be costly while long-run benefits accrue later.

Fifth, keep counterfactual humility. If the policy changes the system, there may be no historical period that perfectly represents what would have happened under the alternative. Evidence can still be strong, but claims should match identification.

Finally, watch for the institution learning the wrong lesson from success. A policy that works once may depend on surprise. A stimulus delivered in an unusual crisis may not have the same effect when anticipated. A retention bonus that prevents departures during one merger may create expectations if repeated. A product launch tactic that exploits a temporary channel advantage may become ineffective after competitors copy it.

Success under one regime is evidence, not a universal constant.

There is an almost comic business version of this. A consultant studies the highest-performing companies, identifies a common practice, and recommends the practice widely. Firms adopt it. If the practice was genuinely causal and scalable, average performance may improve. But the practice may stop differentiating anyone because everybody now does it. If the original advantage depended on rarity, the advice destroys the advantage by succeeding.

Management fashion is policy diffusion with better typography.

This does not make imitation irrational. Many best practices are simply better practices. Standard accounting, quality control, inventory methods, software deployment routines, and safety procedures can spread without losing their value. The key is to ask whether the mechanism is absolute or positional.

An absolute improvement creates value regardless of how many others adopt it. A positional advantage depends on being ahead, scarce, distinctive, or unexpected. Faster internal communication may benefit every firm. A marketing channel may lose value as every firm floods it. Better fraud detection may help many banks, while a trading strategy may disappear under imitation. The first move teaches the field differently depending on the source of advantage.

Policy designers, strategists, and managers need to know which kind they are dealing with.

The Lucas critique is often taught as a warning about macroeconomic models. Its broader moral is about respect for agency. People are not coefficients waiting to be pushed. They form expectations about the pushes. Organizations invest around rules. Markets price anticipated interventions. Employees infer culture from consequences. Citizens learn enforcement patterns. Customers learn promotion schedules.

The policy that ignores this will still change the population.

It will simply be surprised by the population it creates.
