# The Probability of Being Watched

Risk changes when people know how risk is being measured.

This sounds like a specialized problem for banks, insurers, or statisticians. It is also a problem for anyone who has ever slowed down after seeing a police car, spoken differently when a recorder came on, prepared harder because an inspection was possible, or behaved more carefully when an experimenter entered the room.

The probability of observation is itself an input to behavior.

Enforcement systems rely on this. A regulator does not need to inspect every transaction if firms believe a meaningful sample will be inspected. Tax systems do not audit every return. Safety agencies do not stand inside every plant. Quality departments do not examine every unit. Random checks can influence the whole population because people act under uncertainty about whether this case will be observed.

The system governs through a probability distribution.

That distribution can be more powerful than constant surveillance because constant surveillance produces a stable environment that participants learn in detail. Randomness preserves uncertainty.

The idea appears in security, economics, ecology, and games. Mixed strategies matter when predictability creates vulnerability. If a goalkeeper always dives the same direction, a penalty taker adapts. If an inspector always arrives on Tuesday, Tuesday becomes theater. If a platform reviews only accounts that cross an obvious threshold, behavior accumulates just below the threshold.

Predictability makes the boundary valuable.

Randomization can protect the boundary from becoming the whole game.

This does not mean opacity is always good. People deserve understandable rules, due process, and the ability to know what conduct is expected. Random enforcement without clear standards can become arbitrary power.

The useful distinction is between uncertainty about standards and uncertainty about sampling.

A factory should know the safety requirement. It need not know which unit will be audited. A trader should know the law. She need not know which transaction a regulator will review. A student should know the learning objectives. He need not know the exact future test items.

Clear rule, uncertain observation.

This architecture can preserve information because it reduces the return to optimizing one visible checkpoint.

Probability also matters because second-order systems rarely offer certainty. A manager cannot know exactly how employees will respond to a new incentive. A state cannot know whether a sanction will change policy or harden resistance. An investor cannot know whether a crowded trade will unwind next month. A platform cannot know the long-run cultural effect of a ranking change.

The demand for certainty creates its own pathology.

Leaders ask analysts for a number. Analysts compress a distribution into a point estimate. The estimate travels upward without its assumptions. A 60 percent probability becomes “likely.” A scenario becomes forecast. A forecast becomes plan. The plan allocates resources. The allocation changes the probability.

The number hardens as it moves through the hierarchy.

This is partly a communication problem and partly an incentive problem. Executives need decisions, not lectures on every possible world. Analysts who constantly hedge can appear unhelpful. But false precision is especially dangerous in adaptive systems because the intervention itself changes the distribution.

A probability is conditional on a regime.

“What is the probability customers leave?” depends on what prices, service, competitors, and policies do. “What is the probability of default?” depends on credit terms, economic conditions, and lender behavior. “What is the probability this employee quits?” depends on whether the company acts on the prediction by offering a raise, changing work, or excluding the employee from future plans.

Once the prediction triggers treatment, it becomes an intervention policy.

This is the same distinction medicine makes between prognosis and treatment effect. A patient can have high risk under one course of action and much lower risk under another. The clinically relevant question is not only who is at risk but what changes risk.

Businesses often confuse the two.

A churn model identifies customers likely to leave. The company targets them with retention offers. Some stay. The company retrains on outcomes. If it does not record treatment carefully, the model may learn from a world in which high-risk customers received extra attention. The relationship between features and churn has been altered by the company’s own response.

The prediction changed the labels.

This is why causal inference and experimentation belong beside machine learning. Prediction asks what is likely. Causal reasoning asks what happens if we do something. In adaptive systems, the second question often matters more.

The distinction is ancient in practical judgment. A farmer can predict poor yield and also ask whether irrigation changes it. A general can predict defeat under the current plan and change the plan. A manager can predict turnover and change the job. The point of foresight is often to make the forecast false.

A forecast worth acting on should not be embarrassed by being defeated through action.

This sounds obvious, yet organizations sometimes evaluate forecasts as though action were irrelevant. A risk team warns about a failure, leadership intervenes, the failure does not occur, and later someone says the risk team overreacted. The better evaluation asks whether the intervention was justified by the information available and whether the causal mechanism plausibly reduced risk.

Prevention produces missing evidence.

This creates a political economy around rare events. Preparedness budgets are easy to cut because unused capacity looks unproductive. Risk officers can become unpopular because success is uneventful. Security teams make friction visible and attacks invisible. Maintenance interrupts work to prevent breakdowns that will never be observed if maintenance succeeds.

The organization sees the cost and not the counterfactual benefit.

Probability is how we reason about those invisible futures, but humans are not naturally comfortable with it. We overweight vivid examples, neglect base rates, seek patterns in noise, and change judgment depending on framing. Experts are not immune. Institutions can improve by making probabilistic reasoning a process rather than a personality trait.

One useful habit is calibration. A person or model that assigns 70 percent probabilities should, across many comparable cases, be right roughly seven times in ten. Calibration turns confidence into something testable over repeated forecasts.

But calibration itself can be distorted by adaptive action. If a high probability triggers intervention, realized frequency falls. The forecaster looks underconfident even if the forecast correctly represented risk before treatment.

So forecasts need treatment labels.

Was action taken because of the forecast? Did the environment change? Was the forecast conditional on no intervention? These details belong in evaluation.

Another habit is scenario thinking. Scenarios are sometimes mocked because they can become elaborate stories with no probabilities or decisions attached. Used well, they force an organization to inhabit more than one future before committing capital.

The second-order version asks how actors respond inside each scenario.

Suppose a country restricts a critical export. The first-order scenario estimates lost supply. The second-order scenario includes stockpiling, substitution, smuggling, allied coordination, domestic investment, retaliation, and demand destruction. The point is not to predict the exact sequence. It is to avoid freezing everyone else while our protagonist acts.

The same method improves competitive strategy. If we cut price, does the competitor match? If we enter the segment, do they bundle? If we recruit their employees, do they change compensation? If we open the platform, do developers build complements or clones?

A scenario without other actors’ responses is a photograph of a chessboard.

Probability also interacts with character because action under uncertainty reveals tolerance for regret.

A leader deciding whether to evacuate before a storm faces asymmetric errors. Evacuating unnecessarily has costs. Failing to evacuate when needed can be catastrophic. The relevant threshold depends on consequences, not merely which outcome is more likely.

This is decision theory’s practical contribution. Probability alone does not tell us what to do. Loss matters.

A one-percent chance of minor inconvenience is different from a one-percent chance of ruin.

Organizations often ignore this by optimizing expected values without considering tail constraints. An investment with positive expected return can still be unacceptable if it creates a meaningful chance of insolvency. A technology with large expected benefit may require safeguards if a low-probability failure is irreversible.

Risk is probability times consequence only in simplified textbook form. Real decisions involve distributions, uncertainty about the model, correlation, irreversibility, learning, and the option to wait.

Second-order systems add strategic response.

A low-probability event can become more likely because others prepare badly. A safety system can reduce event probability and then be weakened because the event becomes rare. A public warning can increase or decrease probability by changing behavior. Tail risk is socially endogenous.

This is why resilience often deserves a different objective from prediction.

Prediction asks which event will happen. Resilience asks whether we can survive several plausible events.

A company may not know which supplier will fail. It can design substitute capacity. A bank may not know the next crisis trigger. It can hold capital and liquidity. A household may not know which emergency will occur. It can keep savings. A state may not know the exact disaster. It can maintain adaptable institutions.

Robustness buys freedom from prediction.

This can be economically rational when the model is uncertain or adversaries adapt. Instead of guessing the single most likely attack and defending only that path, build systems that fail gracefully. Instead of optimizing a supply chain to the exact forecast, preserve options. Instead of hiring only for today’s tasks, build learning capacity.

The value is easiest to see when forecasts are fragile.

Complex systems often have fat tails, nonlinear responses, network effects, and regime changes. Historical averages can be poor guides to extremes. Yet “complexity” should not become a permission slip for vague doom. We can still measure, model, and compare interventions. The presence of uncertainty makes empirical discipline more important, not less.

A good second-order risk process distinguishes at least three kinds of uncertainty.

There is randomness within a reasonably understood model: the coin may land heads or tails.

There is parameter uncertainty: we do not know the coin’s bias precisely.

And there is model uncertainty: perhaps this is not a coin-flip problem at all.

Adaptive systems frequently live in the third category because behavior changes the structure.

A customer-response model can fail after competitors change. A geopolitical model can fail after an alliance shifts. A risk model can fail after market structure changes. A hiring model can fail after work becomes remote. A content model can fail after generative tools flood supply.

The model’s error is evidence about regime.

Institutions that punish forecast errors indiscriminately can miss this. Analysts learn to stay near consensus because consensus errors are safer. The organization becomes calibrated to social risk rather than reality.

This is one reason prediction markets and structured forecasting can be valuable: they can aggregate dispersed beliefs and reward accuracy. But they are not immune to second-order effects. Forecasts can influence attention, fundraising, policy, or behavior. Thin markets can be noisy. Participants share information sources. The mechanism matters.

No forecasting institution floats above society.

The deepest practical lesson is that probability should make us humbler without making us passive.

We can act under uncertainty, update, preserve options, and design for survivability. We can distinguish predictions we hope come true from warnings we hope action makes false. We can record interventions so later evaluation does not confuse treated outcomes with baseline risk.

And we can remember that the people we are predicting may be reading the prediction.

A probability is not just a number about the future.

In an adaptive system, it can become one of the causes.
