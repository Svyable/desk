# The Score Changes the Work

The first time an institution measures something, it may be learning about the work. The hundredth time careers and budgets depend on the measure, the institution is also learning about its own incentive system.

Modern organizations need numbers. A school district cannot visit every classroom every day. A hospital cannot govern millions of clinical decisions through anecdote. A chief executive cannot personally inspect every customer interaction. Governments need indicators to allocate money and evaluate programs. Investors need comparable accounts. Managers need signals that compress a large, noisy world into something they can act on.

The number is not the mistake.

The mistake is forgetting that people can see the number too.

Donald Campbell, the social psychologist and methodologist, warned in the 1970s that quantitative indicators used heavily for social decision-making become vulnerable to corruption pressures and can distort the processes they are intended to monitor. His point is often compressed into a slogan about measures and targets. The underlying mechanism is more useful. A measure changes the environment when it changes what consequences attach to behavior. Once that happens, the population producing the data is no longer indifferent to the data-generating process.

Consider a school test. In a low-stakes setting, the test samples what students know. Teachers have some reason to prepare students for the underlying subject because the score has limited consequences. Raise the stakes. Tie school ratings, teacher evaluations, funding, intervention, public reputation, and family choice to the result. Everyone now has stronger incentives to move the number.

Some responses are exactly what the system hopes for. Teachers may focus instruction, identify gaps, add tutoring, improve curriculum, or spend more time on material students should know. A rising score can represent real learning. High stakes do not automatically corrupt a measure.

Other responses improve measured performance more than underlying capability. Curriculum can narrow toward tested formats. Time can shift away from untested subjects. Students can become unusually practiced at the local grammar of the test. Administrators can concentrate resources where accountability thresholds make marginal gains most visible. The score may still rise, but the composition of the rise has changed.

From the outside, these responses can look similar.

This is why metric systems need theories of behavior, not just theories of measurement.

A statistician can tell you whether a test is reliable under certain conditions. An organizational designer must ask what happens when careers depend on it. A metric can be psychometrically respectable and institutionally fragile. The more consequential the score, the more energy a population will invest in discovering what the institution is really rewarding.

Sometimes the shortest path to the reward is genuine improvement. Sometimes it is presentation.

Sales organizations know the problem in miniature. A company wants more customer relationships and chooses calls as a proxy for effort. It sets a call target. Calls rise. The change may create more opportunities, but it may also shift effort away from conversations that take longer or prospects whose needs are harder to serve. Management then adds conversion, deal size, sales cycle, or retention. The dashboard grows because each measure captures one dimension of a job that was never one-dimensional.

At some point the organization has to admit what the dashboard cannot do: specify the work completely.

This is an old problem in contracts. A principal wants an agent to act in the principal’s interest, but the principal cannot observe everything that matters. Measurable outputs become proxies for the broader mission. The agent has more local information and can allocate effort among measured and unmeasured dimensions. When incentives are strong, effort migrates toward what counts.

The hidden variable is discretion.

If a task can be fully specified, automation or a detailed procedure may work. Many valuable jobs cannot be fully specified because circumstances vary and quality is multidimensional. A nurse can complete documentation while missing the frightened patient. A software engineer can close tickets while leaving a brittle system. A professor can raise publication counts while mentoring less. A call center can lower average handle time while making customers call twice.

The organization discovers that every proxy casts a shadow.

What makes this second-order rather than merely a story about incentives is the change in the data. The designer chooses a measure based on a historical relationship. Historically, high-performing salespeople made more calls. After the measure is tied to rewards, employees produce calls partly because calls are measured. The causal composition of the variable changes. The old correlation no longer means exactly what it meant before.

This is the practical form of a deeper statistical warning: data generated under one regime may not behave the same under another regime.

The effect tends to be strongest when three conditions coincide. The measure is consequential. Participants can learn what influences it. The underlying objective is harder to observe than the proxy. Consequences provide motive, learnability provides direction, and limited observability creates room for drift.

Remove any one and distortion becomes less likely. A metric nobody cares about attracts little strategic attention. A metric based on a process participants cannot influence may remain informative. An organization that can directly observe the underlying objective can compare the proxy with reality and correct drift.

This helps explain why “choose better metrics” is necessary but incomplete. A better metric matters, but every measure placed inside an incentive system should be treated as a moving part. The question is not only whether it correlates with the objective today. It is whether the relationship is likely to survive attention.

A good measure may be close to the actual outcome. Revenue collected is usually closer to business value than calls made, though even revenue can be borrowed from the future through discounts or poor contracts. It may be difficult to improve without doing the desired thing. It may be balanced by evidence that responds differently to pressure. Or it may be used diagnostically rather than mechanically, leaving room for judgment.

The last option makes many institutions nervous. Judgment sounds subjective, and subjectivity can conceal favoritism. Numbers promise fairness because the same rule can be applied to everyone.

That promise is real. Standardized measures often arose because unchecked discretion was worse. Civil service exams can reduce patronage. Transparent grading criteria can reduce arbitrary evaluation. Financial reporting standards can limit storytelling. A waiting-list rule can prevent a powerful official from quietly moving friends to the front. The alternative to a flawed metric is not pure wisdom. It may be a person with preferences, relationships, and no audit trail.

Second-order design therefore lives in a tension between legibility and discretion.

Too little legibility and power hides. Too much mechanical legibility and behavior collapses toward the visible target.

The mature institution uses both. It makes important criteria explicit, preserves multiple forms of evidence, audits for predictable distortions, allows exceptions with reasons, and examines the effects of the measure on the activity itself. It does not say, “Numbers are objective,” or “Humans should decide.” It asks which combination produces information that remains trustworthy under use.

Take medicine. Outcome measures can expose real differences in quality. But raw outcomes also reflect patient mix. If hospitals are punished mechanically for mortality without adequate adjustment, they may become reluctant to accept unusually difficult cases. Improve the adjustment model and new edge cases appear. Use process measures and clinicians may become excellent at documenting the process. Use patient satisfaction and providers face incentives that do not always align with clinical judgment. None of this means measurement should stop. It means a healthcare metric can change who gets treated, how cases are coded, and what clinicians attend to.

The measure belongs inside the clinical system it measures.

Or take scientific publishing. Citation counts can help identify influential work. Once citations become currency for hiring, grants, rankings, and status, scholars and journals respond to the citation ecology. Publication counts affect how projects are divided. Journal measures shape submission behavior. Researchers learn the system. Again, the result is not that citations become meaningless. It is that their meaning changes under pressure.

A number with social consequences develops a social life.

This is one reason ethnography can be valuable inside highly quantitative organizations. The metric says what changed. Observation can reveal how people made it change. Sit with a team after a new target is introduced and the difference becomes visible. People discuss which activities count. Veterans teach newcomers which fields in the system matter. Managers quietly signal which rules are central and which are ceremonial. Customers learn which channels receive attention. A formal process acquires an informal operating system.

The informal system is often where second-order adaptation lives.

Executives tend to receive cleaned data because the organization knows executives are watching. Frontline workers see the work required to produce the clean data. A dashboard may show that every mandatory check was completed. A close observation may reveal that the check is performed at the end of the shift as a memory exercise because the workflow made real-time completion impossible. The organization can achieve perfect formal compliance and weak information.

This is not necessarily deception. People improvise to keep systems moving. Metrics that ignore the local work required for compliance can create ritual rather than truth.

Ritual is an important category because it sits between genuine performance and deliberate manipulation. Many organizations are full of processes everyone knows are only loosely connected to their stated purpose: annual goal forms written after priorities have changed, training modules completed while attention is elsewhere, risk attestations treated as deadlines rather than investigations, meeting notes crafted for the archive rather than the decision.

The forms survive because they produce evidence of control.

Evidence of control is valuable to institutions under scrutiny. Regulators want records. Boards want assurance. Managers want proof that procedure occurred. The record can gradually displace the reality it was meant to evidence. A company becomes excellent at demonstrating that the policy exists.

Then a crisis arrives and asks whether the capability exists.

This gap between auditable form and lived function is one reason failures can surprise organizations that had green dashboards. The metrics were not necessarily false. They were measuring the production of compliance artifacts in a system that had adapted to produce compliance artifacts.

The usual answer is more inspection. Inspection can reveal performance under different conditions. But inspection also becomes part of the environment. Employees learn what reviewers attend to. Managers stage readiness around recurring concerns. Industries develop professional knowledge about audit expectations. Reviewers then change their methods because the reviewed population has changed its methods.

There is no final move.

That does not make accountability impossible. It changes the design objective from finding a perfect measure to maintaining an evidence system that can notice when measures are changing behavior.

Such a system has a few durable habits.

It keeps some measures low stakes so they remain diagnostic. Not every number needs to determine compensation. A measure can be useful precisely because nobody has a strong reason to beautify it.

It samples more broadly than the primary target. If a school is judged heavily on mathematics and reading, leaders still need to know what is happening in science, arts, attendance, student work, and classroom climate. If a company optimizes conversion, it should still examine returns, complaints, repeat usage, and customer narratives. Adjacent evidence reveals when the main indicator is separating from the mission.

It looks for divergence. Calls rise while sales remain flat. Test scores rise while untested work weakens. Cases close faster while repeat contacts rise. Defects fall while warranty claims increase. The point is not to create an infinite metric stack. It is to notice when one number no longer fits the rest of the evidence.

It asks the people being measured what the measure is doing to their work.

That question is less accusatory than asking who is manipulating the system and often more informative. Frontline workers know which tasks have expanded, which have become invisible, which trade-offs are harder, and which rituals appeared after the dashboard changed. Their testimony is not automatically correct, but it is data about the intervention.

It separates evaluation from learning when possible. If every experiment immediately affects performance reviews, honest reporting becomes expensive. Organizations need places where bad news can appear without instantly becoming a career event. Near misses, failed pilots, confusing cases, and counterexamples are forms of information that strong incentive systems can accidentally suppress.

And it preserves room for revision. A metric written into law, contracts, or compensation becomes politically costly to change. The longer it survives, the more constituencies organize around it. A temporary measure can become infrastructure. Scheduled review and explicit revalidation make adaptation less embarrassing.

The deeper issue is cultural.

People learn what an institution truly values by watching what receives consequences. A company can talk about craftsmanship while paying only for speed. A university can praise teaching while promoting on publication. A government can praise prevention while funding only visible crisis response. The metric is not just a measure. It is a lesson.

That lesson works in both directions. Employees adapt to what is measured, and leaders infer values from what employees optimize. Soon everyone participates in a culture nobody explicitly chose.

A call target becomes “how we work.” A ranking becomes “what excellence means.” A test becomes “what students need.” A compliance score becomes “whether we are safe.” The proxy migrates from accounting into identity.

At that point distortion becomes harder to see because it no longer feels like distortion. People sincerely pursue the metric as though it were the mission.

This is the most dangerous stage. Cynicism at least preserves knowledge that the proxy is a proxy. Cultural capture erases the distinction.

The antidote is not constant rebellion against measurement. It is repeated recontact with purpose. Why was this number chosen? What did it once predict? What behavior has emerged since it became consequential? What important work remains invisible? Which people bear costs the metric does not record? What would convince us to change it?

These questions feel soft only to organizations that have forgotten how hard it is to know what their numbers mean.

The number on the dashboard is a fact about the system.

Once people organize around it, it is also a fact produced by the system.

The difference is where management begins.
