# The Boss Is Being Managed

Every manager believes, at least on a difficult Tuesday, that the problem is getting people to do what the organization needs.

The employee has a second problem: figuring out what the manager really needs to see.

These are not the same problem.

Organizations are full of people managing upward, sideways, and inward. A project team frames progress for an executive review. A department decides which risks belong in the slide deck. A junior employee learns which kinds of disagreement are admired in theory and punished in practice. A plant manager learns which production issues can be fixed quietly and which will trigger a corporate intervention. A salesperson learns the difference between missing a number with an explanation the boss accepts and missing it with one the boss does not.

The formal hierarchy points down. The information hierarchy points both ways.

That is why management is a second-order system. Leaders issue goals, metrics, values, budgets, and questions. Employees do not merely obey or resist. They infer what the leader is actually evaluating and shape the information environment accordingly. The boss is trying to manage behavior while being managed through information about behavior.

This is not necessarily manipulation. It is ordinary organizational intelligence.

A competent employee learns how to communicate with a particular manager. One leader wants raw detail; another wants a recommendation. One panics if warned too early; another becomes angry if warned too late. One rewards independent judgment; another says she does while requiring prior alignment. People adapt because coordination requires adaptation.

The problem appears when information adaptation becomes reality distortion.

Imagine a chief executive who repeatedly asks why a major project is not green. The first few times, managers explain that the schedule contains uncertainty. The executive presses harder. Soon project leads learn that “yellow” produces long meetings and career risk. They break work into different categories, redefine milestones, delay recognition of dependencies, or become more optimistic in estimates. Nobody needs to falsify a spreadsheet. The organization simply becomes skilled at producing the kind of uncertainty the executive can tolerate.

The dashboard improves.

The project does not.

This pattern is sometimes blamed on cowardly middle management. That is convenient for the top. In reality, leaders train information systems through their responses to bad news.

If a manager says “tell me early” and then punishes the messenger, the organization updates. If a board says “we value candor” and visibly humiliates an executive who surfaces a weak quarter, future candor becomes expensive. If a safety culture rewards near-miss reporting but supervisors treat every report as evidence of individual incompetence, near misses decline on paper.

Information has an incentive structure.

This is one reason organizations become more surprised as they become more punitive about surprise.

The irony is profound. Leaders who hate surprises often create them by making early weak signals costly to report. Employees wait until evidence is undeniable. The problem becomes larger and more certain. Management then concludes that people are not escalating soon enough and adds an escalation policy.

The policy cannot repair the emotional economics of the meeting.

Culture lives in these response functions.

Corporate values are usually nouns: integrity, excellence, courage, customer, ownership. Employees need verbs. What happens when someone challenges a high-status person? What happens when a customer request threatens the quarter? What happens when an experiment fails? What happens when a manager’s forecast is wrong? The repeated answers teach culture more effectively than posters.

A culture is a learned model of consequences.

This explains why changing a slogan does little. Employees are Bayesians with memories, though not necessarily formal ones. They have priors about leadership and update on evidence. A new chief executive can announce openness on day one; trust rises only after people watch what happens to the first inconvenient truth.

The first inconvenient truth is the real launch event.

Leaders therefore face a signaling problem. They often want employees to believe something that cannot be made credible by cheap words alone. “Take smart risks.” “Challenge me.” “Put the customer first.” “Protect quality.” These messages are inexpensive to send and therefore weak evidence. Credibility requires costly action.

A leader who cancels a favored project after evidence turns against it sends a stronger signal than one who gives a speech about learning. A company that accepts a short-term revenue hit to protect a customer promise teaches more than a values workshop. A manager who thanks someone for surfacing a mistake before discussing accountability changes the expected payoff of disclosure.

Costly signals build culture because they reveal preference under pressure.

This is also why hypocrisy has such a high organizational multiplier. A leader may think one exception is small. Employees treat exceptions as data about the true rule. If an executive is exempted from an expense policy, the issue is not the expense. It is the revelation that rules map onto status. If a celebrated high performer is allowed to behave badly, employees infer that performance outranks stated values.

A single case can update hundreds of internal models.

The leader’s behavior has informational leverage.

That leverage makes symbolic actions more consequential than their direct economics. Closing an office, promoting an outsider, firing a founder’s friend, restoring a cut benefit, admitting a forecast error—each can become a story retold long after the accounting impact fades. Organizations remember through narrative.

Stories are compressed policy histories.

New employees hear that someone once challenged the chief executive and was promoted. Or that someone once raised a compliance concern and disappeared. The details may distort over time, but the story survives because it answers a practical question: what happens here?

Management literature sometimes treats storytelling as a tool leaders use on employees. Employees tell stories about leaders too.

The boss is being narrated.

This becomes especially visible during change programs. Headquarters announces a transformation. Consultants design new processes. Leaders hold town halls. Employees compare the new language with previous initiatives. If the organization has lived through multiple abandoned programs, the rational response may be patience. Why reorganize your identity around this transformation if the last three vanished after leadership changes?

Cynicism can be institutional memory wearing a bad expression.

A new leader may interpret delay as resistance and increase pressure. Employees interpret pressure as proof that the program is another temporary campaign. Both sides reinforce the other’s model.

The leader sees skeptics and becomes more controlling.

Employees see control and become more skeptical.

A second-order manager asks a harder question: what behavior of mine makes their behavior sensible?

This is not an invitation to self-blame. Employees can be political, lazy, territorial, mistaken, or resistant to necessary change. Power creates incentives on every side. The point is to include leadership behavior in the causal model rather than treating employee response as an exogenous trait.

The same logic applies to performance management.

A rating system intends to describe employee contribution. Employees learn what produces high ratings. Managers learn what rating distributions senior leadership expects. Human resources learns which patterns create legal or retention risk. Executives learn how the ratings map onto budgets. The final number emerges from a negotiation among multiple models of the system.

This is why performance scores often contain more organizational information than their labels admit.

A “meets expectations” rating may describe performance, calibration, manager generosity, department politics, promotion timing, budget constraints, and the company’s appetite for differentiation. Treat the number as pure output and downstream systems inherit all those hidden variables.

Then machine learning arrives and trains on historical ratings.

The model can become very good at reproducing the institution’s past judgments. That may be useful. It may also harden yesterday’s politics into tomorrow’s prediction.

Second-order management requires knowing when a dataset contains responses to previous management systems.

Employee engagement surveys are another example. A survey appears to measure culture. But employees decide how honestly to answer based on whether they trust anonymity, whether previous surveys led to action, whether managers discuss scores defensively, and whether low scores create work for the team. The survey measures attitudes toward the company and attitudes toward the survey process.

A low response rate is data.

So is a suspiciously perfect score.

The mature manager does not ask only, “What did people say?” but “What did participating in this survey mean to them?” If the answer is “nothing changes,” the measurement system has become a ritual. If the answer is “my manager will know,” the data has a fear component. If the answer is “this is our chance to secure resources,” the survey is partly a budget instrument.

Again, none of this makes surveys useless. It makes interpretation organizational.

The same principle explains why anonymous channels can help and fail. Anonymity can reduce fear and surface information. It can also remove context or invite irresponsible accusations. Named reporting can improve follow-up and accountability but increase personal risk. A good system offers multiple routes because no single information channel is robust to every incentive.

Plural channels create epistemic resilience.

Strong leaders often develop informal networks for the same reason. They walk floors, talk to customers, meet skip-level employees, read support tickets, listen to earnings calls from competitors, and maintain relationships outside the official chain. These practices are sometimes described as staying close to the business. Their deeper value is anti-filtering.

Every hierarchy compresses information.

Compression is necessary. A chief executive cannot read everything. The danger is that compression learns the executive’s preferences too well. By the time information reaches the top, it can become a polished reflection of what the top already believes.

Direct contact introduces noise of the useful kind.

But direct contact can create its own theater. When the chief executive visits a site, everything changes. Floors are cleaned. managers rehearse. Employees are selected. The leader’s attempt to bypass filtering becomes an event everyone filters around.

The observer enters the scene.

Good leaders counter this not through detective tricks but through repetition, unpredictability of topic rather than ambush, and relationships in which people have less reason to perform. One visit produces a tour. Fifty visits can produce familiarity.

Time lowers the gain of spectacle.

There is a parallel in research. Ethnographers spend time in a setting partly because participants become less focused on the observer. Complete invisibility is impossible and not always desirable. The goal is to understand how observation itself shapes what is observed and to build enough context to interpret the effect.

Managers are permanent participant-observers with authority.

That last word changes everything. An employee cannot treat a chief executive like a neutral anthropologist because the chief executive controls resources. The leader’s presence always has causal weight. The task is not to eliminate that weight but to account for it.

One useful practice is to separate inquiry from immediate decision. If every question from a leader triggers action, employees learn to protect the leader from raw information. “I’m trying to understand” must sometimes actually mean no instant reorganization.

Another is to ask for disconfirming cases before conclusions harden. What customer does not fit the segment? Which project succeeded under the opposite approach? What evidence would make us change the plan? The questions signal that contradiction has value.

A third is to inspect incentive gradients. What becomes easier or harder for an employee after this metric, meeting, or policy? What information becomes more costly to reveal?

A fourth is to watch stories. Which leadership episodes are being repeated? They often reveal the rules employees think govern the place.

And a fifth is to make revision visible. Leaders often fear that changing their mind looks weak. In adaptive systems, invisible revision teaches employees to protect certainty. Visible revision can teach that evidence has causal power.

This is not the same as indecision. A company needs commitments. People cannot work if strategy changes every afternoon. The art is to distinguish commitment to purpose from attachment to a particular belief.

Stable mission, revisable model.

That combination is rarer than it sounds. Many organizations do the reverse: mission changes with fashion while internal assumptions become sacred.

The boss’s most important second-order job is therefore not predicting every employee response. It is designing an environment in which responses remain informative.

If people can disagree without rehearsing a political performance, the leader learns. If metrics can be questioned without threatening their owners, the organization learns. If bad news can travel before it becomes undeniable, the system learns. If frontline adaptations are treated as evidence rather than insubordination, policy learns.

A leader who controls behavior but destroys information will eventually lose control of behavior too.

The hierarchy points down.

Reality comes back up.
