# Eight Watts

The famous number is almost comic: megawatts in, only a handful of watts radiated as ELF. It is often repeated as evidence that the system was absurd. It is better understood as a clue.

At 76 hertz, even an antenna tens of miles long is electrically tiny. Radiation resistance is punishingly low. Much of the energy goes into ordinary losses rather than useful radiation. Yet the small radiated component can still matter because ELF propagation over global distances is unusually favorable and because the receiver is designed for exactly that signal.

Military engineering is full of systems that look inefficient under the wrong denominator. The Navy did not need Project ELF to win an efficiency contest. It needed a coded signal to reach a submerged receiver without forcing the submarine into a more detectable posture.

Still, the number disciplines the mythology. ELF was not a hidden super-transmitter pouring mysterious energy across the continent. The actual system was, in radio terms, remarkably weak at radiating. Its power came from persistence, frequency, propagation, coding, and the strategic value of the few bits that survived. A whisper can matter if only one listener needs to hear it and the alternative is silence.
