# A Submarine Cannot Hear You

The useful submarine is missing. That is the first requirement. A ballistic-missile boat that can be followed is no longer a secure second-strike force; a boat that can be found becomes a target before it becomes a deterrent. The engineering problem begins precisely where the strategic advantage does. A submarine earns value by entering a medium that is unusually good at hiding it and unusually bad at carrying the radio traffic by which governments command military machines.

For surface forces the solution to distance is usually more power, a better antenna, a relay, a satellite. Underwater, the medium itself refuses the bargain. Conductive seawater attenuates ordinary radio rapidly. A submarine can raise an antenna, trail one nearer the surface, or come shallow enough to listen at frequencies that penetrate farther, but every such maneuver spends some of the concealment the boat was built to preserve.

The Cold War made this more than an inconvenience. A submerged strategic submarine had to remain hard to locate while still belonging to a national command system that could, at least in principle, reach it. Communication could not become a beacon announcing the submarine's position. Nor could the Navy simply install on the boat a shore transmitter of unlimited size and power. One side of the link could be continental. The other had to remain quiet.

That asymmetry produced Project Sanguine. The name sounds almost theatrical now. The engineering was not. Extremely low frequency radio promised a one-way path into seawater at depths ordinary systems could not match. It also imposed a wavelength so gigantic, and an antenna problem so unfriendly, that the Navy's solution began to include the landscape itself.

The book starts there: not with a secret machine, but with a physical refusal. The ocean would hide the submarine or permit easy communication. It would not generously provide both.
