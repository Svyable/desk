# Wisconsin Becomes Part of the Circuit

The Wisconsin test facility near Clam Lake made the abstraction visible. Long transmission lines ran through northern forest on ordinary-looking poles. The decisive part of the antenna was less ordinary: the conductors were grounded, and current entered the earth.

The location mattered because geology mattered. Dry, resistive rock helps force current paths deeper and broader rather than letting them close locally near the surface. Northern Wisconsin and Michigan sat over ancient crystalline terrain useful to the design. The landscape was not merely real estate beneath a radio station. Its electrical properties were part of the machine.

That fact altered the politics. A radar dish has a perimeter. A ground-dipole ELF system has a footprint whose meaning extends past the fence. Residents could reasonably ask what it meant for currents and fields associated with a strategic nuclear communications system to occupy a region otherwise understood as forest, farm, road, lake, and town.

This did not prove danger. It did something more politically important: it made exposure, interference, ecological effect, and consent legitimate questions that could not be answered by saying the transmitter was on Navy property. Once the earth is part of the antenna, the boundary of the installation becomes an argument.
