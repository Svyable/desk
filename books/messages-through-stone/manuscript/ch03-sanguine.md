# Sanguine

Project Sanguine began with ambition proportionate to the strategic problem. By the early 1970s the Navy was studying a system far larger than the experimental facility near Clam Lake, Wisconsin. Court records later summarized a planned grid covering thousands of square miles, with buried antenna cable and multiple transmitters. The scale was not decorative military excess. It followed from trying to radiate useful ELF energy with structures grotesquely short compared with the wavelength.

The name survived better than the design. Sanguine never reached full-scale development. But that failure should not be mistaken for unseriousness. The Navy moved through concept formulation and validation, environmental studies, interference work, biological experiments, ecological surveys, and site evaluation. Institutions do not spend years testing an idea merely because a spectacular diagram exists.

The interesting question is why the idea kept shrinking rather than disappearing. Part of the answer is strategic demand. A submerged ballistic-missile force remained valuable. Part is engineering: even a reduced ELF system could perform a narrower function. Part is politics. An antenna that crosses private land, national forest, county boundaries, watersheds, and political constituencies cannot remain a laboratory object.

Sanguine became a lesson before it became a system. The Navy could solve the physical problem only by making the apparatus geographically conspicuous. Secrecy could hide purpose for a time. It could not hide miles of infrastructure forever.
