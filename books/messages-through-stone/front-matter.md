# Messages Through Stone

## Project Sanguine and the Navy's Impossible Radio

Sven Hardy Benson

A submarine designed to disappear has a communications problem built into its success. Hide it deeper and ordinary radio stops reaching it. Ask it to surface and the command system begins to sabotage the reason the boat exists.

The United States Navy spent decades trying to work around that contradiction with radio waves so slow that a household electrical outlet oscillates hundreds of times faster. The resulting system was not science fiction. It was stranger: long transmission lines in Wisconsin and Michigan, currents deliberately driven through earth, granite chosen partly for electrical properties, a signal powerful enough strategically while radiating remarkably little power efficiently, and messages constrained by bandwidth so severe that the system became a summons rather than a conversation.

Project Sanguine is often remembered as an absurdity: a proposal to lace enormous stretches of the upper Midwest with antenna. That description is not wrong, but it misses why serious engineers kept returning to the idea. Physics had cornered them. The same constraints that made ELF communication useful made it enormous.

This book is about what happened when those constraints escaped the laboratory. Farmers, environmental scientists, judges, governors, senators, Navy engineers, submarine commanders, and biologists all became participants in a communications system because the antenna could not be made small enough to keep politics outside it.
