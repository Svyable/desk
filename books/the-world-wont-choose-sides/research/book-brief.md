# Book Brief

## Commercial proposition

A grand-strategy book for readers tired of maps that turn the world into Washington versus Beijing. The narrative travels through India, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Turkey, Indonesia, Brazil, and South Africa to explain how states convert geography, markets, capital, energy, institutions, and technology into bargaining power.

## Audience

Diplomats, national-security practitioners, international business leaders, investors, graduate students, and general readers of serious geopolitical trade nonfiction.

## Durable question

What does power look like for a state large enough to matter but unwilling or unable to dominate the system?

## Narrative method

Each country chapter begins with a decision where one relationship appears to conflict with another. The chapter reconstructs what the government needed, what alternatives existed, what dependencies constrained it, and what the outcome reveals about selective alignment.

## AI-era relevance

AI enters through the infrastructure of alignment: chips, cloud providers, data centers, export controls, energy, cables, technical standards, cyber capability, surveillance, and administrative capacity. The book does not treat AI as a separate geopolitical scoreboard.

## Target scale

18 chapters plus prologue and back matter. A complete first draft should exceed 65,000 manuscript words, with most chapters in the 3,500–4,500 word range.

## Teaching value

A Kennedy School seminar can assign country pairs and ask students to distinguish autonomy rhetoric from actual alternatives. Simulations can force a government to respond to a Taiwan contingency, sanctions request, technology restriction, or energy shock while preserving several relationships at once.

## Central tensions

- autonomy versus dependence
- optionality versus interoperability
- diversification versus cost
- principle versus transaction
- institutional reform versus bloc formation
- crisis clarity versus peacetime ambiguity

## Research standard

Every country chapter must include at least one decision that could have gone differently, one measure of dependence, one counterexample to the thesis, and one source from the government being analyzed rather than only outside commentary.
