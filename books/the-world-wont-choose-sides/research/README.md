# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

The World Won’t Choose Sides tests whether selective alignment is becoming a durable structural feature of international politics rather than a temporary pause before a new bipolar order.

The strong claim is not that pivotal states can avoid every choice. They cannot. The claim worth testing is narrower: where security, technology, energy, trade, capital, and diplomatic legitimacy travel through overlapping networks, states with sufficient scale or strategic assets can bargain issue by issue and preserve meaningful optionality.

## Mechanisms

1. **Optionality:** multiple credible partners reduce the cost of refusing any single one.
2. **Asset control:** geography, energy, capital, industrial capacity, votes, markets, ports, or military access create bargaining power.
3. **Domain separation:** security alignment may not determine technology, trade, or diplomatic alignment.
4. **Domestic constraint:** governments bargain externally through political coalitions they must survive internally.
5. **Crisis compression:** wars, sanctions, export controls, and interoperability requirements can suddenly make divided alignment harder.

## Falsification and counter-evidence

The book must change if evidence shows that supposedly autonomous states systematically converge into durable blocs across most consequential domains. It must also preserve cases where hedging produces high costs, where partners lose trust, where technology standards force exclusivity, and where military crises leave no usable middle position.

Country chapters should not treat every act of disagreement with Washington as evidence of autonomy or every relationship with Beijing as successful hedging. Measure concrete alternatives, switching costs, dependencies, and outcomes.

## Chapter evidence map

- Chapters 1–3 define alignment, leverage, and optionality.
- Chapters 4–10 test the framework against India, Gulf states, Indonesia, Turkey, Brazil, South Africa, and additional pivotal states.
- Chapters 11–14 examine infrastructure, defense procurement, technology stacks, and coalition bargaining.
- Chapters 15–18 test the limits: the price of autonomy, crisis choices, great-power coercion, and the design of negotiated order.

## Research discipline

Prefer foreign-ministry documents, treaties, procurement records, trade and investment data, official summit texts, central-bank and energy data, and interviews with diplomats from the states being described. Use U.S. and Chinese sources as evidence of great-power expectations, not as substitutes for the strategic reasoning of middle powers themselves.

Record what would make each chapter’s central claim false. Avoid treating rhetoric about autonomy as proof of actual room for maneuver.
