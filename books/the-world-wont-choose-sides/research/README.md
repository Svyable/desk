# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

The World Won’t Choose Sides tests whether a growing set of consequential states can create durable leverage by aligning selectively across security, trade, technology, energy, finance, and institutions rather than accepting an exclusive geopolitical camp.

The strong version — that middle powers can simply play great powers against one another indefinitely — is too flattering and too brittle. The version worth testing is conditional:

> Selective alignment creates autonomy when a state possesses assets others need, has credible alternatives, and can keep issue areas divisible. It fails when dependencies become concentrated, great powers link issues, or crisis demands turn ambiguity into binary operational choices.

## Core empirical tests

1. Do diversified partnerships measurably improve bargaining outcomes, or mainly produce diplomatic symbolism?
2. Which assets generate leverage: geography, markets, energy, minerals, capital, military capability, institutions, population, or legitimacy?
3. When does strategic autonomy reduce exposure, and when does it create expensive duplication or incompatible systems?
4. Do U.S.-China tensions actually force alignment over time, or do states continue to compartmentalize?
5. How do sanctions, export controls, cloud infrastructure, AI chips, payment systems, and standards create hidden alignment?
6. Are BRICS, the G20, and other forums becoming coherent coalitions or remaining issue-specific platforms?
7. What changes in a Taiwan, Gulf, or sanctions crisis when a middle power must grant access, enforce restrictions, or share intelligence?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

The manuscript should weaken its thesis if evidence shows that:

- middle powers consistently converge into durable U.S.- and China-centered blocs;
- diversified arms and technology procurement reduces readiness more than it increases autonomy;
- dependence on finance, chips, clouds, or security guarantees overwhelms diplomatic optionality;
- institutions described as flexible alignments evolve into disciplined strategic coalitions;
- great-power linkage succeeds in making cross-domain compartmentalization impossible;
- domestic politics, regime survival, or elite interests explain apparent strategic autonomy better than national bargaining logic.

## Evidence lanes

Use official foreign-policy speeches, treaty obligations, arms and trade data, energy flows, investment records, export-control rules, sanctions behavior, votes, institutional communiqués, technology infrastructure, and crisis decisions. Separate rhetoric about autonomy from observable ability to refuse a costly request.

## Source discipline

Prefer official records, trade and investment data, treaty text, credible datasets, and contemporaneous reporting. Never infer alignment from one summit, one vote, or one investment. Treat “middle power” as an analytical category rather than a status every government accepts for itself.
