# The Coalition Auction

Every major international initiative asks the same hidden question.

Who else is in?

Sanctions coalitions, infrastructure plans, climate agreements, security initiatives, standards bodies, peace conferences, technology partnerships, and diplomatic statements become more valuable as participation broadens.

This creates a coalition auction.

The word auction can sound cynical.

It should not.

Coalitions are agreements among actors with different interests.

Agreement requires terms.

The terms can involve money.

They can also involve timing, exemptions, language, market access, technology, leadership roles, security assurances, development finance, industrial participation, or the right to remain outside another part of the agenda.

Great powers prefer to describe participation as agreement on principles.

Principles matter.

So do terms.

A state may support the objective while insisting that its development needs, energy constraints, domestic politics, strategic autonomy, or regional concerns be recognized.

That is not necessarily opportunism.

It is coalition formation.

The useful concept is marginal value.

What becomes possible if this state joins?

What becomes harder if it refuses?

A country that adds little to a coalition has limited bargaining leverage.

A country that changes the coalition's legitimacy, logistics, market scale, technology base, regional reach, or enforcement capacity has more.

This is why the same state can command a high price in one coalition and a low price in another.

Its value depends on the function.

An energy exporter matters differently in a sanctions coalition than in an AI standards body.

A maritime state matters differently in a security initiative than in a debt-restructuring negotiation.

A large democracy may add legitimacy that a small treaty ally does not.

The auction is issue-specific.

This is another reason bloc thinking fails.

Membership in one camp does not settle the price of participation in every initiative.

Pivotal states know their marginal value.

They can bargain for financing, technology transfer, market access, exemptions, diplomatic support, leadership positions, language changes, or industrial benefits.

They can also bargain for negative space.

Do not ask us to join this second initiative.

Do not require a public statement on that dispute.

Do not condition this technology partnership on military alignment.

Do not force this vote into a broader package.

Negative space is one of the most important currencies of middle-power diplomacy.

A great power can make an offer more attractive by asking for less.

This is easy to miss because strategy often rewards accumulation.

More commitments.

More statements.

More alignment.

More interoperability.

But every additional demand raises the price of participation.

Coalitions can become smaller because sponsors try to maximize ideological coherence instead of practical reach.

The best coalition is not always the most pure coalition.

It is the coalition sufficient to accomplish the objective.

That suggests a design rule.

Define the minimum common commitment clearly.

Allow diversity beyond it.

A climate coalition may need emissions commitments without security alignment.

A maritime-awareness partnership may need data sharing without a defense treaty.

A sanctions coalition may need financial enforcement without agreement on every geopolitical issue.

A technology standards group may need common technical rules without a shared foreign policy.

Narrow coalitions can become broad networks because the entry price is lower.

This is modular coalition building.

It mirrors the broader logic of selective alignment.

The coalition becomes an auction because competing great powers can make competing offers.

One offers infrastructure finance.

Another offers security cooperation.

Another offers market access.

Another offers technology.

Another tolerates more political disagreement.

Another offers faster delivery.

Another offers fewer governance conditions.

Middle powers compare packages.

The comparison is rarely one-dimensional.

A cheaper loan may carry higher switching costs.

A more advanced weapons system may create deeper supplier dependence.

A larger market may require stricter technology alignment.

A diplomatic partnership may improve international status and create domestic criticism.

The state is choosing a portfolio, not a product.

That means the reservation price is political.

What must the state receive before participation becomes preferable to staying out?

The answer includes opportunity cost.

Joining one coalition can make another relationship harder.

Supporting one sanctions regime can affect trade.

Adopting one technology standard can make another ecosystem less accessible.

Granting military access can alter relations with neighbors.

Backing one diplomatic statement can reduce brokerage credibility elsewhere.

The real price therefore includes what the state gives up outside the negotiation.

Great powers often underprice this.

They see the requested action in isolation.

The middle power sees its network.

This is why a modest concession can be insufficient even when the immediate request looks small.

The request may create a precedent.

Today one export restriction.

Tomorrow a broader technology bloc.

Today one access agreement.

Tomorrow expectations of support in another contingency.

Today one public statement.

Tomorrow a reputation for belonging to one camp.

Middle powers price future inference.

They know partners and rivals will interpret the decision beyond its formal text.

Coalition terms therefore include signaling control.

How publicly must the commitment be announced?

How broadly will it be described?

Can the government explain it domestically as issue-specific?

Will the sponsor respect that explanation or advertise the state as a strategic convert?

This matters because great powers can destroy the value of a deal by overselling it.

A middle power agrees to a narrow partnership.

The major power celebrates a geopolitical realignment.

Domestic critics react.

Other partners react.

The middle power then retreats to reassert autonomy.

The coalition sponsor mistakes the retreat for unreliability.

The original problem was narrative overreach.

Disciplined coalition building lets the participant own its reason for joining.

Political ownership is a form of durability.

A commitment defended as a national choice is more stable than one perceived as compliance with external pressure.

This is especially true in democracies where coalition participation must survive elections, legislatures, courts, business interests, and public debate.

The sponsor should therefore ask not only what concession closes the deal.

What explanation makes the deal sustainable inside the partner's politics?

This is not interference.

It is respect for political reality.

The same applies to nondemocratic systems differently.

Elite coalitions, bureaucracies, militaries, business groups, or regime legitimacy can still constrain policy.

Every government has an internal reservation price.

Foreign pressure can change it in either direction.

A threat may make compliance more attractive materially and less attractive politically.

This is why coercion can backfire.

The external price falls.

The domestic price rises.

The net result may be refusal.

Great powers often misread this as irrationality.

It is multi-market bargaining.

The government is negotiating abroad and at home simultaneously.

This makes timing important.

A concession offered privately before a public ultimatum may work.

The same concession after the ultimatum may look like humiliation.

A state may accept a deal before an election and reject it during a campaign.

A crisis may increase the value of participation and reduce the political room to negotiate visibly.

Coalition diplomacy therefore has a temporal architecture.

When should terms be settled?

What should be pre-negotiated?

Which concessions become politically toxic after escalation begins?

What can be automatic under an existing agreement?

The best coalition builders store bargaining in institutions before the emergency.

Standing finance arrangements.

Technology partnerships.

Consultation mechanisms.

Market-access frameworks.

Pre-agreed exemptions.

These reduce the appearance that a pivotal state is charging a crisis premium.

They also reduce the sponsor's temptation to use last-minute pressure.

Institutionalization turns auction bargaining into relationship management.

This is not the end of bargaining.

It changes the cadence.

Repeated relationships matter because every auction creates a reputation.

A middle power that extracts the maximum concession every time may win individual negotiations and reduce its future value as a reliable partner.

A great power that threatens partners whenever they hesitate may win votes and increase long-term diversification away from its leverage.

The game is repeated.

This changes optimal behavior.

Leverage should be used, not exhausted.

Reliability should be demonstrated, not surrendered.

The state wants partners to believe two things at once.

We have alternatives.

Our commitments still mean something.

Those propositions are compatible.

They create the ideal bargaining position.

A partner with no alternatives can be taken for granted.

A partner whose commitments mean nothing is not worth buying.

The middle power wants to live between dependence and unreliability.

This is why selective alignment requires discipline.

The state should distinguish negotiable participation from core commitments.

If everything is auctioned, nothing is trusted.

A treaty obligation should not be treated like a conference endorsement.

A long-term industrial partnership should not be repriced every month.

A diplomatic vote may be more flexible.

A new infrastructure deal may invite competitive bidding.

The government needs its own hierarchy of commitment.

Which relationships are foundational?

Which are transactional?

Which are experimental?

Which are symbolic?

Which are reversible?

The clearer this hierarchy is internally, the more effectively the state can bargain externally.

Otherwise tactical deals can erode strategic assets accidentally.

The same hierarchy helps great powers.

Sponsors should know what they truly need from a partner.

Do we need the vote?

The base?

The market?

The public endorsement?

The technology standard?

The sanctions enforcement?

The shipping access?

Too often the answer becomes all of the above because the relationship is politically salient.

That raises the price and can collapse the deal.

Strategy requires prioritization.

Buy the coalition function you need.

Do not automatically demand the partner's identity too.

This is especially important in competition between the United States and China because both possess enormous packages of value.

Security.

Markets.

Technology.

Finance.

Infrastructure.

Political access.

The temptation is to bundle domains.

Security cooperation becomes linked to technology.

Technology becomes linked to export policy.

Finance becomes linked to diplomatic positions.

The more domains are bundled, the harder selective alignment becomes.

That can increase great-power leverage in the short term.

It can also increase the middle power's incentive to build alternatives.

Every coercive bundle teaches the partner which dependency needs diversification.

The coalition auction therefore shapes future infrastructure.

A state threatened through payment rails may invest in alternative rails.

A state denied technology may fund domestic substitutes.

A state pressured through arms dependence may diversify suppliers.

Today's leverage changes tomorrow's reservation price.

This is why coercive power can depreciate through use.

The best coalition strategy considers replacement dynamics.

If we use this dependency now, how quickly can the partner reduce it?

Will the immediate gain justify the future loss of leverage?

Middle powers ask the mirror question.

If we threaten defection now, will partners invest in replacing our pivotal role?

A transit state can be bypassed.

A supplier can be dual-sourced.

A diplomatic broker can lose credibility.

A market can become less central over time.

Leverage invites substitution.

This disciplines both sides.

The auction price should stay below the cost of replacement if the state wants the relationship to remain durable.

Coalitions also have internal distribution problems.

A deal that attracts one pivotal state can alienate existing members.

Give one country an exemption and another asks why it complied without one.

Offer one partner technology access and a treaty ally may see unequal treatment.

Give a new entrant leadership status and older members may resent it.

Coalition sponsors therefore bargain with the whole coalition, not only the marginal member.

The cost of inclusion includes effects on insiders.

This is the coalition equivalent of domestic politics.

Every concession has an audience.

The sponsor needs a fairness story.

Why does this state receive different terms?

Because its economic structure differs.

Because the transition period is temporary.

Because it contributes another capability.

Because the coalition objective requires it.

Legitimacy matters inside coalitions too.

If exceptions look arbitrary, cohesion erodes.

This is why transparent categories can help.

Not identical terms.

Explainable terms.

The same logic applies to development finance and climate coalitions.

States at different income levels cannot be expected to bear identical transition costs.

Differentiated responsibility can expand the coalition if the rules feel principled.

A great power that treats every request for accommodation as extortion may lose participants whose constraints are real.

A middle power that labels every domestic preference a structural necessity may lose credibility.

The auction works when both sides can distinguish constraint from bargaining theater.

Information is therefore part of coalition statecraft.

What does the partner actually need?

What can it deliver?

Which domestic claims are real blockers?

Which are opening positions?

Which alternatives are credible?

Which outside offers exist?

Diplomacy is price discovery.

AI can improve parts of it.

Models can map trade exposure, voting behavior, infrastructure dependencies, domestic economic interests, public opinion, and competing offers.

They can estimate which coalition members are pivotal to a particular objective.

They can simulate package combinations.

This can make bargaining more informed.

It can also make it dangerously transactional.

A model may assign a value to a partner as though the partner were a commodity.

Political relationships resist that reduction.

The state remembers whether it was treated as a participant or a target variable.

Some terms cannot be inferred from data because dignity, historical memory, and political identity matter.

The machine can help map constraints.

The diplomat still has to negotiate legitimacy.

The danger for great powers is moralizing the auction.

If every request is treated as opportunism, the coalition can shrink.

If every concession is granted, the coalition can become incoherent.

The strategic skill is distinguishing the price of inclusion from the price of unreliability.

A legitimate price compensates for real cost, creates durable participation, or secures a capability the coalition needs.

An unreliable price is one that must be paid repeatedly because the commitment never stabilizes.

The difference is visible over time.

Does the deal create a relationship?

Or only rent-seeking around the next decision?

Middle powers face the mirror problem.

Bargain too aggressively and partners may stop believing commitments have value.

Under-bargain and the country can surrender optionality without receiving durable benefit.

The answer is not modesty.

It is conversion.

Use bargaining moments to improve the structure beneath the relationship.

Technology capacity.

Market access.

Industrial investment.

Institutional voice.

Infrastructure alternatives.

Skills.

Standards participation.

These benefits remain after the immediate coalition dissolves.

The best coalition auction does not end with a highest bidder.

It ends with a deal in which the participant gains enough autonomy and the coalition gains enough reliability that both prefer the relationship to the alternatives.

That is not the end of geopolitics.

It is what functioning geopolitics looks like when states still have choices.
