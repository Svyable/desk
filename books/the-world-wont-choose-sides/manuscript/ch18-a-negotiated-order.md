# A Negotiated Order

The world will choose sides sometimes.

Wars create sides.

Treaties create sides.

Technology restrictions can create sides.

Sanctions, military access, and security guarantees can make choices binary.

The argument of this book is not that alignment disappears.

It is that alignment no longer arrives as one package for a growing number of consequential states.

The international order is becoming negotiated issue by issue.

That does not make it less ordered.

It changes where order comes from.

The twentieth-century imagination of international alignment was architectural.

Blocs had walls.

Alliances had members.

Institutions had charters.

Trade systems had rules.

A state’s strategic identity was often described by which structure it occupied.

That picture remains useful for some relationships.

It is increasingly incomplete for others.

The more important question is not only where a state belongs.

It is what the state can bargain over while belonging there.

Middle powers help produce this negotiated order because they control assets great powers need and because competing networks give them alternatives.

India can cooperate with the United States on technology and security while preserving strategic traditions that resist formal alliance.

Gulf states can maintain American security relationships while cultivating Chinese trade, technology, and investment.

Turkey can remain inside NATO while negotiating across other political and economic systems.

Indonesia can use scale, geography, resources, and regional diplomacy to widen its room for maneuver.

Brazil and South Africa can convert diplomatic legitimacy into influence over coalition politics.

Other pivotal states matter when particular networks make their ports, markets, votes, resources, manufacturing capacity, airspace, finance, or access unusually valuable.

The common feature is not neutrality.

It is bargaining capacity.

This is why the phrase Global South is often analytically weak.

It describes a political field as though it were a strategic actor.

The countries inside it have different economies, security relationships, domestic politics, historical experiences, and leverage.

They do not bargain as one.

They are often most powerful precisely when outsiders cannot aggregate them into one position.

A negotiated order is therefore granular.

Coalitions form around functions.

One group of states may cooperate on maritime security.

Another on payments.

Another on climate finance.

Another on semiconductor supply chains.

Another on sanctions.

Another on peace mediation.

The overlap changes by issue.

This can look like fragmentation.

It is also a form of modularity.

The system does not require every disagreement to become a complete geopolitical divorce.

Countries can remain connected in one domain while competing in another.

That lowers some barriers to cooperation.

It raises the cost of coordination.

Selective alignment can encourage opportunism.

It can slow coalitions.

It can complicate standards.

It can weaken sanctions.

It can create opaque dependencies and mixed signals.

Autonomy also has a price.

Redundancy is expensive.

Multiple technology stacks are difficult to secure.

Mixed weapons systems complicate logistics.

Alternative payment channels require scale before they become useful.

Diversified suppliers may be less efficient than a single optimized chain.

Diplomatic balancing consumes attention.

Crisis can force choices that years of hedging cannot avoid.

The world does not become frictionless because it becomes flexible.

The question is whether the flexibility produces enough resilience and bargaining space to justify the complexity.

For many states, the answer will be yes.

The reason is not ideological.

It is structural.

Dependence has become easier to weaponize.

Financial systems can transmit sanctions quickly.

Technology suppliers can control updates, compute, chips, licenses, and standards.

Ports and logistics networks can become security assets.

Cloud infrastructure can become jurisdiction.

Insurance can change the economics of maritime access without a formal blockade.

Export controls can affect industries far from the original dispute.

When interdependence carries more political risk, governments buy optionality.

That optionality changes diplomacy.

The state no longer asks only which partner offers the best price.

It asks which dependency is survivable.

Which supplier can be replaced?

Which system has an exit path?

Which government is likely to use access as leverage?

Which relationship tolerates disagreement?

Which network requires exclusivity?

Which one converts membership into hierarchy?

This is where great-power competition can become self-defeating.

A powerful state may respond to uncertainty by demanding more alignment.

More alignment demands create more fear of dependence.

More fear of dependence creates more diversification.

The effort to produce a clean bloc can generate the behavior that keeps the system messy.

The alternative is not passivity.

Great powers still have to compete.

They can compete over the quality of connection.

Which system offers better technology without excessive political control?

Which provides market access?

Which can finance infrastructure?

Which makes security commitments credible?

Which respects sovereignty?

Which partnership survives disagreement?

Which delivers on time?

Which one makes participation increase a state’s agency rather than reduce it?

That last question may become decisive.

The strongest network is not always the one with the largest center.

It may be the one that creates the greatest total value for the nodes.

This is a different model of international power.

Traditional hierarchy concentrates capability at the center and distributes obligations outward.

A network can distribute capability across the members and make participation itself productive.

Access to intelligence can improve national security.

Shared standards can enlarge markets.

Joint infrastructure can reduce costs.

Interoperability can make national systems more useful.

Financial arrangements can create resilience.

Industrial cooperation can expand domestic capacity.

Diplomatic participation can raise status.

When the network does this well, alignment feels less like submission because membership improves the member’s own options.

That is the strategic advantage of a negotiated order.

The bargain is visible.

The member can ask what it receives, what it contributes, what it risks, and what it retains.

This is harder to manage than simple discipline.

It may be more durable because the relationship is repeatedly renewed through value.

Institutions will have to change accordingly.

Many were designed for categories.

Member or nonmember.

Ally or partner.

Developed or developing.

Sanctioning or nonsanctioning.

Trusted or untrusted.

Digital systems make those categories tempting because categories simplify access control.

Politics is becoming more conditional.

A state may be highly trusted for one dataset and excluded from another.

It may receive advanced civilian technology but not military integration.

It may join a maritime coalition while rejecting sanctions.

It may share intelligence on terrorism while competing economically.

This requires institutions capable of layered participation.

The alternative is forcing every relationship toward the lowest common denominator.

That wastes potential cooperation.

Layered participation has risks.

Information can leak across boundaries.

Members can free ride.

Adversaries can exploit mixed networks.

Political expectations can become unclear.

The solution is not to pretend the layers do not exist.

It is to govern them explicitly.

What is shared?

Under what conditions?

What obligations attach?

What can be suspended?

What survives disagreement?

Where is exclusivity genuinely necessary?

Where is it merely inherited habit?

These questions are becoming the constitutional questions of networked geopolitics.

The negotiated order will also be more transactional than some diplomats prefer.

That word is often used critically.

Transactions are not inherently shallow.

A treaty is a transaction with memory.

An alliance is a transaction embedded in institutions.

A trade agreement is a transaction repeated through law.

The issue is whether bargains compound into trust or remain one-off exchanges.

The most successful middle powers will use transactions to build portfolios.

The most successful great powers will use transactions to build relationships.

That difference matters.

A portfolio asks: how many alternatives do I have?

A relationship asks: how much value disappears if this connection breaks?

Stable order emerges when both sides have something to lose.

This is why reliability matters more than rhetoric.

A great power can speak beautifully about partnership and still become unattractive if approvals are slow, sanctions are unpredictable, infrastructure finance never arrives, or technology access changes without warning.

A middle power can speak beautifully about autonomy and still become unattractive if it breaks commitments, leaks sensitive information, or treats every partner as an auction bidder with no expectation of continuity.

Negotiated order is disciplined by reputation.

States remember how others behave under pressure.

So do companies.

So do militaries.

So do bureaucracies.

The record becomes part of the next negotiation.

Artificial intelligence will intensify this memory.

Governments will have easier access to historical agreements, voting patterns, delivery timelines, sanctions behavior, procurement disputes, diplomatic statements, and economic dependencies.

The informational cost of remembering inconsistency will fall.

That makes credibility more measurable.

It also makes context more important.

A model may identify that a state switched positions three times.

A diplomat still needs to know whether the switches reflected opportunism, domestic elections, external coercion, or a changing security environment.

The negotiated order will be data-rich and interpretation-dependent.

That is a recurring pattern of the AI age.

More information does not eliminate politics.

It makes the quality of political judgment more valuable.

The hardest test remains crisis.

Ordinary selective alignment can collapse when sanctions, military access, financial exposure, and technology controls couple together.

Some states will choose clearly.

Some will try to narrow the choice.

Some will discover that years of diversification bought more time than outsiders expected.

Some will discover that their autonomy was mostly ceremonial.

After the crisis, the system will not simply reset.

States will study which dependencies became coercive.

They will diversify away from them where possible.

Companies will change suppliers.

Central banks will reassess settlement exposure.

Militaries will reconsider maintenance chains.

Governments will revise data rules.

The crisis will redesign the next round of alignment.

This means the world will not choose sides once.

It will keep choosing.

Issue by issue.

Bargain by bargain.

Technology stack by technology stack.

Port by port.

Vote by vote.

Crisis by crisis.

The result is not the end of order.

It is an order whose boundaries are continuously negotiated.

That can be uncomfortable for states accustomed to permanent deference.

It can be empowering for states that previously had to accept packages designed elsewhere.

It can also be dangerous if every bargain becomes short-term and every institution becomes optional.

The task is therefore to build enough structure that flexibility does not become chaos.

Rules still matter.

Treaties still matter.

Alliances still matter.

Standards still matter.

International law still matters.

The negotiated order sits on top of those structures and changes how states use them.

It is not post-institutional.

It is institutionally plural.

Power in this system is not only the ability to force a state to choose.

It is the ability to shape the menu.

To make one option cheaper.

To make one network more reliable.

To make one relationship preserve more agency.

To make one partnership more difficult to replace.

That is a harder standard than bloc discipline.

It may be more durable because it recognizes the thing middle powers have been saying through their behavior all along.

They do not want to be outside the system.

They want room inside it.

The map will remain messy because the mess is not unfinished history.

It is the bargaining surface of a world in which more states possess assets that others need and more networks give them partial alternatives.

The future order will contain alliances, rivalries, coercion, hard boundaries, and moments of binary choice.

Between them will be a large political market in which states negotiate the terms of participation.

The great powers that thrive will be those that understand this market without mistaking every negotiation for disloyalty.

The middle powers that thrive will be those that convert optionality into durable capability rather than endless hedging.

The world won’t choose sides once.

It will keep choosing.

And the quality of the order will depend on whether those choices remain bargains states can live with after the crisis passes.
