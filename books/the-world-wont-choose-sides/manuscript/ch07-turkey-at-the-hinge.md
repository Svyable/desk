# Turkey at the Hinge

Turkey is an ally that behaves like a middle power because alliance membership has not ended its search for autonomy.

It sits inside NATO and at the intersection of Europe, the Black Sea, the Caucasus, the Middle East, the eastern Mediterranean, and routes toward Central Asia.

That geography creates leverage.

So does the ability to maintain relationships across rival systems.

Turkey can support NATO missions while bargaining hard with allies. It can compete with Russia in one theater and cooperate in another. It can deepen trade and institutional ties with Asian and Gulf partners while remaining embedded in the transatlantic security architecture.

This looks contradictory only if alignment is assumed to be indivisible.

For Ankara, the contradiction is often the strategy.

## An ally with exits

Formal alliance membership is usually imagined as a reduction in optionality.

A state makes commitments in exchange for security, interoperability, intelligence, and political support. The bargain works because partners become more predictable to one another.

Turkey complicates that model.

It values NATO and also values the ability to demonstrate that NATO is not the only relationship available to it.

That creates bargaining power.

An ally with no alternatives negotiates differently from an ally that can buy weapons elsewhere, trade elsewhere, mediate with rivals, receive capital from several regions, and participate in institutions outside the alliance.

The alternatives do not need to be perfect substitutes.

They need to be credible enough to change the price of saying no.

This is the gap between exit and exclusivity that defines much of middle-power strategy.

But an exit option is useful only if it can be exercised without destroying the asset that makes bargaining possible.

Turkey’s leverage inside NATO depends partly on still being inside NATO.

A threat to diversify suppliers can improve terms.

A move that permanently severs interoperability can reduce future bargaining power.

An institutional veto can produce concessions.

A pattern of vetoes that persuades partners to design around Turkey can erode the value of the veto.

This creates a hinge problem.

Leverage comes from the possibility of movement.

Too much movement can detach the hinge from the structure that gives it force.

## Geography keeps generating options

Turkey’s location makes complete alignment unusually costly.

Russia is not an abstract adversary on a distant map. It is a Black Sea power, energy actor, military presence in neighboring regions, and counterpart in conflicts where Turkish interests are direct.

Europe is not merely a market. It is Turkey’s largest economic neighborhood, a source of investment, an institutional reference point, and a political relationship shaped by migration and security.

The Middle East creates separate security and economic demands.

The Caucasus and Central Asia create linguistic, historical, commercial, and strategic links.

A Black Sea crisis affects shipping, energy, military access, and regional security directly.

A conflict in Syria can create refugee, border, terrorism, and domestic political consequences inside Turkey.

Eastern Mediterranean disputes touch energy, sovereignty, and alliance relationships simultaneously.

The portfolio is not only ambition.

It is a response to multiple theaters that cannot be outsourced to one patron.

## Russia: compete and cooperate

Turkey’s relationship with Russia demonstrates domain separation vividly.

The two states can support opposing sides or interests in one conflict while coordinating pragmatically in another. They can compete for regional influence and still maintain energy, tourism, trade, and diplomatic channels.

This does not mean the relationship is balanced or safe.

It means both governments have learned to compartmentalize.

Compartmentalization is a middle-power skill because it prevents one disagreement from consuming the whole bilateral relationship.

It also creates risk.

A relationship built from several bargains can become difficult to manage if one theater suddenly becomes existential. Economic ties can turn into leverage. Energy dependence can constrain policy. A tactical accommodation can be interpreted by allies as strategic drift.

Selective alignment works until the domains stop staying separate.

The key variable is coupling.

How quickly can a disagreement in one theater spread into energy, trade, tourism, arms, or diplomatic cooperation?

The more tightly coupled the portfolio becomes, the less room Ankara has to compartmentalize.

This makes crisis coupling a better test than ordinary peacetime cooperation.

## The S-400 lesson

Turkey’s acquisition of the Russian S-400 air-defense system became a particularly sharp example of how autonomy can collide with alliance interoperability.

From one perspective, buying outside the alliance demonstrates sovereign choice and supplier diversification.

From another, certain defense systems create technical and intelligence risks that cannot be contained as ordinary procurement diversity.

The dispute showed that not every domain can be separated cleanly.

Weapons connect to networks, training, aircraft, data, maintenance, and trust.

A purchase intended to increase freedom of action can reduce access to other capabilities.

This is the price of technological incompatibility.

Strategic autonomy is therefore not maximized by buying from the largest possible number of suppliers.

It is maximized by preserving useful alternatives without destroying the interoperability that gives existing partnerships value.

That is a much harder optimization problem.

The S-400 case is strategically useful because it makes switching costs visible in both directions.

Buying outside the alliance can reduce dependence on alliance suppliers.

It can also create a new supplier relationship and narrow access to allied systems.

The state has exchanged one dependency profile for another.

The correct question is not whether the purchase was “independent.”

It is whether the resulting portfolio left Turkey with more usable future choices.

That is the autonomy test.

## NATO as asset, not identity

Turkey’s behavior makes more sense if NATO is understood as an extraordinary strategic asset rather than a complete foreign-policy identity.

Alliance membership provides deterrence, planning, military integration, access to exercises, and a seat inside the most consequential security institution in Europe.

Those benefits are substantial.

Ankara’s autonomy strategy depends partly on being able to bargain from inside that institution.

Leaving NATO would not simply increase freedom.

It would destroy one of Turkey’s largest sources of leverage.

This is why Turkish engagement with institutions associated with China and Russia should not automatically be read as preparation for exit.

In September 2026, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan publicly said Turkey would consider full membership in the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation.

The striking fact is not that a NATO member expressed interest in a China- and Russia-led security grouping.

It is that Ankara can use the possibility of overlapping institutional membership as an instrument of statecraft.

The objective may be less to choose a new camp than to refuse the idea that the old camp determines every other affiliation.

The evidence boundary matters.

Interest in another institution is not equivalent to accession.

Accession would not automatically erase NATO membership.

And membership in overlapping organizations would not prove that those organizations have become strategically equivalent.

The analytical point is optionality, not bloc conversion.

## What would make the thesis false?

Turkey is a useful test of the book because it could support either conclusion.

If NATO membership ultimately constrains Ankara to align with the alliance across most consequential domains, then institutional integration may be stronger than selective alignment.

If economic and technology dependencies repeatedly force Turkey to choose one ecosystem, optionality may prove shallower than rhetoric suggests.

If bargaining tactics steadily reduce alliance benefits faster than they produce concessions, autonomy can become self-defeating.

If allies and partners can route around Turkish leverage cheaply enough, geography and procedural power will matter less than the hinge metaphor implies.

But if Turkey continues to preserve meaningful cooperation across rival systems while retaining the strategic benefits of NATO membership, it is strong evidence that alignment can remain divisible even inside a formal alliance.

## Living inside and outside at once

Turkey’s hinge position is not only geographic.

It is institutional: inside Western security structures while cultivating relationships that those structures distrust; connected to European markets while deepening ties across other geographies; able to mediate because it is not fully captured by any single camp’s narrative.

That position creates chronic suspicion.

Partners ask whether Turkey is hedging or defecting.

The more useful question is whether Turkish choices remain issue-specific enough to preserve the hinge, or whether a sequence of crises will force consolidation into a simpler identity.

Hinges can break.

They can also become more valuable as blocs harden elsewhere, because someone still has to talk across the fracture.
