# Technology Without Allegiance

Technology is becoming harder to buy without buying politics.

Semiconductors, AI models, cloud services, telecommunications, cybersecurity, satellite systems, industrial software, digital identity, and payment infrastructure are increasingly governed by export controls, trusted-vendor rules, data restrictions, sanctions, investment screening, and national-security review.

For middle powers, this creates a new pressure toward blocs.

Not because every government wants to choose a camp.

Because technical systems accumulate dependencies that can make the choice before diplomats do.

## The stack chooses slowly

A country rarely wakes up one morning and decides to join a technology bloc.

The decision arrives in layers.

A telecom vendor wins a contract.

A cloud provider becomes standard across government.

A defense ministry adopts software tied to one supplier ecosystem.

Universities train researchers on a particular compute platform.

Banks connect to a payment network.

Manufacturers buy industrial controls that depend on foreign updates.

Data accumulates in proprietary formats.

Developers build applications around APIs that are expensive to replace.

Each decision can be commercially reasonable.

Together they create strategic architecture.

By the time a geopolitical crisis makes the dependence visible, switching may be technically possible and economically brutal.

This is why technology policy has become autonomy policy.

## The myth of the sovereign stack

The obvious response is technological sovereignty.

Build everything at home.

For most countries, that is fantasy.

No modern state independently controls the full semiconductor chain, cloud stack, telecom supply chain, satellite ecosystem, AI research base, cybersecurity toolset, industrial software market, and advanced manufacturing equipment it uses.

Even the largest powers depend on foreign inputs and international talent.

Middle powers should therefore define sovereignty differently.

Not self-sufficiency.

Switching power.

Can the country change suppliers in a critical layer without rebuilding the entire system?

Can it preserve access to its own data?

Can domestic engineers operate the infrastructure if foreign support stops?

Can one external restriction be routed around without national shutdown?

Can the government say no to a political demand without losing a capability it cannot replace?

Sovereignty is partly the cost of exit.

## Hybrid architecture

Governments can preserve optionality by mixing technical ecosystems.

One partner in consumer telecom.

Another in defense systems.

Domestic hosting for sensitive government data.

Foreign hyperscale compute for commercial workloads.

Multiple satellite providers.

Local cybersecurity operations built on tools from several countries.

Open-source software where strategic portability matters.

This produces hybrid architecture.

Hybrid systems are messy.

That mess can be strategic.

A mixed environment prevents one supplier from owning the entire dependency chain.

It can also prevent one great power from translating control of a technology layer into leverage over every policy domain.

But hybridity is not free.

Integration costs rise. Security teams have to defend more interfaces. Procurement becomes harder. Training fragments. Data standards diverge. Some of the deepest technology partnerships may be unavailable to countries unwilling to enter a trusted ecosystem more fully.

Optionality has an engineering bill.

## Interoperability is geopolitical

Open standards and interoperability are usually discussed as competition or efficiency policy.

They are also foreign-policy tools.

A country using portable data formats, documented interfaces, federated identity, and interoperable systems can replace a supplier more cheaply than one locked into proprietary architecture.

That changes bargaining.

The supplier knows the customer can leave.

The government knows a political dispute does not automatically become a technical crisis.

This is one reason standards negotiations have strategic weight far beyond their bureaucratic appearance.

A technical standard can decide whether future markets are contestable.

It can also decide whether a middle power remains able to mix suppliers without losing functionality.

Standards can preserve nonalignment at the machine layer.

## The trusted-network bargain

Great powers have a strong counterargument.

The most sensitive cooperation requires trust.

A country cannot expect unrestricted access to advanced defense software, intelligence systems, leading-edge chips, classified data, or frontier AI infrastructure while simultaneously allowing high-risk vendors deep access to the same environment.

Security boundaries are real.

Some technology ecosystems will become more exclusive because mixing them creates unacceptable espionage, cyber, or supply-chain risk.

This means technology without allegiance reaches a limit.

A middle power may be able to mix commercial infrastructure while discovering that the most valuable military or AI collaboration is available only inside a trusted network.

That is where selective alignment becomes expensive.

The state has to decide which domain is worth the loss of optionality.

## Semiconductors and the invisible coalition

Semiconductors reveal how technology networks create coalitions without treaties.

A leading chip can depend on design software from one country, manufacturing equipment from another, fabrication in another, chemicals and materials from several more, packaging elsewhere, and cloud demand distributed globally.

Export controls imposed at one critical layer can therefore shape what companies in many countries are able to build.

The coalition is embedded in the supply chain.

Middle powers cannot simply declare neutrality toward a technical chokepoint.

They have to possess an alternative or accept the dependency.

This is why local semiconductor strategies often focus on selected layers rather than fantasies of a fully domestic chain.

Packaging.

Design.

Power semiconductors.

Mature-node fabrication.

Materials.

Testing.

A country does not need to dominate the frontier to create bargaining room.

It needs capabilities that lower the cost of disruption in the layers most relevant to its economy and security.

## Cloud sovereignty

Cloud infrastructure makes the issue even clearer because dependence is operational rather than merely industrial.

Government data, enterprise software, AI workloads, cybersecurity logs, identity systems, and digital services can accumulate inside a provider ecosystem.

The provider may be technically reliable and politically exposed to its home jurisdiction.

Data-localization rules address only part of the problem.

A server can sit physically inside the country while key software, encryption management, updates, administrative tooling, or corporate authority remains external.

The relevant questions are functional.

Who can access the data?

Who can suspend the service?

Who controls the keys?

Can workloads move?

Can domestic personnel operate the system independently?

How long would migration take?

A sovereign cloud that cannot be exited is sovereign mostly in branding.

## AI and model dependence

Artificial intelligence introduces another layer of technical alignment.

A state can depend on foreign frontier models, foreign chips, foreign clouds, foreign developer platforms, or foreign safety tooling even if the applications are built domestically.

The dependency can be subtle.

A government may own the data and the application while lacking the compute required to retrain or replace the underlying model.

A local company may fine-tune a foreign model and still depend on licensing terms that can change.

An “open” model may be portable but rely on accelerators or software ecosystems controlled elsewhere.

Middle-power AI strategy should therefore distinguish access from control.

Access answers: can we use this capability today?

Control answers: what happens if the relationship changes tomorrow?

Both matter.

## Telecom and the lesson of sunk cost

Telecommunications networks show why technology choices are politically sticky.

Once equipment is deployed at national scale, replacement can take years and cost billions. Security concerns that appear later collide with sunk investment and operational continuity.

This teaches a broader lesson.

The strategic cost of a vendor should be assessed before dependency becomes infrastructure.

Procurement that looks cheapest at installation can be most expensive at exit.

Governments need lifecycle geopolitical accounting.

What would replacement cost?

What legal jurisdictions govern support?

Which components have substitutes?

What happens during sanctions?

Can security patches continue during political conflict?

The purchase price is not the dependency price.

## Domestic capability as bargaining room

Domestic technology capacity matters even when it is not globally dominant.

A national cybersecurity center that understands its own systems reduces reliance on foreign incident response.

Local cloud capability can provide a fallback for critical services.

Domestic semiconductor packaging can keep part of the supply chain operating.

National satellite capacity can create independent situational awareness.

Indigenous defense software can reduce the leverage of a foreign upgrade cycle.

The objective is not import substitution everywhere.

It is strategic minimum viable capability.

Which domestic functions are necessary to keep choice alive?

That is a more useful question than whether a country can become technologically independent.

## Local control can become local inefficiency

There is a counterargument.

Sovereignty requirements can become protectionism.

Governments can subsidize weak domestic champions indefinitely, force inefficient localization, fragment markets, and reduce access to better technology while calling the result resilience.

This is a real risk.

Autonomy should be measured by the option it creates relative to the cost it imposes.

A domestic system that is dramatically worse, more expensive, and rarely usable as a substitute may not create meaningful leverage.

Resilience has to be credible enough to survive contact with actual users.

The goal is not to maximize local content.

It is to minimize catastrophic dependency at acceptable economic cost.

## The standards auction

Middle powers can also create leverage collectively.

If several large markets insist on interoperable standards, data portability, transparent security requirements, or local governance rights, suppliers have stronger incentives to accommodate them.

One country negotiating alone may be told to accept the platform as designed.

A group of markets can influence the design.

This makes regional organizations and coalitions important in technology governance even when they are not military alliances.

A common regulatory requirement can create bargaining power similar to a shared procurement program.

The market becomes the coalition.

## The crisis of forced choice

The hardest test arrives when great powers make access conditional.

Choose our cloud ecosystem or lose security cooperation.

Remove this telecom vendor or lose intelligence access.

Follow these semiconductor controls or lose access to our equipment.

Reject that model provider or lose participation in our trusted AI network.

At that point, selective alignment can collapse into forced choice.

The middle power’s room depends on preparation.

Does it have an alternative supplier?

Can it segment sensitive systems from commercial ones?

Can it negotiate an exception?

Can it absorb the cost of refusal?

Can it coordinate with other states facing the same demand?

Optionality built after the ultimatum is usually too late.

## Strategic multilingualism

The countries that manage this environment best will not be technologically independent.

Nobody is.

They will be strategically multilingual.

Able to operate across more than one technical ecosystem.

Able to translate data and standards between them.

Able to isolate sensitive domains where trust requires exclusivity.

Able to preserve domestic control over a small number of critical functions.

Able to switch suppliers without national paralysis.

Able to explain to great powers that cooperation in one technology layer does not constitute permanent political allegiance across all others.

This is harder than choosing a camp.

It requires continuous architecture work.

## What would make the thesis false?

Technology may ultimately be the domain that disproves the book’s broad argument.

If leading AI, semiconductor, cloud, telecom, and defense ecosystems become mutually exclusive enough, middle powers may be forced into durable technical blocs regardless of diplomatic preference.

If security requirements make hybrid architectures intolerably risky, strategic multilingualism may fail.

If the cost of maintaining alternatives becomes too high, governments may rationally accept deeper dependence on one network.

Those are plausible futures.

The evidence should decide.

But the outcome is not predetermined because architecture itself is political.

Open standards, modular systems, domestic capability, diversified procurement, and collective regulation can preserve room that closed stacks would eliminate.

## The stack dependency graph

Technology sovereignty becomes clearer when the state maps the stack as a dependency graph.

Not a vendor list.

A graph.

Take one government AI application.

The visible vendor may be domestic.

Under it sits a foreign foundation model.

Under the model sits a cloud API.

Under the cloud sits an accelerator architecture.

Under the accelerator sits a software toolchain.

Under that sits semiconductor equipment, intellectual property, networking, power, and update infrastructure spread across several jurisdictions.

The political dependency may live three layers below the contract the ministry signed.

A stack graph should therefore record for every critical service:

Compute.

Model.

Cloud or hosting.

Identity.

Data storage.

Network transport.

Developer tooling.

Security tooling.

Update path.

Licensing authority.

Critical hardware.

Upstream maintenance.

Legal jurisdiction.

Substitution path.

The purpose is not to eliminate foreign nodes.

It is to know which node can stop the function.

## Exit rights are part of procurement

Governments often negotiate purchase rights more carefully than exit rights.

That is backwards for strategic technology.

A serious procurement asks before signing:

Can the data be exported in usable form?

Are schemas documented?

Can identity records move?

Can encryption keys remain nationally controlled?

Can domestic engineers administer the service if vendor support is suspended?

Is there a transition period after contract termination?

Can the system run on another provider?

Are interfaces documented well enough for a competitor to replace the component?

Can the state continue receiving security fixes if the commercial relationship ends?

Does an update require foreign approval?

Can the provider remotely disable functionality?

Which rights survive sanctions, acquisition, bankruptcy, or geopolitical rupture?

These are exit rights.

They belong in procurement policy because bargaining power is cheapest before lock-in.

Once migration becomes a national emergency, every missing clause becomes leverage for someone else.

## The portability ladder

Portability is not binary.

A state can own its data and still be unable to move the service.

A useful ladder is:

**Readable.** The state can export data in a documented format.

**Reconstructable.** Another team can rebuild the application around the exported data with significant effort.

**Portable.** The workload can move to another environment using documented interfaces and ordinary engineering effort.

**Interoperable.** Multiple providers can support the function with limited redesign.

**Switch-ready.** A tested alternative exists and the state knows the time, cost, and capacity required to move.

**Surge-switchable.** The alternative can absorb crisis demand rather than only a pilot workload.

A procurement marketed as sovereign should state which level it actually reaches.

Data export alone is a weak form of sovereignty if the application, model, identity system, and operational procedures remain inseparable from the provider.

## Update sovereignty

Modern technology is never really purchased once.

It is continuously updated.

That makes update authority a strategic dependency.

Who decides when software changes?

Who can delay a patch?

Who can inspect it?

Can a government remain on a known version?

Can a provider force migration to a new service?

Can a license change remove a capability?

Can an AI model be updated in a way that changes behavior without the customer seeing the underlying change?

Can a hardware platform lose support remotely because of export restrictions?

Update sovereignty does not require freezing technology.

It requires enough control to prevent ordinary maintenance from becoming a silent political veto.

For critical systems, governments should know the maximum period they can operate safely without external update authority.

That period is part of their strategic endurance.

## The revocation problem

Technology access can disappear for reasons unrelated to local technical performance.

Sanctions.

Export controls.

Court orders.

Corporate policy.

A supplier acquisition.

Security restrictions.

Changes in alliance policy.

A licensing dispute.

A platform decision made in another jurisdiction.

The state should therefore model revocation explicitly.

What can the supplier revoke?

Access?

Updates?

Compute quota?

Keys?

Support?

Model weights?

API credentials?

Replacement parts?

Developer tooling?

The dependency is most dangerous when revocation is both technically easy for the supplier and politically hard for the customer to absorb.

This creates a revocation-leverage map.

## Procurement options have states

A second supplier does not create autonomy merely by appearing on an approved list.

Technology options move through operating states.

**Nominal.** The product exists and could theoretically be procured.

**Qualified.** Legal, security, and technical review has been completed.

**Integrated.** Interfaces, data, and operating procedures have been tested with the existing environment.

**Warm.** Personnel use the alternative often enough to retain operational familiarity.

**Switch-ready.** Migration can begin under a known plan without designing the architecture from scratch.

**Surge-capable.** The alternative can absorb the scale required during a crisis.

**Degraded.** The option remains but has lost capacity, support, compatibility, or political availability.

**Dormant.** Reactivation requires substantial work.

Counting nominal alternatives is a common strategic mistake.

The expensive part of optionality is keeping an alternative warm.

## Compatibility tax

Hybrid architecture creates a recurring tax.

Gateways.

Translation layers.

Multiple security certifications.

Duplicate training.

Interface testing.

Data mapping.

Operational complexity.

Inventory duplication.

Specialist staffing.

This is the compatibility tax.

The tax can be worth paying if it preserves political choice.

It can also become so high that the state quietly abandons the alternative and returns to one stack.

Governments should measure the tax explicitly.

How much annual cost is being paid for switchability?

How much delay does hybridity create?

Which interfaces account for most of the burden?

Can standards reduce the burden without recreating monoculture?

Which duplicated systems add genuine failure diversity and which merely add complexity?

Optionality should survive a budget review, not only a strategy speech.

## Minimum viable stack sovereignty

Full-stack sovereignty is unrealistic for most states.

Minimum viable stack sovereignty is not.

The country should identify the smallest set of technical capabilities that must remain under national control for political choice to remain meaningful.

That may include:

Control of critical government data.

Authority over encryption keys.

Emergency identity services.

A domestic incident-response capability.

The ability to operate core networks in degraded mode.

Local administration of selected cloud workloads.

A fallback inference capability for critical AI services.

Domestic maintenance knowledge for strategically important hardware.

Contract and legal authority to move data and workloads.

The floor should be narrow enough to sustain.

Everything above it can use foreign technology where partnership creates more capability than vulnerability.

The floor is not autarky.

It is the part of the stack the state refuses to make someone else’s on/off switch.

## Layered trust zones

Strategic multilingualism does not require every ecosystem to touch every other ecosystem.

A state can use layered trust zones.

Public commercial services can mix suppliers broadly.

Sensitive government workloads can operate under tighter provider rules.

Defense networks can require a smaller trusted ecosystem.

Strategic command functions can have an even narrower technology boundary.

The design question is where the zones meet.

Can data cross in controlled forms?

Can identities federate without exposing privileged systems?

Can a commercial cloud support unclassified analysis without becoming a route into classified networks?

Can a telecom provider remain in one segment while being excluded from another?

Layered trust lets the state preserve broad economic optionality without pretending every provider is acceptable for every mission.

This is more realistic than one national trusted-vendor list applied to everything.

## Forced-choice stress tests

A technology strategy should be tested against political ultimatums before one arrives.

Remove provider X within six months.

Lose access to toolchain Y.

A cloud API becomes unavailable tomorrow.

A model license is revoked.

One accelerator family can no longer be imported.

A trusted partner conditions intelligence sharing on segregation from another vendor.

A sanctions regime reaches a critical software dependency.

Then ask:

Which national functions stop first?

Which can move?

How long does migration take?

Which sectors bear the cost?

Which legal authorities are missing?

Which supposedly independent alternatives share the same upstream dependency?

Which political choices remain possible after the technical shock?

This is the forced-choice stress test.

A technology strategy that works only while geopolitics remains friendly has not solved the problem it was meant to solve.

## Standards need an exit test

Standards are often praised as though openness is self-evident.

A standard can be nominally open and operationally dominated by one implementation.

A useful standards review asks an exit question.

Can two independent suppliers implement the standard without one supplier’s proprietary extension?

Can a customer switch between them without rebuilding the surrounding system?

Is conformance testing available?

Are security requirements transparent?

Can smaller suppliers enter?

Do data and identity move cleanly?

Does the standard reduce switching cost in practice?

If not, the standard may be an interoperability label over platform dependence.

The strategic value of a standard is revealed when somebody tries to leave.

## The stack-autonomy balance sheet

A government should be able to summarize each critical technology domain as a balance sheet.

On the asset side:

Portable data.

Documented interfaces.

Multiple qualified suppliers.

Warm alternatives.

Domestic operational knowledge.

National key control.

Open standards with real implementation diversity.

Tested migration procedures.

Layered trust zones.

Emergency legal authorities.

On the liability side:

Single-provider services.

Long migration time.

Remote revocation rights.

Foreign-controlled updates.

Proprietary data formats.

One model or chip lineage under several products.

Skills available only from the vendor.

Infrastructure whose nominal localization hides external control.

Contracts with weak transition rights.

Political access conditioned on broader alignment.

The balance sheet makes it harder to call any domestic project sovereignty or any foreign service dependence by default.

The question becomes structural.

What choice can the state still make if this relationship changes?

## The switching-time budget

Each critical function should have a switching-time budget.

How long can the country tolerate loss of the primary service?

How long does it take to activate the alternative?

How much degraded operation is acceptable between the two?

If a payment system can be unavailable for hours but a cloud migration takes months, the architecture needs a bridge.

If an AI model can be replaced technically in a week but recertification takes six months, governance rather than engineering is the binding clock.

If telecom replacement takes years, procurement has to preserve political confidence in the vendor relationship or invest in modular replacement before crisis.

The budget converts switching power from rhetoric into time.

A state does not need instant exit from every dependency.

It needs exit fast enough for the political decision the architecture is meant to preserve.

## Collective optionality

Middle powers do not have to build every fallback nationally.

Several states can pool switching power.

Common portability requirements.

Regional cloud capacity.

Shared cyber-response teams.

Mutual recognition of security certifications.

Joint procurement that keeps more than one supplier viable.

Interoperable digital identity.

Regional data spaces with migration rules.

Shared semiconductor packaging or testing capacity.

The result is collective optionality.

This matters because a domestic alternative may be uneconomic at national scale and viable at regional scale.

A coalition can create an exit route none of its members could afford alone.

That is strategic autonomy through interdependence rather than against it.

## Falsifying strategic multilingualism

The claim in this chapter should be tested hard.

Strategic multilingualism fails if hybrid systems repeatedly create unacceptable security exposure, if compatibility taxes exceed the value of preserved choice, if warm alternatives cannot scale in crisis, or if great powers make the most valuable technology domains so exclusive that layered participation becomes impossible.

It fails if exit-right procurement clauses do not materially reduce switching time.

It fails if minimum viable sovereign capability becomes protectionism with no crisis utility.

It fails if collective optionality proves politically too fragile to activate under pressure.

It fails if open standards produce no meaningful implementation diversity.

If those conditions dominate, technical blocs will become deeper than the book’s argument allows.

That possibility has to remain open.

## Technology policy is autonomy policy

The central question is no longer whether a country buys American technology or Chinese technology.

It is whether the technical system preserves the ability to make another political choice later.

That is a deeper standard.

A foreign supplier can be strategically acceptable if the dependency is visible, bounded, replaceable, and governed.

A domestic supplier can be strategically dangerous if it creates a brittle monopoly the state cannot escape.

Autonomy lives in the dependency graph, not the flag on the vendor.

Technology without allegiance is possible only where the architecture keeps choice alive.

That means knowing the stack beneath the contract.

Negotiating exit rights before lock-in.

Keeping selected alternatives warm.

Controlling the update and revocation paths that matter most.

Accepting a compatibility tax where it purchases real political room.

Building minimum viable sovereign floors rather than fantasies of self-sufficiency.

Using trusted zones where security genuinely requires exclusivity.

Stress-testing what happens when access becomes conditional.

The future middle power will not be the country that owns every layer.

It will be the country that knows which layers it can afford not to own—and has designed the rest so ownership by someone else does not become obedience.

The decisive sovereignty test is not whose logo appears on the system.

It is whether the state can still say no after the system is installed.
