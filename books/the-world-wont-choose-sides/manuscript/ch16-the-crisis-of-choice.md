# The Crisis of Choice

Selective alignment works best in ordinary time.

Crises compress domains.

A war produces sanctions. Sanctions affect payment systems. Export controls affect technology. Military access affects diplomacy. Energy prices affect domestic politics. Intelligence sharing becomes conditional. Neutrality becomes harder to explain.

The portfolio starts collapsing into a single question.

Whose system can you still use?

This is the crisis of choice.

Middle powers can often delay it, shape it, or narrow it.

They cannot always avoid it.

A severe U.S.-China confrontation would place extraordinary pressure on states integrated economically with China and strategically with the United States. A major war can force decisions about ports, airspace, sanctions, technology access, and military support that cannot be cleanly compartmentalized.

The important variable is preparation.

A state with diversified energy, finance, technology, and defense relationships can absorb more pressure before one choice determines the rest.

A state whose autonomy is mostly rhetorical reaches the wall quickly.

Domestic politics matters too.

Leaders do not choose in a vacuum. Business groups, militaries, voters, diasporas, ideological movements, and bureaucracies experience different costs from alignment.

Crisis foreign policy is therefore coalition management at home as much as abroad.

This explains why outside pressure can backfire.

A great power that demands immediate loyalty may strengthen domestic factions suspicious of dependence. A more patient approach can allow the partner to move while preserving political ownership.

The crisis of choice is the limit case for selective alignment.

It does not prove the strategy was false.

Insurance is still valuable even if the house eventually burns.

The measure is how much freedom the strategy preserved before the final decision became unavoidable.
