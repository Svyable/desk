# Indonesia and the Maritime Middle

Indonesia is difficult to fit into a bloc map because the country is itself a map.

More than seventeen thousand islands stretch across the sea lanes connecting the Indian and Pacific Oceans. Geography puts Indonesia near the center of maritime Asia without making it a formal ally of any great power.

Its population, resource endowments, domestic market, industrial ambitions, and diplomatic tradition give Jakarta more room than most states to refuse a simple strategic label.

Indonesia occupies the maritime middle.

That middle is not a neutral point between Washington and Beijing.

It is a position from which Indonesia tries to remain the author of its own alignment.

## Free and active

The phrase that matters in Indonesian foreign policy is *bebas aktif*: free and active.

Free does not mean indifferent.

Active does not mean aligned.

The doctrine grew from a postcolonial determination not to let Indonesia become an object in somebody else’s geopolitical contest. In 2026, Indonesia’s foreign minister again described the principle as especially relevant in a fragmented world: the country would avoid being pulled into exclusive blocs while continuing to act in international affairs.

That is a useful correction to the language of hedging.

Hedging sounds like indecision before an eventual choice.

*Bebas aktif* is closer to a theory of agency.

The objective is to preserve the ability to choose differently across issues.

That does not mean Indonesia can avoid every binary decision. It means Jakarta resists allowing one decision to settle all the others.

This is not merely a diplomatic slogan.

It implies a portfolio discipline.

A free-and-active policy is credible only if alternatives survive in the domains that matter.

A state cannot be strategically free in rhetoric while one external actor controls the financing, software, industrial inputs, export market, or military support that makes refusal possible.

The real test is refusal cost.

What can Indonesia say no to without creating an intolerable domestic or strategic loss?

That question turns doctrine into something measurable.

## Geography as bargaining power

Indonesia’s first source of leverage is physical.

The archipelago sits around critical maritime routes. The Malacca, Sunda, Lombok, and Makassar corridors connect energy flows, container traffic, military movement, and commercial supply chains across the Indo-Pacific.

Geography creates value because others need access, stability, information, and cooperation around those routes.

But geography is not automatically power.

A country has to convert location into institutions, maritime capacity, infrastructure, and diplomatic relevance.

This means geographic leverage has several layers.

Observation.

Can the state see what is happening?

Presence.

Can it put credible public authority in the relevant space?

Regulation.

Can rules be enforced consistently?

Infrastructure.

Can ports and logistics support national choices?

Diplomacy.

Can maritime interests be translated into regional bargaining?

Domestic legitimacy.

Can the government exercise authority without creating the perception that it is acting on behalf of an outside power?

A map shows only the first fact: where Indonesia is.

Strategy depends on the rest.

## The nickel experiment

The results are real enough to matter and mixed enough to resist mythology.

The IMF’s 2025 consultation noted that Indonesia’s nickel downstreaming policy attracted foreign investment into refining and further into battery and electric-vehicle supply chains. It also noted that Indonesia’s share of global nickel production had risen dramatically.

The World Bank has similarly documented increased domestic value added and investment while identifying efficiency costs, trade-law disputes, subsidies, and other distortions.

This is exactly the kind of evidence a book about middle-power leverage needs.

Strategic autonomy is not free.

Indonesia can use resource control to force bargaining farther down the value chain.

But the bargaining can create new dependencies.

If the refineries are financed by one foreign partner, run on equipment from another, powered by a particular energy source, and sell into a concentrated market, raw-material sovereignty may simply be transformed into industrial dependency.

The map changes.

Dependence does not disappear.

The relevant question is dependency quality.

Has Indonesia moved from a relationship that is easy for others to bypass to one in which Indonesian labor, infrastructure, firms, and market scale become harder to substitute?

Or has it moved from exporting ore to hosting facilities whose critical technology and buyers remain external?

Both can be true at once.

The balance determines strategic value.

## China inside the strategy

This is where simplistic bloc analysis becomes misleading.

Chinese capital and industrial participation have been central to parts of Indonesia’s downstream mineral expansion.

A bloc map can treat that as evidence that Indonesia is moving into China’s economic orbit.

But Jakarta’s own objective is not necessarily to replace Western dependence with Chinese dependence.

It is to use competition among investors to accelerate domestic development.

That strategy works only if alternatives remain credible.

If one source of capital, technology, market demand, or infrastructure becomes too dominant, optionality narrows.

The policy problem is therefore not whether Indonesia works with China.

It is whether Indonesia can keep bargaining after it has worked with China.

That requires competition from Japan, the United States, Europe, South Korea, Gulf investors, and others. It requires domestic firms and skills strong enough that foreign investment leaves capabilities behind. It requires rules that prevent one partnership from becoming the only feasible route to scale.

The leverage exists in the portfolio.

This also means competition from outside powers can benefit Indonesia even when Jakarta does not switch partners.

A credible alternative changes terms.

The option itself has bargaining value.

That is why middle-power strategy often rewards several suitors more than one patron.

## What would make the thesis false?

The Indonesia case should weaken the book’s argument if optionality proves mostly rhetorical.

If foreign investment becomes so concentrated that Jakarta cannot credibly switch partners, leverage declines.

If technology standards force exclusive ecosystems across critical infrastructure, domain separation narrows.

If downstreaming produces more dependency and rent seeking than durable domestic capability, resource nationalism becomes a costly illusion.

If security crises repeatedly force Indonesia into the same camp across unrelated issues, selective alignment may be a transitional condition rather than a durable strategy.

The thesis should also weaken if ASEAN-centered process loses enough practical relevance that outside powers can ignore it without cost.

It should weaken if archipelagic state-capacity constraints prevent Indonesia from converting geography into consistent maritime authority.

It should weaken if supplier diversification creates so much operational friction that concentrated alignment becomes clearly superior.

Those outcomes are possible.

The point is not to celebrate autonomy because Indonesian leaders invoke it.

The point is to measure whether alternatives remain real.

## The maritime middle

Indonesia’s strategic importance comes from a combination rare enough to matter: geography, scale, resources, markets, regional legitimacy, and a foreign-policy tradition designed explicitly around agency.

None makes the country independent of great powers.

Together they make it expensive to recruit.

Can security cooperation improve national capacity without quietly deciding future policy?

Can ASEAN process preserve regional agency while remaining useful enough that outsiders cannot bypass it?

Can the state implement a sovereign decision across a sprawling archipelago?

Those are the tests of the maritime middle.

It is not a place between two camps waiting for history to decide where it belongs.

It is a position from which the camps have to negotiate.

The success of the strategy will not be measured by how often Indonesia refuses to choose.

It will be measured by how often Indonesia can choose on one issue, absorb the cost, preserve domestic capacity, and still retain meaningful choices when the next issue arrives.
