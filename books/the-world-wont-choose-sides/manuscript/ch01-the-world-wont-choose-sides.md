# The World Won’t Choose Sides

In March 2026, Indonesia’s president, Prabowo Subianto, described his country’s foreign policy in language that would have been familiar to generations of Indonesian diplomats. Indonesia, he said, remained committed to an independent and active policy and did not seek to join any bloc.

The phrasing mattered because the world around it had changed.

Indonesia was no longer a newly independent state trying to preserve room between Washington and Moscow. It was a country of roughly 280 million people, a member of the G20, a major producer of nickel, an increasingly important manufacturing location, a maritime state astride sea lanes that connect the Indian and Pacific Oceans, and a government courted by the United States, China, Japan, Gulf investors, and others for reasons that had little to do with nostalgia for Bandung.

Nonalignment had acquired assets.

The old image of a non-aligned state is a country standing at equal distance from two powers. The contemporary version is more active. Distance is not the objective. Access is.

A government wants Chinese demand without Chinese political control. American security cooperation without automatic participation in every American confrontation. Gulf capital without Gulf foreign policy. Russian energy or weapons where useful without inheriting Russia’s wars. European markets without accepting every European regulatory preference. It may not get all of these things. But the effort to separate them is itself a form of statecraft.

This book calls that strategy selective alignment.

The phrase is less romantic than strategic autonomy and less evasive than hedging. It describes what governments actually do: choose partners issue by issue while trying to prevent any one relationship from becoming so dominant that it dictates the rest.

Selective alignment works best when three conditions hold.

First, the state possesses something other powers want. It may be geography, population, a consumer market, a military, energy, minerals, manufacturing capacity, diplomatic legitimacy, a sovereign wealth fund, or access to a regional institution. Autonomy without assets is mostly rhetoric.

Second, alternatives exist. A bargaining position becomes credible when a country can move at least part of a transaction elsewhere. The alternative need not be equally good. It only needs to be plausible enough that the preferred partner cannot assume permanent capture.

Third, the issue is divisible. A government has more room to maneuver when a defense relationship can be separated from an energy relationship, or an energy relationship from a technology standard. The hardest moments arrive when a great power deliberately links them.

That last condition explains why the present era is so unstable for middle powers. The United States and China increasingly treat economic and technological systems as components of national security. Export controls, investment screening, sanctions, telecommunications rules, data localization, cloud infrastructure, AI chips, critical minerals, payment systems, and shipping can all become instruments of strategic competition. The more issues are linked, the harder it is to maintain a portfolio.

Yet linkage also creates demand for brokers.

A state that can talk to several camps becomes useful when the camps stop talking well to one another. A market that remains open to multiple suppliers becomes valuable when companies need alternative production locations. A country that belongs to several overlapping institutions can carry ideas between them. A sovereign wealth fund can turn geopolitical diversification into a capital-allocation strategy.

This is one reason the category “middle power” has become simultaneously more popular and less precise. The term sounds like a ranking: not a great power, not a small state, somewhere in the middle. But the most consequential middle powers are not simply median countries. Their importance is relational. They sit where several systems meet.

Turkey is obvious because of geography. It controls access between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean under the Montreux Convention, belongs to NATO, borders regions central to Russian, European, and Middle Eastern security, and has built a defense industry that gives it tools other than diplomatic protest. But Turkey’s leverage is not merely the Bosporus. It also comes from the fact that several larger actors repeatedly need Turkish cooperation for different reasons.

The United Arab Emirates is a different kind of junction. Its territory is small, but its capital, logistics, airlines, ports, energy position, technology ambitions, and commercial connectivity make it a switchboard. The strategic question is not whether the UAE is “pro-American” or “pro-Chinese.” That binary obscures the policy problem. Washington wants to know whether Chinese technological ties create security risks inside a relationship that also includes close U.S. defense cooperation. Abu Dhabi wants access to advanced American technology without surrendering the diversification strategy that helped create its leverage in the first place.

India operates at a larger scale. It is too large, too nuclear, too populous, and too ambitious to fit comfortably inside a category defined by middleness. Indian policymakers often prefer the language of great-power aspiration. Yet India is central to this book because it demonstrates the mechanism more clearly than almost any other state.

India has tightened strategic cooperation with the United States while preserving a longstanding defense relationship with Russia. It is a member of the Quad and of BRICS. It participates in institutions that Washington sees as central to balancing China and in institutions that present themselves as vehicles for a more multipolar order. Its leaders do not treat those memberships as mutually exclusive because the memberships do different work.

From Washington, this can look frustratingly inconsistent.

From New Delhi, consistency lies elsewhere: strategic autonomy.

That difference in perspective is not semantic. It shapes bargaining behavior.

The United States often approaches partnership through convergence. If interests align in one increasingly important area, policymakers naturally look for alignment in adjacent areas. Shared concern about China should make India more receptive to American preferences on Russia. Deep defense cooperation should encourage technology alignment. Technology alignment should encourage diplomatic coordination.

India’s portfolio logic runs in the opposite direction. The value of the U.S. relationship partly comes from the fact that it expands India’s options. Turning that relationship into exclusivity would reduce the autonomy the relationship helped create.

This does not mean India can always have both.

No portfolio is immune to a margin call.

A severe U.S.-China confrontation over Taiwan could force governments to decide whether American forces can use facilities, whether Chinese ships can call at ports, whether sanctions will be enforced, whether semiconductor controls will be observed, whether intelligence will be shared, whether airspace will be available, and whether public statements will assign blame. The answer may differ by country and by request. But a crisis converts ambiguity into a sequence of concrete decisions.

That is when analysts tend to declare that the middle power has finally chosen.

Often it has chosen only one action.

The distinction matters because a state can grant access for one operation without joining a permanent coalition, enforce one export control while refusing another, or condemn one violation of international law while resisting an unrelated sanctions regime. Great powers may dislike the granularity. Middle powers live inside it.

Saudi Arabia’s foreign policy illustrates another dimension: the diversification of demand.

For decades, the U.S.-Saudi relationship rested on a dense combination of security cooperation, energy, arms, intelligence, elite ties, and regional strategy. China’s rise did not simply replace the United States. It changed the customer base around Saudi power. China became a crucial buyer of Gulf energy and a major commercial actor. Asian demand made the economic map less Atlantic. Saudi leaders gained reasons to maintain strategic relations with Washington and deep economic relations with Beijing at the same time.

This is sometimes presented as a transfer of allegiance.

A portfolio is a better model.

The Saudi state wants capabilities, investment, markets, technology, and diplomatic room. The suppliers of those goods do not offer identical products. American security relationships remain difficult to substitute. Chinese demand and infrastructure relationships are valuable for different reasons. European technology matters in others. The kingdom’s own capital gives it the ability to be an investor rather than only a recipient.

The result is not independence from the world. It is bargaining inside interdependence.

That phrase is less satisfying than multipolarity because it admits how constrained the system remains. Middle powers can exploit competition among larger actors, but they cannot conjure alternatives where none exist. If one country controls a technological bottleneck, diversification may stop at the bottleneck. If one currency remains dominant in a transaction network, political disagreement does not erase financial exposure. If one military is uniquely capable of providing a security guarantee, rhetoric about a multipolar order does not create another guarantor.

The strongest version of the middle-power thesis therefore needs to be tested against dependence, not only against great-power pressure.

Consider advanced artificial intelligence.

A government can aspire to sovereign AI, local models, national data centers, domestic talent, and regulatory autonomy. But the stack underneath those ambitions may include accelerators designed by a company in one country, manufactured by a foundry in another, using equipment produced in a small number of allied economies, powered by an electrical system financed through global capital markets, connected by undersea cables, and served through clouds operated by a few firms.

A flag on a data center does not make the stack sovereign.

This creates a new kind of alignment pressure. Technical standards can do what military treaties once did more visibly: determine who can connect to whom. Export-control rules can shape a country’s AI ceiling without a single soldier crossing a border. Cloud security requirements can create de facto blocs even when political leaders reject the language of blocs.

Middle powers understand this, which is why so many are trying to move upward in the stack.

Indonesia does not want merely to export nickel ore; it wants more of the processing and manufacturing chain. Gulf states do not want merely to export hydrocarbons; they are investing in data centers, AI companies, advanced manufacturing, logistics, and finance. India has spent years trying to deepen domestic manufacturing and digital infrastructure while preserving access to foreign technology. Turkey has invested in indigenous defense production partly because imported capability comes with political conditions.

These projects are usually discussed as industrial policy. They are also foreign policy.

Every domestic capability that substitutes for a politically vulnerable import changes the bargaining set.

Every failed attempt can reveal how much dependence remains.

There is a temptation to read this entire movement as a revolt against the United States. That is too simple. Many middle powers want more American engagement, not less. They want investment without lectures, security without automatic alignment, technology without extraterritorial control, and respect without the assumption that partnership means obedience.

The same governments often want things from China while resisting Chinese dominance. They welcome infrastructure and trade, then worry about debt, local industry, data, maritime pressure, or political influence. Their objection is not to one hegemon because another hegemon is preferable. It is to being left without alternatives.

This is why BRICS can grow without becoming NATO.

The appeal of a forum is not the same as commitment to a common grand strategy. Brazil, India, South Africa, China, and the expanded membership can agree that global institutions should reflect a wider distribution of power while disagreeing sharply about security, regional rivalries, economic interests, and the kind of order they want. The group can be politically useful precisely because it does not demand the integration that a military alliance requires.

Observers who ask whether BRICS is “anti-Western” often search for a coherence the institution does not need in order to matter.

It can matter as a venue, a signal, a bargaining coalition on selected issues, a source of institutional alternatives, and evidence that many states dislike a world in which legitimacy flows through a small number of Western institutions. None of that requires its members to behave as a bloc when a war begins.

The same principle applies to the G20, regional organizations, minilateral groups, and issue-specific coalitions. The international system is becoming thicker with partial alignments.

That thickness creates work for diplomats.

It also creates analytical errors for anyone who wants a scoreboard.

A vote at the United Nations can be meaningful without revealing a country’s total orientation. An arms purchase can deepen one relationship without closing another. A Chinese-built port can create leverage without converting the host government into a Chinese ally. An American security agreement can impose obligations without erasing a country’s independent interests.

The hard part is identifying which commitments travel across issues.

That is the core problem this book will follow.

Some relationships are compartmentalized. Others are not. Some assets create bargaining power. Others create hidden lock-in. Some middle powers can say no because they have alternatives. Others say no until the cost arrives.

The world will not choose sides all at once.

It will choose, transaction by transaction, crisis by crisis, layer by layer.

The map should be drawn accordingly.
