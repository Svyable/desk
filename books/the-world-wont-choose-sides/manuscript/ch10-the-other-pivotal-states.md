# The Other Pivotal States

The category middle power can become a grab bag.

Too many countries are called pivotal and the word loses meaning.

A useful test is functional.

A state is pivotal on an issue when its choice materially changes the options available to larger powers.

That can happen for different reasons.

Mexico matters through market integration, migration, manufacturing, and geography.

Vietnam matters through its position in Southeast Asia, supply chains, and relationships with both major powers.

South Korea combines advanced technology, industrial depth, military capability, and alliance ties while maintaining major economic exposure to China.

Nigeria carries demographic, economic, and diplomatic weight in Africa.

Egypt controls a critical waterway and occupies a central position in Arab diplomacy.

Kazakhstan and other Central Asian states matter through geography, energy, transport corridors, and proximity to Russia and China.

The point is not to place all of them in one strategic type.

It is to recognize a pattern.

The international system contains many local veto points.

Ports, supply chains, votes, airspace, resources, transit corridors, standards, and regional institutions create issue-specific leverage.

This makes coalition building granular.

A great power cannot simply recruit “the Global South.”

It has to understand which states matter to which problem and what those states actually want.

The pivotal state is therefore not a permanent rank.

It is a role created by a particular network.

As networks overlap, more states get moments of leverage.

The world becomes harder to divide because the decision-maker who matters keeps changing.
