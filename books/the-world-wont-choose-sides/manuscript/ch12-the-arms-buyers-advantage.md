# The Arms Buyer’s Advantage

Weapons purchases create unusually sticky relationships.

A fighter aircraft is not a one-time transaction.

Neither is an air-defense battery.

A submarine.

A drone fleet.

A missile family.

A command system.

Each brings training, maintenance, munitions, software, upgrades, data links, spare parts, doctrine, security rules, and often decades of dependence on the supplier.

Defense procurement therefore looks like alignment.

Middle powers try to make it divisible.

Some buy from multiple suppliers.

Others demand local production, technology transfer, co-development, domestic maintenance, source-code access, integration rights, or the ability to manufacture selected components.

The buyer is trying to turn dependence into bargaining leverage.

The strategic question is not whether dependence exists.

It will.

The question is whether the buyer can choose the shape, depth, duration, and reversibility of that dependence before the supplier relationship becomes expensive to change.

## The bargaining curve changes after signature

Competition among suppliers helps.

If several states are willing to sell, the buyer can demand better financing, offsets, technology access, delivery terms, local jobs, or political conditions.

The most visible competition occurs before contract signature.

That is when suppliers court the buyer.

After the contract, the geometry changes.

Switching costs rise.

The supplier gains leverage.

This is why the arms buyer's advantage is strongest before the purchase and only partly survives afterward.

The sophisticated buyer negotiates the afterlife of the system before signing for the system itself.

Who controls software updates?

Can local industry repair the platform?

Which munitions are compatible?

Can the system connect to equipment from another supplier?

Can the buyer integrate a domestic sensor?

Can the buyer produce spare parts?

Does the supplier retain vetoes over re-export or deployment?

What happens if political relations deteriorate?

How long can the force operate without external support?

These are foreign-policy questions disguised as procurement details.

A cheap platform can create expensive political dependence.

A more expensive platform can buy autonomy if its lifecycle terms are better.

Purchase price is therefore a poor measure of strategic cost.

The real price includes decades of permission.

This can be represented as a **bargaining curve**.

Before signature, the buyer may have several credible suppliers and high leverage.

During integration, alternatives narrow.

After training, infrastructure, munitions, and software become embedded, supplier leverage rises.

Later, domestic capability or a second supplier may restore some buyer leverage.

The curve is not fixed.

Good procurement policy tries to keep it from collapsing immediately after delivery.

## Build a dependency passport before purchase

Every major acquisition should carry a **dependency passport** before contract signature.

Not only technical specifications.

A map of future reliance.

For each critical function, record:

Who controls design authority?

Who controls software updates?

Who controls cryptographic material?

Who provides mission data?

Who can certify repairs?

Who supplies engines, radars, processors, batteries, munitions, or other critical consumables?

Which components have alternate sources?

Which export permissions remain external?

Which maintenance tools are proprietary?

Which remote services are required?

Which data leaves the country?

Which functions can continue offline?

How long can the system operate without the supplier?

How long would substitution take?

The passport makes the future relationship visible while the buyer still has leverage to negotiate it.

## Ownership has layers

This is especially true when software becomes central to military capability.

Older defense relationships depended heavily on physical parts.

Modern systems depend increasingly on code, cryptographic keys, threat libraries, data, cloud services, mission planning, and digital maintenance tools.

The supplier can therefore remain present inside the system long after delivery.

A platform sitting physically inside the buyer's territory may still depend on remote technical authority elsewhere.

This changes sovereignty.

The question is no longer only who owns the hardware.

Who can keep it useful?

A buyer that cannot modify or sustain critical software independently may possess the platform without fully possessing the capability.

This creates a spectrum of ownership.

At one end, the buyer operates a black box.

At the other, it can sustain, modify, integrate, repair, and eventually replace major parts of the system.

Most relationships sit between.

The strategic task is to know where.

A useful framework separates at least five layers.

**Possession.**

The buyer physically controls the platform.

**Operation.**

The buyer can use it without continuous external support.

**Sustainment.**

The buyer can repair, maintain, and replenish it.

**Modification.**

The buyer can integrate new components, software, or weapons.

**Substitution.**

The buyer can replace critical supplier dependencies over time.

A country can score high on possession and low on the other four.

Calling the system sovereign would hide more than it explains.

## Local production needs a functional test

Middle powers often pursue local production to move along this spectrum.

Local assembly is politically attractive.

It creates jobs and visible industrial participation.

But assembly is not the same as technological autonomy.

A factory can bolt together imported kits while remaining dependent on foreign engines, electronics, materials, software, and design authority.

This may still be valuable.

It builds workforce and maintenance capacity.

It may shorten repair chains.

It can create future learning.

But governments should distinguish symbolic localization from functional localization.

The relevant questions are what the local industry can do when the supplier is unavailable.

Can it diagnose faults?

Can it produce replacement parts?

Can it certify repairs?

Can it integrate new payloads?

Can it modify code?

Can it manufacture consumables?

Can it keep the platform operational through a disruption in the relationship?

Those capabilities determine real autonomy.

This suggests a **localization ladder**.

Assembly.

Maintenance.

Component manufacture.

Repair certification.

Software integration.

Subsystem design.

System integration.

Independent upgrade.

Independent replacement.

The higher the buyer climbs, the more bargaining leverage can survive after the original contract.

The ladder also prevents governments from treating every local-content percentage as strategically equivalent.

## Technology transfer must compound

Technology transfer has the same ambiguity.

The phrase sounds larger than it often is.

Training technicians is technology transfer.

Providing manufacturing drawings is technology transfer.

Allowing licensed production is technology transfer.

Sharing design methods is technology transfer.

Jointly developing a new subsystem is technology transfer.

These are not equivalent.

The buyer should negotiate for learning that compounds.

A one-time manufacturing package can disappear when the contract ends.

Engineering capability can be applied to the next program.

The strongest middle-power buyers therefore seek absorptive capacity, not merely local content.

They invest in domestic engineers, test facilities, software teams, certification bodies, and supply chains able to use what is transferred.

Without this, technology transfer can become technology theater.

The supplier shares information the buyer lacks the institutional capacity to convert into independence.

This points to a broader truth.

Procurement leverage depends on domestic capability.

A country with no local industrial base can still negotiate price.

It has less leverage over architecture.

A country with engineers, maintenance organizations, local suppliers, and credible alternatives can demand more because it can use what it receives.

Sovereignty has an absorptive dimension.

You cannot bargain effectively for knowledge you cannot operationalize.

A useful metric is **transfer conversion**.

How much transferred knowledge becomes independent domestic capability within a defined period?

Training completed is not enough.

Can local teams perform the function without supplier supervision?

Can they apply the knowledge to another program?

Can they certify their own work?

If not, the transfer may be useful but not autonomy-producing.

## Diversification creates an integration bill

Diversification is the most obvious middle-power strategy.

Buy aircraft from one country.

Air defense from another.

Drones from a third.

Ships from a fourth.

Domestic systems where possible.

The logic is simple.

No single supplier owns the relationship.

The practical problems are not simple.

Mixed fleets complicate logistics and training.

Different systems may not share data.

Weapons may not integrate.

Maintenance networks multiply.

Security restrictions can prevent one supplier's equipment from connecting to another's.

Operators learn several doctrines.

Spare-parts inventories become fragmented.

The buyer may discover that it replaced one large dependency with five smaller dependencies plus an integration problem.

Diversity is therefore not automatically autonomy.

Autonomy requires interoperability.

This creates a buyer's trilemma.

Depend deeply on one supplier and gain integration.

Diversify suppliers and gain political options.

Develop domestically and gain sovereignty at higher cost and risk.

Few middle powers can maximize all three.

The strategy is to choose where each matters most.

Some systems may justify deep alliance integration.

Others may justify diversified sourcing.

Some technologies may be worth building domestically because they create leverage across the whole force.

The procurement portfolio should reflect political risk as well as military performance.

The hidden variable is **integration cost**.

How much training, software, gateway infrastructure, maintenance complexity, cybersecurity risk, and operational delay does each additional supplier create?

At some point another supplier adds less autonomy than complexity.

The optimal portfolio is not maximum diversity.

It is enough supplier diversity to preserve credible alternatives without making the force itself incoherent.

## Build a procurement topology

A defense ministry should see its force as a **procurement topology**.

Platforms are nodes.

Munitions, data links, maintenance systems, training pipelines, software authorities, and supply chains are edges.

Color the graph by supplier jurisdiction.

Then ask:

Which foreign relationship touches the most mission-critical nodes?

Which apparently diverse platforms share one software, engine, munition, or maintenance dependency?

Which domestic system can bridge multiple suppliers?

Which gateway is a single point of failure?

Which supplier disruption would isolate several parts of the force at once?

The graph reveals concentration hidden by fleet diversity.

A country can buy from four suppliers and still have one critical dependency underneath all four.

## Procurement is a statecraft event

This is a difficult discipline because defense ministries naturally evaluate platforms through capability.

Foreign ministries think about relationships.

Finance ministries think about cost.

Industry ministries think about local production.

Leaders think about prestige and strategic signaling.

A major arms purchase sits at the intersection of all five.

This is why procurement decisions can become statecraft events.

Choosing a supplier sends a signal.

It can reassure one partner.

Alarm another.

Open access to training.

Close access to intelligence.

Create future exercises.

Change sanctions exposure.

Create domestic industrial coalitions.

The platform becomes a node in the country's diplomatic network.

The procurement process should therefore include a **foreign-policy dependency review** before final selection.

Not to let diplomats choose weapons.

To make cross-domain consequences visible.

Which relationships deepen?

Which alternatives narrow?

Which sanctions or export risks attach?

Which intelligence or training opportunities open?

Which future supplier decisions gain leverage over national policy?

Military performance remains central.

The lifecycle relationship belongs in the evaluation too.

## The arms market is an auction with memory

This makes the arms market a geopolitical auction.

Suppliers compete not only on weapon performance but on relationship package.

Financing.

Delivery speed.

Political conditions.

Training.

Technology transfer.

Local assembly.

Munitions access.

Upgrade rights.

Security cooperation.

Diplomatic support.

A middle power can use great-power rivalry to improve that package.

But auctions create another danger.

The buyer may maximize concessions in the short term and degrade strategic trust in the long term.

If every supplier believes the relationship is purely transactional, access to the most sensitive technology may narrow.

Deep technology sharing usually depends on expectations of future alignment.

This is the limit of the arms buyer's advantage.

The best capabilities are not always sold to the highest bidder.

They may be reserved for the most trusted partner.

Trust therefore becomes another currency in procurement.

A middle power can spend autonomy to gain access.

Or preserve autonomy and accept less integration.

Neither choice is inherently superior.

The strategic mistake is pretending the tradeoff does not exist.

This suggests a **reliability premium**.

A buyer known to keep agreements, protect technology, and maintain predictable defense relationships may receive better access than a buyer that constantly threatens supplier substitution for marginal gains.

Reliability is therefore an asset that can be spent.

The buyer should not maximize every auction if doing so reduces the quality of future auctions.

## Software-defined systems change the dependency clock

This becomes sharper in AI-enabled systems.

A conventional platform may have fixed performance at delivery.

A software-defined platform can change continuously.

Models update.

Sensors improve.

Threat libraries change.

New autonomy functions appear.

A supplier that controls the update pipeline controls part of the future capability.

The buyer therefore needs to ask what autonomy means in a system that evolves after purchase.

Can local teams inspect updates?

Can they reject one?

Can they train local models?

Can the platform operate without connection to supplier infrastructure?

Can domestic data improve the system without being exported?

Can the buyer integrate an alternative model later?

These questions will become as important as physical spare parts.

This creates an **update sovereignty** test.

Who decides the software baseline?

How long can the buyer operate on the current version?

Which vulnerabilities require supplier action?

Can the buyer validate an update independently?

Can it roll back?

Can the supplier disable a function remotely or contractually?

Can new operational data change the system without external review?

A platform can be sovereign physically and dependent temporally because every future capability improvement passes through another state or firm.

## Version divergence can split a fleet

If the buyer operates mixed suppliers or locally modified systems, software versions can diverge.

A fleet may share a platform name and no longer share the same operational behavior.

This creates certification and training problems.

The procurement system should maintain a **capability baseline ledger**.

Which version is on which platform?

Which munitions are certified?

Which data links work?

Which local modifications affect interoperability?

Which supplier support agreements apply?

The ledger prevents autonomy-enhancing modifications from quietly creating internal fragmentation.

## Data rights are future bargaining rights

Data rights may become the new munitions rights.

A weapon system that learns from operations generates valuable information.

Who owns that data?

Can the supplier use it elsewhere?

Can the buyer retain it locally?

Does the data improve future versions sold to other customers?

Can operational information expose tactics or vulnerabilities?

The buyer-supplier relationship extends into information sovereignty.

This can deepen dependence invisibly.

The system appears national.

Its learning loop may be transnational.

Data rights should therefore be negotiated across several categories.

Operational data.

Maintenance data.

Model-training data.

Telemetry.

Mission-planning data.

Failure logs.

Threat-library contributions.

The buyer may rationally share some of these because supplier learning improves the system.

The important issue is knowing what is shared, what can be retained, and what future dependency the sharing creates.

## Maintenance can become a remote veto

The same issue appears in maintenance.

Predictive maintenance can reduce downtime.

It may also depend on remote analytics controlled by the supplier.

If the service disappears, does the buyer retain enough diagnostic capability to operate?

The answer should be known before the crisis.

Again, fallback is autonomy.

A useful measure is **support-free endurance**.

How long can the force sustain a defined operational tempo without new supplier support?

Days?

Weeks?

Months?

Include:

Spare parts.

Munitions.

Software.

Diagnostics.

Repair certification.

Technical expertise.

Cryptographic support.

The number should be scenario-specific.

A peacetime training tempo and wartime tempo are different.

Support-free endurance is one of the clearest measures of lifecycle autonomy.

## Maintenance rights can be more valuable than assembly jobs

Political negotiations often emphasize local assembly because it is visible.

Long-run autonomy may depend more on repair authority.

Can local depots perform deep maintenance?

Can they manufacture or source approved parts?

Can they certify work?

Can they inspect software and diagnostics?

Can they keep test equipment current?

A local assembly line that closes after delivery may create less strategic value than a maintenance and engineering capability that remains for thirty years.

The buyer should bargain for the part of the lifecycle where supplier leverage would otherwise be strongest.

## Market scale can buy architecture

The arms buyer also has leverage through market scale.

Large orders can influence design.

A buyer that purchases enough systems may secure a customized variant, local manufacturing, or co-development role.

Smaller buyers can pool demand.

Regional procurement can create scale that individual states lack.

This is where middle-power cooperation can alter supplier relationships.

Several buyers agreeing on common requirements can bargain more effectively than each negotiating alone.

But pooled procurement requires political trust among the buyers.

Who chooses the standard?

Who hosts production?

Who receives jobs?

Who controls upgrades?

The same distributional politics that complicate alliances appear among middle powers too.

Market power has to be organized.

A useful metric is **requirement influence**.

How much of the system architecture changes because of the buyer's demand rather than merely the commercial configuration offered by the seller?

Large orders that secure no influence may still be militarily rational.

They should not be mistaken for strategic bargaining success.

## Munitions are the hidden supplier tether

Lifecycle autonomy also depends on munitions.

A platform without ammunition is a display object.

A buyer may diversify platforms while discovering that munitions remain controlled by one supplier.

Stockpile depth matters.

Compatibility matters.

Local production matters.

Export permissions matter.

Software authorization may matter.

This creates a hidden dependency layer.

The visible platform gets attention.

The consumables determine sustained use.

Sophisticated buyers therefore negotiate the whole kill chain and sustainment chain.

Sensors.

Data links.

Weapons.

Maintenance.

Training.

Repair.

Software.

The autonomy of the platform is only as strong as the least substitutable element.

This suggests a **least-substitutable-component test**.

What one dependency can stop the system even when everything else is available?

A missile.

A battery.

A cryptographic key.

A proprietary diagnostic device.

A software license.

An engine part.

The strategic objective is not to eliminate every single dependency.

It is to know which one can become a political veto and decide whether the military benefit justifies it.

## Switching cost is the true supplier leverage

This is why switching costs are the true measure of supplier leverage.

How expensive would it be to replace the system?

How long would it take?

What other systems would have to change with it?

How much retraining?

How much infrastructure?

How much political damage?

The higher the switching cost, the more the supplier relationship becomes strategic.

Buyers can reduce that cost through open interfaces, common munitions, local maintenance, modular architectures, and contractual data rights.

They cannot reduce it to zero.

Advanced military systems are inherently relational.

That is why defense trade creates alignment even when governments insist the purchase is purely commercial.

The relationship may not determine foreign policy.

It changes the cost of changing foreign policy.

That is enough.

A useful measure is **time to viable supplier exit**.

Not time to announce a new purchase.

Time until a replacement capability can perform the required mission at acceptable readiness.

The clock includes procurement.

Training.

Infrastructure.

Munitions.

Software.

Doctrine.

Certification.

Data links.

The longer the clock, the more political leverage the incumbent supplier possesses.

## Exercise supplier loss

A buyer should tabletop the loss of supplier support.

No new munitions.

No software updates.

No remote diagnostics.

Delayed spare parts.

Export license suspended.

Political relations deteriorating.

Then ask:

What still works tomorrow?

In thirty days?

In six months?

Which local capabilities become binding?

Which alternative supplier can help?

Which contract rights activate?

Which platform functions degrade first?

Which missions can be substituted?

This **supplier-loss exercise** converts procurement autonomy from contract language into operational understanding.

The result may show that dependence is acceptable.

That is fine.

The objective is conscious dependence.

## Procurement leverage has a half-life

The arms buyer's leverage does not disappear at signature.

It decays.

Call this **buyer-leverage half-life**.

The half-life is extended by:

Local sustainment.

Alternative suppliers.

Open interfaces.

Domestic engineering.

Large future order potential.

Data rights.

Competitive upgrade options.

Supplier reputation concerns.

The half-life shortens as switching cost rises and alternatives disappear.

A procurement strategy should therefore ask how much leverage remains five years after delivery, not only how much existed during negotiation.

That is the true measure of the arms buyer's advantage.

## The buyer’s autonomy ledger

These mechanisms can be combined into a **buyer autonomy ledger**.

For each major system, record:

Dependency passport.

Ownership layer.

Localization level.

Transfer conversion.

Supplier concentration.

Integration cost.

Update sovereignty.

Data rights.

Support-free endurance.

Least-substitutable component.

Time to viable supplier exit.

Buyer-leverage half-life.

Reliability premium.

Last supplier-loss exercise.

The ledger should be reviewed over the lifecycle because dependence changes.

A locally maintained platform may become more autonomous with time.

A software-defined platform may become more dependent if update authority centralizes.

A second supplier may enter.

A domestic munition may reduce one tether.

Autonomy is not fixed at contract signature.

It can improve or decay.

## The falsifiable test

The argument is testable.

If buyers with little local sustainment, closed interfaces, concentrated munitions, and high switching costs retain the same foreign-policy flexibility as buyers with deeper lifecycle control, then procurement dependence matters less than argued.

If diversified fleets consistently produce autonomy without major integration costs, the buyer's trilemma is overstated.

If technology transfer reliably creates domestic capacity regardless of absorptive institutions, transfer conversion deserves less emphasis.

The opposite pattern would strengthen the thesis.

If supplier leverage rises as support-free endurance falls, if political disputes reveal software or munitions vetoes, if buyers with local sustainment retain more choice, and if open interfaces or alternative suppliers shorten viable exit time, then arms procurement is indeed negotiated dependence.

## Negotiated dependence

The arms buyer's advantage therefore should be understood as negotiated dependence.

The buyer cannot eliminate dependence entirely without enormous domestic capability.

It can choose its shape.

Concentrated or diversified.

Transparent or hidden.

Reversible or sticky.

Operational or symbolic.

Deep or shallow.

The strategic buyer asks not only which weapon is best.

It asks which dependency is tolerable.

This is an uncomfortable way to think about procurement because military systems are sold through performance.

Range.

Speed.

Payload.

Accuracy.

Survivability.

The middle power must add another column.

Political reversibility.

Can this capability remain useful if the geopolitical relationship changes?

If the answer is no, the purchase may still be worthwhile.

But the alignment cost should be recognized rather than discovered later.

A middle power that can credibly choose among suppliers possesses leverage.

A middle power that can sustain those systems locally retains more of that leverage after purchase.

A middle power that can integrate diverse systems turns procurement plurality into strategic optionality.

A middle power that buys diversity without interoperability may simply purchase several dependencies instead of one.

The buyer's real advantage is not the ability to shop around.

It is the ability to keep shopping around after the first contract has been signed.

And the strongest buyer is the one that can keep saying yes to a useful defense relationship without making that yes permanent across every other domain of national policy.
