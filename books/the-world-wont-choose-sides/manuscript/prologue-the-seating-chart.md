# The Seating Chart

In early September 2026, leaders from China, Russia, India, and a collection of other Eurasian states gathered in Bishkek under the banner of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation. The easy way to read the photograph was as a bloc picture: a lineup of governments demonstrating that a non-Western order had acquired its own institutions, summits, vocabulary, and gravitational pull.

Then Turkey complicated the picture.

Recep Tayyip Erdoğan attended as the leader of a NATO member that had spent years cultivating a deeper relationship with the organisation. On his return, he said Turkey would consider full membership while insisting that closer ties with China and Russia did not mean turning away from the West. The sentence sounded contradictory only if the map in your head allowed one color per country.

India complicated it differently. New Delhi has built a dense relationship with Washington through defense cooperation, technology, the Quad, and a shared concern about China. It has also preserved relationships with Russia, participated in institutions where Beijing is central, and defended what Indian strategists call strategic autonomy. The point is not that India is equidistant from every power. It plainly is not. The point is that alignment can have a different answer depending on the question.

Security is one map. Energy is another. Technology, trade, capital, climate, food, shipping, voting coalitions, diplomatic legitimacy, and domestic politics each draw their own borders.

A country can therefore be close to the United States in one system and deeply exposed to China in another. It can buy weapons from several suppliers, host foreign forces, join a non-Western forum, sign a trade agreement with Europe, reject sanctions, cooperate on maritime security, and still regard all of those choices as parts of one coherent national strategy.

This is uncomfortable for great powers because great powers prefer clarity from other people.

Washington wants to know who will stand with it when competition becomes costly. Beijing wants to know which states will resist American containment. Moscow wants to know which partners will refuse Western isolation. Every major power has a reason to convert a relationship into a durable expectation.

Pivotal states have the opposite incentive. Their leverage often comes from keeping several doors open long enough that closing one becomes a negotiation rather than an assumption.

That does not make them neutral. Neutrality implies distance from the contest. Many middle powers are inside the contest, trading with it, arming within it, hosting parts of it, financing it, and sometimes being pressured by it. Their strategy is not to stand outside. It is to avoid letting one relationship consume the rest.

The term middle power can mislead here. Some of the states in this book are enormous. India is not middle-sized in population, military capability, economic potential, or ambition. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates can move capital and energy markets far beyond what their populations imply. Indonesia sits across sea lanes that connect the Indian and Pacific oceans. Turkey controls access between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean and belongs to NATO while pursuing a distinctly national regional strategy. Brazil carries continental scale and agricultural weight. South Africa has diplomatic and historical significance that cannot be reduced to GDP.

What they share is not a rank. It is a position between systems.

In the Cold War, nonalignment became a political identity as well as a strategy. Bandung in 1955 and the later Non-Aligned Movement gave newly independent states a language for refusing to become pieces on somebody else’s board. That history matters, especially because many Western accounts still treat alignment as the default and autonomy as the deviation.

The twenty-first-century version is more transactional and more networked. A government does not have to reject alliances to resist exclusive alignment. It can join one. Turkey already did. It can participate in a security partnership without accepting the foreign policy of its strongest partner. It can use a Chinese port, an American cloud, a European regulatory standard, a Gulf sovereign wealth fund, a Russian energy relationship, and a domestic technology stack at the same time.

That mix creates leverage. It also creates fragility.

Every additional relationship creates another dependency, another standard, another source of pressure, another constituency, another point where a crisis can force a choice that peacetime diplomacy had successfully postponed. Strategic autonomy is easiest when the costs of contradiction remain manageable. It becomes harder when sanctions, war, export controls, blockades, payment restrictions, or military interoperability turn compatibility into a binary question.

So the argument of this book is not that the world will never choose sides.

It is that the most important countries outside the two largest powers will resist choosing one side across every domain for as long as they can, and that their resistance will shape the balance of power itself.

Great-power competition is often narrated from Washington and Beijing outward. From that altitude, every state seems to be moving closer to one capital or the other.

From Jakarta, Riyadh, Ankara, New Delhi, Brasília, Pretoria, Abu Dhabi, or Hanoi, the geometry looks different. The question is not which superpower owns the future. It is which relationships can be combined without surrendering the freedom to make the next decision.

That is what the seating chart cannot show.
