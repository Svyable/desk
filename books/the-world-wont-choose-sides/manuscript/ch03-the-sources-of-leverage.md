# The Sources of Leverage

A middle power becomes pivotal when somebody else needs something it controls.

The asset may be obvious.

A port.

A market.

Oil.

Gas.

Minerals.

Military access.

Capital.

Votes.

Geography.

Industrial capacity.

The less obvious asset is legitimacy.

A coalition of great powers can look like a great-power project. Participation by states outside the core can change the political meaning of the same initiative.

That gives some governments bargaining power far beyond their military size.

Leverage comes from scarcity and alternatives.

If only one partner wants access, the state has limited bargaining room. If several do, competition raises the value of the asset.

This is why multipolarity can empower states without making them great powers.

They do not need to dominate the system.

They need credible options.

Credibility matters more than rhetoric.

A government can announce strategic autonomy while remaining dependent on one supplier, one market, one security guarantor, or one financial system. That is aspiration, not leverage.

Real leverage can be measured through switching costs.

Can the state move procurement?

Can it finance infrastructure elsewhere?

Can it sell energy to another buyer?

Can it deny access without risking regime-threatening cost?

Can it sustain disagreement long enough to improve the bargain?

The strongest middle powers often control several different assets at once.

India combines scale, geography, market potential, military weight, technology talent, and political legitimacy in the Global South.

Gulf states combine energy, capital, logistics, and diplomatic access.

Turkey controls geography, military capability, alliance membership, and regional connections.

Indonesia combines population, maritime position, resources, and the political weight of a major Southeast Asian state.

These portfolios create leverage because different great powers value different assets.

The state can bargain across domains.

But leverage has maintenance costs.

Ports require investment. Military capability requires spending. Capital depends on credible institutions. Diplomatic legitimacy can be damaged by inconsistency or domestic repression. Optionality disappears if every alternative becomes politically unacceptable.

Leverage is therefore not a possession.

It is a relationship between an asset and somebody else’s need.

The world’s pivotal states matter because the major powers keep creating reasons to need them.
