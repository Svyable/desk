# Alignment by Issue

The language of alignment still assumes a whole country moves at once.

Aligned.

Nonaligned.

Allied.

Neutral.

The categories are useful for treaties and dangerous for analysis.

A state can align with the United States on maritime security, buy energy from Russia, seek investment from the Gulf, trade heavily with China, vote with developing countries at the United Nations, and adopt technology rules borrowed from Europe.

None of these choices is necessarily temporary.

They can coexist because the underlying systems are different.

Security, trade, capital, technology, energy, and diplomacy do not travel through one hierarchy.

This makes alignment divisible.

The correct unit of analysis becomes the issue.

What is the decision?

Which dependency matters for that decision?

Which partner can provide an alternative?

What domestic coalition must the government satisfy?

What would make switching costly?

These questions produce a more accurate map than asking which side the country is “on.”

Issue alignment also explains why great powers often feel betrayed by states that do not believe they changed sides.

Washington may view a security partnership as evidence of broader strategic convergence.

The partner may view it as a transaction in one domain.

Beijing can make the same mistake when economic dependence is interpreted as political deference.

The mismatch creates pressure.

Great powers want portfolio loyalty.

Middle powers often want portfolio diversification.

The strategic autonomy of a state therefore depends partly on its ability to keep domains separated.

That becomes harder as technology connects them.

A cloud contract can become a security issue. A semiconductor rule can become a trade issue. A weapons purchase can create long-term software dependence. A payment system can become a sanctions vulnerability.

The borders among domains are thickening and dissolving at the same time.

This is why selective alignment is not guaranteed to last.

It has to be maintained.

Governments must continuously decide which dependencies they are willing to accept and which alternatives are worth paying for.

Alignment by issue is not indecision.

It is a strategy of keeping more than one network available.
