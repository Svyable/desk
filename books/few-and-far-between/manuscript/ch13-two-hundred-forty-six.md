# Two Hundred Forty-Six

Two hundred forty-six is not a mystical number.

It is a certificate.

The Polymath8b work established an unconditional bound H_1≤246, in the notation where H_1 is the liminf of the difference between consecutive primes. In ordinary language: infinitely often, two consecutive primes occur within 246 of one another.

The number matters because it is explicit. Its deeper importance is logical. There exists a fixed finite interval length, independent of how far out on the number line we go, that captures at least two primes infinitely often.

This is now a theorem.

The temptation is to stare at the distance from 246 down to 2. But before doing that, notice how much infinity is packed into the statement.

Average prime spacing near x grows like log x without bound. At fantastically large x, a typical local gap is much larger than 246. Yet the theorem says some pairs keep returning inside this tiny absolute window. Relative to the ambient average, these pairs become closer and closer.

The primes thin globally while preserving bounded local intimacy.

This is the central paradox of the title.

Few and far between.

Not always.

There is a simple pigeonhole consequence worth spelling out. Because only finitely many even differences up to 246 are possible, at least one such difference occurs infinitely often between primes in the bounded-gap configurations. The theorem does not tell us which difference. The twin prime conjecture says 2 works. The bounded-gap theorem says somebody in the finite room works.

That is a curious form of knowledge.

Imagine being told that one of 123 locked boxes contains a letter and that the same box contains the letter infinitely many times in an infinite sequence of rooms, but the proof does not identify the box. Existence is secure. Identity is withheld.

Mathematics is comfortable with this.

Human intuition is not.

We like named mechanisms. A theorem that says “there exists an even h≤246” can feel incomplete even though it is absolute. But nonconstructive or partially identifying conclusions are common across mathematics. The bounded-gap case is especially vivid because the candidate set is so concrete.

2, 4, 6, ..., 246.

One of these differences recurs infinitely often in the relevant sense.

Which one?

The theorem is silent.

Could further optimization reduce 246? Almost certainly within variants of the known machinery, at least by some amount, if one invests enough effort. But there is no known reason that incremental optimization will reach 2. Structural barriers matter more as the constant approaches the conjectural endpoint.

This is why “only 244 away” is the wrong metric.

The distance between 246 and 2 is measured in missing ideas.

The conditional world makes this clearer. Under stronger distribution hypotheses, particularly variants of Elliott–Halberstam, the sieve machinery yields dramatically smaller bounds. These results are valuable because they map the dependency: if primes are distributed in arithmetic progressions as strongly as conjectured, then certain close-gap conclusions follow.

But a conditional theorem is not a forecast of when the unconditional proof will arrive.

It is a statement about logical implication.

The bounded-gap numbers also illuminate the role of admissible tuples. To force two primes into a short interval, one chooses a collection of candidate shifts packed as tightly as possible while avoiding modular obstructions. The diameter of a good admissible k-tuple contributes directly to the final gap bound.

This creates a finite combinatorial subproblem inside an infinite analytic one.

Find narrow admissible tuples.

Computers are excellent at this.

Proof still has to show that infinitely many translates contain enough primes.

This division of labor is characteristic of modern number theory. A computational search can optimize the finite configuration. Analytic estimates certify infinite recurrence. Neither substitutes for the other.

The number 246 therefore contains several layers of evidence:

a theorem about weighted sieves,

distribution of primes in arithmetic progressions,

optimization of multidimensional test functions,

construction of admissible tuples,

and computational assistance.

When a single constant is quoted without this architecture, it looks arbitrary.

It is not arbitrary.

It is contingent.

Another method might produce another bound from another optimization problem. If twin primes are eventually proved, 246 will become historically obsolete as a record and permanently important as a milestone. Like seventy million, it will retain the shape of the method that first reached it.

There is a broader epistemological point here. Science often wants parameter estimates close to reality. Mathematics often values a grotesquely weak quantitative bound if it proves a qualitatively new regime.

A theorem that a phenomenon happens before 10^(10^100) can be profound if the previous knowledge did not establish it happened at all.

Prime gaps dramatize this inversion.

Seventy million was revolutionary.

246 is sharper.

2 is still conjectural.

The logical hierarchy does not align with ordinary numerical taste.

This is useful discipline beyond mathematics. People routinely confuse more precise estimates with more secure claims. A number with six decimal places can be epistemically weaker than a coarse inequality derived from first principles. Prime-gap history trains the eye to ask what assumptions purchased the number.

What does 246 assume?

No unproved distribution conjecture.

That is why it matters.

There is one final reason to keep the constant around. It resists myth.

If this book simply said “mathematicians made great progress toward twin primes,” the phrase would be too elastic. 246 fixes the progress to a theorem. It tells us both how far methods have come and exactly what they still fail to do.

The best numbers in intellectual history are sometimes boundary markers.

246 is one.

On one side: no unconditional fixed bound known before 2013.

On the other: a finite window proven to recur forever.

Beyond it, two waits.
