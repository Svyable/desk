# The Bound Starts Falling

Once Zhang’s paper appeared, the bound became communal property.

The first improvements arrived quickly enough that the number itself became news. Seventy million dropped to tens of millions, then millions, then hundreds of thousands, then thousands. Each reduction came from some mixture of sharper analysis, better parameter choices, and more efficient admissible tuples.

A casual history can turn this into a sports ticker. That loses the most interesting part.

The falling bound was a diagnostic instrument.

Every improvement asked where the proof was wasting room. Was the admissible tuple too wide? Was a distribution estimate being used with unnecessary uniformity? Could a sieve weight be optimized numerically? Could an inequality be made asymmetrical? Could a combinatorial search find denser permissible constellations?

The constant became a way of interrogating the proof.

This is one reason numerical optimization belongs inside pure mathematics more often than the traditional image of the field suggests. A proof can be exact while containing parameters that must be chosen. The quality of those choices can change the final theorem dramatically. Computation can search an optimization landscape even when the theorem itself remains symbolic.

The Polymath8 project made this visible in public. Blog posts and wiki pages accumulated comments from experts and nonexperts, calculations, discarded routes, code, references, questions that exposed hidden assumptions, and small improvements that would have been too granular for individual publication.

This mode of work is awkward to mythologize because authorship diffuses.

The old story wants a single name attached to an idea. Collaborative mathematics often produces a chain in which no link looks decisive alone. One person notices a parameter can be relaxed. Another proves the needed lemma. A third writes code that finds a better tuple. Someone else catches a sign error. The resulting theorem belongs to the network.

Prime gaps were especially suited to this because Zhang’s architecture had many tunable parts. Once the qualitative wall had fallen, optimization could be modular.

The first Polymath phase eventually improved the Zhang-method bound on H_1—the liminf distance between consecutive primes—to 4,680. That was already a reduction by four orders of magnitude from seventy million.

Then Maynard’s work arrived.

The important point is chronological. Polymath had not merely been polishing a result that would obviously continue down to two. It had been exploring one architecture when a different architecture changed the feasible region. Maynard’s sieve gave 600 for pairs in his original paper and handled multiple primes in bounded intervals. Polymath pivoted.

This is how live research actually behaves. A project acquires sunk costs. Then somebody changes the problem.

Good collaborations know when to abandon optimization of the old machine.

The pair bound eventually reached 246 in the Polymath8b work. That number is still widely quoted because it is concrete. But the project generated more than a number. It explored conditional bounds under Elliott–Halberstam and generalized versions of it, refined admissible tuple computations, and created a detailed public record of the proof-development process.

The retrospective paper on the project describes the progression explicitly: Zhang’s H_1≤70,000,000; Polymath improvements to 4,680; Maynard’s independent new argument with 600; then further Polymath work to 246.

This sequence is useful because it prevents a false continuity.

70,000,000 → 4,680 was primarily the Zhang/GPY machine under collective optimization.

4,680 → 600 was not just a better search. It reflected Maynard’s new sieve design.

600 → 246 again involved optimization and combination.

The numerical line is smooth. The conceptual line has joints.

Mathematics is full of this. A graph of best-known constants can hide changes in method as profound as changes in scientific paradigm. If one studies only records, one misses theory.

There is also a subtle evidentiary point. A best-known bound is not the same thing as the true value of H_1. The twin prime conjecture predicts H_1=2. The theorem H_1≤246 says the true liminf is somewhere among a finite set of even possibilities no larger than 246. It does not assign probabilities among them. It does not say 246 is remotely typical. It is a certificate that the unknown answer lies below a ceiling.

Record bounds are artifacts of methods.

This matters when people interpret progress. Suppose the bound had stalled at 4,680 forever. That would not be evidence that the true value is near 4,680. It would be evidence about what the method could prove.

A theorem’s constant mixes two things: reality and proof technology.

Untangling them is part of reading mathematics well.

The public nature of Polymath adds another layer. Because discussions were visible, one can watch the sociology of proof. Ideas that would normally disappear into notebooks or private email remain archived. Wrong turns are observable. Priority becomes more granular and less theatrical.

This is particularly relevant now, when machine-assisted mathematics is beginning to alter the search process. Polymath showed a pre-AI version of distributed cognition: many humans plus computation plus a shared memory surface. Future proof development may add automated conjecture generation, symbolic verification, formal proof assistants, and code agents. The question of who “had the idea” will become even less clean.

Prime gaps got there early.

Still, collaboration did not dissolve individual contribution. Zhang’s distribution theorem remained Zhang’s. Maynard’s sieve remained a specific conceptual leap. Tao’s parallel insight mattered. The collective work mattered too.

The productive unit was neither solitary genius nor anonymous crowd.

It was an ecology.

That ecology had a measurable output: 246.

The danger is to worship the output and forget the ecology.

The number is memorable because it is small enough to visualize. Put two primes somewhere inside a stretch of 246 integers. Do that infinitely often. We can prove this.

But the real achievement is that a problem once separated from proof by an undefined distance became a system of levers one could pull in public.

The bound fell because the proof became inspectable.

That may be the more enduring pattern.
