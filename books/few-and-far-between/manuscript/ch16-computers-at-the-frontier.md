# Computers at the Frontier

A computer can find the next prime.

It cannot, merely by continuing, reach infinity.

That banal fact explains the proper place of computation in prime-gap research.

Computers are indispensable for discovering record gaps, testing Hardy–Littlewood predictions, optimizing admissible tuples, evaluating sieve integrals numerically, checking finite cases, and exploring residue-class constructions. Yet the central theorems in this book make statements about infinitely many primes or asymptotic behavior. No finite search can settle them by accumulation alone.

The resulting division of labor is unusually clean.

Computation explores.

Proof certifies.

But the boundary is not as simple as that slogan.

Sometimes computation enters the proof through a finite verification. If a theorem reduces an infinite statement to checking a finite list of configurations and software checks them with auditable rigor, the computation is part of the proof. Sometimes numerical optimization identifies a parameter choice later bounded analytically. Sometimes code discovers an admissible tuple whose properties are trivially verifiable once printed.

The epistemic status depends on the role.

This matters especially for prime-gap records.

Suppose a computation finds an enormous gap between two consecutive primes at some height. The result is a fact about that location if primality and consecutiveness have been rigorously certified. It becomes a record relative to all smaller checked ranges if the search is exhaustive and auditable. It does not establish an asymptotic law for maximal gaps.

Record hunting and theorem proving answer different questions.

The internet often collapses them.

A headline announcing “largest prime gap found” sounds like progress on Cramér’s conjecture. It may provide useful data about extreme-value heuristics, but one record cannot determine a limsup. Even millions of records over growing ranges can remain compatible with several asymptotic regimes.

The same caution applies to twin primes.

Computers have found twin-prime pairs of immense size. Their existence is not in doubt. Infinitude is.

This is perhaps the purest example of why mathematical induction from data is not mathematical induction. The theorem does not care how persuasive the trend looks. A counterexample may lie beyond every checked range, or the pattern may simply stop after an unimaginably large threshold.

Number theory is full of cautionary examples. Polynomials can output primes for long initial stretches and then become composite. Inequalities verified to astronomical heights can eventually fail. Prime races can maintain a bias for immense ranges before reversing.

Finite evidence has to be interpreted through theory.

Yet theory also needs computation.

The Polymath bounded-gap work used computational searches for narrow admissible tuples. This finite optimization directly affected the explicit bound. If analytic machinery says a sufficiently large admissible k-tuple will contain at least two primes infinitely often, then reducing the diameter of the best known k-tuple reduces the prime-gap ceiling.

Here computation translates into theorem constants.

It is not merely illustration.

Modern work can push this further. Numerical optimization of sieve test functions can suggest near-extremal shapes. Semidefinite programming, interval arithmetic, symbolic integration and rigorous numerics can convert approximate exploration into certified inequalities. Formal proof systems can in principle check increasingly large parts of the argument.

The prime-gap frontier is therefore a natural laboratory for machine-assisted mathematics.

There are at least four machine roles.

Search: find good tuples, coverings, parameter sets or candidate lemmas.

Calculation: evaluate integrals, constants, error terms and numerical thresholds.

Verification: check algebra, finite cases, or formalized proofs.

Navigation: search literature and connect methods across papers.

The fourth role is where modern AI systems may matter most quickly. Prime-gap research is dense with conditional dependencies. A model that can accurately map which theorem supplies which level of distribution under which modulus restriction could save human time. But hallucination is uniquely dangerous here. One invented lemma can poison an entire proof architecture.

Mathematics gives AI no mercy.

A citation either contains the claim or it does not.

An inequality either follows or it does not.

A formal checker is a useful final court, but most creative mathematics still lives before formalization, in the choice of definitions and intermediate objects. That is exactly where unreliable generated text can sound most convincing.

Prime gaps therefore make a good test for future human-machine research relationships. The subject rewards aggressive conjecture generation but punishes epistemic sloppiness.

A machine can suggest that a parameter regime should work.

The proof must still close.

There is also an aesthetic question. If a future theorem depends on a computer search over billions of configurations, has understanding been lost?

Sometimes yes.

Sometimes the search reveals a structure that can later be compressed.

Sometimes the finite search is itself the right explanation because the landscape genuinely has many cases.

Mathematics has no rule that every truth must have a short human-readable reason.

But mathematicians understandably prefer proofs that tell them why.

Prime gaps have already experienced this tension in modest form. A narrower admissible tuple is useful, but the conceptual breakthrough lies in why admissible tuples matter. A better numerical sieve weight improves a bound, but the conceptual breakthrough lies in the multidimensional architecture.

Records are not the same as reasons.

The best work connects them.

Computers are exceptionally good at the records.

Humans still care about the reasons.

That division may not last forever, but it describes the present frontier better than either techno-utopian or nostalgic stories do.

For now, the machine can walk very far down the number line.

Infinity remains a proof obligation.
