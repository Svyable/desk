# Polymath at Work

A theorem usually arrives after its mess has been removed.

The paper contains definitions, lemmas, proof. Failed attempts disappear. Numerical experiments may be compressed to a sentence. Questions that consumed a week vanish if the answer becomes obvious in hindsight. The finished object is intentionally unlike the process that made it.

Polymath left the process lying around.

The bounded-gaps projects unfolded through public blog discussions, shared documents, code, and formal writeups under the collective D. H. J. Polymath name. The archive records mathematics in an intermediate state: partly checked, partly speculative, sometimes wrong, often surprisingly efficient.

This matters because prime gaps were unusually well suited to decomposed collaboration.

Zhang’s theorem had analytic estimates to sharpen.

Admissible tuples had to be optimized.

Sieve parameters could be searched.

Conditional variants could be separated.

Computational results could feed back into proof choices.

The work could be parallelized without becoming incoherent.

The common stereotype of collaborative mathematics is that many people cannot think deeply about one proof because the cognitive context is too large. Polymath did not abolish that constraint. Instead it externalized context. Blog posts and wiki pages became working memory.

This is not merely sociology. It affected which questions could be asked.

A single researcher deciding whether a tiny constant improvement is worth several days may reasonably say no. In a distributed project, somebody else may already have the relevant code or lemma. Small gains accumulate. More importantly, one participant’s offhand observation can redirect another’s expertise.

The bounded-gap retrospective reports that the first Polymath phase pushed H_1 from Zhang’s seventy million to 4,680. After Maynard’s sieve entered the picture, the project helped push the unconditional pair bound to 246 and explored stronger conditional results.

These outputs are easy to cite.

The input was conversation.

Mathematics has always been conversational. Letters between mathematicians, seminars, blackboards, private correspondence and conferences have shaped proofs for centuries. What changed was persistence and access. A public thread leaves a searchable trace of the unfinished argument.

That trace can reveal how expert judgment works.

One sees participants distinguishing a likely dead end from an estimate worth chasing. One sees numerical evidence used as triage rather than proof. One sees people translate between analytic and combinatorial formulations. One sees a conjectural calculation harden into a lemma after somebody notices the right normalization.

This is a different epistemic object from a paper.

The paper certifies.

The discussion navigates.

Both are necessary.

Polymath also forces a difficult question about credit. Traditional authorship compresses contribution into a list of names. Collective authorship avoids ranking dozens of micro-contributions, but it can also flatten meaningful differences. Some participants contribute central ideas; others computations, corrections, exposition, or references.

There is no perfect accounting system.

Prime gaps made the problem visible before AI systems made it unavoidable.

If future mathematical research uses language models to search literature, formal provers to verify steps, symbolic engines to optimize inequalities, and human teams to choose directions, what counts as authorship? The bounded-gap Polymath archive offers an early model in which contribution is already modular and partly machine-mediated through computation.

The right answer may not be one new attribution rule. It may be more detailed provenance.

For this book, the important point is methodological. The hunt for prime-gap patterns became faster when the field stopped insisting that discovery and exposition share the same format.

Discovery could be noisy.

Exposition could remain exact.

That separation protects rigor rather than weakening it.

There is a romantic fear that collaborative optimization produces incrementalism. Sometimes it does. But the bounded-gap story contains the opposite example. Public optimization of Zhang’s framework was underway when Maynard introduced a genuinely new sieve architecture. The project did not reject the discontinuity; it reorganized around it.

Healthy collaboration has to be able to admit that yesterday’s best plan is obsolete.

That is harder than sharing code.

There is another reason Polymath belongs in a book about patterns. The project itself was a search for patterns in proof space.

Which admissible tuples are efficient?

Which parameter regimes recur?

Which inequalities bind?

Which parts of Zhang’s argument are robust under modification?

Once one starts optimizing a proof, the proof becomes an object of empirical study. Run computations. Sweep parameters. Compare constructions. Guess the extremizer. Then prove what the experiments suggest.

Pure mathematics has always contained this loop, but the public record makes it harder to pretend otherwise.

Computation did not reduce the theorem to experiment. It changed where human attention was spent.

This is a distinction worth carrying into every discussion of machine-assisted mathematics. A computer can generate a million numerical cases without proving the infinite claim. But those cases can reveal where a proof might live. Optimization can identify candidate constants. Formal systems can remove uncertainty from verification. None of these activities is identical to conceptual explanation.

The bounded-gap work used each kind of knowledge where it fit.

That pragmatism is one of its quiet achievements.

There is a temptation, after seeing a project like Polymath succeed, to declare a new era and predict that solitary mathematics is over. That would be silly. Zhang’s theorem itself is evidence against the claim. Maynard’s key idea was not crowd-sourced into existence.

Research ecosystems need different cognitive tempos.

Some problems reward private obsession.

Some reward public decomposition.

Some require both in sequence.

Prime gaps did.

The lone paper created a shared problem.

The crowd improved it.

Another lone idea changed the machine.

The crowd improved that.

If there is a pattern here, it is not that collaboration wins.

It is that the method of collaboration should fit the mathematical object as carefully as the sieve weight fits the tuple.
