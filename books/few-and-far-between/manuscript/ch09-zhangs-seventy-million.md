# Zhang’s Seventy Million

Yitang Zhang’s first bound was seventy million.

This number should be printed large because history has a habit of shrinking it after the fact.

The theorem Zhang submitted in April 2013 showed that there are infinitely many pairs of distinct primes whose difference is less than 70,000,000. More precisely, in the language of consecutive primes, he proved the liminf of p_{n+1}-p_n is below 7×10^7. For the first time, an unconditional finite ceiling existed.

Seventy million is absurdly larger than two.

It was also enough to end a drought of centuries.

The proof used the GPY framework, but the crucial new ingredient was a strengthened form of Bombieri–Vinogradov for a special family of moduli. Zhang did not prove the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture. He did not establish a general level of distribution above 1/2 for all moduli. He showed that for moduli free of large prime factors—smooth moduli—one could get the distributional leverage GPY needed.

That is the empirical core of the breakthrough, and it is worth protecting from the myth that Zhang simply “found a clever trick.”

He found a restricted theorem strong enough to substitute for a conjectural general theorem in one application.

The restriction mattered because the sieve could be arranged to use those moduli.

This is the kind of fit between analytic estimate and combinatorial architecture that outsiders rarely see when a result is compressed to a headline. The theorem is not a single idea. It is a negotiated settlement among several pieces of mathematics, each with tolerances.

Zhang’s personal story amplified the attention. He had held temporary and nontraditional academic positions, including work outside the standard research ladder, before joining the University of New Hampshire. Accounts of the breakthrough often turned him into a fable about the overlooked genius. The facts are dramatic enough without embroidery. What matters for the mathematics is that the paper survived expert scrutiny and altered the frontier almost immediately.

The speed of the response was extraordinary.

Once a finite bound existed, mathematicians could attack every source of slack. Better admissible tuples reduced diameter. Sharper estimates improved parameters. Computation searched for efficient configurations. Terence Tao launched what became the Polymath8 project, a public collaborative effort to optimize Zhang’s method.

The bound began falling almost in real time.

This is where seventy million becomes easy to misunderstand. The later reductions can make the original constant look crude, as if Zhang had done a first draft. But a breakthrough constant often contains deliberate safety margins. The first job is to make the machine run. Once existence is established, specialists can disassemble it and optimize.

Airplanes did not become less revolutionary when later airplanes flew farther.

The proof also changed what counted as a sensible question. Before 2013, asking for the best unconditional fixed bound between infinitely many prime pairs was premature because no finite bound was known. After Zhang, the problem became numerical enough to invite optimization.

This transition from qualitative ignorance to quantitative competition is one of the recurring signatures of mathematical breakthroughs.

A new theorem creates a scoreboard.

The scoreboard can then become a distraction.

The bound 70,000,000 fell so quickly that popular coverage sometimes treated the story like a race to two. But there was no reason to believe parameter optimization alone would reach the twin-prime conjecture. Every sieve architecture has structural limits. Shrinking a constant is valuable, especially because the process exposes which inequalities are wasteful, but a descending sequence of bounds does not imply convergence to the conjectured optimum.

Then James Maynard changed the game.

His 2013 preprint, followed by the 2015 Annals paper, introduced a refinement of the GPY sieve that proved a bound of 600 for pairs and, more importantly, showed that bounded intervals could contain arbitrarily many primes infinitely often. Terence Tao independently found a similar multidimensional idea. The Polymath project absorbed the new method and pushed the pair bound to 246.

The path from seventy million to 246 therefore contains a phase transition of its own. Part was optimization of Zhang. Part was a new sieve.

It is tempting to ask what Zhang “really proved” if the bound was soon superseded. He proved bounded gaps.

That theorem does not expire when the constant improves.

The 2014 Annals paper states the qualitative result cleanly:

liminf (p_{n+1}-p_n) < 7×10^7.

The left-hand side is finite.

That is the shock.

Everything after the inequality sign is the first price paid for the proof.

There is a human lesson here too, though mathematics does not require one. Research communities often reward polished programs built around known techniques. Frontier work sometimes comes from somebody willing to spend years on a narrow obstruction whose payoff is uncertain. Zhang reportedly worked on the problem largely alone. Polymath then demonstrated almost the opposite mode: rapid, public, distributed improvement.

The same theorem family contains solitude and crowd intelligence.

That contrast will matter later when we discuss how mathematics changes when proof becomes more collaborative and computational. Prime gaps were an early public demonstration that online coordination could produce serious research, not merely commentary.

But before the crowd came the number.

70,000,000.

A terrible approximation to two.

A magnificent approximation to infinity.
