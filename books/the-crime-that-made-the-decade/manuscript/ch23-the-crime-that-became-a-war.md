# Chapter 23 — The Crime That Became a War

## 1996–2005: September 11 and the reorganization of the American state

Nineteen hijackers committed a crime so large that the United States answered with war.

That sentence contains the problem of the decade.

On September 11, 2001, al Qaeda operatives hijacked four commercial airplanes. American Airlines Flight 11 and United Airlines Flight 175 struck the World Trade Center towers in New York. American Airlines Flight 77 struck the Pentagon. Passengers and crew on United Airlines Flight 93 fought the hijackers; the plane crashed in Pennsylvania before reaching the attackers' intended target in Washington.

Nearly three thousand people were killed.

The crimes included murder, aircraft piracy, terrorism, conspiracy, destruction, and attacks on federal institutions. They were also understood as an act of war by a transnational organization operating from abroad.

The United States invaded Afghanistan to destroy al Qaeda's sanctuary and remove the Taliban government that had hosted it. Congress enacted the USA PATRIOT Act. The federal government created the Department of Homeland Security and reorganized intelligence. Airports became security checkpoints. Surveillance authorities expanded. Military detention and interrogation policies produced years of legal and moral controversy. The Iraq War followed in 2003 under a separate set of arguments in which the trauma of September 11 shaped the political environment even though Iraq had not carried out the attacks.

No other crime in the first 250 years of the United States produced a larger immediate redesign of national institutions.

### The morning of ordinary systems

The attackers did not arrive in bombers.

They boarded scheduled commercial flights.

That detail explains much of the shock.

The systems they used were ordinary: visas, flight schools, airline tickets, airport screening, domestic transportation, financial transfers, hotel rooms, driver's licenses, telephones.

The attack converted civilian infrastructure into weapons.

This is the same structural trick as Tylenol at a radically larger scale.

Trust is necessary for the system to function.

The attacker uses the trust.

Before September 11, commercial aviation security was designed around a threat model shaped by earlier hijackings. Cockpit doors, passenger screening, baggage rules, and law-enforcement assumptions reflected expectations that no longer matched a suicide hijacking designed to turn the aircraft itself into a missile.

The system was optimized for the last crime.

### “The system was blinking red”

The 9/11 Commission later reconstructed a disturbing preattack picture.

American intelligence knew al Qaeda wanted to attack the United States. Agencies held fragments involving future hijackers. Watch-listing and information-sharing failures prevented those fragments from becoming operational prevention. FBI offices encountered suspicious activity that did not connect effectively to national intelligence.

The Commission described four broad failures: imagination, policy, capabilities, and management.

That formulation is unusually valuable because it refuses the comforting theory that one missed memo caused September 11.

Complex systems fail through interaction.

An agency can possess information and lack authority.

Another can possess authority and lack information.

A warning can be accurate but nonspecific.

An analyst can see a pattern without a process for escalating it.

A bureaucracy can be organized around foreign and domestic distinctions attackers do not respect.

The failure is not that nobody knew anything.

The failure is that the knowledge did not become coordinated action in time.

### Crime or war?

September 11 forced a category decision.

The United States had prosecuted international terrorists in federal courts before. The 1993 World Trade Center bombers were criminal defendants. Embassy-bombing conspirators were prosecuted. Terrorism fit criminal law.

But September 11 was also an organized external attack by a group based abroad, causing casualties on the scale of military operations and targeting the Pentagon as well as civilians.

Congress authorized the use of military force against those responsible and those who harbored them. The Bush administration treated the conflict as an armed struggle against al Qaeda and associated forces.

The choice changed everything.

A criminal suspect has rights within a judicial system.

An enemy combatant can be detained under laws of war.

Police gather evidence to prosecute.

Military forces gather intelligence and neutralize threats.

Courts impose sentences.

Wars may end without trials.

Modern counterterrorism began operating in both frameworks simultaneously.

That duality produced recurring legal conflict over detention, military commissions, habeas corpus, targeted force, interrogation, surveillance, and the geographic reach of war powers.

The crime became a war without ceasing to be crime.

### The airport becomes a border

One of September 11's most universal afterlives is the airport security checkpoint.

Congress created the Transportation Security Administration. Passenger screening became a federal responsibility. Cockpit doors were reinforced. Screening rules evolved through later attempted attacks involving shoes, liquids, and explosives concealed on bodies or in luggage.

Domestic air travel acquired a ritual once associated more strongly with crossing national borders.

Identification.

Search.

Restricted liquids.

Prohibited objects.

Behavior observation.

Secure zones.

The physical experience of travel became a memorial system no plaque announces.

Every passenger rehearses the afterlife.

### The Department of Homeland Security

In 2002, Congress created the Department of Homeland Security, combining twenty-two federal entities or components into a cabinet department intended to improve coordination around domestic security.

The scale of reorganization was extraordinary.

Customs, immigration functions, emergency management, transportation security, Coast Guard operations, protective missions, infrastructure security, and other responsibilities were pulled into a new architecture.

The logic was unity of effort.

The risk was bureaucratic recombination.

Moving boxes on an organizational chart does not automatically create information sharing. Large departments generate their own boundaries, cultures, and coordination problems.

September 11 therefore illustrates a common institutional response to failure: when fragmentation is blamed, government consolidates.

Later failures test whether consolidation solved the right problem.

### The intelligence state expands

The USA PATRIOT Act and subsequent intelligence reforms expanded or altered authorities involving surveillance, information sharing, financial investigation, material support, roving wiretaps, business records, and other tools. The Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004 created the Director of National Intelligence and the National Counterterrorism Center.

These measures addressed real failures.

They also created civil-liberties conflicts that continue.

Security powers rarely remain mentally attached to the emergency that created them. Once legal tools exist, agencies find other problems they can solve.

The democratic test is therefore not only whether a power was reasonable on September 12, 2001.

It is whether the power remains justified, bounded, audited, and correctable years later.

Crime creates urgency.

Constitutional government has to create expiration, review, or constraint.

### Guantánamo and the cost of exceptional categories

The United States detained terrorism suspects at Guantánamo Bay, Cuba, beginning in 2002. The administration initially argued that detainees could be held with limited access to federal courts. Supreme Court decisions over subsequent years rejected broad versions of those claims and recognized judicial roles in reviewing detention and military commission structures.

The legal story is too large for one chapter, but its relevance is direct.

September 11 created pressure to build a category of person outside ordinary criminal procedure because ordinary procedure was perceived as insufficient to wartime threat.

Exceptional categories can solve operational problems.

They also tempt governments to treat uncertainty as permission.

Who is actually an enemy combatant?

What evidence proves it?

How long can detention last?

What process tests mistakes?

Those are the Vesey questions in global counterterrorism form: preventive power acting before the future harm can be observed, under conditions where the state controls much of the evidence.

### The Iraq problem

September 11 and the Iraq War must be connected carefully.

Al Qaeda carried out the September 11 attacks.

Saddam Hussein's Iraq did not.

The Bush administration's case for invading Iraq centered on alleged weapons of mass destruction, enforcement of United Nations resolutions, regime conduct, and broader security arguments. Public fear after September 11 made worst-case reasoning politically powerful, and administration rhetoric sometimes placed Iraq inside a broader terrorism frame.

No large stockpiles of the anticipated weapons were found.

The distinction matters because criminal afterlife includes decisions justified in the atmosphere a crime creates even when those decisions are not direct responses to the perpetrator.

Trauma changes the burden of proof.

After a catastrophic intelligence failure, officials become afraid of the cost of underestimating the next threat.

The system can overcorrect from false negative to false positive.

September 11's afterlife therefore includes not only failures to imagine danger but dangers imagined too confidently afterward.

### Why September 11 over Enron

The decade contains Enron, WorldCom, the Catholic Church abuse scandal's criminal dimensions, the D.C. sniper attacks, corporate accounting fraud, cybercrime, and other events with enormous institutional consequences.

None comes close under the book's selection rule.

September 11 changed war policy, intelligence architecture, aviation, immigration, border systems, federal organization, surveillance, criminal law, diplomacy, military detention, emergency response, and the daily behavior of hundreds of millions of people.

It is the rare obvious choice that becomes more important under scrutiny rather than less.

### The criminal afterlife

The attacks lasted hours.

The wars lasted decades.

The security architecture remains.

The Department of Homeland Security still exists. TSA checkpoints remain. Intelligence-sharing structures remain. Surveillance debates remain. The legal consequences of detention and war powers remain. The memorials remain. The health effects on responders and survivors remain.

September 11 is therefore the crime whose afterlife most clearly escaped criminal law.

The state did not merely investigate.

It reorganized.

It invaded.

It surveilled.

It detained.

It legislated.

It built agencies.

It changed doors, databases, cockpit locks, visas, checkpoints, and the meaning of the word “homeland.”

Nineteen men boarded four airplanes.

The country that landed afterward had a different government.
