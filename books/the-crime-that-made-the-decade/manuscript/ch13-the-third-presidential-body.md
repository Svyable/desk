# Chapter 13 — The Third Presidential Body

## 1896–1905: William McKinley and the invention of permanent protection

The first murdered president can look like tragedy.

The second can look like coincidence.

The third is a system failure.

On September 6, 1901, President William McKinley stood in the Temple of Music at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York, greeting members of the public. The event was designed around accessibility. Citizens approached the president one by one to shake his hand.

Leon Czolgosz joined the line.

He concealed a revolver beneath a handkerchief wrapped around his right hand. When he reached McKinley, he fired twice at close range.

The president initially appeared likely to survive. One bullet had grazed him; the other entered his abdomen. Doctors operated but did not locate the bullet. McKinley rallied, then developed infection and gangrene. He died on September 14.

Czolgosz was tried, convicted, and executed in the electric chair six weeks after the shooting.

Abraham Lincoln had been assassinated in 1865.

James Garfield had been shot in 1881 and died weeks later.

Now McKinley was dead.

Three presidents murdered in thirty-six years.

The United States finally treated presidential protection not as an occasional precaution but as a permanent institutional mission.

That is the criminal afterlife that makes McKinley's assassination the crime of 1896–1905.

### The democratic handshake

The assassination happened inside a political ideal.

American presidents were supposed to remain accessible in ways European monarchs did not. Public receptions, train travel, open events, and handshaking ceremonies reflected a republic suspicious of physical distance between ruler and ruled.

The openness was partly mythology. Presidents already lived inside privilege and security. Lincoln's wartime environment had included guards. Authorities understood assassination threats. Garfield's death had demonstrated vulnerability.

But the cultural presumption survived: the president should be able to meet citizens.

Czolgosz weaponized the handshake.

He used the ritual's expectation of approach to close the distance required for a pistol.

This is the core problem of protective security.

The protected person has a job that often requires contact with the public.

The attacker needs proximity.

Every barrier that reduces risk can also reduce access, spontaneity, and democratic symbolism.

The problem has no perfect solution because zero risk would require eliminating the public function being protected.

Security therefore becomes architecture around acceptable vulnerability.

McKinley's death changed the acceptable level.

### Anarchism and the immigrant threat

Czolgosz identified with anarchist ideas and had attended a speech by Emma Goldman. He was the son of Polish immigrants, born in the United States. His politics immediately fed a wider fear of anarchism associated in the public mind with bombings, assassinations, and European radicalism.

Haymarket had already made “anarchist” a criminalized social category in American culture. McKinley's murder intensified the association.

The reaction spread beyond the killer.

Congress and immigration authorities moved toward stronger restrictions against anarchists and other politically defined categories. The Immigration Act of 1903, sometimes called the Anarchist Exclusion Act, authorized exclusion of anarchists and became part of a growing federal system deciding that political belief could affect admissibility to the United States.

The crime therefore produced two security responses.

Protect the president from physical access.

Protect the country from ideological entrants.

The first became durable and widely accepted.

The second opened a longer argument over whether immigration law should be used to police beliefs.

One assassin expanded both the protective perimeter and the ideological border.

### Czolgosz as anti-celebrity

Czolgosz did not have Booth's theatrical charisma or Guiteau's courtroom circus. He was quiet, withdrawn, and alienated. He had worked in industrial settings, experienced unemployment, and absorbed radical politics in an era of enormous inequality and labor conflict.

He said he believed it was wrong for one man to have so much service while others had none and that he had done his duty by killing the president.

The temptation is to make social condition into causal explanation.

Industrial capitalism did not pull the trigger.

Anarchist theory did not mechanically cause assassination.

Millions of workers experienced hardship without murdering anyone. Many anarchists rejected individual acts of violence or differed over tactics. Political ideology matters to motive, but motive is not destiny.

The more useful question is why Czolgosz could get close enough.

That answer is institutional.

The office had not yet built a permanent professional protection system commensurate with the demonstrated threat.

### The agency that already existed

The most elegant part of the story is that the United States did not need to invent a new agency.

It repurposed one.

The United States Secret Service had been created in 1865 inside the Treasury Department primarily to combat counterfeiting. Counterfeit currency was a serious threat to a national economy with chaotic paper money. Secret Service agents developed investigative and protective capabilities over subsequent decades and sometimes provided presidential protection informally.

After McKinley's assassination, presidential protection became a regular Secret Service responsibility. Congress would formalize and expand the mission over time.

This is institutional evolution through task migration.

An agency built to protect money became responsible for protecting the president.

Bureaucracies often grow this way. A crisis reveals a need. Policymakers look for an existing organization with adjacent capability. The mission expands. Temporary practice becomes permanent authorization.

That pattern can be efficient.

It can also produce mission creep.

The distinction depends on whether the new task has a coherent relationship to capacity and democratic oversight.

In the Secret Service case, the connection eventually became foundational to the agency's identity.

Counterfeiters gave it investigators.

Assassins gave it presidents.

### Three deaths as data

Institutions are reluctant to infer patterns from rare events.

Usually that is wise.

Rare events can cluster by chance. Policy written around anomalies can waste resources or create burdens larger than the risk.

But rare events become different when the protected object is uniquely consequential.

Three assassinated presidents out of twenty-five by 1901 was not merely a statistical oddity. The office concentrates executive authority and national political legitimacy in one person. The expected harm from even a low-probability event is enormous.

Protective security therefore operates under asymmetric risk.

A false alarm costs inconvenience and money.

A missed assassin can change national policy and succession.

That asymmetry pushes systems toward caution.

The modern presidency's security bubble—motorcades, screening, advance teams, restricted spaces, intelligence coordination, counter-sniper units, hardened communications—can seem excessive when nothing happens.

Nothing happening is the product being purchased.

This makes protective institutions hard to evaluate. Success is invisible. Failure is spectacular.

McKinley helped create a bureaucracy whose best day is one history never notices.

### The trial that barely happened

Czolgosz's legal process moved with extraordinary speed.

McKinley died September 14. Czolgosz went on trial September 23. The trial lasted little more than a day. The defense faced overwhelming evidence and struggled with the defendant's unwillingness to assist. The jury convicted him after brief deliberation. He was executed October 29.

The speed reflects a period comfortable with rapid capital punishment in an open-and-shut assassination case.

It also reveals how little the criminal trial was expected to explain.

The identity of the shooter was not mysterious.

The real national questions lived elsewhere.

Why did he do it?

What should be done about anarchism?

How should presidents be protected?

What happens when political ideology crosses borders?

The courtroom answered the narrowest question—criminal guilt—almost immediately.

The state spent years answering the rest.

This is an important distinction in famous crime.

Trials are not designed to explain society.

They determine charges against defendants.

When the public asks a criminal court to provide meaning beyond that function, disappointment follows. Czolgosz's conviction could punish Czolgosz. It could not solve anarchism, industrial inequality, political alienation, or presidential vulnerability.

Institutions had to do that work separately.

### Roosevelt enters history through a wound

McKinley's death made Theodore Roosevelt president at forty-two, the youngest person to assume the office.

Roosevelt's presidency changed American domestic reform, conservation, antitrust politics, executive power, naval policy, and international diplomacy. The United States that emerged from his administration was more visibly a national regulatory and global power.

Again, assassination becomes policy theft.

Voters had elected McKinley and Roosevelt as a ticket, so succession was constitutional and democratic in a formal sense. But voters had not selected Roosevelt to become president in September 1901. Czolgosz accelerated that succession by years.

We should avoid counterfactual fantasy about what McKinley would have done. His second term had barely begun. Roosevelt might have become president later anyway. Many Progressive Era forces existed independently.

But timing matters in politics.

A person arriving in office at one historical moment has opportunities another moment may not provide.

The assassin cannot control the afterlife.

He can open the door through which someone else walks.

### Protecting democracy by separating it from people

Permanent presidential protection contains a paradox.

To preserve democratic choice, the state must physically separate the chosen leader from ordinary citizens.

Every screening checkpoint around a president is a small acknowledgment that the public contains potential attackers.

The security system therefore creates two publics:

people who may approach,

and people who may not.

Access becomes credentialed, searched, observed, and planned. Spontaneity shrinks.

This can distort politics. Leaders inside protective bubbles experience public life through selected spaces. Protesters are moved. Motorcades disrupt cities. Events become controlled. Security can become a justification for limiting speech or visibility if officials are not careful.

Yet the alternative is not romantic openness.

It is the demonstrated risk of assassination.

The problem is one of design: maximize legitimate access subject to protection, rather than treating protection as a reason to eliminate democratic contact.

McKinley's receiving line was one extreme.

A bunker is the other.

Modern protection lives between them.

### The ideological border after the bullet

The 1903 immigration law excluding anarchists deserves attention because it shows how quickly crime can alter categories of belonging.

Immigration law has long allowed the federal government to make distinctions that would be constitutionally difficult if applied to citizens already inside the country. McKinley's assassination helped strengthen the idea that political ideology could be a border-screening concern.

The logic was preventive.

Czolgosz was a citizen, so excluding foreign anarchists would not have stopped him.

That did not prevent the crime from being used to justify restrictions focused on immigrants.

This is a common post-crime mismatch.

A harm exposes fear.

Policy targets a population associated with the fear rather than the actual pathway of the harm.

After a crime by a member of a group, lawmakers may regulate the group because group membership is easier to identify than future criminal intent.

The result can be security theater or discrimination disguised as prevention.

That does not mean ideological screening is never relevant. A person who openly advocates and plans political violence can present a legitimate security concern.

The problem is substituting labels for conduct.

Haymarket had already shown the danger inside criminal law.

McKinley moved it to the border.

### Why McKinley rather than the first modern serial killer?

The 1896–1905 period contains sensational crimes that fit modern true-crime tastes more naturally. H. H. Holmes had been executed in 1896 after murder and fraud cases that later grew into legend. Labor violence, lynching, organized vice, corporate fraud, and urban murder filled the era.

McKinley is less mysterious and therefore more important.

We know who shot him.

We know where.

We know the verdict.

The unresolved question is institutional, not forensic.

How many assassinated presidents does a republic need before accessibility becomes negligence?

In 1901, the answer became three.

### The criminal afterlife

Lincoln taught the United States that presidents could be assassinated.

Garfield taught it the lesson again.

McKinley turned repetition into infrastructure.

The Secret Service's protective mission became permanent and increasingly sophisticated. The assassination also intensified fear of anarchism and helped shape ideological exclusions in immigration law. Theodore Roosevelt entered the presidency years earlier than ordinary succession would have placed him there.

One crime therefore changed security, borders, and leadership.

But its most enduring legacy is almost invisible.

Every modern presidential event that ends uneventfully carries a trace of Buffalo.

The magnetometer.

The advance sweep.

The agent watching hands.

The controlled rope line.

The motorcade route.

The secured hotel floor.

The person in the crowd who never gets close enough.

McKinley's crime scene did not stay in the Temple of Music.

It became a moving perimeter around the presidency.
