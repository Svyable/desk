# Chapter 2 — The Tax and the Test

## 1786–1795: The Whiskey Rebellion and the first question of federal force

The new government had a tax problem.

That sounds smaller than what followed.

Taxes are paperwork until someone refuses to pay them. Then they become a theory of government.

In 1791 Congress imposed an excise on distilled spirits. Alexander Hamilton wanted revenue to help stabilize the national finances of a country emerging from war with enormous debts and a fragile reputation for repayment. To officials looking from the capital, distilled spirits were a taxable commodity. To many people in western Pennsylvania, whiskey was also a practical form of value in a place where cash could be scarce, grain could be difficult to transport, and distance made national policy feel like something imposed from another economy.

Resistance began with complaint, organization, evasion, intimidation, and refusal.

Then it became violent.

Federal revenue officers and people associated with enforcement were threatened, humiliated, assaulted, and driven from their work. Some opponents used tar and feathering. In July 1794, when United States Marshal David Lenox and revenue inspector John Neville attempted to carry out federal process, armed resistance escalated. Neville's home at Bower Hill was attacked. Fighting followed. The house was burned.

The argument had moved from whether the tax was wise to whether the law existed in practice.

That distinction made the Whiskey Rebellion the defining crime of the country's second ten-year period.

### The republic discovers the verb “enforce”

Writing a law and enforcing a law are different political acts.

The first can be done by representatives in a chamber. The second must happen where the person who objects lives.

That was especially visible in the early republic. The Constitution had created a stronger national government than the Articles of Confederation, but parchment does not collect excise taxes. Statutes do not ride mountain roads. Courts do not automatically make warrants obeyed. Every assertion of national authority had to be converted into local action by actual people entering actual communities.

The whiskey excise turned that conversion into a crisis.

Opponents did not all think alike. Some objected to the tax's economic burden. Some distrusted the concentration of federal power. Some resented procedures that could require defendants to travel long distances for court. Some participated in democratic protest. Some engaged in intimidation and violence. It is historically convenient but analytically useless to compress all of them into “rebels” as though every petition signer, tax evader, armed attacker, and political organizer held the same intent.

The federal government faced the inverse danger. If it treated every criticism as insurrection, the Constitution's new power would begin life by confusing opposition with treason. If it treated organized violent obstruction as ordinary disagreement, federal law could become optional wherever resistance was sufficiently intimidating.

George Washington's administration tried conciliation and warned against violence. Congress adjusted parts of the tax law. Federal officials continued enforcement.

The resistance did not dissolve.

In August 1794, Washington issued a proclamation describing combinations that had obstructed the laws through threats, assaults, property destruction, armed disguise, and attacks on federal officers. He invoked the possibility that some acts amounted to treason. The national government prepared militia forces from several states.

By autumn, nearly 13,000 militia were mobilized. Washington himself traveled with the force part of the way west—the only sitting president to personally take the field in this fashion.

The overwhelming army encountered no grand climactic battle.

The rebellion dispersed.

That anticlimax was the point.

The government had demonstrated that it could convert a federal statute into enough organized force that open resistance became unsustainable.

The crime made the state visible.

### A rebellion about a drink that was not really about a drink

The phrase “Whiskey Rebellion” invites a folk image: rough frontier farmers angry about liquor.

It is not wrong. It is insufficient.

Whiskey condensed larger problems of political economy into something portable. Grain grown in western areas could be expensive to move over bad roads and mountains. Distillation converted bulky crops into a product that traveled and traded more easily. Cash shortages shaped local exchange. An excise collected differently from an import duty could feel invasive in ways an eastern merchant's tariff did not.

Hamilton's fiscal program also represented a national project larger than this single tax. The federal assumption of state debts, creation of public credit, and efforts to establish durable revenue were part of an argument that the United States needed enough financial capacity to survive as a nation. The excise was therefore connected to a fundamental disagreement about what kind of republic the Revolution had produced.

That is why the violence could not remain merely local.

If a region could make federal tax collection impossible through organized intimidation, then Congress's taxing power depended on consent at the point of enforcement. If the administration answered every resistance with troops, then opponents could plausibly fear that the stronger Constitution had merely created a distant sovereign with better tools.

The new republic had to find a line between impotence and coercion.

It found one with an army large enough that it mostly did not need to fight.

### Treason without a scaffold

The Whiskey Rebellion also provided an early test of the constitutional law of treason that had not existed during Arnold's plot.

Federal authorities arrested suspected insurgents. Around twenty people faced treason proceedings. Two were convicted. Washington later pardoned them.

That sequence matters.

The administration used the strongest political-criminal category available to the constitutional order—levying war against the United States—yet the episode did not end in mass execution. Force restored federal control; courts processed individual cases; executive clemency closed the most severe convictions.

It would be too generous to convert this into a flawless textbook example of measured statecraft. The federal expedition frightened communities, produced arrests, and demonstrated a coercive power that critics believed confirmed their warnings. Nor did the episode settle the politics of taxation or federal authority. The excise remained hated and was later repealed under Thomas Jefferson.

But the criminal afterlife of the rebellion lies partly in the combination.

The United States asserted that armed organized resistance to federal law could cross from politics into crime. It also demonstrated that suppressing resistance did not require treating every opponent as permanently outside the political community.

Power and clemency appeared in the same story.

That pairing would recur throughout American history, though rarely so neatly.

### Why not ordinary murder?

This book's selection rule becomes important here.

There were murders between 1786 and 1795. There were robberies, assaults, frauds, maritime crimes, slave resistance, and violence on a frontier where state authority was uneven and conflicts with Native nations were profound. A narrative true-crime anthology could choose a more intimate case and produce a more conventional mystery.

The Whiskey Rebellion wins because it altered the operating meaning of the Constitution.

The Constitution granted power. The rebellion asked whether the power could travel.

That is an institutional afterlife larger than the immediate criminal prosecutions.

The event taught later governments and later dissidents that the federal state possessed a coercive reserve. The militia clauses were not ornamental. Federal courts could charge treason. The president could call forces into service. A law passed in Philadelphia could be defended in western Pennsylvania.

At the same time, the rebellion entered the American tradition of suspicion toward centralized enforcement. To supporters of Washington's action, the lesson was that republican government could not survive if armed minorities nullified laws by violence. To critics, the episode warned how quickly fiscal policy could become military coercion. Both memories survived because both emerge from the same facts.

A mature political system does not eliminate this tension.

It manages it.

### The hidden crime in “resistance”

Americans admire resistance in retrospect.

The national origin story encourages it. Colonists resisted taxes. Crowds defied British authority. Smugglers frustrated imperial customs. Committees organized outside normal institutions. Armed revolutionaries levied war against their sovereign and won.

That inheritance creates a permanent rhetorical opportunity. Almost every later movement confronting law can borrow the Revolution's language. The government enforcing law can borrow the Constitution's.

The Whiskey Rebellion was the first major moment when successful revolutionaries had to stand on the other side of the equation.

Washington had commanded an army born from rebellion. Now he led the government against an insurrection.

The irony is easy to score and easy to overstate. The American Revolution and the Whiskey Rebellion were not identical events distinguished only by who had won office. The constitutional government had procedures for representation, petition, legislation, and elections unavailable in the same form under imperial rule. But that answer does not make the problem vanish, because legal avenues do not automatically make every law just or every violent resistance illegitimate.

The durable issue is narrower: who gets to decide when resistance becomes crime?

Government always possesses a dangerous advantage in answering, because government writes and enforces the law. Dissidents possess a dangerous temptation in the other direction, because calling an act “resistance” can become a way of avoiding responsibility for violence committed in a political cause.

The book will encounter this tension repeatedly.

Nat Turner led a revolt against slavery, an institution of enormous injustice, and his group killed white adults and children. The Christiana resisters opposed the Fugitive Slave Act, a law designed to return human beings to slavery, and a slaveholder was killed in the confrontation. Reconstruction-era white paramilitaries called their violence political resistance while murdering Black citizens and overthrowing biracial government. Labor radicals, segregationists, anti-government extremists, and people attacking the Capitol would all inhabit different moral and legal universes while using some of the same language about tyranny and defense.

“Resistance” describes a relationship to power.

It does not, by itself, tell us whether an act is lawful, moral, or wise.

### The administration's dangerous success

A government that successfully suppresses an insurrection learns something about itself.

It learns that force works.

That can be necessary knowledge. It can also be intoxicating.

The Whiskey Rebellion ended without a major battle between the federal militia and the insurgents. In one sense, the mobilization was proportionate to the administration's objective because its overwhelming size deterred further armed resistance. In another sense, the absence of a battle complicates any claim that so large a force was indispensable. History cannot rerun the episode with eight thousand fewer militia and tell us what would have happened.

This uncertainty is important because emergency responses are often judged by the catastrophe that does **not** occur.

If force deters violence, critics can say the threat was exaggerated because violence did not happen. If government uses too little force and violence expands, critics can say authorities ignored obvious warnings. The same counterfactual problem appears in terrorism prevention, riot control, protective security, public health, and financial regulation.

Officials make choices before they know the outcome.

Historians write after they know it.

The gap is where false certainty grows.

The proper conclusion is not that Washington's response was obviously minimal or obviously tyrannical. It is that the new government chose to establish a precedent: federal law would not be abandoned because organized opponents could intimidate local enforcement.

The precedent had enormous value for state capacity.

State capacity has no guaranteed ideology.

The same ability to enforce national law could later collect taxes, prosecute civil-rights violations, enforce fugitive-slave statutes, dismantle monopolies, protect voting, wage drug wars, regulate markets, imprison dissenters under bad laws, and pursue terrorists. The instrument is not morally self-executing. Its legitimacy depends on the law, the facts, the procedures, the proportionality of force, and the constitutional order around it.

The Whiskey Rebellion helps create the instrument.

Later America fights over what to do with it.

### A federal government that can arrive

The most important consequence of the rebellion is almost too basic to notice.

The federal government became something that could arrive.

It could arrive with a marshal carrying process. It could arrive with a revenue officer. It could arrive with a court summons. And if those were driven away, it could arrive with militia from multiple states under presidential authority.

For western farmers accustomed to distance, that arrival could feel like invasion.

For supporters of the Constitution, it was proof that the Union was real.

Both perceptions help explain why the event mattered.

A weak government can be unjust because it cannot protect people, enforce judgments, secure rights, or maintain public order. A strong government can be unjust because it can impose bad laws efficiently. American political development is filled with arguments that mistakenly treat strength and justice as synonyms or opposites.

They are neither.

Capacity answers: can the state do it?

Legitimacy answers: should it?

The Whiskey Rebellion forced the first question into the open before the country had much experience answering the second.

Washington's government showed that the answer to “can it enforce?” was yes.

Then elections, parties, courts, legislatures, and future conflicts inherited the harder work.

### The criminal afterlife

The whiskey tax itself did not last forever.

The argument did.

Federal authority was no longer only a constitutional promise. The government had mobilized across state lines to suppress organized violent resistance. Treason prosecutions had tested the new constitutional category. Presidential pardons had demonstrated that enforcement and reintegration could coexist. Critics had acquired a vivid example of centralized power in action. Supporters had acquired a vivid example of why power was necessary.

That is why this is the crime of 1786–1795.

Not because whiskey was the most important product in America.

Not because the rebellion was the bloodiest violence of the era.

Because a tax dispute forced the United States to answer the first operational question every government eventually faces:

What happens when people say no and have guns?

The Constitution had supplied nouns—Congress, president, courts, militia, law.

Western Pennsylvania supplied the verb.

Enforce.
