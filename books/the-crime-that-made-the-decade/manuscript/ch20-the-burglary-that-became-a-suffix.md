# Chapter 20 — The Burglary That Became a Suffix

## 1966–1975: Watergate and the crime that taught scandal to speak

The burglars were caught because a security guard noticed tape.

That detail should make every powerful person uncomfortable.

On the night of June 16 into June 17, 1972, five men entered the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate office complex in Washington, D.C. They intended to photograph documents and install or service electronic surveillance equipment. They had connections to the Committee to Re-elect the President and to former intelligence and law-enforcement figures operating inside Richard Nixon's political orbit.

Security guard Frank Wills noticed tape covering a door latch and removed it. Later he found the door taped again.

He called police.

Officers arrested the burglars inside.

A seemingly ordinary break-in began a chain of investigation that exposed campaign-finance abuses, political espionage, hush money, misuse of executive agencies, obstruction of justice, and a White House cover-up. Senate hearings riveted the country. A special prosecutor sought presidential recordings. The Supreme Court unanimously ordered Nixon to comply with a subpoena in *United States v. Nixon*. The House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment.

On August 9, 1974, Nixon resigned.

Watergate is the defining crime of 1966–1975 because it changed the American language of scandal, the law of executive privilege, campaign-finance regulation, congressional oversight, journalism, and public trust.

Five burglars entered an office.

The presidency came out.

### The small crime theory of history

Watergate is a warning against measuring consequence by the sophistication of the initial offense.

The burglary itself was reckless and unnecessary. Nixon was leading in the 1972 presidential race and would win reelection overwhelmingly. There was no plausible political need for a break-in at Democratic headquarters capable of justifying the risk even on cynical terms.

This is common in organizational scandal.

Institutions are not always destroyed by the largest underlying wrongdoing.

They are destroyed by the unnecessary act that creates discoverable evidence.

A private email.

A backdated form.

A petty reimbursement.

A lie under oath.

A taped door.

The act is small enough that participants treat it casually.

Then investigators pull the thread.

Watergate's power comes partly from disproportion. A trivial physical clue unlocked a constitutional crisis.

### The crime after the crime

The most important Watergate crimes occurred after the burglars were caught.

Officials and campaign associates raised and distributed money to support defendants and encourage silence. White House figures discussed using the CIA to pressure the FBI to limit parts of the investigation on national-security grounds. False stories were offered. Records were concealed. Public statements denied connections later shown to exist.

The cover-up enlarged the scandal from campaign misconduct into abuse of presidential power.

This is why “the cover-up is worse than the crime” became a cliché.

The cliché is not always true. Covering up a murder is not morally worse than committing it. Concealing fraud may be less harmful than stealing billions.

Watergate's point is narrower.

Cover-ups create new crimes and new evidence while pulling higher-level actors into conduct they may not have joined originally.

An organization can survive one subordinate's wrongdoing.

It can implicate leadership by making concealment an institutional project.

The impulse is understandable.

Protect the election.

Protect the president.

Protect the agency.

Protect the brand.

Protect the stock price.

Protect morale.

The first protective lie changes what must be protected next.

Soon the institution is defending the lie rather than the mission.

### The tapes

Nixon had installed a secret taping system that recorded conversations in the Oval Office and other locations.

The system was intended to preserve presidential conversations for history and private use.

It became evidence.

Again, trust becomes an attack surface—but this time the president created the vulnerability himself.

When Senate investigators learned of the tapes, they acquired the possibility of something rare in political scandal: direct records of conversations otherwise dependent on competing memories.

Special prosecutor Archibald Cox subpoenaed recordings. Nixon resisted, invoking executive privilege.

The conflict produced the Saturday Night Massacre of October 1973, when Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire Cox. Richardson resigned. Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus also left rather than carry out the order. Solicitor General Robert Bork then dismissed Cox.

The attempt to regain control deepened the crisis.

Public outrage intensified.

The institution had reached the point where firing the investigator became evidence that investigation was necessary.

### United States v. Nixon

The tapes eventually produced one of the most important Supreme Court decisions on presidential power.

In July 1974, the Court unanimously held that while presidents have a legitimate interest in confidentiality of communications, a generalized claim of executive privilege could not defeat a specific judicial need for evidence in a criminal trial.

The Court ordered compliance with the subpoena.

This was not a declaration that executive privilege does not exist.

It was a boundary.

Presidential confidentiality is constitutionally important.

It is not absolute.

The decision matters because the criminal process forced constitutional doctrine to become concrete.

Abstractly, everyone can agree the president is not above the law while also agreeing the presidency requires confidential advice.

A subpoena for tapes makes the two principles collide.

Watergate required the Court to decide which one yielded in a criminal case.

The presidency lost the absolute claim.

The institution survived.

That distinction is one of the book's recurring lessons.

Limiting a president can strengthen the presidency by separating the office from the temporary person occupying it.

### Congress learns the camera

The Senate Watergate hearings began in 1973 and were televised nationally. Witnesses described campaign espionage, payments, meetings, and conversations. Senator Howard Baker's famous question—what did the president know, and when did he know it?—became the investigative grammar of executive accountability.

Congress had learned investigatory power during Teapot Dome.

Television changed the audience.

Oversight was no longer only a committee's evidentiary process. It was mass civic theater.

That produced benefits and distortions.

Citizens could watch witnesses testify under oath rather than receive only partisan summaries. Public attention increased pressure for disclosure. The hearings created a shared factual forum in a fractured political environment.

Television also rewarded performance. Members could ask questions for the audience rather than for information. Future hearings would become increasingly shaped by clips, confrontation, and partisan branding.

Watergate showed that transparency can improve accountability and transform accountability into entertainment at the same time.

The tool contains both possibilities.

### Journalism and the mythology of two reporters

The *Washington Post* reporting of Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein became central to Watergate's public story. Their investigation connected the burglars to campaign funds and followed the expanding network of political operations.

Later books and films turned the reporters into archetypes of investigative journalism.

That mythology is deserved in part and dangerous in part.

Watergate was not solved by two reporters alone.

Police arrested the burglars. Federal prosecutors built cases. Grand juries issued indictments. Judge John Sirica pressed defendants. Congressional committees investigated. FBI agents followed evidence. Senate staff worked records. The special prosecutor's office litigated. The Supreme Court ruled. Insiders testified. Alexander Butterfield revealed the taping system.

Journalism mattered because it kept connections visible and sustained public inquiry while official institutions moved.

The stronger lesson is institutional redundancy.

Democracy is safer when no single gatekeeper controls the truth.

If the White House can stop one investigation, another continues.

If prosecutors move slowly, reporters publish.

If reporters make mistakes, courts require evidence.

If agencies are compromised, Congress can subpoena.

Watergate is a story of many imperfect institutions whose overlap produced accountability.

### The pardon

One month after Nixon resigned, President Gerald Ford granted him a full and unconditional pardon for offenses he had committed or might have committed against the United States while president.

The pardon remains one of the most debated acts of executive clemency in American history.

Ford argued that prolonged criminal proceedings against Nixon would keep the country trapped in Watergate and that the pardon served national healing. Critics believed it placed a former president above ordinary accountability and created suspicion that some deal had been made.

The pardon had enormous political cost for Ford.

It also created a question that returns in later decades:

Is reconciliation achieved by punishment or by ending punishment?

There is no universal answer.

Washington pardoned Whiskey rebels after demonstrating federal capacity.

Altgeld pardoned Haymarket defendants because he believed the trial unjust.

Georgia pardoned Leo Frank narrowly because the state failed to protect him.

Ford pardoned Nixon before any federal criminal trial.

Clemency can be mercy, correction, prudence, favoritism, political settlement, or abuse.

The Constitution supplies the power.

History supplies the argument.

### Reform after scandal

Watergate produced a wave of reforms aimed at campaign finance, government ethics, surveillance, records, and independent investigation. Some reforms built on earlier laws and some were later altered by courts or Congress, but the political environment changed sharply.

The Federal Election Campaign Act was amended in 1974 to strengthen contribution limits, disclosure, and the Federal Election Commission. Congress enacted the Ethics in Government Act in 1978, creating financial-disclosure rules and the independent-counsel mechanism that would later generate controversies of its own. The Presidential Records Act of 1978 asserted public ownership of presidential records, a direct institutional answer to disputes over Nixon's materials.

The pattern resembles Teapot Dome.

Crime reveals a hidden pathway of influence.

Reform makes the pathway visible or harder to use.

Then political actors adapt.

No ethics law ends corruption.

It changes the cost and evidence of corruption.

### The suffix

Perhaps Watergate's strangest afterlife is linguistic.

“-gate” became a suffix for scandal.

Political controversies in the United States and around the world acquired names ending in “gate” whether or not they involved burglary, criminal conduct, executive power, or anything resembling the original event.

Language compressed a constitutional crisis into a reusable label.

This is both tribute and dilution.

The suffix keeps the scandal alive.

It also makes every controversy sound like Watergate.

When everything is “-gate,” the original scale disappears.

The habit reveals how societies remember crime. We preserve a name while losing the mechanism.

Benedict Arnold became “traitor.”

Watergate became “scandal.”

The words travel farther than the facts.

### Why Watergate rather than the Manson murders

The era is full of crimes embedded in American memory: the assassinations of Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert F. Kennedy, the Manson murders, organized-crime killings, the Attica uprising and retaking, antiwar bombings, corruption, and urban violence.

The King assassination is the strongest moral and political competitor. His murder affected civil-rights leadership and triggered unrest across American cities.

Watergate wins under the institutional-afterlife rule because it directly altered the presidency, Congress, campaign finance, executive privilege, records law, investigative journalism, and the national vocabulary of political legitimacy.

The crime changed how every later administration understood investigation.

### The criminal afterlife

The burglars were arrested inside an office.

The evidence climbed.

Campaign money led toward political operations. Political operations led toward White House officials. The cover-up created obstruction. The tapes created a constitutional confrontation. Congress investigated. The Supreme Court ordered disclosure. The House Judiciary Committee approved impeachment articles. Nixon resigned.

No tank entered the White House.

No opposition party seized power.

The system removed a president through evidence, politics, law, and the president's decision to resign before the House could impeach and the Senate could try him.

That is why Watergate defines 1966–1975.

The crime demonstrated that an American presidency could become criminally entangled without making the constitutional order itself collapse.

It also demonstrated how close collapse can feel while institutions are still working.

Frank Wills saw tape on a door.

He did not know he was touching the edge of executive privilege, campaign-finance law, impeachment, presidential records, journalism mythology, and a suffix that would outlive everyone involved.

He called the police.

Sometimes constitutional history begins exactly that small.
