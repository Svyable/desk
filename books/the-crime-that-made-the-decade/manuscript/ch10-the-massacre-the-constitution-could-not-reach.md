# Chapter 10 — The Massacre the Constitution Could Not Reach

## 1866–1875: Colfax, Reconstruction, and the shrinking of federal protection

On Easter Sunday 1873, the courthouse at Colfax, Louisiana, became a fortress.

The state election of 1872 had produced competing claims to local office and a wider crisis over who controlled Reconstruction government. Black Republicans gathered at the Grant Parish courthouse to defend officials they believed had been lawfully elected. Armed white men, many connected to Democratic and white-supremacist paramilitary organization, assembled against them.

The confrontation ended in massacre.

The exact number of dead is uncertain. More than one hundred Black men were killed; National Archives and federal judicial histories describe estimates rising toward 150. Some died during fighting. Others were killed after surrendering. White casualties were few by comparison.

The federal government prosecuted participants under Reconstruction-era civil-rights laws.

Then the Supreme Court helped break the prosecution.

In *United States v. Cruikshank* in 1876, the Court held that the convictions as charged could not stand and adopted a narrow view of federal power under the Fourteenth Amendment and the Enforcement Acts. The decision limited the national government's ability to prosecute private racial violence and pushed responsibility back toward states that were often unwilling or unable to protect Black citizens.

Colfax is therefore not only the crime of 1866–1875.

It is the crime whose prosecution revealed that a right written into the Constitution can become fragile when the government with the will to protect it is told it lacks the power, and the government with the power is unwilling to act.

### Freedom without security

The Civil War destroyed slavery as a legal institution.

It did not automatically create safe freedom.

After emancipation, Black Americans built families outside slave control, negotiated labor, founded schools and churches, acquired land where possible, voted, held office, organized politically, and claimed citizenship through the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. Reconstruction governments attempted to build new political orders across the former Confederacy.

White supremacist resistance was immediate and violent.

The Ku Klux Klan and related organizations used assassination, whipping, intimidation, arson, and terror to suppress Black voting and Republican political organization. The targets were not random. Violence functioned as governance.

Kill a local leader.

Whip a teacher.

Threaten a voter.

Burn a church.

Attack a meeting.

Make participation costly enough and a constitutional right can remain formally intact while disappearing in practice.

This is the difference between rights on paper and rights with security.

A ballot is not meaningful if casting it can get you murdered and no institution will punish the murderer.

Colfax made that truth spectacular.

### An election with two governments

Louisiana's 1872 election was bitterly disputed. Rival Republican and Democratic factions claimed victory in state and local races. At Grant Parish, control of the courthouse became physical evidence of political authority.

Black Republicans occupied and defended the courthouse.

White armed groups mobilized to remove them.

The confrontation was not a conventional race riot in which two equivalent crowds lost control. It emerged from an organized struggle over Reconstruction power in a society where Black political participation itself was a target of white violence.

On April 13, the white force attacked.

After hours of fighting, the courthouse caught fire. Defenders tried to surrender or flee. Many were shot. Prisoners were killed.

The massacre's scale horrified federal officials.

The local system could not be trusted to prosecute effectively.

So the national government did.

That choice was exactly what Reconstruction amendments and Enforcement Acts were supposed to make possible: federal intervention where state structures could not or would not protect newly secured rights.

The prosecutions initially produced convictions against several defendants.

Then constitutional doctrine narrowed the road.

### The problem of private violence

The Fourteenth Amendment says no state shall deny any person equal protection of the laws.

The word “state” became a battlefield.

If a state legislature passes a discriminatory law, federal power can confront state action.

But what if private people organize to murder citizens because they are Black and politically active?

If local officials refuse to protect the victims, is the constitutional injury the private violence, the state's failure, or both?

Reconstruction legislation attempted to reach conspiracies that deprived people of federal rights. The federal prosecution after Colfax relied on that framework.

In *Cruikshank*, the Supreme Court took a restrictive view. The Court treated many fundamental rights as protections against government interference rather than rights the federal government could broadly enforce against private individuals. It also found the indictments deficient in specifying racial or federal-rights elements.

The technical legal details matter, but the practical effect was larger.

Federal civil-rights enforcement against white paramilitary violence became harder.

The decision did not invent white supremacy.

It reduced one of the government's tools for opposing it.

That is criminal afterlife by judicial subtraction.

### When jurisdiction decides reality

Most people do not experience jurisdiction as an abstract doctrine.

They experience it as whether someone comes when they call.

Can the local sheriff arrest the attackers?

Will the state prosecutor charge them?

Can a federal grand jury indict?

Does the statute cover the conduct?

Will the Supreme Court permit the prosecution?

Each question is a gate.

If every gate closes, the right still exists in a civics textbook while violence governs the street.

Colfax shows why jurisdiction can be morally consequential without judges ever announcing a moral preference for the violence.

A court can say: this indictment is defective.

This amendment does not reach private conduct in this way.

This right arises from state rather than federal citizenship.

These statements can sound technical.

Technical rules allocate protection.

When the state system is hostile, narrowing federal authority is not neutral in effect. It leaves victims with the institution least likely to help them.

This is one of the most important recurring patterns in American civil-rights history.

Who acts when local power is the problem?

### The state-action doctrine's shadow

The Fourteenth Amendment transformed constitutional law, but its state-action requirement became a recurring limit on federal remedies for private discrimination and violence.

Later Congress and courts would develop other constitutional and statutory bases for reaching private conduct, including commerce power and specific civil-rights statutes. Twentieth-century federal criminal civil-rights enforcement would become far stronger than it was in the 1870s.

But the post-Civil War retreat mattered enormously.

As Reconstruction weakened, white “Redeemer” governments regained control across the South. Paramilitary violence, electoral intimidation, fraud, and political compromise helped destroy biracial democratic governments. The promise of Black citizenship survived in constitutional text while its enforcement collapsed across large parts of the region.

Colfax was not the sole cause of that collapse.

No single massacre or court decision was.

But Colfax and *Cruikshank* show the interaction between violence and doctrine.

Violence makes rights expensive.

Doctrine can decide which government is allowed to pay the enforcement cost.

If nobody does, violence wins.

### Massacre as political technology

The men who attacked Colfax did not need to kill every Black voter in Louisiana.

They needed the story to travel.

Terror works through demonstration.

One public killing can discipline people who never see the body. A massacre can turn an election procedure into a risk calculation in surrounding counties. If perpetrators escape meaningful punishment, the demonstration becomes stronger: not only can this happen, it can happen without consequence.

That is why impunity is part of political violence.

The crime communicates two messages.

We can hurt you.

The state will not stop us.

The second message can matter more than the first.

Modern terrorism theory often emphasizes communication and audience. Reconstruction-era white-supremacist violence understood the principle without needing the terminology. The direct victims were Black citizens and Republicans. The broader audience was every person deciding whether to vote, organize, run for office, teach, testify, buy land, or challenge white political restoration.

Colfax was violence against a crowd.

It was also a campaign ad written in blood.

### The Constitution as a promise requiring a carrier

Americans often speak of constitutional rights as though the text itself protects them.

Text matters enormously. It changes what claims are legitimate, what statutes Congress can pass, what courts can enforce, and what future movements can demand.

But text needs carriers.

Officials willing to prosecute.

Marshals willing to arrest.

Witnesses willing to testify.

Jurors willing to convict.

Courts willing to sustain jurisdiction.

Executives willing to deploy force.

Citizens willing to insist the right belongs to people they may not like.

Reconstruction failed in part because the national commitment to carrying the amendments weakened before white southern resistance did.

Colfax sits at that transition.

The federal government was still willing to bring a major prosecution.

The legal and political system was becoming less willing to sustain the reach required.

Rights without carriers become archives.

### The victims disappear behind the case name

Legal history can commit a second injury by turning massacres into doctrine.

*United States v. Cruikshank* is a case name.

Colfax was a place where men died.

The doctrinal afterlife is why this chapter selected the crime, but the law must not erase the dead. Many victims were Black men defending a courthouse and a political order that, for a brief period after slavery, allowed them to participate as citizens. Some were armed. Some fought. Some surrendered. Some were executed after resistance had collapsed.

Their deaths belong to a larger American history in which Black political participation repeatedly attracted organized violence.

The white attackers' purpose cannot be understood as ordinary criminality detached from politics.

This was not robbery.

It was political restoration through force.

That distinction explains why local criminal enforcement was so difficult. Prosecuting attackers could mean asking white jurors and officials to criminalize the very project of racial and political control many supported.

Law does not operate outside society.

A statute can prohibit murder while a community decides some murders defend the social order.

Carthage showed a mob killing unpopular religious prisoners.

Colfax showed organized racial violence helping determine who would govern.

The scale of the threat had grown from custody to democracy.

### Why not the Ku Klux Klan itself?

The decade contains thousands of crimes associated with the Ku Klux Klan and other white-supremacist organizations. Why select Colfax rather than a broader campaign?

Because one event allows the institutional chain to be seen end to end.

Political conflict.

Mass violence.

Federal prosecution.

Conviction.

Supreme Court review.

Narrowed federal power.

Long-term enforcement consequence.

Colfax is not representative of every Reconstruction killing. It is analytically concentrated.

The crime scene reaches the Supreme Court.

That is the book's selection rule in its purest form.

### A decision that lived longer than Reconstruction

*Cruikshank* also entered constitutional histories beyond Reconstruction because of its treatment of rights including assembly and bearing arms. Later doctrine would evolve dramatically, and modern Second Amendment law does not simply replicate the 1876 framework. But the case remained a reference point in arguments over incorporation, state action, and federal rights.

This creates an important warning about legal afterlife.

A court case can survive after the political world that produced it disappears.

Lawyers cite propositions, not atmospheres.

A decision born from a massacre can migrate into disputes whose participants know little about the massacre. Doctrine becomes modular.

That can be useful; legal systems require continuity.

It can also sterilize origins.

Understanding where a rule came from does not automatically tell us whether it is correct today.

It tells us what stakes were present when the rule acquired authority.

Colfax's stakes were Black citizens trying to hold political power in the face of organized white violence.

That context should never be treated as decorative.

### The federal government leaves the room

By the end of Reconstruction, the United States had retreated from sustained protection of Black political rights in the South. The Compromise of 1877 and withdrawal of federal troops symbolize the shift, though the retreat was broader and more gradual.

The result was not peace.

It was a transfer of coercive advantage.

White supremacist governments built Jim Crow through statutes, constitutional conventions, fraud, violence, convict leasing, economic coercion, and disenfranchisement. Lynching became a recurring instrument of racial terror. Federal protection remained weak for generations.

The 1963 Birmingham church bombing later in this book is separated from Colfax by ninety years.

The continuity is not direct but unmistakable.

Private racial violence repeatedly flourished where local systems protected perpetrators and national enforcement arrived late, weakly, or not at all.

The twentieth-century civil-rights movement would demand, among many other things, that the federal government return to the question Colfax had posed:

Will national citizenship mean anything when local power refuses to protect it?

### The criminal afterlife

The Colfax Massacre killed more than a hundred Black men and helped terrorize a political community.

The federal prosecution tried to make the killings a national civil-rights crime.

The Supreme Court narrowed the route.

That is why Colfax is the crime of 1866–1875.

The massacre showed that elections can be overturned without changing a ballot if violence determines who is safe enough to participate.

The case showed that rights can fail not because they are absent from the Constitution but because authority to enforce them is fragmented.

The aftermath showed that private violence can become public government when the people committing it suffer no meaningful consequence and their political project succeeds.

Emancipation had answered one question.

Are Black people legally enslaved?

The answer was no.

Colfax exposed the next question.

Who will keep freedom alive when men with guns disagree?

For too long, the answer was nobody with enough power.
