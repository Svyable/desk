# Chapter 12 — The Bomb and the Ideas on Trial

## 1886–1895: Haymarket and the danger of prosecuting a worldview

Nobody has ever proved who threw the bomb.

That should be the first sentence in every confident retelling of Haymarket.

On the evening of May 4, 1886, labor activists gathered near Haymarket Square in Chicago to protest police violence and support the movement for an eight-hour workday. The rally had been largely peaceful and was winding down when a large police formation advanced and ordered the meeting dispersed.

Then someone threw a bomb into the police ranks.

The explosion killed an officer. Gunfire erupted. Police fired. Protesters scattered. Several police officers and civilians died in the bomb blast and shooting; some police deaths were likely caused by friendly fire.

Chicago demanded punishment.

Authorities arrested anarchists.

Eight men were ultimately tried for murder as accessories: August Spies, Albert Parsons, Adolph Fischer, George Engel, Louis Lingg, Michael Schwab, Samuel Fielden, and Oscar Neebe.

The prosecution did not prove that any one of them threw the bomb.

It argued that their speeches, writings, organizing, and advocacy of revolutionary violence had encouraged the unknown bomber and made them legally responsible for the murder.

All eight were convicted.

Four were hanged. Lingg died in his cell before execution, apparently by suicide using an explosive. Schwab and Fielden had their death sentences commuted. Neebe received a prison term. In 1893, Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three surviving imprisoned defendants, issuing a long critique of the trial.

Haymarket is the defining crime of 1886–1895 because it asks a question every frightened democracy eventually faces:

When a terrible act is committed by an unknown person, how much can the state punish people for ideas that seem to have made the act imaginable?

### Eight hours

The bombing cannot be separated from labor conditions.

Industrial Chicago was growing violently fast. Workers labored long days in dangerous factories for low wages. Immigration reshaped neighborhoods and workplaces. Employers used private security and replacement workers. Police were frequently deployed in labor conflict. Strikes could become battles.

The eight-hour movement attempted to reduce the workday. On May 1, 1886, hundreds of thousands of workers around the country participated in strikes and demonstrations. In Chicago, the movement was especially strong and included socialists and anarchists who believed ordinary electoral politics could not adequately challenge concentrated industrial power.

On May 3, police fired on workers during conflict near the McCormick Reaper Works, killing and wounding strikers.

The Haymarket rally the next evening was called partly in response.

That context matters.

It does not make bombing police lawful.

It explains why a public meeting occurred and why speeches were angry.

After every act of political violence, there is pressure to make the surrounding grievance either disappear or become justification. Both are errors.

Police violence against workers was real.

Industrial exploitation was real.

A bomb was thrown at police.

People died.

The challenge is to understand the political environment without converting context into excuse.

### The unknown hand

The identity of the bomber became the central absence around which the prosecution built a case.

No defendant was proven to have thrown the device.

Some were not at Haymarket at the time the bomb was thrown. Parsons had left and later returned. Engel was elsewhere. Neebe's connection to the event itself was weak. Lingg was linked to bomb-making and revolutionary preparation but not proven to have thrown the Haymarket bomb.

The prosecution therefore relied on a theory of shared responsibility.

The defendants had advocated violent revolution. Some had urged workers to arm themselves. Anarchist publications used incendiary language. Prosecutors argued that these ideas and preparations formed a common design from which the bomb naturally flowed.

This is where criminal law becomes political philosophy.

If a speaker explicitly tells a specific person to kill a specific officer and the person immediately does it, complicity is conceptually easy.

If a writer says capitalism will only end through revolutionary violence and an unknown reader later throws a bomb, responsibility is far harder.

Between those examples lie conspiracy, solicitation, aiding and abetting, incitement, material support, and other doctrines attempting to identify when speech becomes participation.

Haymarket sat dangerously close to the point where the defendants' worldview became the substitute for the missing bomber.

### The trial as performance

The Haymarket trial became a national spectacle.

The prosecution presented bombs, radical publications, speeches, witnesses, and evidence of anarchist organization. The courtroom narrative linked the defendants' political doctrine to the explosion. The defense attacked the evidence and argued the men were being tried for beliefs rather than the act.

The jury selection itself reflected the climate. Defense critics later argued that the process produced a jury predisposed against the defendants. Judge Joseph Gary's rulings became controversial. Newspapers often presented the anarchists as enemies of social order before guilt had been individually established.

A trial held under public fear is still a trial.

That does not make it fair.

Legal procedure is designed partly to slow the emotional speed of accusation. Rules of evidence, individualized guilt, cross-examination, burden of proof, jury impartiality, appellate review: each creates friction between public certainty and legal judgment.

When those systems bend toward the mood outside, the trial becomes a ritual of confirmation.

Haymarket's enduring controversy comes from the sense that the jury was asked less “Which defendant committed which crime?” than “What should society do with men who preach ideas that produce bombs?”

That is a different question.

Criminal law is most dangerous when it quietly switches questions.

### The anarchist as category

“Anarchist” became a threat label.

Many defendants were immigrants. Their politics, accents, newspapers, and working-class communities allowed nativism to merge with fear of radicalism. The bombing made it easy to treat ideological identity as evidence of criminal propensity.

This is an old pattern with new vocabulary.

In 1822, Black antislavery speech could become evidence of insurrectionary conspiracy.

In the twentieth century, communist affiliation would become evidence of subversion.

After September 11, Muslim religious and political identity would sometimes be treated as a proxy for terrorist suspicion.

Labels save cognitive effort.

They also destroy individualization.

An ideology can genuinely encourage violence. Groups can coordinate crimes around shared doctrine. Investigators would be foolish to ignore beliefs that explain motive and networks.

But ideology is not a fingerprint.

Millions of people can hold radical beliefs without committing the crime under investigation. The closer criminal liability moves toward belief alone, the more law punishes dangerous identity rather than dangerous conduct.

Haymarket's defendants occupied the worst possible political position for resisting that slide.

Some did advocate revolutionary violence.

Their speech was not genteel reform.

That made it easier for the state to say the bomb merely completed a sentence they had begun.

### Governor Altgeld's pardon

Seven years after the trial, Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned Neebe, Fielden, and Schwab.

His pardon message was not a declaration that the bombing never happened or that all anarchist conduct was admirable. It was an attack on the legal process. Altgeld argued that the jury had been improperly selected, that the judge had shown prejudice, and that the evidence did not establish the defendants' guilt for the murder as charged.

The pardon was politically explosive.

Altgeld was condemned as a friend of anarchists. His action damaged his national political standing. Yet the document became one of the most famous uses of executive clemency to challenge a criminal judgment.

This is the second major clemency story in the book after Washington's pardons following the Whiskey Rebellion, and it illustrates a different function.

Clemency can show mercy after accepted guilt.

It can also serve as a constitutional safety valve when the executive believes the legal system produced injustice.

That power is itself dangerous. Governors and presidents can pardon allies, donors, political supporters, or people whose cases serve ideological goals. Clemency is not inherently corrective merely because it reverses punishment.

Altgeld's pardon matters because it reopened public evaluation of a trial that legal appeals had failed to undo.

The criminal afterlife continued through executive disagreement with judicial finality.

### Labor needed martyrs; the state created them

The executions transformed the Haymarket defendants into international symbols of labor repression.

The event helped shape May Day as an international workers' commemoration. Images of the executed anarchists circulated. The trial entered labor memory as proof that capitalist institutions and courts would kill radicals to preserve the existing order.

The state intended punishment to deter.

It also produced martyrs.

This is another recurring consequence of political prosecution.

A government can remove a leader physically while increasing the leader's symbolic power. Executed dissidents become harder to compromise. Imprisoned radicals become evidence for the radical critique. Censorship can increase curiosity. Surveillance can validate claims of persecution.

This does not mean the state should never prosecute political actors who commit crimes.

It means symbolic effects are part of the afterlife.

Booth turned Lincoln into a martyr.

The Charleston prosecutions turned Vesey into a disputed martyr of antislavery resistance.

The Haymarket executions turned men many Americans had regarded as dangerous extremists into icons for workers far beyond Chicago.

Power can punish bodies more easily than meanings.

### The eight-hour day outlives the bomb

The most remarkable irony of Haymarket is that the labor demand surrounding the event eventually became ordinary.

An eight-hour workday was radical enough to help bring hundreds of thousands into strike action in 1886. Later, federal and state law, union contracts, and employer practice normalized the idea of a standard workday and overtime.

The anarchist revolutionary project did not become the American political system.

The labor grievance helped reshape it.

This is a recurring form of democratic absorption.

A radical movement identifies a real problem.

The system rejects the movement's maximal solution.

Over time, part of the demand becomes mainstream.

That does not happen automatically, and it can take decades of organizing, conflict, and lawmaking. But it complicates the state's impulse to treat radical criticism as proof of criminal threat.

Some ideas attached to frightening movements become ordinary reforms once separated from the movement's most extreme methods.

A government that criminalizes the entire ideological field can make that separation harder.

### The bomb changes policing

Haymarket also accelerated the professional surveillance of radical political movements.

Chicago police and private security organizations collected information on anarchists and labor activists. Employers and authorities increasingly treated radical networks as an intelligence problem. The event helped normalize the idea that political ideology could be a legitimate target of preventive policing when linked to possible violence.

This became a foundation for later “red squads,” domestic intelligence programs, and political surveillance.

Again, the problem is not that police should ignore genuine plots.

The problem is category creep.

Investigating a planned bombing is police work.

Keeping files on lawful dissent because people share some beliefs with bombers is political policing.

The line is clear in principle and difficult in practice because prevention happens before the crime.

Haymarket inherits the Vesey problem in an industrial democracy:

How do authorities identify a future violent conspiracy without turning radical speech into the crime itself?

### The missing bomber as permanent warning

The unknown bomber gives the case its moral geometry.

If authorities had identified the bomber with overwhelming evidence and proven that particular defendants knowingly helped build and deploy the device, Haymarket would still be historically important but less troubling.

The absence forced the state to bridge from ideology to homicide.

That bridge is what later generations keep inspecting.

The problem is not unique to political crime. Prosecutors use conspiracy and accomplice theories in organized crime, gang cases, fraud, drug distribution, and terrorism. Group crimes require legal doctrines capable of reaching people who plan, finance, direct, or facilitate without personally carrying out the final act.

Those doctrines are indispensable.

They are also capable of guilt by association if courts allow the bridge to be built from identity rather than agreement and conduct.

Haymarket's lesson is therefore not “never prosecute conspirators.”

It is “make the bridge visible.”

What did this person agree to?

What did this person know?

What did this person do?

What crime was intended?

How did the act help cause it?

The more inflammatory the defendant's beliefs, the more rigorously those questions matter.

### Why Haymarket rather than Jack the Ripper's American imitators

The decade produced sensational murders, train robberies, urban vice, fraud, and the beginning of modern police institutions. It was also the era of the Homestead conflict and other deadly labor confrontations.

Haymarket wins because one bomb connected labor, immigration, ideology, policing, free speech, conspiracy, capital punishment, executive clemency, and international political memory.

The identity of the direct perpetrator remains unknown.

The institutional consequences are unmistakable.

That combination is almost the definition of this book.

### The criminal afterlife

The Haymarket bomb killed.

The trial tried ideas.

The executions created martyrs.

The pardon put the trial itself on trial.

The labor movement carried the memory internationally. Police and employers learned to treat radical politics as an intelligence target. American law continued struggling with the boundary between advocacy and criminal participation.

And the bomber disappeared into history.

That absence is useful.

It prevents the case from collapsing into biography.

We cannot spend the chapter diagnosing one killer and pretending the diagnosis explains the decade.

Instead we have to watch institutions react to uncertainty.

A bomb landed among police.

The public demanded a culprit.

The state found men whose ideas made them look like the answer.

The question that survived them is still ours:

When the hand is unknown, how much guilt may law infer from the mind?
