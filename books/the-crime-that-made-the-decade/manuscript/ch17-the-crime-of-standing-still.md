# Chapter 17 — The Crime of Standing Still

## 1936–1945: Fred Korematsu and the prosecution of presence

Fred Korematsu's crime was staying where he lived.

That sentence sounds polemical only because the law that produced it now sounds so difficult to defend.

After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, the United States entered a war in which fear of invasion, sabotage, and espionage became immediate national concerns. On February 19, 1942, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, authorizing military authorities to designate exclusion zones. The order did not name Japanese Americans. The military program built under it did.

People of Japanese ancestry on the West Coast—most of them American citizens—were subjected to curfews, exclusion, forced removal, and incarceration. Families were ordered to leave homes and businesses with little time to settle affairs. They passed through assembly centers and incarceration camps administered by the federal government.

Fred Toyosaburo Korematsu was a twenty-three-year-old American citizen living in California. He did not report for removal when ordered. He was arrested in May 1942, prosecuted in federal court, convicted, and placed on probation before being sent into the incarceration system.

His case reached the Supreme Court.

In 1944, the Court upheld his conviction for violating the exclusion order.

Nearly forty years later, a legal team reopened the criminal case using the extraordinary writ of coram nobis. Newly uncovered government records showed that intelligence reports undermining the military's claims of broad necessity had been suppressed, altered in presentation, or destroyed. In November 1983, federal judge Marilyn Hall Patel vacated Korematsu's conviction.

The Supreme Court did not reverse its 1944 judgment in 1983.

The criminal conviction was vacated by the federal district court.

That procedural distinction is not trivia. It is the chapter.

Korematsu is the crime of 1936–1945 because it turns the book's central question inside out. The important crime is not an attack on the state. It is the state's decision to criminalize a citizen's refusal to submit to a policy later recognized as a profound injustice.

### The government defines the defendant

The law did not prosecute Korematsu for espionage.

It did not prove sabotage.

It did not prove he had aided Japan.

His offense was remaining in a place from which the military had ordered people of Japanese ancestry excluded.

The distinction exposes how criminal law can move from conduct to status through geography.

The military order said people in a racial ancestry category could not be present in a designated region after a deadline. Once the government created that rule, ordinary presence became prosecutable conduct.

This mechanism has appeared throughout history in different forms.

A person is told not to enter.

Not to remain.

Not to assemble.

Not to cross a boundary.

Not to sit at a lunch counter.

Not to sleep in a public place.

Not to live in a neighborhood.

Not to return after deportation.

Criminal law then enforces the line.

The morality of the prosecution depends on the legitimacy of the line.

That is why “he broke the law” is never the end of analysis.

Every unjust regime can produce technically accurate convictions if the statute itself does the unjust work.

### Fear after Pearl Harbor

The attack on Pearl Harbor was real. Japanese military power was real. Espionage was a legitimate wartime concern. The West Coast contained military facilities, ports, aircraft production, and infrastructure essential to the war effort.

Those facts matter because caricaturing the government's fear makes the case easier than it was.

The error was not imagining that wartime security mattered.

The error was converting ancestry into a proxy for dangerousness without individualized evidence sufficient to justify mass exclusion.

That conversion was supported by racism with a long prewar history. Japanese immigrants had faced alien land laws, exclusion from naturalization, segregated schooling disputes, and anti-Asian political campaigns. Wartime necessity entered a social environment already prepared to view Japanese ancestry as foreign regardless of citizenship.

Security did not invent the prejudice.

It activated it.

Crises often work that way.

They do not create every institutional weakness from nothing. They make existing weaknesses easier to operationalize.

### The Supreme Court's narrow language and broad reality

Korematsu's case arrived at the Supreme Court after the exclusion program had already uprooted communities. The Court framed the question around exclusion rather than the entire incarceration system. Justice Hugo Black's majority opinion acknowledged that legal restrictions curtailing the civil rights of a single racial group are immediately suspect and subject to rigid scrutiny, language that later generations would associate with strict judicial review.

Then the Court accepted the military justification.

The contradiction is one reason the decision became infamous.

The opinion did not announce that racial prejudice was constitutionally acceptable. It said pressing public necessity could justify the restriction at issue and deferred heavily to military judgments about danger.

That form of reasoning is more durable—and more dangerous—than explicit prejudice because it can survive inside neutral vocabulary.

Emergency.

Necessity.

Expertise.

Deference.

These are legitimate concepts. Courts cannot personally command armies or evaluate every classified threat. But deference becomes risky when the institution requesting it controls the evidence that supposedly justifies the emergency.

Korematsu eventually became a case about what happens when that evidence is not candidly presented.

### The files that came back

In the early 1980s, researcher Aiko Herzig-Yoshinaga and the legal team challenging the wartime convictions found government documents that transformed the evidentiary picture.

Military and intelligence agencies had produced reports contradicting broad claims that Japanese Americans as a group posed a security threat. The Justice Department had expressed concerns about the factual basis for some military assertions. Material had been suppressed, edited, or omitted from the government's presentation to the Supreme Court.

The coram nobis petition argued that the conviction had been obtained through serious governmental misconduct.

Judge Patel agreed sufficiently to vacate the conviction.

The image is almost too perfect for this book.

In 1806, George Wythe's murder trial showed a court prevented from hearing a Black witness because law excluded the voice.

In 1944, Korematsu showed a court hearing the government without receiving the full record the government possessed.

Different century.

Same institutional question.

What is a court allowed to know?

### A conviction can be lawful and wrong

Korematsu's conviction was not an accident produced by a rogue police officer.

The prosecution passed through the machinery of law.

Congress had criminalized violation of military restrictions issued under executive authority. Federal prosecutors charged Korematsu. Courts sustained the case. The Supreme Court affirmed the conviction.

This matters because Americans often imagine injustice as a deviation from institutions.

Sometimes injustice is institutional success.

The officer follows the order.

The prosecutor files the charge.

The judge applies the statute.

The appellate court affirms.

The prison receives the defendant.

Every component can operate as designed while the design itself is wrong.

The chapter therefore belongs beside Christiana. In 1851, federal law criminalized interference with the return of alleged fugitives to slavery. In 1942, federal law criminalized violation of exclusion orders directed at Japanese Americans. In both cases, the state could truthfully say it was enforcing law.

History asks whether the law deserved enforcement.

### Individual suspicion versus group suspicion

Security systems prefer categories because categories are efficient.

Individual investigation is expensive.

Group classification is cheap.

If every person must be assessed separately, the government needs investigators, evidence, hearings, and time. If ancestry itself is treated as a risk factor strong enough to justify removal, the administrative problem becomes simple.

Simplicity was part of the injustice.

The government did not need to prove that Korematsu personally posed a threat.

It needed to prove he belonged to a class subject to exclusion and violated the order.

This is why equal protection and due process have procedural value beyond moral symbolism. Individualized process forces the state to replace a category with evidence about a person.

It is slower.

That slowness is protective friction.

The desire to eliminate it becomes strongest in emergencies.

That is exactly when it can matter most.

### The case America kept trying to bury

After the war, the exclusion and incarceration program became increasingly difficult to defend publicly. Japanese American veterans had served in the U.S. military with distinction. Families returned to lost property, destroyed businesses, hostility, and the work of rebuilding lives.

The political and legal reckoning took decades.

The Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians concluded in the 1980s that the policies were not justified by military necessity but were shaped by race prejudice, war hysteria, and failures of political leadership. Congress passed the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, apologizing and providing reparations to surviving Japanese Americans who had been incarcerated.

Korematsu received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1998.

The criminal defendant became a civil-rights symbol.

That reversal is itself a criminal afterlife.

A country that once used a man's conviction to prove obedience later used the same man's resistance to teach constitutional caution.

### The Supreme Court's later repudiation

In 2018, in *Trump v. Hawaii*, Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that Korematsu was gravely wrong the day it was decided and had no place in law under the Constitution.

The statement was historically significant.

It also created an important legal nuance.

The Court's repudiation did not retroactively transform the 1983 coram nobis ruling into a Supreme Court reversal of Korematsu's conviction. The 1944 case remained in the reporter as a notorious precedent whose reasoning the modern Court disavowed.

This is how legal systems carry shame.

A decision can be overruled, limited, distinguished, repudiated, or rendered obsolete. Those verbs have different legal meanings.

Public memory tends to collapse them into “the Court reversed itself.”

Precision matters because institutional accountability depends on knowing who corrected what.

The district court vacated Korematsu's conviction.

Congress apologized through statute.

A later Supreme Court repudiated the precedent.

No single institution performed the entire correction.

### Why Korematsu instead of organized crime

The years 1936–1945 contain crimes central to the mythology of organized crime, espionage, wartime sabotage, labor racketeering, and political corruption. They also contain the internment-era convictions of Gordon Hirabayashi and Minoru Yasui, whose challenges raised related curfew and exclusion questions and whose convictions were later vacated.

Korematsu wins because his name became the constitutional shorthand for the whole machinery.

The selection is not an argument that his personal prosecution caused the incarceration program. The program produced the case, not the reverse.

The crime's importance lies in the afterlife of the judgment: a conviction under wartime emergency power became one of the country's most enduring warnings about judicial deference, racial classification, and the state's ability to transform identity into prohibited presence.

### The criminal afterlife

Korematsu did not attack anyone.

He did not steal.

He did not spy.

He did not sabotage a shipyard.

He stayed.

The state turned that into a federal crime because the state had first turned Japanese ancestry into a basis for exclusion.

The conviction survived the Supreme Court in 1944. It did not survive the archive.

Four decades later, government records showed that the evidentiary presentation underlying wartime claims had been compromised. A district court vacated the conviction. Congress apologized. The Supreme Court later repudiated the precedent.

That is why this is the crime of 1936–1945.

The case proves that sometimes the most important crime in a decade is the one written into the statute book before the defendant ever acts.

The arrest occurs last.

The line was drawn first.
