# Chapter 14 — The State Lost Its Prisoner

## 1906–1915: Leo Frank, Mary Phagan, and the lynching after the verdict

Mary Phagan was thirteen years old when she was murdered.

That fact should come before Leo Frank.

On April 27, 1913, Phagan's body was found in the basement of the National Pencil Company factory in Atlanta, where she had worked. She had been beaten and strangled. The investigation quickly became a public obsession shaped by class, race, gender, industrial anxiety, sensational newspaper competition, and antisemitism.

Leo Frank, the Jewish superintendent of the factory, was arrested, tried, convicted of Phagan's murder, and sentenced to death.

The case became national controversy.

Supporters of the conviction believed the evidence, including crucial testimony from Black factory janitor Jim Conley, proved Frank guilty. Frank's defenders argued that the trial had been poisoned by an inflammatory atmosphere, unreliable testimony, antisemitism, and a crowd outside the courthouse demanding conviction. Appeals failed. The United States Supreme Court rejected Frank's final constitutional challenge by a divided vote.

Then Georgia governor John Slaton reviewed the record and commuted Frank's death sentence to life imprisonment.

A mob answered by kidnapping Frank from the state prison in Milledgeville, transporting him roughly 150 miles to Marietta, near Phagan's hometown, and lynching him on August 17, 1915.

No one was convicted for the lynching.

In 1986, Georgia's Board of Pardons and Paroles granted Frank a posthumous pardon—but explicitly **without deciding whether he was innocent of Phagan's murder**. The board cited the state's failure to protect Frank while he was in custody and its failure to bring his killers to justice, which had deprived him of the opportunity to continue legal efforts concerning his conviction.

The distinction matters.

Frank's case is not the crime of 1906–1915 because this book can prove who killed Mary Phagan.

It is the defining crime because after a murder conviction, after appellate process, after executive commutation, a private mob stole the state's prisoner and substituted hanging for law.

The government lost physical custody of a man whose life the governor had just lawfully spared.

### The child at the center

Famous cases distort attention.

The accused becomes a symbol. Lawyers become characters. Newspapers become institutions. Later activists inherit the case. The victim can disappear beneath the argument over whether the state convicted the right person.

Mary Phagan must not disappear.

She was a child laborer in an industrial city. Her death was real regardless of later dispute over Frank's guilt. A book concerned with institutional afterlife can become grotesque if a murdered child is treated merely as the first domino in a story about antisemitism and mob justice.

The crime began with her.

The uncertainty begins after.

Investigators focused on several people before the case centered on Frank and Conley. Conley gave multiple statements and ultimately testified that Frank had killed Phagan and enlisted him to move the body. The prosecution relied heavily on that account. The defense attacked Conley's credibility and offered an alternative reconstruction.

The jury convicted Frank.

That verdict is a legal fact.

It is not the end of historical inquiry.

### A courtroom with a crowd outside

The atmosphere around the trial became part of Frank's later appeals and public defense.

Crowds gathered. Newspapers competed aggressively. Antisemitic rhetoric circulated. Frank, a northern-born Jew managing a factory employing young working-class women, could be cast by different factions as outsider, capitalist exploiter, sexual predator, or victim of prejudice. Conley, a Black witness in Jim Crow Georgia, was subjected to racist assumptions by both sides and by public commentary, sometimes treated as inherently unreliable and sometimes strategically used to fit competing racial narratives.

The case demonstrates how prejudice can move in several directions at once.

Antisemitism shaped perceptions of Frank.

Racism shaped perceptions of Conley.

Class shaped perceptions of factory management and child labor.

Gender and sexual rumor shaped the imagined motive.

A criminal trial does not occur outside these forces merely because the judge instructs jurors to consider evidence.

That does not prove the verdict was wrong.

It shows why procedural legitimacy matters independently of factual guilt.

A guilty person is entitled to a fair trial.

An innocent person can be destroyed by an unfair one.

The legitimacy of criminal justice cannot depend on knowing which kind of defendant is in the room before procedure begins.

### The Supreme Court says no

Frank's lawyers pursued state and federal appeals. His case reached the United States Supreme Court in *Frank v. Mangum* in 1915. The majority rejected his petition, emphasizing the state appellate process available to review allegations that mob pressure had undermined the trial.

Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., joined by Justice Charles Evans Hughes, dissented. Holmes argued that if a trial had occurred under mob domination, formal procedure could not make the result constitutional simply because state courts later declined relief.

The dissent contained an idea that would grow in American due-process law:

Procedure must be real, not ceremonial.

A courtroom can have a judge, jury, lawyers, and verdict and still fail if external coercion determines the outcome.

The Frank case therefore lived not only in the history of antisemitism but in the law's struggle to decide when federal courts may intervene in state criminal proceedings.

Again, the case did not settle the question in one stroke.

Its afterlife contributed to a broader constitutional development in which federal habeas corpus and due-process doctrine became more meaningful tools for reviewing state convictions.

The criminal case stretched beyond the factory basement into federalism.

### Slaton reads the record

Governor John Slaton faced a choice no elected official wants.

Frank's legal appeals had largely run out. The execution date approached. Slaton, exercising Georgia's clemency authority, conducted his own review of the evidence and became sufficiently troubled to commute the death sentence to life imprisonment.

He did not acquit Frank.

He changed the punishment.

The decision triggered fury. Slaton faced threats and crowds. His political career suffered.

Clemency here functioned as a constitutional brake.

The executive did not claim the judicial system had no authority. He used an authority assigned to him within the system to prevent an irreversible punishment because he doubted whether execution was justified.

Death penalty systems need such brakes because courts can be legally final without being epistemically perfect.

But clemency also contains a democratic vulnerability.

A governor who spares an unpopular prisoner can be accused of overriding a jury.

A governor who refuses can hide behind the jury.

The power is personal enough that moral courage and political calculation become difficult to separate.

Slaton chose commutation.

The mob chose reversal.

### The prison raid

After commutation, Frank was moved to the state prison farm at Milledgeville.

An organized group of prominent and locally connected men calling themselves the Knights of Mary Phagan planned the abduction. They cut communications, overpowered prison personnel, seized Frank, and drove him through the night to Cobb County.

The operation required planning, vehicles, route knowledge, weapons, and confidence.

That confidence is the most revealing fact.

Mob violence becomes institutionally consequential when participants believe law will not punish them.

The lynchers did not behave like people expecting a massive state response.

They behaved like an alternative sovereign.

They took custody.

They selected the site.

They imposed the sentence the governor had refused to permit.

They left the body for public discovery.

The act was not spontaneous fury.

It was organized nullification of executive authority.

This makes Frank's lynching structurally similar to other political and racial terror in American history even though the victim and context differ.

A private group decided the formal state's judgment was illegitimate and used force to replace it.

That is not merely murder.

It is rival government.

### Antisemitism and the reborn Klan

Frank's case became deeply entangled with American antisemitism.

National Jewish organizations rallied to his defense. The Anti-Defamation League, founded in 1913, made the case one of its formative concerns. After the lynching, white supremacist activists connected to the episode and its political culture helped revive the Ku Klux Klan at Stone Mountain later in 1915.

The second Klan was not simply the Reconstruction Klan restarted. It built a mass movement around white Protestant nationalism, anti-Black racism, antisemitism, anti-Catholicism, nativism, and moral policing.

Frank's lynching helped supply mythology and momentum.

A single criminal case therefore fed two durable organizations on opposite sides of American pluralism: an expanding Jewish civil-rights defense movement and a revived white supremacist secret society.

That is an unusually large cultural afterlife.

It also demonstrates that crime can polarize institutional formation.

One group says: the system failed us; organize for rights.

Another says: the system failed us; organize outside it to impose our will.

The same event becomes evidence for opposite projects.

### The 1986 pardon that did not exonerate

Decades later, the case returned.

In 1982, Alonzo Mann, who had been an office boy at the factory, said that as a teenager he had seen Conley carrying Phagan's body and had remained silent after threats and family instruction. Frank's supporters treated the account as powerful evidence supporting innocence. Others challenged its decisiveness.

A pardon request based on innocence was initially denied.

In 1986, the Georgia Board of Pardons and Paroles granted a pardon on a narrower ground.

The language matters enough to paraphrase carefully: the board said it was not deciding guilt or innocence. It recognized the state's failure to protect Frank and thereby preserve his ability to pursue legal remedies, and the state's failure to bring his killers to justice.

This is one of the most intellectually honest clemency acts in American crime history because it refused to claim more than the state could establish.

The state could not say with legal certainty, seventy years later, that Frank did not kill Phagan.

It could say with certainty that Georgia had failed in its custodial duty and failed again in accountability for the lynching.

That distinction is not a weakness.

It is the point.

Institutions should be able to admit a proven failure without inventing certainty on unresolved questions.

### Why the pardon matters to the method of this book

True-crime culture loves exoneration because it gives narrative closure.

Wrong man.

New evidence.

System corrected.

Frank's pardon denies us that satisfaction.

It leaves two layers separate.

The Phagan murder conviction remains historically contested.

The Frank lynching was indefensible.

One does not depend on resolving the other.

Even if a reader believes Frank was guilty, the mob had no lawful right to execute him after commutation.

Even if a reader believes Frank was innocent, the injustice of the lynching does not by itself prove who killed Phagan.

This separation is a civic skill.

People often evaluate process based on whether they like the defendant. We excuse illegal searches when we think police found the right person. We excuse mob tactics when we think the target deserves condemnation. We discount due process when the evidence looks overwhelming. We defend procedure only when it protects someone we sympathize with.

That is how procedure becomes optional.

Frank's case insists that custody, appeal, clemency, and protection matter even under unresolved guilt.

### The state cannot outsource punishment to popularity

Lynching is often described as lawlessness.

That is true but incomplete.

American lynching frequently operated with the tolerance, participation, or deliberate inaction of local authorities. Mobs could include respectable citizens. Law enforcement could fail to protect prisoners or fail to identify killers everyone locally knew. Grand juries could decline indictments. Juries could acquit.

The violence therefore existed beside law, not outside society.

Frank's lynching makes the structure especially visible because state authority had just exercised clemency.

The mob was not filling a vacuum.

It was vetoing a governor.

That is why the failure to prosecute the lynchers mattered so much. Without accountability, the state effectively allowed private actors to impose the punishment it had lawfully rejected.

A pardon without protection is only paper.

A commutation without custody is only intention.

Law requires physical control of the outcome.

### Why Frank over other lynchings

The early twentieth century contained thousands of lynchings, overwhelmingly targeting Black Americans. Many involved horrifying torture, public spectacle, false accusation, sexual mythology, and total local impunity. Selecting a white Jewish victim risks distorting the scale and racial center of American lynching.

That risk must be stated.

Frank is not representative.

He is selected because the case connects a contested murder trial, national media, antisemitism, Supreme Court due-process doctrine, executive clemency, prison security, organized mob abduction, Klan revival, and a later pardon precisely limited to state failure.

The institutional chain is unusually complete.

The chapter should therefore point outward, not imply that Frank's suffering was uniquely serious. The machinery that failed him had failed Black victims on a vastly larger scale and would continue doing so.

Frank's national prominence made the failure impossible for some Americans to ignore.

That asymmetry in attention is itself part of the history.

### The criminal afterlife

Mary Phagan was murdered.

Leo Frank was convicted.

The legal system disputed his claims of unfairness.

The governor spared his life.

A mob took it anyway.

The killers went unpunished.

The case strengthened Jewish civil-rights organizing and fed white supremacist organizing. It became part of the development of federal due-process thought. Seven decades later, Georgia pardoned Frank not by declaring him innocent but by admitting what the state could no longer deny about itself.

That is why this is the crime of 1906–1915.

The deepest failure was not that Georgia reached a controversial verdict.

Legal systems will sometimes reach verdicts later generations dispute.

The deepest failure was that after the courts and the governor had acted, the state surrendered its prisoner to men who believed their certainty outranked law.

A government can survive disagreement over guilt.

It cannot claim rule of law if the final appellate court is a rope.
