# Chapter 9 — The Night the Government Was Targeted

## 1856–1865: Lincoln's assassination and the conspiracy larger than one bullet

The most famous fact about April 14, 1865, is too small.

John Wilkes Booth shot Abraham Lincoln at Ford's Theatre.

That is true.

It is not the whole crime.

That same night, Lewis Powell entered the Washington home of Secretary of State William H. Seward and slashed and stabbed his way toward a wounded cabinet officer, attacking members of Seward's household before escaping. George Atzerodt had been assigned to kill Vice President Andrew Johnson but failed to carry out the plan.

The assassination was not only an attempt to kill a president.

It was a coordinated attack on the leadership of the United States government at the moment the Civil War was ending.

Lincoln died the next morning.

Seward survived.

Johnson became president.

Booth fled and was killed during capture twelve days later. Other alleged conspirators were tried before a military commission. Four—Mary Surratt, Lewis Powell, David Herold, and George Atzerodt—were hanged.

The murder is the obvious selection for 1856–1865, but obvious cases can become lazy chapters. The important question is not whether Lincoln's assassination changed history. Of course it did.

The question is **how** the crime changed the country's understanding of political violence, succession, emergency justice, and the vulnerability of republican government precisely when victory made it look strongest.

### Victory night

Washington in April 1865 was experiencing the emotional whiplash of war ending.

Richmond had fallen. Robert E. Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Ulysses S. Grant at Appomattox Court House on April 9. The Confederacy was not yet fully extinguished, but the central military struggle was collapsing.

Lincoln had won reelection. The Union had survived. Slavery had been struck by emancipation and would soon be constitutionally abolished by the Thirteenth Amendment.

This was the moment Booth chose.

He was an actor and Confederate sympathizer who had earlier participated in a scheme to kidnap Lincoln. As Confederate defeat became clear, the plan escalated into assassination. Booth assigned Powell to Seward and Atzerodt to Johnson while he targeted Lincoln.

The target list reveals intent larger than personal hatred.

President.

Vice president.

Secretary of state.

The sequence of succession at the time differed from today's, but the symbolic objective is unmistakable. Multiple leaders were to be struck in the same evening.

The crime was designed as political decapitation.

That makes it closer to a terrorist conspiracy than to the solitary gunman mythology that later memory often imposes.

### The theater box

Booth knew Ford's Theatre.

He was famous enough to move through theatrical spaces without looking like an intruder. He learned that Lincoln would attend the performance of *Our American Cousin*. The president's security was nothing like modern presidential protection. Washington was full of soldiers, but the office itself remained physically accessible by twenty-first-century standards.

Booth entered the presidential box and fired a single-shot derringer into the back of Lincoln's head.

Major Henry Rathbone, sitting with the Lincolns, tried to stop him. Booth slashed Rathbone with a knife and escaped from the box to the stage, injuring his leg in the flight.

The assassination became theater in the most literal possible way: a celebrated actor used a stage, an audience, and his own capacity for performance to commit political murder.

That coincidence has supplied generations of symbolism.

The more important institutional fact is simpler.

The president had almost no dedicated protective architecture capable of defeating an attacker with insider-like familiarity and physical access.

The assassination exposed the vulnerability of the office.

It would take two more murdered presidents before the United States fully institutionalized permanent presidential protection.

Crime often teaches slowly when the lesson requires creating a bureaucracy.

### The other bedroom

While national memory concentrates on Ford's Theatre, Lewis Powell's attack on Seward prevents the story from shrinking into one man and one gun.

Seward had recently been injured in a carriage accident and was recovering at home with a jaw splint and other supports. Powell arrived claiming to bring medicine. He forced his way upstairs, beat Seward's son Frederick, entered the secretary's room, and attacked him with a knife.

The surgical apparatus from Seward's recent accident may have helped prevent fatal wounds. Members of the household intervened. Powell fled.

Seward survived and later continued as secretary of state under Andrew Johnson, including during the acquisition of Alaska.

Think about the contingency inside that survival.

If Powell kills Seward, American diplomacy after the war changes immediately.

If Atzerodt kills Johnson, the succession crisis becomes far more severe.

If all three attacks succeed, the United States confronts an unprecedented decapitation at the end of civil war.

History did not take that path.

The intended crime still matters because the target set reveals what Booth's conspiracy imagined it was doing.

It was not only revenge.

It was an attempt to use murder as political transition.

### The president as mortal infrastructure

Modern states try to make leadership continuity independent of one body.

Constitutions specify succession. Cabinets distribute functions. Agencies maintain operations. Records preserve institutional memory. Protective services reduce physical risk. Communications systems allow continuity during attack.

In 1865, the American state had some of that resilience and little of the rest.

The Constitution provided for vice-presidential succession, though ambiguities remained. Johnson could assume office after Lincoln's death. That mattered enormously. The murder did not leave the executive branch without a president.

But the individual president's death still transformed policy.

Lincoln's plans for Reconstruction were not a complete blueprint, and historians should resist the seductive counterfactual that he certainly would have solved the postwar settlement. He had changed positions before. Congress had its own agenda. White southern resistance, Black political demands, economic destruction, constitutional amendments, and party conflict would have constrained any president.

Yet leadership matters.

Johnson was not Lincoln.

His approach to Reconstruction, conflict with congressional Republicans, racism, vetoes, and later impeachment struggle shaped the postwar period profoundly. Lincoln's murder therefore changed the personnel through which the country would answer the most consequential domestic question after emancipation: what freedom and citizenship would mean after slavery.

The killer did not author Johnson's choices.

He made Johnson president.

That is enough to give the bullet an enormous institutional afterlife.

### Military justice in a civilian republic

The trial of the conspirators created another afterlife.

Eight defendants were tried by a military commission rather than an ordinary civilian court: Mary Surratt, Lewis Powell, David Herold, George Atzerodt, Samuel Mudd, Samuel Arnold, Michael O'Laughlen, and Edman Spangler.

The government justified military process in the aftermath of civil war and presidential assassination. The commission convicted all eight, imposing death sentences on Surratt, Powell, Herold, and Atzerodt. They were executed in July 1865.

The use of a military tribunal became controversial, especially because civilian courts in Washington were functioning. The legal debate intersected with a broader postwar question later sharpened by the Supreme Court's 1866 decision in *Ex parte Milligan*: when civil courts are open, how far may military jurisdiction extend over civilians?

The assassination trial did not itself produce a single clean doctrinal rule that settled the issue. It became part of the permanent American argument over emergency justice.

That argument returns whenever national trauma creates pressure for exceptional procedure.

Saboteurs.

Spies.

Terrorists.

Enemy combatants.

Citizens accused of aiding enemies.

The state says ordinary process is inadequate to the threat.

Critics say emergency courts are most dangerous precisely when public fury is greatest.

Lincoln's murder placed that tension at the birth of Reconstruction.

### Mary Surratt and the irreversibility problem

Mary Surratt's execution remains the most controversial individual punishment from the military commission.

She owned the Washington boardinghouse where Booth and associates had met and was accused of involvement in the conspiracy. Her defenders argued the evidence did not justify death and questioned the commission process. Five commission members recommended clemency in a document whose handling later became disputed. President Johnson did not intervene to stop the execution.

Surratt became the first woman executed by the federal government.

The point here is not to retry her.

It is to see how national trauma changes tolerance for uncertainty.

Capital punishment makes error irreversible. Military procedure reduces some features of ordinary civilian adjudication. A murdered president produces enormous public pressure for accountability. Put those three conditions together and the risk of punishment outrunning certainty becomes acute.

The problem is structural.

A government wants to show that attacks on leadership will be punished decisively.

Justice requires individualized proof.

The more symbolically important the victim, the harder it can be to keep the defendant from becoming a symbol too.

Crime can place justice under reputational pressure.

The state wants not only the right verdict but a verdict that demonstrates control.

### The hunt for Booth

Booth's escape turned assassination into manhunt.

He and David Herold moved through Maryland and into Virginia, aided at points by people whose degree of knowledge varied. Federal authorities mobilized enormous resources and public attention. Wanted posters offered rewards. Soldiers searched. Informants supplied leads.

On April 26, troops cornered Booth and Herold in a tobacco barn near Port Royal, Virginia. Herold surrendered. Booth refused. The barn was set on fire. Sergeant Boston Corbett shot Booth, who died hours later.

The principal assassin therefore never faced trial.

Like Benedict Arnold, Booth became a national criminal archetype without an ordinary courtroom testing the full case against him. Unlike Arnold, he died during capture.

That absence helped elevate the conspiracy trial into a substitute site for national judgment.

The state could not sentence Booth.

It could sentence the network around him.

This dynamic recurs after dead attackers. When a perpetrator dies by suicide, police gunfire, or military action, investigators and prosecutors often shift attention toward accomplices, financiers, facilitators, and institutional failures. Public desire for explanation expands because punishment of the central actor is impossible.

The story moves from “who did it?” to “who helped?” and “who failed to stop it?”

Those are often the more consequential questions anyway.

### Assassination as policy theft

Political murder has a distinctive democratic harm.

It does not merely kill a person.

It attempts to change public policy through private violence.

Voters had selected Lincoln for a second term. Booth replaced that electoral outcome with a different president. Even if the assassin cannot control what the successor does, the act changes the political decision tree without consent.

That is why assassination is more than murder with a famous victim.

It is policy theft.

The crime takes a decision assigned to elections and constitutional succession and forcibly edits it.

The United States has repeatedly confronted this danger. Garfield's murder accelerated civil-service reform. McKinley's murder elevated Theodore Roosevelt. Kennedy's assassination placed Lyndon Johnson in office during civil rights and Vietnam. Attempts on other presidents altered campaigns and security even when they failed.

Protecting political leaders therefore serves not only the leader.

It protects the public's chosen process from being rewritten by a gun.

That insight is part of the long road toward professional presidential protection.

### Why Lincoln over the Civil War's other crimes?

The decade from 1856 through 1865 contains violence on a scale no ordinary crime anthology can accommodate.

Bleeding Kansas.

John Brown's raid and execution.

Mass wartime death.

Guerrilla atrocities.

Prison-camp horrors.

Draft riots and racial murder.

Crimes against enslaved people that law often did not recognize as crimes.

Sherman's march, Confederate massacres, and countless acts that later legal categories might judge differently from contemporaneous ones.

Why choose Lincoln's assassination?

Because the book is not selecting the worst wrongdoing.

It is selecting the crime with the widest criminal afterlife inside the American institutional story.

Lincoln's murder changed the presidency, succession in practice, Reconstruction leadership, security, conspiracy law, military justice, and the national understanding of assassination as an attack on democratic continuity.

John Brown is the strongest runner-up because his 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry and subsequent treason trial electrified sectional politics and helped accelerate the road to war. In another book, Brown might easily define the era.

Here, Lincoln wins because the crime occurred at the hinge from war to peace and changed who controlled that hinge.

### The nation after the gunshot

Lincoln's death produced a vast public mourning culture.

His body traveled by funeral train through northern cities on a journey back to Springfield, Illinois. Crowds gathered. Photographs, broadsides, sermons, poems, relics, and memory turned the assassination into civic ritual.

This cultural afterlife helped transform Lincoln from controversial politician into national martyr.

That transformation matters because murdered leaders become harder to argue with.

Death freezes a political figure before later compromises, failures, or changing coalitions can complicate reputation. The assassination therefore affected not only policy but memory. Lincoln's unfinished second term could become a canvas for hopes about Reconstruction precisely because he never had to make the decisions that would have disappointed some constituency.

Martyrdom is a political technology the martyr does not control.

Booth wanted to punish Lincoln.

He helped immortalize him.

### The criminal afterlife

On April 14, Booth's group attempted to strike three senior officials.

Only one target died.

That was enough.

The presidency changed hands. Reconstruction changed leadership. The country conducted a controversial military trial. Four conspirators were executed. The assassination entered American political memory as proof that electoral and military outcomes could be attacked by private violence. Presidential security would, through later assassinations, become a permanent national institution.

The crime also establishes a principle that will return in the final chapter.

Political violence does not need to conquer the government to injure democracy.

It can change who governs.

It can delay or disrupt constitutional process.

It can intimidate officeholders.

It can force emergency measures.

It can alter what citizens believe is possible.

Booth did not save the Confederacy.

He did not reverse Union victory.

He did something narrower and, in its way, more durable.

He proved that one man with access to a theater box could take a national election and insert a bullet between the vote and the future.
