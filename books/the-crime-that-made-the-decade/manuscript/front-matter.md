# The Crime That Made the Decade

## 250 Years, 25 Crimes, and the America They Left Behind

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

## Author's note

This is not a ranking of evil.

There is no honest scale on which the poisoning of one old man can be set against the murder of children in a church, the destruction of a federal building, or the coordinated killing of thousands on a September morning. Bodies are not points. Suffering is not a statistic that becomes morally cleaner when sorted into descending order.

This book asks a different question.

For each ten-year period in the first 250 years of the United States, what crime most changed the country that came after it?

Not the most famous. Not necessarily the deadliest. Not always the one that filled the most newspaper columns. The most important crime, as I use the phrase here, is the one with the largest **criminal afterlife**: the event that forced America to redraw some boundary around power, evidence, punishment, security, citizenship, government, commerce, political legitimacy, or the meaning of justice itself.

That definition produces strange results.

A vice president shooting a former treasury secretary in an illegal duel matters because law and custom briefly occupied the same riverbank and custom won. A poisoning in 1806 matters because the most important fact in the courtroom may have been the testimony the law would not allow a Black witness to give. An alleged slave conspiracy in 1822 matters partly because historians still disagree over how much conspiracy existed outside the prosecutions themselves. A man refusing an exclusion order in 1942 belongs in a book about crime because the United States made his continued presence in his own region a federal offense. Seven unsolved poisonings in 1982 changed the bottle in your medicine cabinet. Five burglars arrested in an office complex in 1972 gave the English language a suffix for scandal.

The point is not that every consequence was good. Often the response to crime enlarged government power, narrowed rights, accelerated punishment, or created tools that later reached far beyond the original emergency. Sometimes a crime revealed an institution that was too weak to protect people. Sometimes it revealed an institution powerful enough to define innocent conduct as criminal. Sometimes the legal response became a second story as important as the first.

And sometimes the most important question is whether the courtroom knew what it claimed to know.

That is why this is a book about crimes **and the stories behind them**. “What happened?” is only the beginning. The larger questions are: What did authorities say happened? What could they prove? Who was permitted to testify? What facts did a jury hear? What did later investigators learn? What law changed? What agency acquired power? What institution lost legitimacy? What did Americans become newly afraid of? What did they become newly unwilling to tolerate?

Twenty-five chapters cannot contain American crime. They can only cut twenty-five windows into it. Every selection necessarily excludes another case with a claim on our attention. I have tried to make the choices arguable rather than obvious, and where the obvious case wins—Lincoln, Watergate, September 11—it wins because its afterlife is impossible to explain away, not because familiarity excuses analysis.

I have also used exact ten-year periods rather than conventional calendar decades. The first chapter covers 1776 through 1785. The last covers 2016 through 2025. Between them are twenty-three equal windows. The architecture therefore spans exactly 250 years, from the declaration of a new country through the last completed year before its semiquincentennial.

The date boundaries occasionally make a familiar crime land in an unfamiliar “decade.” That is a feature, not a mistake. History did not wait for years ending in zero before changing direction.

One more boundary matters. “Crime” in this book is not shorthand for “bad thing.” War is not automatically crime. Political disagreement is not crime. A scandal is not a crime merely because it offended the public. I use the category where there is criminal conduct, a criminal prosecution, an alleged criminal conspiracy with major legal consequences, or a case in which the state's decision to criminalize conduct is itself central to the historical story. When guilt was disputed, acquitted, pardoned, vacated, unsolved, or never adjudicated, the text says so.

The distinction is not legalistic fussiness. It is the subject.

America's history can be read through constitutions, elections, wars, inventions, migrations, fortunes, speeches, and movements. It can also be read through the moments when someone crossed a line and the country had to decide whether the line was real, where it belonged, and what should happen next.

Those decisions leave tracks.

We still live on them.

# Prologue — The Scene After the Scene

A crime scene is supposed to have an edge.

There is tape. A doorway. A field. A hotel room. A street corner. A broken lock. Investigators move inward while everyone else is pushed back. Evidence is collected because contamination is a kind of forgetting. The scene is bounded so that, later, someone can make a claim about what happened inside it.

History has no tape.

The scene keeps expanding.

The bullet fired at Alexander Hamilton does not stop at his body. It travels into the decline of dueling, into Aaron Burr's ruined political future, into the mythology of honor, into our later astonishment that two national political figures could arrange lethal private combat while the law stood nearby and mostly watched.

The dynamite at Birmingham's Sixteenth Street Baptist Church does not stop at brick and glass. It travels through federal and state investigations, through the unbearable image of four murdered girls, through the civil-rights struggle, through delayed prosecutions decades later, through every argument about what justice means when the clock between crime and accountability runs for a generation.

The tape on the Watergate door does not stop at the door.

That is the premise of this book: the most important part of a crime may be the part that happens after the scene is cleared.

Call it the criminal afterlife.

A crime has an immediate life. Someone acts. Someone is harmed. Police investigate or fail to. Prosecutors charge or do not. A jury hears a case or never gets one. Newspapers and broadcasters tell a story. Families begin living with an absence.

Then the second life begins.

Congress writes a law. A court limits a power. An agency gains a mission. A product is redesigned. A president resigns. A movement acquires a martyr. A region tightens repression. A government discovers that a statute cannot reach what it wants to punish. A defendant becomes an example. An acquittal becomes an indictment of the system that produced it. A pardon reopens a case without erasing the past. A crime teaches other criminals. A prosecution teaches government what it can get away with. A public learns a new category of fear.

The afterlife can last longer than the event by centuries.

Consider the first selection in this book. Benedict Arnold's plot to surrender West Point is older than the Constitution. The republic as we know it did not yet exist. There was no Federal Bureau of Investigation, no Justice Department, no federal criminal code in the modern sense, not even a completed war for independence. Yet Arnold gives the country one of its oldest political archetypes: the insider who converts trust into access and access into betrayal. His name becomes so synonymous with treason that the biography nearly disappears into the insult.

Then consider the final selection. On January 6, 2021, people attacked police, breached the Capitol, entered restricted spaces, and disrupted the joint session convened to count electoral votes. The legal afterlife did not settle quickly. It produced misdemeanor and felony prosecutions, seditious-conspiracy convictions, appellate litigation, a Supreme Court decision narrowing the reach of one obstruction statute, and, in January 2025, sweeping presidential clemency for federal defendants covered by a proclamation. By the end of the book's final ten-year window, the event had become not one legal story but several: conduct, prosecution, interpretation, judicial review, clemency, and political memory.

The distance from Arnold to January 6 is not a straight line. It should not be forced into one. But both cases pose an old question in different institutional languages: what does a political community do when people inside its own civic or military structure act against the authority that structure claims?

Across 250 years, the answer changes because the country changes.

At the founding, criminal law was overwhelmingly local and state-based. Federal criminal jurisdiction was narrow and contested. Communications moved at the speed of riders, ships, and printing presses. Forensic science as we know it did not exist. Enslaved people could be treated as property, denied legal personhood, and barred from testimony. Women were excluded from political power and constrained by doctrines that shaped property and legal identity. Native nations confronted a legal order that often treated sovereignty as an obstacle to expansion. Mobs could operate with local sympathy. Private violence could carry the etiquette of honor. A president moved through public space with nothing like the security architecture that surrounds the office today.

By the twenty-first century, crime could move at network speed. Evidence could mean server logs, geolocation, financial databases, security video, DNA, intercepted communications, and millions of social-media posts. Federal criminal law touched terrorism, securities markets, civil rights, organized crime, consumer tampering, cyberconduct, and public corruption. The president traveled inside a permanent protective system. An attack seen live around the world could generate hundreds of prosecutions whose records themselves became a massive digital archive.

But sophistication does not end uncertainty.

One of the easiest mistakes in writing crime history is to assume that newer systems know more simply because they possess more information. They often do know more. They also produce new forms of noise, institutional bias, secrecy, overreach, missed signals, and hindsight. The Madoff chapter is impossible without understanding that regulators had received credible warnings. The Korematsu chapter is impossible without understanding that government intelligence evidence was withheld or destroyed while the exclusion policy was defended. The Vesey chapter is impossible without confronting a record filtered through a slaveholding legal system that interrogated, edited, convicted, and executed.

Evidence is never merely what exists.

Evidence is what a system can collect, what it is willing to preserve, what rules allow into the room, what witnesses are allowed to say, what officials choose to disclose, what a jury believes, what an appellate court is empowered to review, and what later generations can still recover.

That is why the murder of George Wythe belongs here.

Wythe was among the most revered legal minds of the founding generation: teacher of Thomas Jefferson, signer of the Declaration of Independence, judge, abolition-minded slaveholder-turned-emancipator in a society built on slavery. In 1806, he died after an apparent poisoning. His grandnephew, George Wythe Sweeney, was tried for murder and acquitted. Among the barriers to conviction was a rule that prevented Black testimony against a white defendant. The court was not simply missing evidence. The legal system had declared some human observation inadmissible because of the observer's race.

A country can be changed by what it refuses to hear.

It can also be changed by what it hears too loudly.

The Haymarket bombing occurred inside a moment of labor conflict and public fear of anarchism. Eight anarchists were prosecuted for murder as accessories; the identity of the bomb thrower was never conclusively established. The trial became inseparable from political ideas attributed to the defendants. Years later, Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the surviving defendants and condemned the proceeding. The case outlived the bomb because it became an argument about whether a legal system can fairly try unpopular ideas when the public demands punishment for an act.

Move forward and the recurring tension becomes visible: a terrifying crime creates pressure for a larger response, and the response creates a new system that may long outlive the original danger.

The Lindbergh kidnapping helped accelerate federal kidnapping jurisdiction. The Tylenol murders produced tamper-resistant packaging rules and a federal anti-tampering law. Presidential assassinations reshaped protection of the presidency. September 11 reorganized American security on a scale almost impossible to overstate. Each response can be judged on its own merits, but first it must be seen as a response—as an institution built in the emotional and political wake of a particular harm.

Crime is therefore one of history's hidden legislative engines.

Not the only one. Not always the wisest one. But a powerful one.

That observation creates a moral risk for a book like this. If every victim becomes merely the prelude to a statute, a court case, or an agency, then the people at the center disappear into our appetite for consequences. I have tried not to let that happen. Institutions matter because people are forced to live inside them. Law matters because it changes what happens to the next person who calls for help, gets arrested, tries to testify, buys a product, enters an airport, challenges a government order, invests a retirement account, runs for office, or trusts the state to protect someone in custody.

The dead are not illustrations of policy.

Policy is part of what the living do with the fact that they died.

There is another risk: making criminals the protagonists of American development. Some were calculating. Some were fanatical. Some were corrupt. Some were caught inside mental illness or delusion. Some remain unknown. Some were people convicted under laws later regarded as unjust. None deserves the false grandeur of being called a secret author of the nation.

A crime does not “make” a decade by itself.

The title is deliberately provocative because it directs attention to the transaction between act and response. Benedict Arnold did not create American ideas of loyalty. The Whiskey rebels did not create federal power. Charles Guiteau did not single-handedly create civil-service reform. The Birmingham bombers did not create the civil-rights movement. The Watergate burglars did not invent constitutional checks and balances. The September 11 hijackers did not author every security decision made afterward.

The country did the making.

That is the story behind the stories.

Every chapter begins with a crime and ends somewhere larger: with a government discovering its reach, a court discovering its limit, a public discovering a fear, or a victim's story exposing the moral cost of rules that had seemed normal before the case put pressure on them.

The tape comes down.

The scene expands.

And America keeps walking through it.
