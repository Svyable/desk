# Chapter 1 — The Name for Treason

## 1776–1785: Benedict Arnold and the plot to sell West Point

The strange thing about Benedict Arnold is that he had already done enough to become an American hero.

Before his name became an insult, it was attached to bravery. He had marched north in the terrible Quebec expedition. He had fought on Lake Champlain. At Saratoga he drove into battle with such ferocity that a wounded leg became part of his legend. He had enemies in the Continental Army and grievances against Congress, but nobody needed to invent his military usefulness after the fact. Arnold had earned it.

That is what makes the crime matter.

Betrayal by an obvious enemy is an attack. Betrayal by a trusted insider is a conversion: the very qualities that made the person valuable become the tools that make the damage possible.

In 1780, Arnold obtained command of West Point, the American fortress system controlling a strategic stretch of the Hudson River. The position was not incidental. Control of the Hudson had haunted American strategy from the beginning of the Revolution because British control could help divide New England from the rest of the rebellion. West Point was a physical answer to that vulnerability. Fortifications, batteries, troops, supply arrangements, and the great chain across the river made the site one of the most important military positions in the American system.

Arnold intended to make that importance marketable.

He had been communicating secretly with the British through Major John André, an officer serving under British commander Sir Henry Clinton. The deal had political motives, personal motives, financial motives, and the resentments of a man who believed his services had not been rewarded in proportion to his sacrifices. It is tempting to turn all of that into psychological certainty. The surviving record permits something firmer and simpler: Arnold negotiated with the enemy and agreed to facilitate the surrender of West Point.

The Revolution nearly acquired its defining insider threat.

### The papers in the boot

On September 23, 1780, André was captured while traveling through territory north of New York City. The men who stopped him found papers connected to West Point hidden on his person. Their importance was not immediately theatrical. There was no instant national broadcast, no secure command channel, no computerized system that could freeze credentials and alert every checkpoint. Information moved through human hands while the man most endangered by it still held command.

Arnold learned that André had been captured before George Washington understood the full implication.

That gap was enough.

On September 25, Arnold fled down the Hudson to the British ship *Vulture*. Washington arrived at West Point expecting to meet a subordinate and instead discovered that the subordinate had defected. The following day Washington informed the army that treason had been discovered. The plot had failed, but its structure was unmistakable: a senior American officer, given responsibility for a crucial position, had planned to deliver that position to the enemy.

André did not escape. A board of American officers found him guilty as a spy. He was hanged on October 2.

Arnold did escape. He entered British service and survived the war.

That asymmetry helped give the story its peculiar durability. The captured intermediary died; the principal betrayer lived. There would be no American treason trial of Benedict Arnold, no courtroom in which witnesses reconstructed the plot under evidentiary rules, no sentence imposed by the new republic. The most famous traitor in American memory became famous without the legal closure that the word “traitor” can make us assume.

His punishment was historical.

His name became the sentence.

### Why this crime, in a decade full of violence?

The first ten years of the United States do not lack candidates for a book about crime. The Revolution itself was a landscape of violence, espionage, confiscation, desertion, privateering, mob action, contested loyalties, and legal systems operating under the pressure of war. People who called themselves patriots could call loyalists traitors; loyalists could call the Revolution itself rebellion. Property changed hands under political authority that the opposing side denied. Soldiers deserted. Spies crossed lines. Civilians were punished for allegiance.

Why choose Arnold?

Because his plot reveals a problem that every later American system of power would inherit: **trust is an attack surface**.

The phrase is modern. The problem is ancient.

Organizations cannot function if every person is treated as an enemy. Armies must delegate command. Governments must share information. Banks must allow someone to move money. Corporations must grant employees access to systems. Intelligence agencies must reveal secrets to analysts. Presidents must trust advisers. Police departments must authorize officers to use force. The more consequential the institution, the more dangerous an insider can become precisely because the institution has correctly trusted that person enough to let the person do the job.

Arnold was not dangerous despite his record. He was dangerous because of it.

A mediocre officer without Washington's confidence could not have offered West Point in the same way. The betrayal required accumulated credibility. Years of service were not merely background to the crime; they were part of its enabling infrastructure.

That makes Arnold more than a morality tale about loyalty. It makes him an early case study in the governance of trust.

### A republic before its machinery

There is another reason the West Point plot belongs first.

It occurs before the United States possessed the legal and bureaucratic machinery modern Americans associate with national security. The Constitution had not yet been ratified. The federal treason clause did not yet exist. There was no presidency as later generations would understand the office, no Department of Justice, no standing national police force, no national classification system, no professional counterintelligence bureaucracy.

Yet the underlying questions were already present.

What does loyalty mean in a political order still being created? How does a government distinguish dissent from betrayal? What procedures govern spies? How much authority should military leaders have in wartime? How does an institution protect itself from a person whose position gives him knowledge of its weaknesses?

The revolutionary government answered these questions through wartime structures rather than the later constitutional system. André faced a board of officers. Arnold's defection became military and political fact rather than the subject of an American criminal trial. The response belonged to a nation in formation.

A few years later, the Constitution would define treason against the United States with unusual narrowness: levying war against them, or adhering to their enemies, giving them aid and comfort, with conviction requiring two witnesses to the same overt act or confession in open court. That language grew from the framers' awareness of the political abuse of treason law in European history. Treason was too powerful an accusation to leave entirely elastic.

Arnold's conduct appears almost custom-built for the category—an officer secretly assisting the enemy in wartime—but the constitutional rule came after the plot. The chronological order matters. America first acquired the archetype, then later acquired the constitutional machinery designed to constrain the charge.

The country learned two things at once: betrayal could be existential, and accusations of betrayal could be dangerous.

Both lessons survive.

### The convenience of a villain

Nations like simple traitors.

A traitor is useful because he concentrates anxiety into a person. The Revolution was not simple. Americans disagreed over independence, taxation, property, military service, religion, imperial allegiance, local authority, and the distribution of sacrifice. The Continental Army struggled with pay and supply. Congress could be dysfunctional. Officers competed for rank. State and national claims overlapped. The revolutionaries themselves were committing treason in the eyes of the Crown.

Then Benedict Arnold offered the new country a villain whose betrayal was unusually easy to narrate.

He had been one of us.

He sold us out.

We caught the plot.

He fled.

That story does real explanatory work, but it also makes history cleaner than it was. Arnold's grievances were not imaginary merely because his response to them was treasonous. The Continental system did mishandle recognition and reimbursement. Factionalism was real. Officers did believe themselves slighted. A mature account must be able to hold two thoughts at once: institutions can treat people badly, and people remain responsible for what they choose to do with grievance.

This distinction recurs throughout the book.

The Whiskey rebels had economic and geographic complaints about federal taxation; armed resistance still forced the government to decide whether law could be enforced. Laborers in Haymarket-era Chicago faced brutal conditions and police violence; a bombing still killed. Political movements can contain legitimate grievances and criminal acts at the same time. One cannot erase the other without turning history into recruitment literature.

Arnold is an early warning against that flattening.

Understanding is not acquittal.

Condemnation is not explanation.

A country that wants to learn from crime needs both.

### The security problem hidden inside the hero story

Arnold's afterlife is so culturally complete that it can obscure the specific institutional lesson.

“Benedict Arnold” means traitor. Children can learn the name before they know what West Point was. The phrase works even when the historical details have disappeared. In one sense that is extraordinary historical survival. In another, it is information loss. When a person's name becomes a category, the category can replace the mechanism.

The mechanism was access.

Arnold knew the defenses because he was supposed to know them. He could influence the condition and disposition of forces because he was supposed to command them. His communications with the British mattered because he could convert knowledge into action. His value to the enemy rose with his value to the Americans.

Modern security systems call this an insider threat, but the vocabulary can encourage us to imagine that technology created the problem. It did not. Technology changes the scale, speed, and detectability of betrayal. It does not change the structural fact that trusted people occupy privileged positions.

The challenge is not to eliminate trust. That would eliminate the institution.

The challenge is to design trust so that no single person's grievance, corruption, ideology, coercion, or ambition can invisibly convert too much authority into damage.

That principle appears later in financial controls that separate functions, intelligence rules that compartment information, procurement systems that require disclosure, audit procedures, conflict-of-interest rules, judicial review, chain-of-command requirements, and the ordinary organizational instinct to make consequential actions visible to more than one person.

None of those systems is a direct descendant of West Point. History rarely provides such tidy lines. But Arnold gives the republic an early dramatic demonstration of why concentrated trust creates concentrated vulnerability.

### The betrayal that failed

It is easy to forget another feature of the story: the crime's intended outcome did not occur.

West Point did not fall.

The British did not gain the position through Arnold's plot. The Revolution did not collapse because of the betrayal. The plan was interrupted by the capture of André, the discovery of papers, and a chain of decisions that denied Arnold the result he wanted.

This makes the selection unusual for a book about consequential crimes. The importance lies not in completed material destruction but in the narrow avoidance of it and the clarity with which the attempt exposed vulnerability.

Near misses can change institutions.

A failed assassination can transform protection. An unsuccessful attack can reveal a security hole. An attempted conspiracy can justify—sometimes dangerously—new powers. A crime does not need to achieve its maximal intended harm to acquire a large afterlife.

The West Point plot survives partly because Americans could imagine the counterfactual.

What if André had passed through?

What if the documents had not been found?

What if Arnold had remained in command long enough to prepare the surrender?

Counterfactual history becomes irresponsible when it pretends to know the alternate world. But controlled counterfactuals are useful for measuring consequence. We do not need to claim that the British would certainly have won the war to understand why surrendering a strategic fortification from inside American command was a profound threat.

The gap between intended harm and actual harm is itself part of the lesson.

### The first line in a 250-year ledger

Every later chapter will complicate the word “crime.”

The Whiskey Rebellion asks when resistance to government becomes criminal insurrection. The Wythe murder asks what a court can know when law excludes a witness. Denmark Vesey asks whether an alleged conspiracy can alter society even when the surviving evidence does not permit later historians to reconstruct it with confidence. Korematsu asks what happens when the state criminalizes refusal to obey an unjust exclusion order. January 6 asks how criminal judgments coexist with later clemency and political reinterpretation.

Arnold is comparatively simple.

He held American command while secretly negotiating to give a strategic American position to the British enemy.

That clarity makes him a useful beginning. Before the book enters two and a half centuries of contested evidence, unequal law, changing jurisdiction, mass media, forensic science, federal expansion, civil rights, terrorism, regulatory failure, and political polarization, it starts with a betrayal recognizable across eras.

But even this simple case contains the book's central pattern.

The act is only half the story.

The other half is what Americans did with it.

They turned Arnold into a civic warning. They made his surname portable. They carried the story into a constitutional order that defined treason narrowly. They learned, again and again, that people with access can injure institutions in ways outsiders cannot. They built systems that depend on trust while fearing the moment trust becomes leverage.

The Revolution produced many heroes whose names became places.

Arnold's became a category.

That may be the most complete criminal afterlife in American history.

A man tried to sell a fortress.

He failed.

He sold his name instead.
