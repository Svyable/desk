# Chapter 7 — The Jailhouse Without a State

## 1836–1845: The murder of Joseph and Hyrum Smith

A jail is a promise.

The promise is not that the prisoner is guilty. A jail exists before guilt. Its more basic promise is that once the state takes control of a person's body, private enemies do not get to decide what happens next.

On June 27, 1844, that promise collapsed at Carthage, Illinois.

Joseph Smith, founder of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, and his brother Hyrum were being held in the county jail while Joseph awaited proceedings on a treason charge connected to his declaration of martial law in Nauvoo. A large armed mob stormed the building. Hyrum was shot and killed. Joseph was shot while attempting to escape through a second-story window, fell outside, and was killed.

The men were prisoners of the state.

The state did not keep them alive.

That fact makes the Carthage murders the defining crime of 1836–1845.

The story is not simply religious persecution, though anti-Mormon hostility was central. It is not simply mob murder, though a mob committed it. It is a story about what happens when local government, democratic conflict, religious community, private militia, press power, economic grievance, and criminal law all become entangled until custody itself stops meaning protection.

The murder moved an American religion west.

It also revealed a weakness in the American state: law can arrest a controversial person more easily than it can protect him from a community that has decided law is too slow.

### Nauvoo as a political problem

By the early 1840s, Nauvoo was not merely a church settlement.

It was a growing city with economic power, political organization, a charter, courts, and a militia—the Nauvoo Legion—that alarmed many neighbors. Latter-day Saints had already experienced violent expulsion and conflict in Missouri. In Illinois, concentration created safety through numbers but also intensified the perception that the community had become a political bloc capable of negotiating with parties and resisting outside authority.

Joseph Smith was therefore several things at once.

Religious prophet.

Mayor.

Militia leader.

Presidential candidate.

Defendant.

To followers, those roles could look like necessary defense against persecution.

To opponents, they could look like theocratic concentration of power.

Conflict sharpened around criticism of Smith, including accusations involving plural marriage and political control. In June 1844, dissidents published the first issue of the *Nauvoo Expositor*, a newspaper attacking Smith and church leadership. The Nauvoo City Council declared the press a nuisance and ordered it destroyed. The city marshal carried out the order.

That decision transformed political and religious conflict into a legal crisis.

Destroying a printing press in a republic with a strong culture of press freedom created outrage far beyond people who cared about Mormon theology. Smith and others faced charges. Smith declared martial law and mobilized the Nauvoo Legion before eventually submitting to authorities.

He and Hyrum were taken to Carthage.

There, the power equation reversed.

Inside Nauvoo, the church possessed numbers, organization, and armed force.

Inside a county jail surrounded by hostile territory, Joseph Smith depended on the state.

The state failed.

### The danger of the complicated victim

Carthage presents a challenge to historical writing because Joseph Smith is not a simple victim before the jail door closes.

He exercised power. He authorized or participated in actions that opponents regarded as abuses, including destruction of the *Expositor* press. His religious claims and political conduct were deeply controversial. Some former followers became fierce critics. The Nauvoo charter and municipal court had been used in ways outsiders believed insulated church leadership from normal legal process.

A writer trying to explain the murder can therefore be tempted into one of two dishonest simplifications.

The first sanctifies Smith so completely that every conflict becomes persecution.

The second treats his controversial conduct as though it reduced the state's obligation to protect him once he was in custody.

Neither is acceptable.

Rights do not belong only to uncomplicated people.

Custody is not a prize for good character.

The state had taken responsibility for Smith's body so that law, rather than private force, could decide the charges against him. Whatever one thinks of the destruction of the press, the organization of Nauvoo, Smith's theology, plural marriage, or his political ambitions, none gave a mob lawful authority to execute him.

The difference between rule of law and mob justice is tested most seriously when the prisoner is disliked.

Protecting a beloved defendant proves little.

Protecting the hated one is the institution's examination.

Carthage failed it.

### The mob and the mask of community

The attackers numbered roughly 150 to 200 according to the National Park Service's account. They were armed. Some blackened or otherwise concealed their faces. The disguise is revealing.

Mob violence often depends on a paradox: collective participation creates public power while anonymity distributes private responsibility.

Everyone knows.

No one did it.

A mob can represent itself as the community while making prosecution difficult because individual acts disappear into group action. Witnesses fear retaliation. Local juries may sympathize with defendants. Officials may share the mob's grievances or simply lack the will to confront neighbors.

This problem will become catastrophic after the Civil War, when white paramilitary groups and mobs use collective violence to suppress Black political participation. It will appear in lynching, labor conflict, anti-immigrant riots, race massacres, and attacks on civil-rights workers.

Carthage is an early national case of the state's custody being penetrated by organized local violence.

The jail was not an unknown location.

The prisoners were not hidden.

Authorities knew tensions were severe.

The danger was foreseeable.

Yet the mob reached the room.

A failure like that is not merely a security lapse. It is a statement about sovereignty.

Who controls the jail?

If the answer can shift from sheriff to crowd in an afternoon, then criminal procedure is conditional on local tolerance.

### Murder as migration policy

The most extraordinary criminal afterlife of Carthage was geographic.

The murders did not end anti-Mormon conflict in Illinois. They worsened instability inside the church and hostility outside it. Brigham Young ultimately emerged as the principal leader of the largest body of Latter-day Saints. Facing continuing pressure and violence, church members began leaving Nauvoo in 1846 and traveled west in one of the most consequential religious migrations in American history.

Joseph Smith's death did not single-handedly “cause Utah.”

That would compress too much history into one crime. The Saints had already been displaced before. Western migration had multiple strategic, religious, and political causes. Federal territorial development, war with Mexico, Native lands, and internal church decisions all shaped what followed.

But the murders removed Smith and Hyrum, accelerated a leadership transition, deepened the conviction that coexistence in Illinois had failed, and became central to a communal memory of persecution.

A mob at one county jail therefore helped change the map of American settlement.

That is criminal afterlife in its most literal form.

People moved.

### The press at the beginning of the chain

It is impossible to tell the Carthage story honestly without the *Nauvoo Expositor*.

The destruction of the press does not justify the murder.

It explains the crisis.

That distinction matters because free-press disputes can become too easily moralized after the fact. Americans revere the First Amendment, but local governments and political movements have repeatedly found reasons to treat hostile publication as dangerous. Newspapers have incited violence. They have also exposed abuse. A free press can be irresponsible without the government acquiring the right to destroy it.

The Nauvoo council's nuisance theory attempted to use local authority against a publication attacking the city's dominant religious and political leadership. Opponents saw the destruction as proof that Smith's government was despotic.

The result was a collision between two forms of lawlessness.

Municipal suppression of a hostile press.

Mob execution of men in custody.

One wrong did not cancel the other.

Instead, each helped radicalize the next audience.

This is a recurring escalation pattern.

An institution overreaches.

Opponents conclude ordinary remedies are useless.

They justify extraordinary resistance.

The extraordinary resistance produces new fear.

Authorities respond with greater force.

Each side cites the previous escalation as proof that its own escalation was necessary.

A stable legal order exists partly to interrupt that chain by offering remedies that do not depend on reciprocal force.

Carthage shows the chain running faster than the remedy.

### A trial after a lynching before the age of “lynching”

Five men were eventually tried for the murders and acquitted.

The verdicts reflected the profound difficulty of prosecuting community violence where witnesses, jurors, and defendants lived inside the same local political world. The acquittals did not create national closure. They helped preserve the murders as a grievance beyond ordinary law.

This matters because failed prosecution changes memory.

When a community believes law adequately punished an offender, the legal process can absorb part of the event's meaning. When no one is convicted, the crime remains available as proof that institutions cannot or will not protect the victim group.

That memory can persist across generations.

For Latter-day Saints, the murders of Joseph and Hyrum became martyrdom. The story entered sermons, family history, migration narratives, and church identity. It reinforced a collective understanding that formal American guarantees had repeatedly failed under pressure from local hostility.

Outsiders may disagree with elements of that communal interpretation.

They cannot understand the church's later relationship to federal power without taking the memory seriously.

Collective memory is not the same as verified fact.

It is a political force built from facts, interpretations, omissions, and repeated stories.

Crime produces memory institutions as surely as it produces statutes.

### The state has two duties to a defendant

Carthage reveals a distinction often lost in criminal justice debates.

The state has a duty to restrain defendants lawfully.

It also has a duty to protect them.

Those obligations can feel contradictory only if custody is imagined entirely as punishment. Before conviction, detention is not supposed to erase personhood. Even after conviction, incarceration does not authorize private violence.

A jail therefore represents monopolized force: the state claims the right to confine and simultaneously excludes everyone else's right to punish.

When a mob penetrates that monopoly, the institution loses more than a prisoner.

It loses the argument that people should surrender vengeance to law.

Why should victims' families trust courts if mobs can free a defendant?

Why should defendants surrender if mobs can kill them in custody?

Why should minority communities accept local authority if sheriffs cannot protect prisoners from hostile majorities?

The rule of law depends on all sides believing the state can hold the middle.

Carthage did not.

### Religious liberty is not theological approval

The murders also belong to the history of American religious freedom.

Religious liberty is easy to endorse when religion is private, familiar, and politically weak.

It becomes difficult when a religious movement builds a city, votes as a bloc, commands a militia, practices controversial family arrangements, claims revelation, and gives religious leaders civil authority.

The Latter-day Saint movement forced nineteenth-century America to confront that harder version.

Some criticisms of Smith concerned genuine questions of public power, fraud, property, marriage, courts, and militia authority. Others were inseparable from religious prejudice and fear. The categories overlapped.

A pluralist system must be capable of saying two things:

A religious leader can break ordinary law.

A religious leader remains protected by ordinary law.

The first prevents theology from becoming immunity.

The second prevents unpopularity from becoming outlawry.

Carthage is what happens when the second proposition fails.

### Why this crime and not another mob?

The era contained many instances of mob violence. Anti-abolitionist mobs attacked speakers and presses. Anti-Catholic and anti-immigrant violence grew. Racial violence remained embedded in slavery and frontier expansion. The 1837 murder of abolitionist editor Elijah Lovejoy in Alton, Illinois, by a pro-slavery mob could easily anchor a chapter about press freedom and abolition.

Lovejoy is the strongest runner-up.

Carthage wins because its afterlife combines more institutional systems at once: religious liberty, municipal power, militia power, press suppression, criminal custody, mob impunity, migration, and the governance of a rapidly growing religious minority.

It also creates a direct geographic consequence stretching into the later territorial conflict between the Latter-day Saint community and the federal government.

The crime killed two men.

The failure around it helped move a people.

### The criminal afterlife

Joseph and Hyrum Smith were killed because a mob did not trust, respect, or wish to wait for legal process.

The local prosecution that followed did not produce convictions.

The church's largest body left Illinois.

The American West gained a religious settlement project that would become Utah.

The federal government would later confront the same community over territorial governance and plural marriage, producing another long argument over the boundary between religious practice and national law.

Carthage did not settle that boundary.

It poisoned the relationship in which the boundary would be negotiated.

That is why the jail matters more than the gunfire alone.

A government can have statutes, judges, sheriffs, warrants, and cells and still fail at law if a sufficiently angry crowd becomes the final court.

Joseph Smith entered Carthage accused of crime.

He should have left it through legal process.

Instead, the wall between accusation and execution was one wooden door and a group of men who decided they represented justice better than the state did.

The door failed.

The consequences crossed a continent.
