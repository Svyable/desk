# Chapter 25 — The Crime and the Clemency

## 2016–2025: January 6 and the legal afterlife of an attack on the electoral count

The final crime in this book does not have one ending.

That is why it belongs last.

On January 6, 2021, Congress met in joint session at the United States Capitol to count electoral votes from the 2020 presidential election. Outside, a large crowd gathered after weeks of false claims that the election had been stolen. Many people remained outside lawfully. Others pushed through barricades, assaulted police, broke windows and doors, entered restricted areas, and breached the Capitol.

The joint session stopped.

Members of Congress evacuated or sheltered. Vice President Mike Pence was moved to safety. The electoral count resumed that night and was completed early on January 7.

Federal prosecutors ultimately charged more than a thousand people with offenses ranging from misdemeanor unlawful entry to assault, obstruction, destruction of property, and conspiracy. Juries convicted leaders of the Oath Keepers and Proud Boys of seditious conspiracy. Other defendants pleaded guilty or were convicted after trials.

Then the legal story changed.

In June 2024, the Supreme Court decided *Fischer v. United States*, narrowing the reach of one statute—18 U.S.C. §1512(c)(2)—that prosecutors had used in many January 6 cases. The Court held that the provision requires proof tied to impairing the availability or integrity of records, documents, objects, or other things used in an official proceeding, rather than covering every form of obstructive conduct.

Then the political story changed.

On January 20, 2025, President Donald Trump issued a proclamation commuting specified sentences, granting full pardons to other people convicted of offenses related to events at or near the Capitol, and directing the attorney general to dismiss covered pending federal indictments with prejudice.

The proclamation called the prosecutions a grave national injustice and invoked reconciliation.

The clemency did not function as an appellate ruling.

It did not erase historical facts found in trials.

It exercised the president's constitutional pardon power over federal offenses.

That distinction is the final lesson of 250 years.

A crime can be investigated, adjudicated, narrowed by the Supreme Court, and then transformed again by clemency without any one of those events becoming the whole truth.

### The attack on a procedure

January 6 was unusual because the immediate target was not only a building or person.

It was a constitutional procedure occurring inside the building.

The joint session counting electoral votes is ceremonial in appearance and legally consequential in function. The transfer of presidential power depends on institutions accepting results through prescribed procedures even when political actors dispute them.

The attackers did not need to occupy the Capitol permanently to succeed in harming the process.

They delayed it.

That matters because democratic violence can target time.

Stop the vote.

Delay certification.

Prevent officials from reaching the chamber.

Intimidate them into changing action.

Create enough chaos that ordinary procedure becomes impossible.

The objective may be temporary.

The constitutional injury is that force enters a process designed to count political power without force.

### Protest, riot, and conspiracy are not synonyms

The crowd on January 6 contained people engaged in different conduct.

Some attended a political rally and never entered restricted Capitol grounds.

Some crossed barriers but committed no violence.

Some entered the building.

Some stole or damaged property.

Some assaulted police.

Some groups coordinated before the day around the use of force to oppose the transfer of presidential power.

Criminal justice requires those distinctions.

A mass event tempts collective language.

“The rioters did X.”

“The protesters did Y.”

“The crowd wanted Z.”

Those phrases can become inaccurate because criminal liability is individualized.

Federal prosecutors charged defendants based on evidence of their own conduct and, where applicable, conspiratorial agreements.

The seditious-conspiracy trials are important precisely because the government had to prove agreement and intent rather than merely show that defendants belonged to organizations present at the Capitol.

In 2022, a jury convicted Oath Keepers founder Stewart Rhodes and Kelly Meggs of seditious conspiracy. In 2023, a jury convicted Proud Boys leaders Enrique Tarrio, Ethan Nordean, Joseph Biggs, and Zachary Rehl of seditious conspiracy.

These were trial judgments under the criminal standard of proof.

Later clemency changed punishment.

It did not convert the verdicts into verdicts that had never occurred.

### The statute from Enron's era

One of the most consequential legal disputes involved a statute written after a different crime story.

Section 1512(c) was enacted as part of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002 after corporate scandals including Enron exposed destruction of documents and obstruction of investigations. The provision criminalized corrupt impairment of evidence and, in a following clause, other obstruction of official proceedings.

Prosecutors used §1512(c)(2) against many January 6 defendants for obstructing the electoral-count proceeding.

The Supreme Court in *Fischer* rejected the government's broadest reading. The Court held that subsection (c)(2) must be understood in relation to the evidence-focused language preceding it and requires impairment of the availability or integrity of records, documents, objects, or other things used in an official proceeding, or an attempt to do so.

The ruling did not hold that January 6 conduct was lawful.

It interpreted one criminal statute.

That sentence should be repeated because political arguments often inflate statutory decisions into moral verdicts.

A court can say “this charge does not fit this law” without saying “nothing wrong occurred.”

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, joining the majority, emphasized that distinction in concurrence: the question was the scope of a particular crime, not whether the attack was morally defensible.

This is Haymarket's lesson matured.

Bad conduct does not authorize prosecutors to stretch a statute beyond its text.

Rule of law limits the state most importantly when the defendant is unpopular.

### Clemency is power, not exoneration

President Trump's January 20, 2025 proclamation used Article II clemency power on a sweeping scale.

It commuted the sentences of named defendants including Rhodes and others to time served. It granted full pardons to other individuals convicted of offenses related to January 6. It directed dismissal of pending federal indictments covered by the proclamation.

Supporters described the prosecutions as excessive or politically motivated and viewed the clemency as correction.

Critics argued the pardons minimized political violence, erased accountability, and rewarded people who attacked police or attempted to disrupt the electoral process.

The legal fact is narrower than either political interpretation.

A pardon removes or forgives federal criminal consequences within the scope of the grant. A commutation reduces punishment. Neither is an appellate acquittal.

The Constitution deliberately gives presidents a power capable of interrupting criminal judgments.

Washington used it after the Whiskey Rebellion.

Ford used it for Nixon.

The final chapter therefore returns to one of the book's oldest mechanisms.

The executive can decide that punishment should stop even after courts have spoken.

Whether that decision is wise is political and historical judgment.

What the power does is legal fact.

### The police line

More than 140 officers from the U.S. Capitol Police and Metropolitan Police Department were assaulted on January 6 according to federal authorities. Officers were struck, sprayed with chemical irritants, crushed in crowds, and attacked with objects.

The police violence complicates attempts to frame the event solely as symbolic trespass.

At the same time, not every defendant assaulted an officer, and the criminal system reflected that through different charges and sentences.

Again, individualization is the discipline.

The book refuses two opposite simplifications:

Everyone present was a violent insurrectionist.

No one present committed serious violence because some participants were peaceful.

Mass events contain both.

Law's job is to sort.

### The evidence was digital and self-produced

January 6 became one of the most documented mass-crime events in American history.

Capitol security cameras recorded movement. News cameras captured breaches. Participants livestreamed. Social-media posts documented travel, intent, celebration, and later deletion. Cell-phone data, text messages, encrypted chats, photos, videos, crowdfunding records, and open-source investigators created an enormous evidentiary field.

Compare that with Denmark Vesey.

In 1822, historians inherited an official record filtered through coercive proceedings and edited publications.

In 2021, investigators faced the opposite problem.

Too much record.

The epistemic challenge shifted from scarcity to volume.

Which account is authentic?

Who is visible in the frame?

What happened before the clip begins?

What does a deleted message imply?

What belongs to protected political speech and what proves criminal agreement?

Technology did not eliminate interpretation.

It multiplied the evidence requiring it.

### The peaceful transfer as infrastructure

Political systems depend on expectations that are stronger than enforcement.

A losing candidate concedes or at least leaves office.

Officials certify results.

Courts decide election disputes.

Supporters accept that another election will come.

Police do not need to defend every counting room from crowds because most people treat procedure as binding.

That expectation is infrastructure.

January 6 damaged it by making physical disruption of electoral certification newly imaginable on a national scale.

The building was repaired quickly.

The expectation takes longer.

This is why political violence can have a large afterlife even when the immediate objective fails.

The electoral votes were counted.

Joe Biden became president on January 20, 2021.

The constitutional transfer occurred.

But future election administrators, police departments, lawmakers, prosecutors, political parties, and citizens now plan inside a world where the Capitol breach happened once.

A prior impossibility becomes a scenario.

### January 6 and the danger of partisan history

No chapter in this book is easier to misuse.

The event remains embedded in partisan identity. Readers may approach every noun as a signal of allegiance: riot, insurrection, protest, attack, defendants, hostages, political prisoners, mob, patriots.

The safest response is not euphemism.

It is source discipline.

Describe conduct supported by video, court records, guilty pleas, verdicts, opinions, and official acts.

Separate defendants from crowds.

Separate legal conclusions from political rhetoric.

Acknowledge the Supreme Court's narrowing of §1512(c)(2) without implying every conviction collapsed.

Acknowledge the 2025 pardons without calling them exonerations.

Acknowledge competing interpretations without pretending factual claims are matters of taste.

Neutrality is not refusing to name assault.

It is applying the same evidentiary standard regardless of whose politics benefit from the fact.

### Why January 6 rather than a mass shooting or cyberattack

The final period contains crimes with staggering human cost: mass shootings in Las Vegas, Parkland, El Paso, Uvalde, Buffalo, and elsewhere; ransomware attacks; opioid-related corporate crime; fraud; political violence; hate crimes; and attacks on public officials.

January 6 is selected because its afterlife crosses the constitutional system itself.

It generated one of the largest federal investigations in American history, hundreds of convictions, rare seditious-conspiracy verdicts, Supreme Court statutory interpretation, security redesign at the Capitol, congressional investigation, and sweeping presidential clemency.

It also closes thematic circles opened by the first chapters.

Arnold: insider betrayal and loyalty.

Whiskey: resistance to federal authority.

Hamilton–Burr: political conflict and illegal violence.

Vesey: conspiracy and preventive prosecution.

Lincoln: political violence against constitutional transition.

Colfax: force aimed at democratic participation.

Watergate: obstruction and executive accountability.

January 6 contains echoes of all of them without being identical to any.

### The criminal afterlife

The attack failed to stop the transfer of power.

The prosecutions did not end the argument.

The Supreme Court narrowed one statute.

The clemency did not erase the event.

That layered ending is appropriate for the final chapter because crime history is not a row of verdicts.

It is a sequence of institutions answering one another.

Police arrest.

Prosecutors charge.

Juries decide.

Appellate courts interpret.

Presidents pardon.

Historians reconstruct.

Citizens remember selectively.

The same conduct changes meaning as it moves through those institutions, but the underlying facts do not become optional merely because the legal consequence changes.

The first chapter began with Benedict Arnold, whose betrayal failed to deliver West Point but succeeded in becoming a national archetype.

The last ends with an attack that failed to stop the electoral count but became a permanent test of how Americans describe political violence committed by fellow citizens.

The country's first 250 years do not end with a solved question.

They end with a method.

Keep the act separate from the accusation.

Keep the verdict separate from the pardon.

Keep the law separate from justice long enough to examine where they diverge.

Then follow the afterlife.

That is where the country changes.
