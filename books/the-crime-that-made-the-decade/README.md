*The Crime That Made the Decade* is not a ranking of evil or a conventional true-crime anthology. It selects one crime for each exact ten-year period in the first 250 years of the United States—1776–1785 through 2016–2025—and asks which event had the largest **criminal afterlife**: the most durable effect on law, government power, evidence, civil rights, political legitimacy, security, markets, media, or the practical meaning of justice.

The central surprise is that the “most important” crime is often not the bloodiest or most famous. A poisoning matters because racial evidence law prevented a Black witness from speaking. An alleged slave conspiracy matters because the prosecution may be part of the event historians are trying to reconstruct. A kidnapping changes federal jurisdiction. An unsolved product poisoning redesigns packaging. A fraud matters because regulators had already received the warning. The final chapter distinguishes criminal judgments, Supreme Court statutory interpretation, and presidential clemency rather than treating any one as a substitute for the others.

## Current editorial boundary

This is a complete **first draft**, not a release candidate. The next Desk pass should expand every numbered chapter to the repository's substantive floor without padding, perform a chapter-by-chapter primary-source audit, add pinpoint source notes where disputed facts require them, and then run the standard length and repository-integrity gates on one frozen source.

No claim is made that this is the first decade-by-decade American crime book. Prior art includes twentieth-century decade-by-decade crime/trial collections. The distinct proposition here is the exact 250-year architecture, broad crime category, and institutional-afterlife selection rule.
