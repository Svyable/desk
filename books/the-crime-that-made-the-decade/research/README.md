# Research brief — The Crime That Made the Decade

## Governing question

What was the most important crime of each ten-year period in the first 250 years of the United States—not the bloodiest crime, the most famous crime, or the crime with the most lurid facts, but the crime whose **afterlife** most changed American law, government, evidence, media, markets, security, civil rights, or the practical meaning of justice?

The manuscript uses twenty-five exact ten-year windows beginning with 1776–1785 and ending with 2016–2025. This is deliberate. Calendar decades would force either twenty-six chapters or an arbitrary partial opening/closing decade. The exact windows make the architecture itself correspond to the country's first 250 years.

## The selection rule

“Most important” is an argument, not a measurable historical fact. Each choice is therefore treated as a thesis to defend rather than a ranking to announce. A candidate rises when its consequences travel beyond the direct victims and defendants. The central test is the **criminal afterlife**: what did the event cause institutions, courts, legislators, journalists, investigators, businesses, movements, or ordinary citizens to do differently afterward?

Five questions guide selection:

1. Did the crime alter the practical reach or legitimacy of government power?
2. Did it produce a durable change in law, enforcement, evidence, security, regulation, or institutional design?
3. Did it create a lasting cultural vocabulary for a category of wrongdoing or injustice?
4. Does understanding it illuminate a larger American conflict that a more famous crime would obscure?
5. If this event disappeared from the decade, would the country's later legal or political development plausibly look meaningfully different?

The manuscript does **not** add scores to these questions. They are editorial disciplines, not a pseudo-quantitative ranking system.

## What counts as a crime

The book deliberately tests the edge of the category. Some chapters concern straightforward crimes with identified perpetrators and convictions. Some concern conspiracies, political violence, terrorism, corruption, mass violence, or crimes that produced contested prosecutions. A few concern conduct that the state criminalized even though later judgment treats the prosecution itself as a profound injustice. That breadth is part of the thesis: a history of American crime is also a history of who had the power to define crime.

The manuscript distinguishes carefully among:

- allegation, indictment, conviction, acquittal, pardon, commutation, and exoneration;
- criminal conduct and conduct merely described as criminal by political actors;
- settled facts and disputed historical interpretations;
- a pardon that forgives or responds to injustice and a judicial finding that vacates a conviction;
- an unsolved crime and a crime for which someone was convicted of a related offense;
- institutional consequence and simplistic single-cause claims.

Where a case is unresolved or historiographically contested, that uncertainty belongs in the chapter rather than in a footnote designed to disappear it.

| 1796–1805 | Hamilton–Burr duel | Illegal private violence, elite honor, political rivalry, and the boundary between custom and law collide. |

## Why these are not simply “the most famous crimes”

Several obvious cases are absent. That is intentional. The Osage murders, Sacco and Vanzetti, the Scottsboro prosecutions, the Rosenbergs, the Black Dahlia, the Zodiac killings, the Manson murders, the Pentagon Papers case, the Iran-Contra prosecutions, the Unabomber, Columbine, Enron, and many others could anchor outstanding books of their own. Some fall into windows where another event had a wider institutional afterlife; others would duplicate a theme already carried more powerfully elsewhere in the manuscript.

The omissions are not verdicts on suffering. Importance here is not moral worth. A crime with one victim can change more law than a crime with a hundred victims; a prosecution later regarded as unjust can alter the country more than an open-and-shut conviction. The book refuses to turn body count into historical significance.

## Prior-art boundary

A title-and-concept search found substantial prior literature on American crime history and at least one twentieth-century book built around a decade-by-decade sequence of sensational murder trials: Mark J. Phillips and Aryn Z. Phillips, *Trials of the Century: A Decade-by-Decade Look at Ten of America's Most Sensational Crimes* (2016). That work covers ten twentieth-century trials selected for sensational public attention. The present manuscript therefore does **not** claim to invent decade-by-decade crime writing.

The distinctive proposition here is narrower and more defensible: twenty-five exact ten-year eras spanning 1776–2025; a category broader than murder trials; and a selection rule based on institutional afterlife rather than sensationalism. A broader recent scholarly reference, *The Routledge History of Crime in America*, is thematic and multi-author rather than one defining crime per ten-year era. These boundaries should remain explicit in any publication copy; avoid “first ever” or “never before” claims unless a formal bibliographic review later supports them.

## Evidence policy

The preferred evidence hierarchy is:

1. court opinions, indictments, trial records, statutes, proclamations, congressional records, and contemporaneous official documents;
2. National Archives, Library of Congress, National Park Service, Federal Judicial Center, FBI, DOJ, FDA, SEC, Senate Historical Office, 9/11 Commission, and equivalent institutional histories that link back to records;
3. high-quality academic scholarship used especially where primary records are structurally incomplete or historiographically contested;
4. reputable secondary histories for synthesis and scene-setting, never as a substitute for checking legal status.

Official sources are not treated as neutral simply because they are official. In chapters about Vesey, Reconstruction violence, internment, civil rights, Watergate, terrorism, and January 6, government records can themselves be evidence of institutional perspective and must be read against adversarial records and later scholarship.

## High-risk factual boundaries

### George Wythe

The book may say that George Wythe was likely poisoned and that his grandnephew George Wythe Sweeney was tried and acquitted. It should explain the exclusion of Black testimony under Virginia law as a major evidentiary boundary. Avoid claiming that modern historical consensus can supply a criminal conviction the 1806 court did not produce.

### Denmark Vesey

Use “alleged conspiracy,” “reported plot,” or equivalent language when discussing the historical event as a whole. The 1822 official trial report treated the conspiracy as real and named Vesey as its leader. Later historians have disputed whether the surviving testimony proves a large organized plot or reflects coercive, edited, and institutionally interested proceedings. The historiographical dispute is the chapter's core, not an inconvenience to be resolved by authorial certainty.

### Haymarket

Do not state that the executed defendants threw the bomb. The bomber was never conclusively identified. The chapter should separate the bombing from the legal theory used to convict anarchist defendants as accessories to murder and from Governor John Peter Altgeld's later pardons of the surviving defendants.

### Leo Frank

Frank was convicted of Mary Phagan's murder. Governor John Slaton commuted the death sentence. A mob abducted Frank from state custody and lynched him in 1915. Georgia's 1986 posthumous pardon explicitly did **not** decide guilt or innocence; it cited the state's failure to protect Frank and failure to bring his killers to justice. The manuscript must preserve that distinction.

### Korematsu

Fred Korematsu's criminal conviction was upheld by the Supreme Court in 1944. A federal district court vacated the conviction in 1983 after evidence emerged that relevant government intelligence reports had been suppressed or destroyed. Avoid compressing those two proceedings into the false claim that the Supreme Court itself later reversed the 1944 judgment.

### Emmett Till

Roy Bryant and J. W. Milam were acquitted of murder by an all-white jury and later admitted killing Till in a magazine interview. DOJ's 2021 closure of a renewed investigation found insufficient evidence to prove a later-reported recantation attributed to Carolyn Bryant Donham. Do not repeat the supposed recantation as fact.

### Tylenol

Seven Chicago-area residents died after ingesting cyanide-laced Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules in 1982. James Lewis was convicted of extortion related to the crisis, not of the murders. The FBI states that no one could be charged with the murders for lack of evidence. Keep “unsolved” separate from the extortion conviction.

### Madoff

Madoff pleaded guilty to eleven federal felonies in 2009 and was sentenced to 150 years. His fraud's represented balances and claimed losses should not be casually interchanged with actual principal losses or later victim recoveries. Use precise measures and labels whenever amounts appear.

### January 6

This chapter must remain especially disciplined because the legal and political afterlife continued through the end of the book's 2016–2025 window. DOJ trial records establish numerous federal convictions, including seditious-conspiracy convictions of Oath Keepers and Proud Boys leaders. On January 20, 2025, President Donald Trump commuted specified sentences, granted full pardons to other people convicted of offenses relating to the events at or near the Capitol, and directed dismissal with prejudice of pending federal indictments covered by the proclamation. Clemency is a constitutional legal act; it should not be mislabeled an appellate reversal or historical finding that the underlying conduct did not occur. The chapter should describe competing political interpretations only where necessary and should anchor statements about criminal liability in actual judgments, opinions, and the clemency instrument.

## Falsification tests

The thesis weakens if any of the following are true:

- The selected event had little durable consequence beyond publicity, while a plausible runner-up clearly changed more law or institutional practice.
- The book repeatedly mistakes correlation for causation by saying a crime “created” a reform that was already substantially underway.
- The architecture produces twenty-five isolated true-crime stories rather than one cumulative history of American power and justice.
- The selection method systematically privileges crimes against elites or nationally covered crimes while overlooking violence against people denied access to national media and courts.
- “Crime” becomes so elastic that the category loses analytical meaning.
- The final chapters become partisan adjudication rather than evidence-led history.
- The storytelling depends on invented interior thoughts, reconstructed dialogue, or scene details unsupported by records.

A revision should be willing to replace any chapter if those tests fail.

## Narrative discipline

Scenes may be cinematic but must be documentable. When exact weather, clothing, room layout, dialogue, sequence, or gesture is not supported, do not invent it merely to make the opening vivid. Use the record's uncertainty as texture. Often the most powerful sentence in a crime story is not “we know what happened.” It is “this is the point where the record stops agreeing with itself.”

Each chapter should answer four things without turning them into visible boilerplate: what happened; what the legal system said happened; what the country changed afterward; and why this crime rather than the obvious runner-up defines the era for this book.

## Editorial promise

The manuscript is neither a hall of villains nor a museum of gore. Victims are not props used to make institutions interesting. Perpetrators are not made charismatic by excess attention. The object is to tell twenty-five gripping stories while showing a larger pattern: America is changed not only by crimes, but by what it chooses to call a crime, whom it can hear as a witness, which harms it can prosecute, which powers it grants in response, and what it decides to remember when the courtroom is empty.
