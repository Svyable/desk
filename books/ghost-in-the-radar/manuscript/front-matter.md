# Ghost in the Radar

## When the Sensor Can Be Lied To

Sven Hardy Benson

This is a book about a dangerous grammatical error.

A radar saw an object.

Sometimes it did not.

Radar receives energy, processes it, rejects some of it, promotes some of it, estimates range and motion, and presents a machine-made proposition to a human or another machine. The proposition may correspond beautifully to an airplane. It may correspond badly to a balloon. It may be weather. It may be a processing artifact. And, in at least one documented Cold War intelligence program, the proposition could be deliberately manufactured by somebody who wanted the radar operator to believe an airplane was present.

That does not make radar useless. It makes radar interesting.

The modern UAP argument often treats multiple sensors as though they were independent witnesses in a courtroom: radar plus infrared plus eyesight equals three votes for an object. This book asks the uglier engineering question first. What exactly did each sensor measure, what transformations stood between measurement and display, which errors could be correlated, and could an adversary deliberately manufacture one or more of those observables?

Project PALLADIUM is the historical wedge. Public accounts by CIA technical officer Gene Poteat describe a Cold War electronic-deception system built to inject false targets into Soviet radar and then exploit the adversary's reaction as intelligence. The ghost was useful because it was false. Its unreality was the instrument.

The book will not claim PALLADIUM explains modern UAP incidents. It does not. Nor will it treat every anomalous return as spoofing. Physical unknowns remain possible wherever the evidence leaves them possible. The point is methodological: once deception, artifacts, geometry and processing can all generate object-like observables, `sensor detection` and `physical object` must be separate evidentiary rungs.

Four evidence classes govern the manuscript.

**Class 1 — verified, declassified, documented.** Official program records, released government histories, primary technical papers, official case-resolution reports, reproducible sensor geometry and other records whose provenance can be checked.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete.** Attributed testimony and technical recollection from people plausibly positioned to know, incomplete official records, or well-sourced reporting whose key operational details cannot yet be independently reconstructed.

**Class 3 — disputed or unconfirmed.** Specific claims of secret continuation programs, deliberate spoofing in named modern UAP incidents, extraordinary performance, hidden technologies or attribution that lacks independent public confirmation.

**Class 4 — synthesis and conjecture.** The book's own model of adversarial phenomenology: the possibility that the most important unit in anomaly analysis is not the object but the complete sensor-to-belief chain, including an intelligent opponent capable of attacking that chain.

Testimony is not promoted into physics. A patent proves filing, not efficacy. Classification is not evidence that the classified answer is extraordinary. An unresolved case is unresolved, not a reserve category for the author's favorite explanation.

The manuscript loses its strongest thesis if modern multisensor systems can be shown, under realistic operational conditions, to make correlated spoofing/artifact explanations negligible without relying on unavailable data or circular assumptions. It also loses if PALLADIUM proves too poorly documented to sustain the historical role assigned to it. Those are acceptable outcomes. A book about sensors should be willing to be measured.
