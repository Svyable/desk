# Rare Minerals and Missing Worlds

Most minerals are not common. That fact is easy to miss because museums select for spectacle and textbooks select for importance.

Rare mineral species are useful because they reveal how thin some formation pathways are. A peculiar combination of elements, oxidation states and fluid conditions may occur in one deposit and nowhere else yet sampled. Rarity is therefore evidence about pathway frequency, not necessarily about cosmic improbability.

This is where exoplanet mineralogy becomes strange. We may someday infer bulk crustal chemistry remotely while knowing almost nothing about its long tail of rare phases. Two planets with similar elemental inventories could possess very different mineral distributions because one has oceans, one has stagnant-lid tectonics, one has oxygen, one has life, one has industry.

The missing-world problem is that we have only one richly cataloged world. Any claim that a mineral suite is a biosignature or technosignature must survive this sample-size embarrassment.

A good response is not paralysis. It is to build generative models: given bulk chemistry and planetary processes, what mineral families become likely? Then ask which observations are difficult to produce without adding a new process.

That is the logic used throughout anomaly science when it is working properly. The anomaly is not the answer. It is the residual after the model earns the right to have one.
