# A Planet Leaves Crystals

A mineral cabinet is a planetary crime scene arranged by color.

The specimens look static because we meet them after the interesting part. Quartz does not advertise the fluid that precipitated it. A zircon does not explain the magma from which it crystallized. A copper carbonate does not announce the oxygenated water that had to move through an older sulfide deposit before blue and green crusts became possible. Yet every crystal is conditional. It is a frozen permission slip from a particular chemical environment.

This is the first useful inversion. We usually ask what minerals a planet contains. A harder question is what had to happen for those minerals to become possible.

Robert Hazen and colleagues pushed this question into a historical framework they called mineral evolution: mineral diversity increased as planetary processes opened new chemical pathways. The terminology can mislead. Minerals do not reproduce or undergo Darwinian selection. The evolution is historical diversification of mineral species through changing physical, chemical and biological environments.

That distinction matters because the larger argument depends on not smuggling agency into geology. A mineral assemblage can be diagnostic without being designed. Oxygen-bearing minerals can record oxidation without telling us whether oxygen came from photosynthesis, photochemistry, or some combination. Industrial compounds can look exotic without being impossible in nature.

The interesting territory begins after those cautions. Earth has thousands of recognized mineral species. Many are rare. Many depend on narrow combinations of elements, fluids, pressures, temperatures and redox states. Some became abundant only after biology altered the atmosphere and oceans. Humans have now produced an enormous parallel inventory of crystalline compounds, alloys, ceramics, slags, pigments and electronic materials.

A planet therefore casts what I will call a mineral shadow: the set of durable materials made possible by its history but not predictable from bulk composition alone. The shadow of a sterile basaltic world differs from the shadow of an oxygenated biosphere. The shadow of an industrial civilization may differ again.

The rest of the book asks how far that statement can be made quantitative before it becomes poetry.
