# The Oxygen Mineral Explosion

Oxygen did not merely change what animals could eventually breathe. It changed what rocks were allowed to become.

The Great Oxidation Event, beginning roughly 2.4 billion years ago, reorganized surface chemistry. Oxidized forms of iron, copper, uranium and other elements became stable in environments where earlier reducing conditions had favored different phases. Hazen's mineral-evolution work argues that a large fraction of Earth's mineral diversity is directly or indirectly associated with biological oxygenation.

The phrase mineral explosion is tempting, but it needs discipline. Mineral species counts depend on discovery, naming rules and preservation. Oxygenation was neither instantaneous nor globally uniform. Some oxidized minerals can form abiotically in local settings. The evidentiary claim is not that a particular red mineral proves cyanobacteria.

The stronger claim is statistical and historical: atmospheric and oceanic redox evolution opened enormous new regions of mineral phase space.

That gives us a planetary lesson. A biosphere can alter geology without leaving a recognizable fossil. It changes the reaction network. Life becomes legible in what the planet can make.

If we eventually obtain mineralogical inventories from Mars, the Moon, asteroids and returned samples from other worlds, the comparison may be more revealing than any single specimen. Diversity itself becomes evidence—but only after correcting for parent-body size, water history, differentiation, temperature, pressure and sampling effort.

The mineral shadow is not a magic fingerprint. It is a distribution.
