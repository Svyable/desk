# The Mineral Shadow

A planet does not merely have geology. It acquires a mineral vocabulary.

Earth began with a comparatively small repertoire of crystalline compounds. Then water worked it. Plate tectonics worked it. Oxygen worked it. Life worked it. Eventually we did too. The result is a planet whose mineral diversity contains a history of processes that cannot be read from elemental abundance alone.

This book follows an odd proposition: mineral diversity may be a planetary memory system—not memory in the mystical sense, but durable path dependence. Certain minerals exist because earlier events created chemical environments in which they could form. Biology changed those environments. Technology changes them again.

That makes mineralogy unexpectedly relevant to astrobiology and technosignatures. The claim is not that an unfamiliar crystal is evidence of life, still less intelligence. The claim is narrower and more useful: sufficiently unusual mineral assemblages can encode constraints on a world's history, and some industrial mineral products may be difficult to obtain through known natural pathways.

The evidentiary rule throughout is simple. A mineral is an object. Its origin is an inference.
