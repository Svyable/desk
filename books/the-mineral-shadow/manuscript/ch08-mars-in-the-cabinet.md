# Mars in the Cabinet

Mars is the obvious laboratory because we already have pieces of it.

Martian meteorites deliver igneous minerals and alteration products to terrestrial laboratories, although ejection, transit and terrestrial residence complicate every sample. Rovers add in-situ mineralogy: clays, sulfates, silica, iron oxides and other phases that constrain past water chemistry.

None of these minerals by itself establishes life. That is precisely why Mars is useful for this book. It forces mineralogical inference to operate under disappointment.

A clay can tell us water and rock interacted. Sulfates can tell us about evaporation and acidity. Redox-sensitive minerals constrain environmental conditions. Those conditions may be habitable without having been inhabited.

The temptation is to convert habitability into biography. The better move is to construct a sequence of increasingly demanding hypotheses: water existed; chemical gradients existed; organics were preserved; disequilibria are difficult to explain abiotically; microtextures or isotopic patterns support biological processing; multiple lines cohere in context.

Sample return, if achieved with uncontested custody and contamination control, would sharpen this ladder enormously. Until then Mars teaches a useful discipline: geology can approach the threshold of biology without crossing it for us.
