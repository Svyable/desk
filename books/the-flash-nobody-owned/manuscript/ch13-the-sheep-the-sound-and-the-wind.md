# 13 — The Sheep, the Sound, and the Wind

Decades after the flash, the most interesting evidence was no longer in orbit.

It was underwater and inside thyroid glands.

Lars-Erik De Geer and Christopher Wright revisited the Vela incident using declassified technical material, hydroacoustic evidence and historical measurements of iodine-131 in Australian sheep. Their 2017–2018 work argues that the combined record is consistent with a small atmospheric nuclear explosion near the Prince Edward Islands.

The hydroacoustic argument is physically attractive. Sound in the ocean can propagate extraordinary distances through low-loss channels. If a low-yield atmospheric or near-surface nuclear event coupled energy into the ocean, distant hydrophones might record arrivals. Later NRL analysis reportedly identified signals interpreted as consistent with a maritime nuclear shot.

The sheep argument is stranger, which is why it deserves care.

Iodine-131 is a short-lived fission product. Historical monitoring programs collected animal thyroids because grazing animals integrate deposition over pasture and the thyroid concentrates iodine. De Geer and Wright argue that samples from sheep slaughtered in Melbourne after the event contained iodine-131 and that reconstructed atmospheric transport from the Prince Edward region, plus rainfall, provides a plausible pathway.

This is serious evidence.

It is not a signed confession.

The reconstruction depends on old measurements, source exclusion, transport modeling, sample provenance and the assumption that the relevant iodine came from a nuclear explosion in the hypothesized region. The fact that the data were not generated by investigators trying to prove the Vela event cuts down one source of bias; the retrospective selection of which observations matter introduces another.

The right question is cumulative.

Does optical + hydroacoustic + radionuclide evidence fit a nuclear event better than the alternatives?

Later critics of the meteoroid hypothesis say yes. The Ruina panel, with a different evidentiary set and at a different time, said probably no.

The unresolved status of the case is not an excuse to split the difference mechanically. Evidence can become stronger over time.

But the book will not convert *stronger* into *settled*.
