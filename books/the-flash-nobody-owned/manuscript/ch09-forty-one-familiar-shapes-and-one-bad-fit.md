# 9 — Forty-One Familiar Shapes and One Bad Fit

The power of the 22 September signal came from resemblance.

The weakness came from imperfect resemblance.

Later accounts note that Vela bhangmeters had recorded the characteristic double-pulse signature of known atmospheric nuclear tests many times before. The 1979 trace fell into that visual family. But the second-pulse amplitudes reported by the paired sensors differed in a way the Ruina panel treated as important.

This is the problem with signatures: the closer an event is to the template, the more heavily small deviations get weighted.

Imagine a face-recognition system that is 95 percent sure it sees a wanted person. If the stakes are low, you flag the image. If arrest, war or sanctions follow, the residual five percent becomes the whole problem.

The White House panel searched a huge archive of non-nuclear Vela signals for analogues. It found odd optical events attributed or plausibly attributable to meteoroids and other effects. A hypothesis emerged in which a small meteoroid struck the spacecraft and sunlight reflected from resulting debris, producing an unusual double signal.

That explanation solved one institutional problem elegantly: it allowed the optical trace to be real without requiring a clandestine nuclear test.

But elegance is not evidence.

The later critique by Christopher Wright and Lars-Erik De Geer argues that the impact/reflection hypothesis lacks a persuasive physical model and that aspects of the disputed optical behavior were not as unique as the panel implied. Their work is not a government confession. It is a technical challenge to the alternative explanation.

The correct posture is to keep the hypotheses symmetrical.

If “double flash” does not equal “bomb,” then “possible meteoroid analogue” does not equal “meteoroid.” Both require predicted consequences.

This is where the 1979 case diverges sharply from gamma-ray bursts. For the cosmic events, repeat observations accumulated and independent satellites confirmed the phenomenon. For the Vela flash, the event was singular enough that investigators had to interrogate traces left elsewhere in the world.

The machine saw one shape.

The argument became a hunt for its shadow.
