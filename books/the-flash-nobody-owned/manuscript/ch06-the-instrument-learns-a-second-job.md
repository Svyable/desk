# 6 — The Instrument Learns a Second Job

By the late Vela era, the spacecraft had become accidental observatories.

Vela 5B, launched in 1969, operated until June 1979—far beyond a nominal short mission. NASA’s HEASARC archive describes instruments spanning X-rays into gamma rays and lists science results involving X-ray binaries, transients and gamma-ray bursts. A verification platform had acquired a scientific afterlife while still belonging to a security lineage.

Long-lived sensors accumulate personality. Engineers learn their quirks. Analysts learn which disturbances recur. Thresholds are tuned. Background behavior becomes familiar. This tacit knowledge is valuable and dangerous.

Valuable, because an experienced analyst can recognize when a signal is genuinely unusual.

Dangerous, because familiarity invites overconfidence. Old hardware is not young hardware. Radiation damage accumulates. Optical surfaces age. electronics drift. A classifier trained on one set of known events may face another under different geometry.

The 1979 Vela satellite was old.

That fact sits at the center of the later debate because the event’s signal was both familiar and imperfect. The optical trace resembled the canonical double flash of an atmospheric nuclear explosion, but it contained discrepancies that the White House scientific panel took seriously.

The same institutional culture that had learned not to call every gamma-ray trigger a bomb now faced the inverse problem: what if a bomb looked almost, but not exactly, like a bomb?

Anomaly science and verification science share instruments but not burdens of proof.

Astronomy can publish *we found something new*.

A president deciding whether another state violated a nuclear treaty needs a noun, an actor and a confidence level.

Vela’s second job had prepared it to find wonders.

Its first job was about to become much harder.
