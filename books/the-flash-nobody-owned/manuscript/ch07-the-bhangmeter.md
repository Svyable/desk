# 7 — The Bhangmeter

The name sounds unserious. The instrument was not.

A bhangmeter is an optical sensor designed to recognize the characteristic time history of an atmospheric nuclear explosion. The basic physics produces a double-pulse light curve. The first flash is intense and brief. As the expanding shock-heated fireball becomes opaque, apparent brightness falls. As the shock evolves and the fireball again becomes radiatively visible, a broader second maximum emerges.

The shape is not a mystical fingerprint. It is a physical consequence of explosion hydrodynamics and radiation transport. That is why it can be useful for discrimination.

Vela carried paired optical sensors so that an event could be compared across channels. A true nuclear event should generate a family resemblance to known tests, while artifacts, glints, lightning and particle effects might not.

Yet no signature is infinitely exclusive.

This point becomes crucial because popular retellings often treat “double flash” as synonymous with “nuclear test.” It is evidence, not identity. The Vela system itself accumulated non-nuclear optical events—sometimes called zoo events—that could imitate parts of the expected pattern.

The epistemic burden is therefore Bayesian even if nobody in the control room uses that word. How likely is this signal if a nuclear test occurred? How likely if a meteoroid struck the satellite? How likely under lightning, reflected sunlight, sensor pathology or coincident phenomena? What independent evidence should exist under each hypothesis?

A good warning system does not merely score the signal. It predicts the corroboration.

A nuclear explosion at sea might create hydroacoustic energy, radionuclides, ionospheric disturbances, electromagnetic effects, perhaps seismic signatures depending on coupling and yield. A local satellite impact should not create those same remote consequences.

That means the Vela incident was never just an optical question.

It became one because the optical data arrived first.
