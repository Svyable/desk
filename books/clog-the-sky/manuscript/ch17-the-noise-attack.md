# The Noise Attack

A denial-of-service attack is conceptually simple. You do not need to destroy the target. You need to give it more work than it can finish.

The Robertson Panel described the analog version before computer networks made the metaphor obvious.

Its members worried that irrelevant UFO reports could clog communication channels and that repeated false alarms could degrade response to a real threat. They also worried an adversary might exploit that condition. Those statements are documented. The stronger claim — that a foreign intelligence service actually ran such an operation through the UFO phenomenon — is not.

The distinction suggests an experiment in historical reasoning.

Suppose every unexplained object in 1952 had been mundane. The security concern still survives. Enough mundane reports arriving at enough sensitive nodes can consume real capacity. Now reverse the thought experiment. Suppose one report represented an important foreign technology. The presence of thousands of mundane reports makes that one harder to find. In both worlds, the classifier is the strategic terrain.

This is why the book has spent less time on saucer shapes than readers may have expected.

The shape is downstream.

A noise attack can target collection, triage, analysis, command or public trust. At collection, false reports increase the input rate. At triage, ambiguous reports force expensive checking. At analysis, inconsistent data consumes specialist time. At command, repeated alerts alter response thresholds. At the public layer, contradictory official explanations reduce trust in future warnings.

The same system can fail from too much skepticism or too little.

Set the threshold high and a real intrusion disappears as clutter. Set it low and the organization spends itself on balloons. Automate the filter and systematic sensor artifacts may become systematic classifications. Rely on witnesses and cultural expectation enters the data. Keep everything classified and outsiders cannot correct errors. Publish everything and adversaries learn what the system notices.

There is no threshold at which uncertainty disappears.

There are only better and worse tradeoffs.

The UFO archive is useful because the tradeoffs became visible early. Anomaly reporting was open-ended. Public participation was enormous. Official explanations were constrained by secrecy. The category attracted hoaxes, sincere mistakes, high-quality observations and political pressure. The feedback loop lasted long enough to create its own institutions.

In that sense, the “UFO problem” was a prototype for modern information security.

A contemporary defender faces synthetic media, cheap drones, spoofed telemetry, mass reporting, automated bots, open-source intelligence communities and sensors whose outputs can be detached from their calibration context and circulated globally. An attacker can create false evidence more cheaply than an institution can investigate it. The asymmetry is brutal. One plausible clip can take minutes to generate and days to resolve.

The Robertson Panel’s answer was partly to reduce the public appetite for the reports.

That approach scales badly in a society where every person carries a camera and every platform rewards novelty.

The modern answer has to be architectural. Preserve original data. Authenticate provenance. Correlate independent sensors. Publish enough methodology that outsiders can understand why a case is unresolved. Separate “unresolved because data are poor” from “unresolved despite strong data.” Track false-alarm rates. Track the cases that reporting culture suppresses. Keep classified explanations linked to public case IDs so declassification can repair the historical record later.

Most of this sounds like database design because, in part, it is.

Mystery thrives in broken joins.

A witness report in one archive, a radar log in another, a classified flight schedule in a third and a public denial in a fourth can generate fifty years of mythology simply because no later system reconnects them. The absence of integration becomes evidence of hidden meaning.

An adversary does not have to cause that dysfunction to benefit from it.

The cheapest noise attack may be the one the target has been running on itself for years.
