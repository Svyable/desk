# A Threat Without a Craft

The most consequential sentence in the Robertson report is also the easiest to misread.

The panel said the UFO evidence did not show a direct physical threat to national security. It then described a national-security threat created by the reporting itself.

Those propositions are not contradictory. A burglar alarm can malfunction without a burglar being present, and a building can still become less secure if the malfunction exhausts the guards. An email system can receive no real attack and still be taken offline by spam. An air-defense network can process reports of objects that are mostly mundane and still lose capacity if those reports arrive at the wrong scale.

The panel’s language belongs to a time before “denial of service” became a common security metaphor. Its members wrote about clogged channels of communication and the danger that repeated false alarms could lead authorities to discount a real signal. The basic logic is the same.

What makes the case historically interesting is that the system was partly human.

Radar did not decide to scramble fighters. People did. Telephone operators routed calls. Intelligence analysts sorted reports. Officers decided what deserved escalation. Newspapers changed public attention. Citizens generated new inputs. The reporting architecture included everyone who noticed, transmitted, interpreted and reacted.

This makes capacity difficult to measure. A radar has bandwidth. A phone trunk has circuits. An office has staff hours. Public attention has no single gauge. Yet all of them can saturate.

The panel worried that an adversary could exploit this. That idea needs disciplined handling because it is exactly where speculation can masquerade as history. The report establishes that officials considered hostile exploitation plausible. It does not establish a Soviet program to create UFO sightings. No public tasking order, agent network, planted-report ledger or operational after-action report is presented in the record used here.

The hypothesis remains useful because it describes a class of attack that does not require ownership of the original phenomenon.

Suppose a country already contains a widely believed anomaly. Reports arise naturally from ambiguous stimuli, sincere mistakes, rare events and a small number of deliberate fabrications. An adversary seeking to create confusion has several cheap options. It can add more reports. It can seed contradictory explanations. It can time decoys to known alert periods. It can impersonate trusted sources. It can amplify the most sensational cases. Or it can do nothing and simply study how the defender responds.

The defender faces the same dilemma in each case: tightening the filter risks missing the real intrusion, while loosening it increases noise.

This is not unique to UFOs. Air defense has always had to discriminate birds, weather, friendly traffic, equipment faults and actual hostile aircraft. Electronic warfare depends on creating deceptive signals. Radar countermeasures do not persuade the radar philosophically; they manipulate what the system can distinguish. The peculiar contribution of the UFO case is that popular culture can become part of the sensor environment.

A citizen who calls a base after seeing an odd light is not an adversary. But the call enters the same finite organization that handles adversarial possibilities.

That is why the panel’s security argument deserves more than ridicule. It also deserves more skepticism than it gave itself.

Once officials define public attention as part of the threat, almost any effort to reduce attention can be recast as protective. Bad publicity becomes operational risk. Persistent civilian investigators become potential channels of disruption. Skepticism becomes security policy rather than merely an evidentiary stance. That is a powerful institutional incentive, and power changes epistemology.

If the objective is to keep the warning network clean, unresolved cases become irritating regardless of their scientific value. The better the case, the more public attention it may draw; the more attention it draws, the more reports arrive; the more reports arrive, the stronger the incentive to close the case. A bureaucracy can therefore become most motivated to dismiss the cases that most resist dismissal.

There is no need to accuse anyone of bad faith. Workload is enough.

This is one reason the Robertson Panel cannot be reduced to either heroes of skepticism or villains of suppression. It identified a genuine systems problem, then proposed a solution with epistemic side effects. The recommendation was not simply “investigate better.” It was also “reduce fascination.”

That move turned a security argument into a cultural intervention.

The panel had found a threat without a craft. The harder question was what a government was allowed to do about it.
