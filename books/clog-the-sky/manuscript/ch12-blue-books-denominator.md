# Blue Book’s Denominator

The spectacular cases have names. The denominator has microfilm.

That difference has distorted nearly every public argument about Project BLUE BOOK.

The National Archives describes a collection spread across administrative files, case files, Office of Special Investigations material, photographs and ninety-four rolls of microfilm. Most of it is not culturally famous because most reports did not remain mysterious. The archive is the record of an institution processing volume.

Volume changes interpretation.

If one reads only the strongest unresolved cases, BLUE BOOK can look like a parade of impossible objects. If one reads only the Air Force’s final conclusions, the same program can look like a tedious exercise in explaining planets and balloons. Both views are selected.

The denominator includes people who reported sincerely and were wrong, people who reported accurately but incompletely, hoaxers, radar operators seeing propagation effects, observers of aircraft they did not recognize, witnesses to astronomical events, and a remainder that resisted the available explanations. The investigative question was never whether strange reports existed. It was whether the distribution of reports required a new cause.

That is a statistical question before it is a philosophical one.

Project BLUE BOOK Special Report No. 14, produced with Battelle involvement in the 1950s, has often been invoked in arguments over whether higher-quality cases were more likely to remain unidentified. The claim is potentially important. If improved data quality makes ordinary explanations more successful, the extraordinary hypothesis weakens. If high-quality cases remain unidentified at unusually high rates, the residue deserves closer attention.

But the numbers are easy to misuse because the categories, quality ratings and original sample design matter. This draft does not lean on remembered percentages. The next research pass needs the authoritative report in hand, its definitions reconstructed, and the analysis reproduced rather than quoted from decades of secondary UFO literature.

That methodological restraint is not a decorative caveat. It is the book’s point.

A reporting system creates its own statistics. Change the questionnaire and the case mix changes. Change the threshold for “insufficient data” and the unidentified count changes. Improve access to flight records and cases migrate categories. Declassify a reconnaissance program and a class of historical unknowns becomes identifiable. None of this requires manipulation. Classification is sensitive to the information available to the classifier.

The denominator also reveals the operational cost of collection. Every solved case had to be solved by somebody. A light eventually identified as a planet still consumed time before becoming a planet in the file. The more successful the investigative machinery, the less interesting its work looks in retrospect.

This creates a perverse historical incentive. The office is remembered for the cases it could not finish.

The Robertson Panel wanted to reduce the input load partly because the investigative burden was not free. BLUE BOOK’s later history shows why that concern persisted. An organization given responsibility for a culturally explosive anomaly will attract reports beyond its staffing, receive pressure from senior officials and the press, and be judged simultaneously for taking the subject too seriously and not seriously enough.

The obvious administrative solution is triage.

Triage is also where bias enters.

A low-quality report can be closed quickly. A high-quality report may demand scarce specialist attention. If the organization has already concluded that the category is unlikely to yield a national-security threat, the rational bureaucratic allocation is to spend less on each difficult case. The remaining unknowns can therefore reflect low expected value rather than maximal mystery.

That is not an accusation. It is how finite organizations behave.

The public tends to interpret unresolved official cases as the cases government could not explain despite bringing all its power to bear. The archive often tells a less cinematic story. Government power is compartmented, uneven, busy and sometimes uninterested. One office may not know what another is flying. A local investigator may not receive the radar tape in time. A witness may answer the questionnaire weeks later. Weather records may be missing. The extraordinary state is still made of ordinary process.

BLUE BOOK’s denominator is therefore not background material. It is the control group for the mythology.

Without it, every unresolved case floats free of the thousands that show how easily the sky can become strange without becoming alien.
