# Clog the Sky

*The Robertson Panel, UFO Panic, and the Security Value of Noise*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a sentence buried in a January 1953 intelligence report that deserves more attention than most of the flying saucers in the file.

The scientists who wrote it had spent four days reviewing the best UFO material the U.S. government could put in front of them. They did not announce visitors. They did not announce a Soviet breakthrough. They did not announce a new branch of physics. Their conclusion on the objects themselves was aggressively unromantic: the evidence presented did not show a direct physical threat to national security.

Then they changed the subject.

The reporting could become the threat.

Too many irrelevant reports could clog communications. Repeated false alarms could train people to ignore the real alarm. Public excitement could produce behavior harmful to authority. An enemy might take advantage of the whole thing. The panel was describing, in the idiom of early Cold War air defense, a problem we would now recognize instantly: a security system can be attacked by exhausting its attention.

That does not mean the Soviet Union created the flying-saucer wave. There is no public evidence in the record used for this book that it did. It does not mean the sightings were all fake. Some were mundane, some were badly documented, some were classified American aircraft, some were radar effects, some were hoaxes, some were sincere observations that remained unresolved. “UFO” was never one thing, which is part of the trouble.

The interesting object in this book is therefore not a silver disc. It is an inbox.

What happens when a state builds an official channel for reports of things people cannot identify, then receives more reports than the category can coherently hold? What happens when the same state is flying aircraft it cannot admit exist? What happens when journalists discover that ambiguity is printable, citizens discover that official uncertainty is suspicious, and intelligence officers discover that even nonsense can have strategic consequences if enough people have to process it?

The UFO debate usually asks the ontological question first: what were they?

This book asks the operational question that arrived in Washington before anyone had settled the ontology: what does a defense system do when the sky becomes noisy?
