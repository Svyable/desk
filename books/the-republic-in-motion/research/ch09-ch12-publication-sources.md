## Chapter 9 — USS Monitor

### Naval History and Heritage Command — *Monitor I (Ironclad Monitor)*

https://www.history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/m/monitor-i.html

Use for:

- prime contract awarded to John Ericsson on October 4, 1861;
- distributed construction: Continental Iron Works hull, Delamater machinery, Novelty Iron Works turret;
- launch January 30, 1862;
- commissioning February 25, 1862;
- design and armament details;
- operational chronology.

Publication caution: the vehicle is a networked industrial product, not a lone-inventor story.

### U.S. Navy / DOD historical shipwreck-management documentation

https://www.history.navy.mil/content/dam/nhhc/research/underwater-archaeology/PDF/UA_USSTecumsehMgt.pdf

Use for:

- the October 4, 1861 contract's one-hundred-day readiness guarantee;
- strategic urgency created by Confederate conversion of the former USS *Merrimack* into CSS *Virginia*;
- federal concern that the ironclad could disrupt the Hampton Roads blockade.

Publication caution: the hundred-day language is a contract/schedule fact, not proof that all construction milestones were literally completed within exactly one hundred calendar days.

### National Park Service — *Battle of the Ironclads*

https://home.nps.gov/articles/battle-of-the-ironclads.htm

Use for:

- March 8 destruction inflicted by CSS *Virginia* on wooden Union warships;
- March 9, 1862 engagement between *Monitor* and *Virginia*;
- approximately four-hour battle;
- low profile and twenty-foot turret;
- armor deflecting shot and signaling a new era of steam-powered iron ships;
- the battle ending without decisive destruction of either ironclad.

Publication caution: “wooden battle fleets became obsolete in a weekend” is rhetorical synthesis. The chapter explicitly qualifies that wooden ships remained in service and that the change was in strategic assumptions, not instant physical disappearance.

## Chapter 10 — Pullman sleeping car

### National Park Service — *A Brief Overview of the Pullman Story*

https://www.nps.gov/pull/learn/historyculture/a-brief-overview-of-the-pullman-story.htm

Use for:

- *Pioneer* sleeping car built in 1864;
- national attention from inclusion in Abraham Lincoln's funeral train cortege;
- Pullman Palace Car Company founded in 1867;
- sleeping, hotel, parlor, and dining-car product range;
- cars being too expensive for many railroads to purchase outright;
- Pullman's lease-and-service model, including employees supplied with the cars.

Publication caution: do not imply the *Pioneer* invented sleeping accommodation on railroads. The chapter's claim is about service standardization and business model, not sole invention.

### National Park Service — George M. Pullman

https://www.nps.gov/people/george-m-pullman.htm

Use for:

- premium-price positioning of sleeping cars;
- 1867 “hotel on wheels” development;
- employment of African American freedmen as Pullman porters;
- company chronology.

Publication caution: later company-town and labor-conflict history belongs outside the 1866–1875 window except where explicitly labeled as persistence.

### National Park Service — *Pullman Porters*

https://www.nps.gov/pull/learn/historyculture/pullman-porters.htm

Use for later consequence and labor context:

- racial segmentation of railroad occupations;
- African Americans concentrated in service positions;
- Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters founded in 1925;
- A. Philip Randolph's leadership;
- porters' later economic and civic significance.

Publication caution: the union story is not evidence for conditions already visible in exactly the same form in 1867. It is used only to show the long institutional consequence of the labor category created by the service model.

## Chapter 11 — bicycle / high-wheel personal mobility

### Smithsonian National Museum of American History — 1881 Columbia Bicycle

https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_843021

Use for:

- Standard Columbia made around 1881;
- approximately $95 retail price for the cited example;
- 54-inch front wheel and high-wheel geometry;
- Albert A. Pope and domestic Columbia production;
- production in the Weed Sewing Machine Company's Hartford factory by 1879.

Publication caution: the exact Smithsonian wording varies across pages between Pope beginning manufacture in 1878 and production in the Hartford factory in 1879. The manuscript therefore uses the safer phrasing “in 1878 and 1879” for the transition and “by 1879” for factory production.

### Smithsonian Institution — *Rise of the Ordinary*

https://www.si.edu/spotlight/si-bikes/si-bikes-ordinary

Use for:

- high-wheel design lineage;
- American importation of English machines;
- Pope as first American bicycle manufacturer;
- Columbia production beginning around 1878;
- 1879 organized bicycle tour featuring Charles E. Pratt and Albert Pope;
- danger of headers caused by high-wheel geometry.

### Smithsonian National Museum of American History — *The Safety Bicycle and Beyond*

https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object-groups/si-bikes/si-bikes-safety

Use for:

- League of American Wheelmen founded May 30, 1880 in Newport, Rhode Island;
- bicycle as inexpensive individual transport in the later safety-bicycle era;
- estimated production growth from about 200,000 bicycles in 1889 to 1,000,000 in 1899;
- bicycle-industry transfer of ball bearings, steel tubing, wire wheels, gearing, and other practices into early automobile development;
- many early automobile and aviation figures having bicycle-manufacturing backgrounds.

Publication caution: the broad democratic bicycle boom belongs primarily to the 1890s. Chapter 11 must remain a “system seed” chapter rather than claiming 1890s scale in 1880.

### Smithsonian National Museum of American History — Columbia High-Wheel Bicycle

https://www.si.edu/object/columbia-high-wheel-bicycle-1886%3Anmah_1281900

Use for:

- high-wheel bicycles as an early common form of personal mechanized transportation;
- Pope's Columbia market influence in the 1880s;
- connection between cycling, road advocacy, and the concept of personal mobility independent of trains.

## Chapter 12 — electric streetcar

### Smithsonian National Museum of American History — *A Streetcar City*

https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/america-on-the-move/online/streetcar-city

Use for:

- Washington's conversion from horse/cable cars to electric streetcars beginning in 1888;
- streetcars enabling people to live farther from commercial-center jobs;
- trolley lines helping create the modern commuter and streetcar suburb;
- real-estate developers building lines to promote new neighborhoods;
- trolley access enlarging the customer geography of central markets;
- racial customs in public transit and the later Jim Crow context;
- labor context around long streetcar working days near 1900.

Publication caution: 1900 labor statistics and later segregation developments are explicitly labeled as later evidence of the operating/social structure created during the chapter's period.

### Library of Congress / Historic American Engineering Record — Fifth Street Viaduct, Richmond

https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/pnp/habshaer/va/va1600/va1668/data/va1668data.pdf

Use for:

- Richmond's 1888 electric streetcar network as an early extensive U.S. electric transit system operating beyond a few demonstration blocks;
- direct role of streetcar travel in Northside urban development;
- Highland Park Company development in 1890;
- new bridges/viaducts built to carry streetcars into developing suburbs.

Publication caution: avoid unqualified “first electric streetcar” language. The defensible claim is that Richmond was a decisive early city-scale proof and is described in the cited HAER documentation as the first U.S. electric transit system operating over more than a few city blocks.

### Library of Congress / HAER — electric traction history

https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/pnp/habshaer/ct/ct0500/ct0568/data/ct0568data.pdf

Use for:

- Sprague's Richmond system as a practical city-scale demonstration;
- rapid electrification of urban transit in the late 1880s and 1890s;
- displacement of animal traction and its urban waste burden.

## Block-level editorial conclusions

Before final publication, Chapters 9–12 should receive an additional scholarly-book pass beyond the government/museum/archive spine above.

Priority additions:

- Civil War naval-industrial scholarship comparing *Monitor* with broader Union railroad logistics;
- Pullman scholarship centered on service capitalism, porter labor, and passenger-class formation;
- bicycle scholarship on gender, class, road politics, and the transition from high-wheel to safety design;
- urban-history scholarship on streetcar suburbs, franchise politics, segregation, and land development.

The current evidence is sufficient for a strong sourced draft. It is not yet the final bibliography.
