# Raw Intelligence

On January 23, 1980, somebody sat down to describe a place he could not see.

The target was not a playing card or a photograph in another room. The United States had hostages inside the embassy compound in Tehran. The session report that survives in the declassified STARGATE collection is stamped with the bureaucratic language of a government trying to make an impossible proposition administratively ordinary. The viewer's impressions, it says, were being supplied as “raw intelligence data.” They had not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation or collation. Interpretation belonged to the requester.

That phrase is better than most of the literature written about remote viewing.

Raw intelligence is not truth. It is material entering a system that has not yet decided what the material means. A satellite image can be raw intelligence. So can an intercepted conversation, a frightened walk-in, a radar return, a photograph whose date is uncertain, a source with excellent access and terrible motives. Intelligence agencies do not possess a category called information that is strange enough to be exempt from the ordinary problem of being wrong.

The remote-viewing report is stranger because the collection method itself was the disputed thing. The Army was not merely asking whether the viewer's description matched Tehran. It was entertaining, at least operationally, the possibility that a human being could acquire information without a recognized sensory channel. The session report does not prove that possibility. Its existence proves something narrower and, historically, more interesting: people inside the national-security apparatus were willing to task it.

That distinction has been abused in both directions ever since. Believers point to the files and say: the government studied this for years, therefore there was something there. Skeptics point to the termination of the program and say: the government stopped, therefore there was nothing there. Neither inference is licensed by the administrative facts. Governments investigate false alarms. Governments also abandon real effects that cannot be made useful. Duration is not efficacy. Cancellation is not a controlled experiment.

The harder evidence sits in between.

CIA's own later public account says the research began in the 1970s, moved through Defense Department stewardship, and eventually returned to CIA for an independent evaluation in the mid-1990s. The agency summarizes the 1995 conclusion in language almost designed to irritate everybody: enough accurate experiences existed to defy randomness, but the phenomenon was too unreliable, inconsistent and sporadic to be useful for intelligence. CIA chose not to restore the program.

Suppose that sentence is approximately right. It creates a category that popular argument handles badly: statistically interesting failure.

A roulette wheel that lands on red 50.8 percent of the time under carefully controlled conditions may be a scientific scandal if the deviation survives enough trials and every mechanical explanation. It may still be a terrible way to choose which building contains a hostage. The first problem asks whether chance is an adequate model of the observations. The second asks whether an uncertain observation improves a decision after base rates, alternatives, timing and cost are included. A tiny reproducible effect could overturn physics and still be useless before lunch.

Remote viewing lived inside that gap.

The declassified operational reports are often fascinating objects because they preserve the ambiguity before later narrators tidy it. A viewer produces shapes, textures, temperatures, impressions of structures or people. Some details can look specific. Others are broad enough to fit many targets. The requester knows things the viewer may not. Analysts may know still more. Feedback arrives. Memory edits. The impressive correspondence survives conversation better than the wrong corridor, the absent tower, the person placed in the wrong building. This is not an accusation of fraud. It is what human judgment does to noisy material unless the scoring system prevents it.

The phrase “raw intelligence” quietly admits the problem. A report can enter the intelligence stream before its reliability is known. Conventional collection has exactly the same problem, which is why agencies develop source ratings, corroboration practices, confidence language and tradecraft. A human source can be right six times and lie on the seventh. An imagery analyst can correctly identify an airframe and misread its readiness. A radar can measure something real while geometry makes the apparent motion absurd. The unusual claim in remote viewing was not that intelligence could be noisy. Intelligence is born noisy. The unusual claim was that some portion of the noise contained information for which nobody could name the channel.

There are at least three ways for that appearance to arise.

One is that an anomalous information channel exists. “Psi” is a label, not an explanation, and it should not be allowed to smuggle a mechanism into the premise. Telepathy, clairvoyance and precognition make different claims about what information crosses what boundary. A statistical departure from chance does not tell us which story, if any, is true.

Another possibility is that ordinary information leaks through experimental systems in ways investigators fail to notice. Cueing can be microscopic. Target pools can carry structure. Judges can make choices with knowledge they should not have. Randomization can be less random than assumed. Experimenters can stop, exclude, transform or analyze data in ways that become consequential only after many degrees of freedom accumulate. Publication can select the surprising tail of a null distribution and leave the boring center in file drawers.

The third possibility is less dramatic and therefore easy to neglect: several small biases can coexist, vary by laboratory and era, and produce a literature whose aggregate behavior does not reduce to one fatal flaw. A field can contain sincere investigators, improved protocols, residual weaknesses, occasional misconduct, genuine statistical anomalies and failed replications at the same time. “Is psi real?” then becomes a bad first question because it asks a heterogeneous research history to return a Boolean.

The intelligence problem is less forgiving. A hostage is either helped or not.

That is why the Tehran files matter beyond their paranormal furniture. They force the argument out of the laboratory. In one surviving session, the viewer was tasked to locate and describe the surroundings of a hostage and was then moved forward in time to a future date. The report preserved impressions as raw data and left interpretation downstream. Imagine removing every paranormal word from that procedure. What remains is a forecasting system producing low-bandwidth, difficult-to-score descriptions for an analyst who desperately wants information. The analyst's desire is not incidental. It changes the cost of a false positive, the temptation to match after the fact, and the value of even a weak independent clue.

If a viewer says “large pale building, water nearby, fence, vehicles,” what is the likelihood of that description if the target is the embassy compound? More importantly, what is the likelihood of the same description across plausible alternative targets? Without the second number, specificity is theatrical. A description can feel uncanny and carry little information.

This is where intelligence analysis and experimental psychology unexpectedly meet. Both need denominators.

The later laboratory literature tried to make the judgment problem cleaner. Ganzfeld experiments, in one common form, place a receiver in a homogenized sensory environment while a target is selected and, depending on protocol, a sender attends to it elsewhere. The receiver later judges candidate targets. The architecture attempts to turn an impression into a forced comparison with a known chance baseline. That is an enormous epistemic improvement over reading a transcript and circling phrases that feel right. It also creates new arguments about protocol quality, independence, selection and meta-analysis.

Those arguments have not vanished. A 2024 registered-report meta-analysis by Patrizio Tressoldi and Lance Storm examined more than forty years of ganzfeld research and reported an overall effect size around 0.08, with uncertainty intervals above zero in their models. Two reviewers approved the final version and one did not. The number is small. It is also, if the analysis and underlying literature survive scrutiny, precisely the kind of number this book cares about.

An effect of 0.08 is not a man reading a missile-site blueprint from California. It is not proof of telepathy. It does not tell us what physical quantity moved, whether anything moved, or whether the aggregate estimate is clean of bias. It says that a body of experiments, processed through a particular statistical model, contains a small departure the authors argue is anomalous. The scientific task begins there rather than ends there.

There is a useful cruelty in small effects. Large effects can sometimes bully their way through mediocre methods. If aspirin made a severed finger grow back overnight, the first clinical trial could be a mess and the phenomenon would still demand attention. An effect barely above noise receives no such charity. Every mundane imperfection is now of comparable scale. A little leakage matters. A little publication bias matters. A slightly flexible exclusion rule matters. So does a genuine weak signal. When signal and artifact occupy the same numerical neighborhood, epistemology becomes part of the instrument.

This is one reason the government history remains relevant even after the program's end. STARGATE was not simply a long laboratory experiment. It mixed research and operations, different viewers, different tasking, different scoring conditions, shifting sponsors and a Cold War incentive structure in which Soviet interest could justify American attention before American scientists had established a phenomenon. Intelligence organizations routinely study adversary claims they do not believe. They also study low-probability capabilities because the cost of unilateral surprise can be large. “The Soviets are spending money on it” is a reason to investigate. It is not evidence that the Soviets succeeded.

The mirror effect matters. Once two states believe the other may be investigating an exotic capability, each side's investigation becomes evidence in the other's threat file. Expenditure can bootstrap expenditure without efficacy ever entering the loop. Nuclear weapons do not fit this pattern because explosions settle the argument. Psi research is almost maximally vulnerable to it because alleged success is intermittent, mechanisms are obscure, operational results can be classified, and failures are easy to attribute to the viewer, the target, the protocol or the conditions.

Secrecy makes the ecology worse. A spectacular anecdote can circulate without the denominator of failed sessions. An intelligence consumer may remember the one description that helped and forget twenty that did not. A program manager can know that laboratory statistics look interesting without being able to show that the next operational product will be timely or discriminating. Outsiders, seeing classification, can interpret missing evidence as hidden success. Insiders, seeing only their compartment, can do a milder version of the same thing.

None of this proves the null hypothesis.

That sentence needs to remain uncomfortable. Skepticism earns authority by explaining evidence, not by making an unpopular subject socially expensive. If well-controlled experiments repeatedly produce a small effect, “people are gullible” is not an analysis. The controls, randomization, scoring, exclusions, target construction and data pipeline have to be inspected. If the effect disappears under stronger methods, that disappearance is evidence. If it does not, the burden shifts.

The same discipline applies to believers. A p-value cannot be promoted into a mechanism. A meta-analytic effect cannot be promoted into an operational capability. A declassified tasking document cannot be promoted into proof that the tasking worked. The fact that an intelligence agency once stamped SECRET on a remote-viewing session tells us what the agency was protecting at the time, not what nature permits.

The most interesting historical judgment may therefore be the least cinematic one: something can look non-random and still fail.

Science is accustomed to asking whether an effect exists. Engineering asks whether it can be controlled. Intelligence asks whether it changes a decision. Those questions form a sequence, and remote viewing has spent fifty years being dragged backward and forward across it.

The rest of this book will keep the sequence intact.
