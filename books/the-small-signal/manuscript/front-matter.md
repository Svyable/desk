# The Small Signal

*What Happens When an Anomaly Is Statistically Real and Practically Useless*

Sven Hardy Benson

This book begins with an irritating possibility: the argument over psychic phenomena may have been asking one question while intelligence agencies had to answer another.

A laboratory can care about whether a distribution departs from chance. An intelligence officer has to care whether the next report changes a decision before somebody gets hurt. Those standards overlap, but they are not the same standard. A phenomenon can be statistically detectable and operationally worthless. It can also be operationally interesting before anybody understands its mechanism. Confusing those possibilities has damaged both sides of the argument.

The historical spine is public. The CIA and Defense Department funded remote-viewing research and operations over many years. Declassified STARGATE records contain session reports, protocols, tasking and later evaluation. In 1995 the American Institutes for Research evaluated the program for CIA. Jessica Utts argued that laboratory evidence supported an anomalous effect; Ray Hyman disputed what could responsibly be inferred and emphasized methodological and replication problems. CIA did not restart the program. None of that establishes telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition or any other paranormal mechanism.

The modern scientific record is equally awkward. Meta-analyses continue to report small departures from chance in ganzfeld and anticipatory-physiology literatures. Critics dispute publication bias, analytical flexibility, sensory leakage, heterogeneity, replication quality and interpretation. A 2024 registered-report meta-analysis of more than forty years of ganzfeld studies reported a small positive effect. A meta-analysis is not a new force of nature. It is an invitation to inspect exactly what generated the number.

I will use four evidence classes throughout.

**Documented.** Declassified records, official histories, contemporaneous program documents, public evaluations, preregistered or peer-reviewed papers, and claims those sources can directly establish.

**Credible but incomplete.** Attributed participant testimony, secondary reporting with identifiable sourcing, statistical findings whose interpretation remains contested, and historical reconstructions missing decisive primary material.

**Disputed or unconfirmed.** Claims of specific psychic mechanisms, spectacular operational successes without sufficient independent documentation, foreign capabilities inferred from intelligence reporting rather than demonstrated publicly, and anecdotes that improve in the retelling.

**Speculative synthesis.** The book's own proposition: that the most interesting residue may be a small-signal problem. If anomalous cognition exists only as a weak, unstable effect, the correct comparison is not magic versus fraud. It is signal detection under ugly conditions: base rates, calibration, selection, feedback, false positives, analyst priors and decision cost.

That proposition is allowed to fail. If prospective, preregistered, independently replicated experiments with strong leakage controls converge on chance, the small-signal interpretation loses its empirical reason to exist. If robust effects emerge that identify targets reliably enough to beat conventional collection under blinded operational scoring, the book's emphasis on practical uselessness becomes obsolete. If the historical record shows that apparently successful intelligence cases collapse when misses, cueing and retrospective matching are restored, that matters more than the romance of the program having existed.

The point is not to rescue psi from embarrassment. It is to stop embarrassment from doing statistical work.
