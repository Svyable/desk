# Chronology

- **1951** — United States and Denmark sign the Defense of Greenland Agreement.
- **1959** — U.S. Army Corps of Engineers begins construction of Camp Century inland from Thule Air Base.
- **1960–1961** — PM-2A portable nuclear power plant is transported, assembled and brought into operation at Camp Century.
- **1961–1962** — Reactor startup and shielding/thermal-hydraulic testing documented; subsurface railway and camp engineering continue.
- **1962** — Army advances Project Iceworm as a proposal for a large distributed ballistic-missile network beneath the Greenland Ice Sheet.
- **1963** — Iceworm is rejected; no missile network is deployed.
- **1964** — Year-round Camp Century operations cease; later work becomes seasonal.
- **1966** — Deep drilling at Camp Century reaches the bed, producing a complete Greenland ice core and basal material.
- **1967** — Camp Century is abandoned with minimal removal of the physical installation; reactor core/pressure-vessel components do not remain as an intact operating plant.
- **1968** — A U.S. B-52 carrying nuclear weapons crashes near Thule Air Base. This is a separate event but becomes central to later Greenland nuclear-policy history.
- **1969** — CRREL personnel revisit the abandoned Camp Century site and document its condition.
- **1970** — Austin Kovacs's pictorial report on the 1969 visit is published.
- **1990s** — Danish archival investigations and declassification bring Project Iceworm's strategic purpose much more clearly into public view.
- **2001** — Erik D. Weiss publishes a major archival reconstruction of the Army's Iceworm bid.
- **2011 / 2014** — NASA Operation IceBridge radar profiles detect subsurface disturbances consistent with Camp Century remains.
- **2016** — Colgan et al. publish a high-emissions scenario analysis warning that future net ablation and meltwater could eventually remobilize abandoned material.
- **2017** — Denmark, in agreement with Greenland, initiates the Camp Century Climate Monitoring Programme; GEUS installs instruments, cores firn, and maps debris with radar.
- **2018–2019** — Monitoring and radar results are published; more than 80 km of radar survey data help constrain debris location and depth.
- **2021** — GEUS reports updated modeling indicating that Camp Century remains buried and meltwater is not expected to reach debris through 2100 under available scenarios. Christ et al. publish evidence from basal sediment that the site was once ice-free and vegetated during the Quaternary.
- **April 2024** — NASA UAVSAR observes Camp Century with a side-looking radar geometry that reveals individual structural elements more distinctly than prior profiles.
- **25 November 2024** — NASA Earth Observatory publishes the new view and explicitly notes earlier detections.
- **2025–2026** — Camp Century remains a monitored, buried legacy site rather than an exposed remediation site; modern reporting renews interest in its military and climate history.

## Chronology boundary

Dates above mark documented program or research milestones. They do not imply that a classified event occurred merely because it would fit between known dates. Where exact closure, decommissioning or policy decisions require deeper primary-file reconstruction, the manuscript uses approximate institutional sequences rather than invented precision.
