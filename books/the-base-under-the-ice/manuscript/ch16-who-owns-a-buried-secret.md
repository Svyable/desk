# Who Owns a Buried Secret

A secret has an owner while it is classified. Waste is less cooperative.

The United States built Camp Century. Denmark exercised sovereignty over Greenland within the constitutional order of the period. Greenland later acquired home rule and then self-government, changing who could legitimately speak for the people and territory affected by the site's afterlife. Climate risk emerged after all of those arrangements had shifted.

That sequence creates a question with no satisfying one-word answer: who owns the buried camp?

Ownership here is not title. Nobody wants the steel arches. The relevant ownership is responsibility: who monitors, who pays, who decides whether intervention is necessary, who bears risk, who has standing to demand information, and whose historical consent counts.

The 2016 paper made this explicit by describing potential waste remobilization as a new pathway for political dispute. “New” matters. The 1951 defense agreement could authorize military activity without answering a climate-remediation question that did not exist in the same form. The abandonment practices of the 1960s could satisfy the institutions involved without binding future Greenlandic governments to accept every consequence.

In 2017 Denmark established the Camp Century Climate Monitoring Programme in agreement with Greenland. That governance structure is more interesting than a simple assignment of blame because it acknowledges that present stewardship has to be built by present actors.

The United States remains part of the historical chain, even when monitoring is conducted by Danish and Greenlandic institutions. If substantial remediation ever became necessary, arguments over American responsibility would be unavoidable. But those arguments should follow evidence about actual pathways and costs rather than using history as a substitute for risk assessment.

This is where environmental justice and technical analysis sometimes talk past each other. A community can reasonably object to having inherited a foreign military waste site even if the measured near-term exposure risk is low. Low risk does not erase lack of consent. Conversely, historical injustice does not determine which remediation technique is safest.

Both statements can remain true without canceling each other.

Greenland's broader Cold War history makes this particularly sensitive. The expansion of Thule Air Base contributed to the forced relocation of the Inughuit community from Uummannaq to Qaanaaq in the 1950s. That history is separate from Camp Century's waste inventory, but it matters to the political context in which assurances from Copenhagen or Washington are heard. Technical trust is not created in a vacuum.

A long-lived site accumulates these layers. First engineering. Then secrecy. Then abandonment. Then memory. Then law. Then new science.

Institutions prefer liabilities that stay inside one layer. A contaminated factory can be handled through property and environmental law. A declassified document can be handled through archives. Camp Century is awkward because its physical and documentary remains cross jurisdictions and generations.

This is one reason transparency around the monitoring data matters beyond scientific reproducibility. Public reporting changes the politics of inheritance. Greenlandic authorities and citizens do not have to accept an American or Danish assurance that the site is safe on faith; the measurements, methods and projections can be examined.

That is a modest improvement over the original arrangement.

Project Iceworm depended on uncertainty being concentrated inside military channels. The site's afterlife is better served by distributing uncertainty openly.

The buried secret no longer belongs to the people who made it.
