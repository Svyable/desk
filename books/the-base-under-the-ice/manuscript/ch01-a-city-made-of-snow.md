# A City Made of Snow

The first useful fact about Camp Century is that it looked temporary even when its builders described it as a prototype for permanence. Nothing about a trench cut into firn resembles a granite bunker. The walls are white. The ceiling is white. The material holding up the roof is also the material slowly trying to close the room.

In 1959 the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers began building the installation roughly two hundred kilometers inland from Thule Air Base in northwestern Greenland. The method was almost agricultural in its bluntness. Machines cut long trenches into compacted snow. Steel arches went in. Snow went back over the top. Inside those buried tubes came barracks, a kitchen, a hospital, communications, workshops, laboratories, storage and eventually a portable nuclear power plant. The finished network ran for kilometers. At peak strength it could support something like two hundred people beneath the surface of the ice sheet.

The Army called it Camp Century, a name with the confidence of a world's fair. Photographs and films presented an engineering frontier: men in parkas, tracked vehicles, clean tunnel geometry, a reactor shipped into a place where fuel normally arrived by punishing over-snow convoys. The public story was not entirely false. The camp did support real scientific research. Cold-regions engineering mattered enormously to the military. The deep drilling program would produce one of the foundational Greenland ice cores. Scientists working there were not props.

But the Army also had a harder question in mind. Could the ice sheet itself become military terrain?

That is different from asking whether soldiers can survive there. Survival had already been demonstrated repeatedly across polar expeditions, air bases and weather stations. The Camp Century problem was whether a dynamic ice mass could host infrastructure with strategic value: tunnels, railways, power, communications and eventually weapons. A glacier would have to become something like land without ceasing to be ice.

The distinction matters because later retellings often describe Project Iceworm as if somebody in Washington simply decided to hide missiles under a glacier and then discovered that glaciers move. The Army was not ignorant of deformation. Camp Century was built in part to measure it. Engineers installed instrumentation, watched closures, altered roof shapes and recorded how loads migrated through the surrounding firn. The point of a prototype is to encounter the insult delivered by reality before repeating the mistake at scale.

The insults arrived quickly.

Snow accumulated at the surface while deeper ice flowed laterally. Roof arches bent. Tunnels narrowed. Maintenance crews shaved and excavated to preserve usable space. A trench was not a hole in a static solid. It was a cavity inside a material under continuous stress. The camp could be built. Keeping it built was another problem.

This is where Camp Century begins to escape the usual categories of secret history. If the project had remained nothing more than a classified missile concept, its later fascination would be mostly archival. We would argue over memoranda and maps. But the Army carved its hypothesis into a physical system that kept running after the experimenters went home. The glacier became the longest-lived participant.

A glacier is not fast on human terms. That is part of its danger to institutions. A roof that moves centimeters can be repaired. A tunnel that closes over years looks like a maintenance problem. A waste site that remains buried for decades looks solved. A climate regime that appears stable over the planning horizon can be treated as background. Slow processes are easy to demote from actors to scenery.

Camp Century was full of slow processes.

The Army's own 1965 technical history, *Camp Century: Evolution of Concept and History of Design, Construction, and Performance*, is striking because it is neither a confession nor a fairy tale. It is an engineering document. The authors conclude that subsurface ice-cap camps are feasible and that nuclear power offers major logistical advantages. That conclusion was not absurd. Camp Century had functioned. People had lived and worked there. A reactor had produced power. Trains had run through experimental passages. Deep drilling had advanced.

The same record also contains the seeds of the larger failure. Feasible is a dangerous word when it migrates from component to system. A trench can be feasible while a continent-scale tunnel network is not. A reactor can work while the maintenance burden around it makes the total concept brittle. A camp can survive while a missile force needs tolerances the camp never had to meet.

The later story is often told backward from Project Iceworm's cancellation. That makes every engineering difficulty look like an omen. It was not. The people building Camp Century did not know which problem would become decisive, which problem would be engineered around, or whether strategic fashion would move faster than the ice. They were doing what military engineering organizations do when they do not know: building the argument at full scale.

The result was a city made of snow that taught the Army more than the Army wanted to learn.
