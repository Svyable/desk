# Six Hundred Missiles

Six hundred is the number that survives because it is large enough to make Project Iceworm instantly visible in the mind. Six hundred missiles under Greenland. Six hundred moving points on a map. Six hundred chances for an attacker to guess wrong.

A number can clarify a plan and conceal it at the same time.

The appeal of a dispersed missile network was statistical before it was architectural. If a Soviet planner could not know which launch point held which missile at a given moment, then destroying the force required attacking far more locations than the number of actual weapons. Mobility and concealment inflate the adversary's target list. The defender buys survivability with uncertainty.

That logic is familiar from rail-mobile and road-mobile missiles. Iceworm added the ice sheet as concealment. A large buried network could, in principle, separate the location of a missile from the location of a visible silo. The attacker would face an enormous aim-point problem.

But the same geometry works backward on the owner. A force that cannot be located easily by the enemy still has to be located perfectly by itself.

Every missile requires custody. Every movement requires routing. Every launch point requires surveying. Every command path has to be authenticated. Maintenance teams must find equipment that the adversary cannot. Crews must distinguish ordinary deformation from a condition that affects launch readiness. Navigation under the ice cannot rely on the same visual references available on the surface. A distributed network is a bookkeeping machine with nuclear consequences.

The Army's proposed Iceman missile was conceived as an adaptation of available ballistic-missile technology rather than a magical Arctic weapon. That was part of the concept's appeal. The revolutionary element was supposed to be basing, not propulsion. The missile could inherit mature technology while the ice supplied survivability.

This division of novelty is common in ambitious systems. Keep the weapon familiar; make the environment do the strange work. In practice, the environment does not remain a passive platform. Temperature changes lubricants. Snow enters mechanisms. Survey points move. Power lines stretch. Tunnels narrow. Humans get cold and tired. The supposedly non-revolutionary missile inherits every instability of its base.

Iceworm's scale also forced a second question: how much infrastructure can be hidden by calling it dispersed?

A network serving hundreds of missiles would have required extraordinary excavation, power generation, ventilation, transport, maintenance and personnel. The more tunnels the Army built, the more surface logistics had to support their construction. Concealment from Soviet attack did not mean invisibility from satellites, reconnaissance, diplomats, contractors, budgets or Greenlandic residents. A secret installation can hide its purpose more easily than its mass.

This is one of Camp Century's most useful lessons for modern claims about hidden technology. Physical programs generate secondary evidence. The artifact is only the center. Around it appear roads, unusual procurement, specialized labor, safety rules, training, waste, test infrastructure, power demands, accidents, maintenance and institutional turf wars. A mature program's footprint depends on its scale even when its purpose remains secret.

Iceworm never reached the scale at which that footprint would have become unavoidable. Camp Century did, however, reveal the direction. The camp needed over-snow supply, specialized construction equipment, reactor operators, cold-regions engineers, drilling teams and continuous maintenance. Multiply a prototype by the number imagined in the planning documents and secrecy becomes a different problem.

The Army's proposal therefore contained a paradox. Dispersion promised strategic invisibility by producing physical enormity.

The same paradox appears in many Cold War projects. A radar network is hard to surprise but impossible to hide. A hardened command system survives attack by becoming massive. A mobile missile force avoids fixed targets but requires roads, security and communications. The engineering that creates survivability also creates signatures.

There was another complication. By the time Iceworm was being considered, the United States was moving rapidly toward Minuteman silos and Polaris submarines. These alternatives solved the survivability problem differently. Minuteman made solid-fueled missiles comparatively ready and hardened in fixed fields. Polaris moved missiles into the ocean on nuclear submarines, where mobility did not require excavating a continent-scale network through a material that kept squeezing it shut.

Technologies do not compete against the problem they were designed to solve in isolation. They compete against other ways of solving it.

That is why the phrase “it could have worked” is so weak. Many systems can work. The question is whether they work better, sooner, cheaper, more safely or more politically acceptably than the alternatives arriving at the same moment.

Six hundred missiles makes Iceworm sound powerful.

Six hundred maintenance problems makes it sound like a procurement hearing.
