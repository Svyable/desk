# The 2016 Alarm

In August 2016 a six-page research letter turned Camp Century into a climate headline.

William Colgan and colleagues modeled the abandoned base under a high-emissions scenario and concluded that a transition from net annual accumulation to net ablation at the site was plausible within the following decades. If that transition persisted, material buried by snowfall could eventually move toward the surface, and increasing meltwater could interact with waste earlier through percolation. The authors explicitly described physical, chemical, biological and radiological material left at the camp.

The paper did what a good short paper can do. It connected archives, a waste inventory, ice-flow estimates and climate projections tightly enough to reveal a governance problem nobody had budgeted for.

Then the phrase “nuclear waste” took over.

News accounts understandably emphasized the reactor history and the possibility of radioactive material emerging from the ice. Camp Century had everything a headline wants: a secret nuclear missile plan, a buried base, climate change, Greenland and waste. The details were almost offensively cooperative.

But the paper itself was more careful. It did not say the reactor remained under the ice. It distinguished waste classes. It presented more than one climate-model outcome. One forcing combination maintained positive surface mass balance through 2100; another crossed into net ablation. The timing of direct surface exposure depended on the amount of overlying firn and future ablation rates. Meltwater interaction could occur earlier, but that too was a modeled pathway rather than an observed leak.

The important result was not a date. It was the discovery that the legal and political assumption of permanent isolation was climate-dependent.

That alone was enough to create a sovereignty problem. The installation had been American. The territory was Greenlandic within the Danish realm. The disposal decision belonged to a different political era. If climate change mobilized contaminants, responsibility would not be determined by the direction of ice flow.

The paper called this a potential new pathway of political dispute. That phrase aged better than the most dramatic headlines.

Greenland's government responded with understandable concern. Denmark could not simply point to a sixty-year-old military arrangement and call the matter closed. The result was not immediate remediation. It was a monitoring program established by the Danish government in agreement with the Government of Greenland and led by GEUS.

This is worth emphasizing because policy often fails in the space between alarm and certainty. A projection raises a plausible high-consequence risk. Critics demand proof before action. Advocates demand action before uncertainty can be narrowed. Camp Century got something better: measurement.

The 2017 field program installed an automated climate station, drilled firn and ice, and surveyed the buried debris by radar. The monitoring goals were explicit: observe relevant climate variables, update the probability of meltwater reaching the abandoned material, map the debris field, and report the results publicly.

The program converted a Cold War secret into a transparent environmental observing problem.

That does not erase the earlier secrecy. It changes the epistemic structure around the site. Instead of arguing from old Army maps and global climate scenarios alone, governments could acquire site-specific data. Instead of treating the 2016 projection as either prophecy or embarrassment, researchers could test its assumptions.

The answer, when it came, was less alarming.

That is not a failure of the original paper. It is what the paper made possible.
