# The Archive Has a Temperature

Some archives are kept in boxes. Camp Century has at least three, and two of them require refrigeration.

The documentary archive contains plans, memoranda, engineering reports, diplomatic correspondence and later declassification work. It tells us what institutions proposed, measured and argued. The scientific archive contains the Camp Century ice core and basal sediments, preserved because physical samples can outlive the questions that produced them. The third archive is the site itself: crushed structures, frozen wastewater, tanks, debris and disturbed layers moving through the Greenland Ice Sheet.

All three can decay.

Paper disappears through classification, poor cataloging, redaction or ordinary bureaucratic neglect. Samples disappear if labels are lost, freezers fail or curators decide a box has no value. The physical site changes because ice flows, chemicals diffuse, tanks rupture and climate alters melt conditions.

The phrase “the archive has a temperature” is literal at Camp Century. Raise the temperature enough and two of the three archives change state.

This makes the site a useful answer to a persistent fantasy about secrets: that evidence waits patiently for disclosure. It does not. Physical evidence degrades while governments decide whether it may be discussed. Witnesses die. instruments are scrapped. magnetic media fail. contaminants move. ice flows. By the time a classification barrier falls, the material world may have rewritten the scene.

Camp Century was fortunate in one respect. Army engineers documented a great deal. The Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory reports are unusually rich. The core was preserved. Later Danish archival investigations recovered the strategic context of Iceworm. Modern scientists could link old maps to radar observations.

Even here, uncertainties remain. Some waste quantities are reconstructed rather than measured. Political knowledge was uneven. Exact program decisions have to be inferred from surviving records. The condition of buried material cannot be inspected directly. Every archive has blind spots.

The temptation is to fill those blind spots with one of two stories. The conspiratorial story says missing records prove deeper concealment. The officialist story says absent evidence proves nothing important happened. Both treat the archive as complete in opposite directions.

Camp Century offers a better habit. Ask what physical consequences a claim should have produced, then look for them across independent archives.

If Iceworm had become a deployed network of hundreds of missiles, the residue would have been enormous: excavation, personnel, missile handling, reactors or other power systems, command infrastructure, procurement, diplomatic consequences and maintenance. None of that secondary ecology exists at deployment scale because the system was never fielded. We do have the smaller ecology of Camp Century because the camp was real.

If the camp's waste were currently interacting with surface meltwater at meaningful scale, monitoring should begin to detect changes in the relevant firn and hydrological conditions. Current evidence does not show that pathway through 2100 under the modeled scenarios.

If northwestern Greenland had been continuously ice-covered for millions of years, the basal sediment should not contain the preserved evidence of an exposed vegetated landscape that researchers found.

Different archives constrain different stories.

This is the epistemic gift of Camp Century. It is famous for secrecy, but the strongest conclusions come from overlap: Army engineering plus diplomatic records; historical maps plus radar; ice cores plus modern dating; climate models plus field stations. No single source carries the whole book.

Secrets age badly when the world leaves receipts in several materials.
