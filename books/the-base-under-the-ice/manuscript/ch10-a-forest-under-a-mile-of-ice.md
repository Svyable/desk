# A Forest Under a Mile of Ice

The material at the bottom of the Camp Century core looked like the least glamorous part of the archive: frozen sediment, not transparent ice. Its value emerged much later.

In 2021 a team led by Andrew Christ and colleagues reported fossils and biomolecules from the subglacial sediment beneath Camp Century, including moss and plant fragments. Their dating work indicated that northwestern Greenland had been ice-free within roughly the last million years, and likely substantially more recently than the ancient, permanently frozen Greenland imagined in older models. The result was not that a forest had been found intact under the modern ice sheet. The popular image is too lush. The evidence pointed to a vegetated landscape—tundra-like ground exposed when the ice sheet was much smaller or absent at that location.

That distinction matters because “forest under a mile of ice” is irresistible and slightly wrong in exactly the way Camp Century stories tend to become irresistible and slightly wrong.

The scientific claim is already astonishing without help. A site now buried beneath more than a kilometer of ice once supported plants. Greenland's present ice cover is therefore not an eternal feature of the landscape. It has retreated dramatically under climate conditions that can be investigated with geochronology rather than mythology.

The Camp Century sediment added to other evidence that parts of Greenland are more vulnerable over geological time than a simple picture of an immovable ice cap suggests. Later work on basal materials and exposure histories has continued to revise estimates of when and where Greenland was ice-free. The details remain active science; dating methods carry uncertainties, and one site does not tell the history of the entire ice sheet.

Still, the irony is hard to avoid. The Army wanted the ice because it looked permanent enough to hide a strategic system. The deepest material recovered from its own camp eventually became evidence that the ice was not permanent on geological timescales.

This does not mean Army engineers should have known in 1959 that anthropogenic warming would alter the site's future waste risk. That is hindsight theater. Military engineering works on decades, not interglacials. The better question is how institutions choose the boundary of the future they consider relevant.

Iceworm's planners had a weapons-system horizon. Camp Century's waste assumptions had a disposal horizon. The ice-core scientists had a paleoclimate horizon. The physical site had all three at once.

That mismatch appears everywhere in long-lived technology. A reactor operates for decades while some wastes remain hazardous far longer. A dam is financed on one schedule while sediment accumulates on another. A coastal city depreciates buildings over decades while sea level moves across centuries. Strategic planners discount the distant future because adversaries and technology change faster; geology does not care.

The Camp Century core turns this into something you can hold. The top of the core belonged to weather remembered by people. Deeper layers belonged to climates before states, before weapons, before Greenland had its present political relationship with Denmark. At the bottom sat a landscape from a world in which the local ice sheet had retreated enough for vegetation to grow.

The core therefore outlived the strategic problem that made it possible. Polaris submarines became obsolete. Minuteman generations changed. Denmark's and Greenland's political relationship evolved. Thule changed names. The core kept accumulating meanings.

There is an epistemic lesson here that does not require metaphysics. Evidence can become more informative after the people who collected it have lost interest in the original question. New instruments create new observations from old objects. The sample does not change. The questions around it do.

That makes physical archives different from documentary ones. A declassified memo tells us what somebody wrote. A preserved sediment sample can answer a question nobody had language for when it was collected.

Camp Century is usually introduced as the city under the ice.

Its deepest legacy may be the dirt under the city.
