# The Reactor Arrives

The reactor came in pieces.

That detail is more important than the romance of a nuclear plant under the ice. PM-2A was not a reactor in the way a commercial power station is a reactor: a fixed industrial monument built around a site. It belonged to the Army Nuclear Power Program's attempt to make atomic energy modular enough to serve remote military installations. The plant had to be manufactured, transported, assembled, tested and operated in a place where every gallon of conventional fuel represented a logistics problem.

Camp Century made the argument almost perfectly. Diesel could run generators, but diesel had to get there. Supplies moved from Thule over the ice. Distance, weather, vehicle wear and personnel time accumulated around every delivered drum. Nuclear fuel offered an extraordinary ratio between energy and transported mass. In the Arctic, that ratio was not a laboratory abstraction. It was freight.

The Army's 1961 final report on constructing PM-2A and the 1962 startup-test report are useful antidotes to the phrase “nuclear city under the ice.” They are dense with ordinary engineering: shielding, piping, instrumentation, thermal hydraulics, radiochemistry, component layout, installation sequence. The extraordinary machine appears as a stack of practical problems.

One of those problems was radiation shielding. Startup testing identified shielding deficiencies that required modification. The reactor worked, but “worked” included discovery, correction and additional material. This matters for the same reason tunnel closure matters. Frontier systems rarely divide cleanly into success and failure. They enter service through a sequence of specific embarrassments.

The reactor supplied heat and electricity to the camp and reduced the amount of fuel that had to be hauled across the ice. That was a real accomplishment. It also produced waste, required trained operators and embedded a nuclear system inside a facility whose surrounding medium was slowly deforming. When PM-2A was eventually removed, the reactor pressure vessel went back to the United States for examination. The infrastructure around the camp did not leave so neatly.

Later environmental discussions sometimes compress this into a claim that a nuclear reactor was abandoned beneath Greenland. That is wrong in the way that matters. The reactor itself was removed. The site retained other radiological material associated with operations, including waste coolant discharged into a sump, and it retained the much larger inventory of non-radiological material that abandoned installations usually leave: structures, fuel, sewage, chemicals and equipment.

The distinction is not pedantic. “Nuclear” exerts gravity on a story. Once the word appears, every other hazard starts orbiting it. At Camp Century the 2016 waste inventory estimated roughly 9,200 metric tons of physical material, about 200,000 liters of diesel fuel, millions of liters of wastewater and a probable burden of persistent chemicals such as PCBs. The estimated radioactivity of disposed reactor coolant was real but, by the authors' own comparison, far below the contamination released in the 1968 B-52 crash near Thule.

The environmental afterlife of Camp Century is therefore stranger than the familiar abandoned-reactor story. The reactor was the part designed to be removable. The camp was the disposable part.

This inversion runs through Cold War infrastructure. High-value, dangerous components receive procedures. Ordinary material disappears into the category of site restoration, burial, landfill or abandonment. But ordinary material becomes extraordinary when the landscape around it changes. A drum of diesel is mundane until the tank ruptures inside a moving ice sheet. Sewage is mundane until it is frozen into a hydrological system that may later reorganize. Paint is mundane until its chemistry includes persistent compounds that outlive the institution that used them.

PM-2A also complicates the idea that Camp Century was merely camouflage for Iceworm. The reactor program had its own institutional logic. The Army genuinely wanted compact reactors for remote bases. Engineers genuinely learned from operating one in Greenland. The camp could serve multiple agendas simultaneously without any one of them being fake.

This is how secrecy usually looks in functioning bureaucracies. The secret purpose does not replace the visible purpose. It parasitizes it, overlaps it, borrows its infrastructure and sometimes depends on the visible work being real enough to sustain the project.

Camp Century's scientists needed power. Its soldiers needed heat. Its engineers needed a test bed. Its strategists needed evidence that a hidden Arctic network could be supplied without an endless fuel train. PM-2A answered all four.

Then it left.

What remained under the snow would become somebody else's definition problem.
