# What Was Left Behind

There are two ways to make Camp Century sound more radioactive than it was. One is to say the reactor is still there. It is not. The other is to mention radioactivity first and stop counting.

The 2016 waste inventory by Colgan and colleagues is useful because it refuses that instinct. Their estimate included roughly 9,200 metric tons of physical infrastructure, about 200,000 liters of diesel fuel, at least 24 million liters of grey water and sewage, likely PCB-containing materials, and radiological waste associated with reactor operations. The radiological component was not imaginary. Waste coolant with measurable radioactivity was discharged into an unlined sump. But the authors estimated its original activity at about 1.2 billion becquerels, far below the contamination dispersed in the 1968 Thule B-52 accident.

Numbers need comparisons or they become mood lighting.

The physical waste is easier to visualize than the chemical one: steel arches, structural components, equipment and tunnel infrastructure crushed and carried within the ice. Chemical waste is more troublesome because it can move differently from solid structures. Diesel stored in tanks may remain liquid under some conditions. Wastewater freezes into porous firn and follows a different burial history. PCBs, if present in the quantities inferred from period materials, persist and can be transported if meltwater eventually reaches them.

The inventory contains uncertainty because nobody has excavated the site. Researchers combine historical records, engineering maps, known material practices, ice-flow models and radar data. Some values are estimates. The condition of individual tanks and sumps is not directly observed. The word “buried” is accurate; “contained” is less so.

This is where chain of custody becomes geographical. A laboratory sample has a label. A buried military site has a map, then ice flow changes the map. Researchers trying to reconstruct Camp Century's waste field must propagate uncertainty from the 1960s position through decades of horizontal and vertical advection. Radar can see strong structural contrasts, but it cannot identify every liter of fuel or sewage by name.

In 2017 and 2018, the Camp Century Climate Monitoring Programme used more than eighty kilometers of radar profiles to survey the site. The work helped map the debris field and improve estimates of depth. The result was not a crisp image of an intact city. The camp had deformed into a collection of subsurface reflectors whose relationship to the original plan had to be inferred.

This is an important antidote to another popular image: the lost base waiting in recognizable rooms under the ice. The tunnels did not freeze in place like a museum. Snow loading and ice deformation crushed the architecture. “City under the ice” is now more archaeological than literal.

The waste also exists inside a hierarchy of hazards that can change over time. A low-level radiological source decays. PCBs persist. Diesel degrades and disperses. Sewage carries biological and chemical concerns that depend on pathway. Steel is mostly a physical object unless it carries coatings or contaminants. The risk is not one number.

That makes remediation hard to discuss before exposure exists. Digging tens of meters into a moving ice sheet to recover dispersed material would itself create cost, worker risk and disturbance. Waiting preserves the material in cold isolation but leaves a future uncertainty. Monitoring buys information without pretending that information is cleanup.

There is no satisfying moral here. The 1960s disposal practice would not satisfy modern expectations. That does not automatically mean immediate excavation is sensible now. Environmental responsibility is not measured by how dramatic the machinery looks.

For the moment, the most defensible policy is almost boring: know where the site is, measure the climate and firn, update the model, keep the data public, and preserve the ability to act if the pathway changes.

Camp Century began as an argument for extraordinary engineering.

Its afterlife is an argument for competent monitoring.
