# Front Matter

**The Base Under the Ice**

*Camp Century, Project Iceworm, and the Future We Buried in Greenland*

Sven Hardy Benson

Camp Century is easy to misremember because every version of the story is true enough to crowd out the others. It was an Arctic research station. It was a military engineering experiment. It supported a portable nuclear reactor. It produced an ice core that helped change climate science. It sat inside a strategic plan for a missile network under Greenland. It became a buried waste site. It became a diplomatic problem. It became a radar target. It became, much later, evidence that parts of Greenland had once supported tundra when the present ice sheet was dramatically reduced.

The temptation is to choose the version that produces the cleanest story. The secret base. The nuclear city. The environmental time bomb. The heroic science station. The Cold War absurdity. The climate parable.

This book resists the sorting.

Camp Century matters because the categories fail at the same place. The Army wanted to know whether ice could be treated as infrastructure. Scientists wanted a deep core. Engineers wanted a reactor that could remove fuel convoys from the Arctic logistics problem. Strategists wanted mobility, concealment, survivability, and reach. Denmark wanted sovereignty without losing the American security guarantee. Greenlanders had far less power over decisions made on and under their land than later accounts sometimes admit. The ice itself behaved like none of the institutions wished it would. It flowed.

That last fact does most of the work.

The ice moved the tunnels. It deformed the roofs. It made maintenance expensive. It buried what was left. It carried the scientific record downward and later brought political attention back upward. Camp Century's afterlife is not a supernatural secret refusing to die. It is more interesting than that. Physical systems do not honor classification schedules.

The evidence is uneven, and the book says so. Army engineering reports establish what was built and what the engineers observed. Declassified and archival work establishes Project Iceworm and much of its strategic logic. Scientific papers establish what later researchers measured in the ice and at the site. Public statements and official monitoring establish present-day governance. Claims about who knew exactly what, when, and with what authority remain messier. The radioactive-waste story is often told badly. So is the climate story. The point is not to drain Camp Century of drama. It has plenty. The point is to put the drama back where the evidence can support it.

The result is a book about a failed military project that did not stay failed. It kept producing consequences long after the original mission disappeared.
