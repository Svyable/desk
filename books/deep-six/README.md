# Deep Six

## Operation CHASE and the Years America Sank Chemical Weapons at Sea

**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson

## Thesis

Before modern chemical-demilitarization plants, the United States disposed of obsolete chemical warfare material by treating distance, depth and dilution as engineering controls. Operation CHASE belongs inside that larger disposal history. The book asks a narrower and more useful question than whether ocean dumping was simply reckless: what did military planners, scientists, Congress and regulators believe the ocean could safely absorb; what evidence changed that belief; and what happens when a disposal method designed to make a weapon disappear instead creates a problem whose location, condition and future behavior become difficult to know?

The central collision is between chemical-weapons logistics and the history of environmental knowledge. A weapon can become waste without becoming harmless.

## Evidence contract

1. **Verified/documented:** official records establishing U.S. chemical stockpiles, historical deep-ocean disposal, later scientific objection, the 1972 legal prohibition, and subsequent land-based demilitarization programs.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** ship-by-ship CHASE cargoes, exact dump coordinates, packaging condition, contemporary risk calculations and institutional disagreements where surviving public records are partial.
3. **Disputed/unconfirmed:** claims about specific present-day leaks, ecological injuries or recovered objects unless tied to sampling, chain of custody and location evidence.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** the argument that ocean disposal converted a bounded inventory problem into a long-duration epistemic problem—material whose future condition became harder to observe precisely because disposal succeeded operationally.

No patent, recollection, press report or later map is treated as proof of an individual dump event. Program-level disposal evidence is not silently converted into a claim about a particular seabed object.
