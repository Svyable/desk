# 1 — Deep Six

There are several ways to destroy a chemical weapon. Fire is one. Chemistry is another. Time, if you are willing to wait long enough, will eventually do something to almost anything.

And then there is geography.

Put the weapon somewhere else.

For a surprisingly long stretch of the twentieth century, this counted as disposal.

The old vocabulary gives away the confidence. Deep-water disposal. Sea disposal. Dumping. Scuttling. None of these phrases sounds particularly technical now. At the time they could sit inside the same bureaucratic sentence as demilitarization. A munition had become obsolete. It occupied storage space. Its casing was aging. Keeping it created one set of risks. Moving it created another. Taking it apart created another. The ocean offered an answer with almost no moving parts.

Load it. Sail it. Sink it.

That is the ugly elegance at the center of this book.

The United States did not invent ocean dumping, nor was chemical warfare material the only dangerous thing nations put over the side. But the chemical stockpile makes the logic unusually stark because nobody involved could plausibly confuse the cargo with ordinary refuse. Mustard and nerve agents were manufactured because small quantities could injure or kill. Their danger was the reason they existed. Once they became militarily inconvenient, the problem was how to stop possessing them without paying forever for the privilege.

The sea looked infinite.

Infinity is an engineering material people reach for when they do not have to price it.

Decades later, a Government Accountability Office history of chemical demilitarization could state the earlier practice with bureaucratic economy: before 1969 the Army disposed of obsolete lethal chemical munitions by several methods, including deep-ocean dumping and open-pit burning. In 1969, the National Academy of Sciences recommended avoiding ocean dumping and studying alternatives. Congress followed with the Marine Protection, Research, and Sanctuaries Act of 1972, ending further ocean disposal of chemical agents.

Three sentences. An entire change in civilization can hide inside three sentences.

Before: the ocean is a disposal technology.

After: putting chemical agents into it is something the law has to stop.

The interesting territory is between those sentences.

It is easy, from this side of modern environmental law, to turn the earlier people into cartoons. Men in uniforms shove poison off ships because nobody in 1960 has heard of ecology. That version is satisfying and mostly useless. It erases the actual machinery of bad decisions: inventories, storage hazards, aging munitions, cost, competing risks, partial science, organizational jurisdiction and the strange moral discounts created by distance.

The harder question is what the disposal system believed it was doing.

If a ship carrying unwanted munitions sinks in very deep water, what exactly is the safety case? That the containers remain intact forever? Almost certainly not. Steel corrodes. That the agent chemically degrades before meaningful release? Perhaps, depending on agent, packaging and environment. That release is sufficiently slow and diluted that it does not matter? That the benthic ecosystem is expendable? That nobody will fish there? That nobody will find the site? That the ocean is simply too large for the quantity to count?

Those are different claims. They require different evidence. They also fail differently.

This distinction matters because the phrase *ocean dumping* compresses an engineering argument into a moral image. The image may be deserved. But if we want to understand how technically competent institutions authorize things that later look insane, the argument is more valuable than the image.

Operation CHASE—an acronym commonly expanded as Cut Holes And Sink 'Em—was the brutal poetry of the method. Obsolete material could be loaded into vessels intended to go down with it. The ship was not merely transportation. It became packaging. The seabed became the final storage location. There would be no warehouse guard, no ventilation system, no future maintenance crew, no line item for replacing a roof.

There would also be no easy inspection.

That second property is the one I cannot stop looking at.

Burial on land leaves coordinates. A bunker leaves doors. A tank leaves valves. A warehouse leaves an inventory that can, at least in principle, be opened and counted. A ship sunk in deep water takes a dangerous object and removes not only access but observability.

This is normally described as a benefit. In military disposal, inaccessible can mean secure.

It can also mean unknowable.

Where exactly did the vessel settle? Did it break apart? What was the condition of every container at loading? Which manifests survive? Which coordinates were recorded accurately? What moved after impact? What corroded first? Did an agent hydrolyze? Did a casing remain buried in sediment? Did fishermen later drag gear across an area that planners assumed nobody would disturb? If a modern survey finds a munition, can it be connected confidently to the historical operation people think placed it there?

The ocean is very good at keeping secrets and terrible at maintaining archives.

This is where CHASE becomes more than a story about chemical weapons.

It is a story about the difference between removing a hazard from human sight and removing the hazard.

That difference is embarrassingly durable.

Modern waste policy is full of descendants of the same argument. Carbon goes underground. Nuclear waste goes into geological repositories. Mine tailings sit behind engineered dams. Contaminated soil is capped. PFAS is transferred from water into filters that then become another waste stream. Every disposal technology eventually encounters the same impolite question: where did the thing actually go?

There is no away. There are only places with different monitoring budgets.

Chemical weapons make this visible because the inventory is finite enough to imagine. A shell. A drum. A ton container. A bomb. A vessel. One can point to the object before disposal and say: this exists here. After disposal, the object still exists somewhere, unless chemistry has transformed it. The administrative status changes instantly. The physical status does not.

That gap between administrative disappearance and physical persistence is the territory of *Deep Six*.

It would be tempting to write this as a scandal book. There are plenty of sharp words available: poison, secrecy, dumping, forgotten weapons. They practically arrange themselves on a cover.

But scandal can become another kind of dumping. It lets us throw complexity overboard.

The historical record needs more discipline than that. The existence of U.S. ocean disposal is documented. It does not follow that every cylinder photographed on a seabed contains chemical agent. The existence of CHASE does not authenticate every later story about a particular dump site. Program-level records do not establish individual exposure. A historical coordinate does not guarantee meter-scale modern location. Corrosion is not the same thing as catastrophic release. Finding contamination near a military disposal area does not, by itself, identify the source.

Those distinctions are not defenses of the program. They are what make an indictment, where the evidence earns one, worth reading.

There is another reason to be precise. The people making disposal decisions were dealing with a real problem.

Chemical munitions age badly.

Storage is not neutral. Keeping obsolete nerve-agent rockets in a depot is itself a decision with consequences. Leaking containers, deteriorating seals, unstable energetic components, workforce exposure, accident risk and security all accumulate. By the 1980s and 1990s, the United States would spend enormous sums designing a destruction system precisely because leaving the stockpile in place indefinitely was not acceptable. National Academies studies of later demilitarization describe the continuing hazard of aging chemical weapons and the technical difficulty of destroying them safely.

The mistake, then, was not noticing that obsolete chemical weapons required disposal.

The question is why the ocean looked like a sufficient answer.

One clue is scale.

Humans have trouble believing they can injure things larger than themselves. A river seems capable of carrying away a factory's discharge because the discharge vanishes downstream. The atmosphere seems capable of absorbing smoke because the chimney clears. The ocean seems especially invulnerable because its visible scale humiliates the individual observer. Stand on a ship and look outward. The proposition that anything in the hold could meaningfully alter that water feels absurd.

But environmental harm is rarely required to conquer an entire ocean.

It only has to matter somewhere.

A leaking object does not need to poison the Atlantic. It needs to alter the chemistry around itself, expose an organism, enter a food web, create a navigation or fishing hazard, or remain dangerous to the person who eventually encounters it. Dilution at planetary scale is irrelevant if the process is local.

That sounds obvious now. It was not always operationally decisive.

The 1969 National Academy intervention matters for precisely this reason. It marks a moment when the disposal problem stopped being only a logistics calculation and became a scientific argument about what the receiving environment could be assumed to do. Congress's 1972 prohibition then converted that changing scientific and political judgment into a boundary.

The sea stopped being free infrastructure.

What replaced it was expensive.

Neutralization. Incineration. Purpose-built plants. Monitoring. Environmental impact statements. Public hearings. Treaty deadlines. Contractor disputes. Cost overruns. Communities asking what comes out of the stack. Engineers arguing over secondary waste. The later chemical demilitarization program can look absurdly cumbersome compared with sailing an old ship offshore and opening it to the sea.

That cumbersome machinery is partly what accountability looks like when the disposal location remains inside politics.

The ocean had allowed a different arrangement. Distance performed governance.

Once a ship crossed the horizon, most citizens could not see the operation. Once it sank, almost nobody could see the material. The act could be completed in a day while the chemistry continued for decades.

This asymmetry—fast administrative closure, slow physical consequence—is the most important clock in the book.

There will be others.

The clock of corrosion.

The clock of hydrolysis.

The clock of environmental science.

The clock of classification.

The clock of congressional attention.

The clock of memory, which is unreliable in both directions. Institutions forget things they once documented. The public remembers things in forms the documents do not support.

A serious history has to keep all of those clocks running at once.

So we will follow the cargo.

Not metaphorically. Literally, where the record permits it.

What was in the stockpile? Why was it obsolete? How was it packaged? Who authorized movement? What did planners believe happened after sinking? What observations supported that belief? What did contemporary scientists object to? Which records identify sites and which merely repeat later summaries? What can modern oceanography detect? What does corrosion actually imply? Where do present-day claims outrun chain of custody?

And above all: what did the word *disposal* mean at each point in the story?

Because that word is doing more work than it admits.

To a quartermaster, disposal can mean removal from inventory.

To an engineer, it can mean rendering a system unusable.

To a chemist, destruction means changing the molecule.

To an environmental regulator, disposal describes placement and management of waste.

To the sailor watching a ship disappear beneath the surface, it may have meant something simpler.

Gone.

The rest of this book is about the distance between gone and destroyed.
