# The Experiment That Would Change the Answer

A book about an unresolved technology should eventually risk being wrong.

The decisive experiment is not another demonstration that a neutrino beam crosses rock. That question is settled. It is not another paper showing that submarine communication is strategically desirable. That problem is settled too.

The missing experiment is a realistic mobile receiver test.

Constrain the receiver before the test begins. Give it a mass, volume and power budget that a submarine designer would not laugh out of the room. Specify whether sensors can sit outside the pressure hull. Specify hydrodynamic limits. Put the device in seawater rather than a quiet experimental cavern. Move it. Add optical and radiological backgrounds. Vary depth and orientation.

Then illuminate it with a known neutrino source from a geometry that is deliberately imperfect.

Do not score the experiment by asking whether any neutrino-induced event was observed. Encode messages. Use a preregistered decoding rule. Report false alarms, misses, decoded bit rate, latency and energy consumed per reliably delivered information bit. Blind some trials so the analysis team does not know whether the beam carried a codeword. Publish failures.

The second experiment concerns pointing. Define a realistic region of receiver uncertainty and sweep or broaden the beam according to a declared strategy. Measure how quickly a tiny strategic codeword can be delivered throughout the region. This turns the phrase “communicate with a submarine” into a coverage problem.

The third experiment concerns source scale. Report the actual wall-plug power, beam power, target stress and maintenance burden associated with the communication run. Extrapolate honestly, with uncertainty, to strategic range and useful coverage. Do not let borrowed accelerator infrastructure vanish from the cost model.

The fourth experiment is institutional rather than physical. Compare the resulting channel with existing low-frequency and relay options under a small set of abstract failure scenarios. How much unique resilience does the neutrino path buy? What must an adversary destroy to defeat it? What must the operator maintain?

If these tests produced a compact receiver, bounded latency and a transmitter architecture that did not require absurd infrastructure, this manuscript’s skeptical center would collapse.

That is the point of writing the test down.

The opposite result would be useful too. Suppose the receiver works only when the source delivers flux far beyond practical strategic scale. Suppose patrol-box uncertainty destroys event rate. Suppose optical backgrounds turn a clever seawater detector into an unreliable machine. Suppose a VLF modernization buys more survivability per dollar.

Then neutrino communication would remain a genuine scientific achievement and a poor military system.

There is no shame in that outcome. Technologies do not fail by being physically real but economically or operationally uncompetitive. Most of engineering consists of discovering which real effects deserve to become infrastructure.

This experimental program also disciplines classified-technology speculation. If public experiments show that a practical link requires, say, three orders of magnitude improvement in effective receiver aperture, then a claim of secret deployment has acquired a concrete hidden requirement. The debate shifts from “the government has secret technology” to “the government would need a detector improvement of this approximate size, integrated on this approximate platform.”

That claim can be investigated.

Mystery becomes engineering when the missing miracle has units.
