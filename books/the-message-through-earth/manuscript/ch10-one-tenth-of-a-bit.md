# One Tenth of a Bit

A tenth of a bit per second is either useless or enough to start a war. The difference is protocol.

Modern communications culture makes bandwidth feel synonymous with capability. That is a luxury of applications in which humans improvise. Strategic systems can prearrange meaning. If both sides share a finite message set, a tiny number of bits can select among actions far larger than the message itself.

This is why the Fermilab link’s low data rate cannot by itself dismiss submarine use. A neutrino channel would not have to replace satellite communications or carry ordinary traffic. It might exist as a last-resort alert path: an instruction to shift to another receive mode, a time synchronization marker, a coded emergency signal, or one component in a multi-channel authentication procedure.

The Navy’s experience with ELF already demonstrates this logic. Extreme penetration was purchased with vanishing bandwidth because the mission did not require a normal conversation. The receiver could be told, in effect, to go somewhere else for the rest.

That is the strongest operational case for neutrinos, and it deserves to be made without embarrassment.

The problem is that the data rate measured at Fermilab was achieved with a major accelerator and a large detector at close range by strategic standards. Extending the path through much more Earth does not attenuate neutrinos in the way rock attenuates radio at ordinary frequencies, but geometric spreading and beam divergence still matter. A mobile receiver is smaller than MINERvA. A strategic transmitter must be reliable, survivable and available on command. One tenth of a bit is not a universal property of neutrino communication; it is a performance result for one particular link.

A fielded system therefore needs a complete latency budget. How long does the transmitter require to deliver a codeword with specified probability? How long must the receiver integrate? How often can the system repeat? What happens if the platform is near the edge of the beam? What is the false-alarm probability under realistic background? How much error-correction overhead is required? How much of the source can be devoted to communication rather than other purposes?

These questions sound pedestrian because they are.

That is their value.

Extraordinary technology becomes ordinary the moment someone depends on it. A commanding officer does not care that the carrier won a Nobel Prize. The officer cares whether the receiver declares a valid message in the required interval.

Low rate also creates a subtle security advantage. A receiver expecting only a few codewords can perform strong statistical tests and authentication around a narrow protocol. Yet protocol compression creates brittleness. If circumstances do not fit the prearranged vocabulary, the channel cannot improvise. A few bits can select an enormous action only because the enormous action was written down somewhere else.

The information is not really absent. It has been moved into doctrine.

That observation links communications engineering to secrecy. A hypothetical neutrino system might leave less radio-frequency evidence than conventional broadcasting, but it would require an unusually elaborate institutional shell: codebooks, receiver certification, message timing, command procedures, training and test events. The lower the channel capacity, the more meaning must be pre-positioned outside the channel.

This is another kind of footprint.

A secret technology is not only hardware. It is the bureaucracy required to make sparse signals mean the right thing under stress.

One tenth of a bit per second is therefore not a punchline. It is a question.

How much institution are you willing to build around a bit?
