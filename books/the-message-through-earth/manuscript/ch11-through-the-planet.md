# Through the Planet

The phrase that keeps neutrino communication alive is simple: *through the Earth*.

Fermilab routinely sends accelerator neutrinos through hundreds of kilometers of crust to distant detectors for oscillation experiments. No tunnel connects the endpoints. The beam enters the planet, the planet mostly fails to stop it, and a small fraction of the particles are detected far away.

For communications, that means line of sight can pass downward.

A radio engineer working on terrestrial frequencies worries about horizons, terrain, ionosphere, ducts and water. A neutrino engineer can draw a chord through the globe. In the abstract this is revolutionary. A transmitter in one country might illuminate a detector on the far side of the planet without satellites, repeaters or undersea cables.

The planet, however, is not merely transparent space. The beam still has direction and finite divergence. The Earth rotates. The receiver moves. At very high energies, neutrino absorption through long Earth chords eventually becomes non-negligible, so even the slogan of perfect transparency has an energy-dependent boundary. For the energy regimes relevant to particular proposals, those effects must be calculated rather than assumed away.

Submarines add a geometrical insult. Their location is uncertain by design.

One can imagine a transmitter sweeping a patrol region. But sweeping dilutes dwell time. One can broaden the beam, but broadening dilutes flux. One can use multiple sites, but that multiplies infrastructure. One can transmit into predefined ocean boxes at scheduled times, but this begins to reveal how much the supposed freedom of neutrino propagation depends on operational choreography.

The Earth is transparent. The targeting problem remains.

There is also a political geography. An accelerator capable of sending an intense beam through the planet is not physically confined to the country operating it. The neutrinos are harmless at ordinary experimental fluxes in the everyday sense, but a strategic system would raise questions about beam paths, safety analysis, verification and dual use. At sufficiently high beam intensities and energies, radiation doses from secondary interactions become a serious design consideration rather than a joke about ghost particles.

These are not objections to physics. They are reminders that the phrase *through the Earth* erases everything except propagation.

The most interesting comparison may be with satellite communication. Satellites require a path through contested space, can be tracked, can be attacked, and generally do not reach a deeply submerged receiver directly at useful frequencies. Yet they are flexible. Antennas can point. Coverage can be broad. Networks can route around failures. The infrastructure is large but distributed across a mature industrial base.

A neutrino link offers a radically different topology: perhaps fixed terrestrial sources projecting narrow invisible beams into volumes conventional radio cannot reach. Whether that topology is valuable depends on whether the recipient’s approximate location and timing are known well enough to exploit it.

This is where proposals become testable. Pick a patrol box. Specify its dimensions and depth distribution. Choose a source concept and plausible beam divergence. Choose a receiver effective area. Compute the expected event rate across the worst part of the box. Then calculate the time required to decode a small message at a specified false-alarm and miss probability.

No secrecy argument can substitute for that table.

A mature classified program might hide the real numbers. But public physics can still tell us what orders of magnitude a claimed system would have to defeat.

The planet is indeed transparent to neutrinos.

That is not the same thing as saying the planet is covered.
