# Building a Neutrino Beam

A neutrino transmitter begins with something much less ghostly: a large machine smashing ordinary matter.

In Fermilab’s NuMI beamline, high-energy protons strike a graphite target. The collisions produce secondary particles, including pions and kaons. Magnetic horns focus charged secondaries into a decay pipe. As those particles decay, neutrinos emerge. Rock and shielding absorb the charged debris while the neutrinos continue.

This is a beautiful way to make a clean beam if one already owns an accelerator complex.

That qualification matters.

The 2012 communication demonstration was economical in scientific terms because it borrowed a machine built for particle physics. Its communication result did not imply that the communications application could afford the source on its own. This is a recurring trap in technology assessment: a prototype can inherit infrastructure whose cost and scale disappear from the demonstration narrative.

Imagine evaluating commercial aviation by proving that a payload can fly aboard an experimental rocket and then pricing only the payload.

A fielded neutrino transmitter would need some combination of high beam power, reliable targetry, focusing, decay space, shielding, beam steering or suitable fixed geometry, timing and control. Components exposed to intense beams live hard lives. Targets heat. Radiation activates structures. Maintenance becomes remote and procedural. A strategic system must tolerate outages differently from a physics experiment whose schedule can accommodate maintenance campaigns.

The source is also difficult to hide in the simple sense. A large accelerator facility consumes electricity, requires specialized equipment and produces radiation-management obligations. Classification can conceal purpose, design details and performance. It cannot make heat and power demand metaphysical.

This does not prove that a compact classified source cannot exist. That would merely invert the same epistemic mistake. We should instead ask what source performance a submarine link requires and whether any known technology approaches that performance at the necessary size.

Muon-storage-ring concepts are one proposed route because decaying muons can generate well-characterized neutrino beams. Huber’s submarine-communication paper explored this family of source assumptions. But a proposal that begins with a muon storage ring is already telling us something about the scale of the problem. One does not invoke an accelerator architecture normally discussed for advanced particle physics because the transmitter is easy.

Directionality adds another wrinkle. High-energy neutrino beams are naturally forward-directed by relativistic kinematics and beamline design. That helps deliver flux to a known receiver. It also means the beam points somewhere. A fixed underground beamline is not a lighthouse that can illuminate arbitrary ocean coordinates without constraint.

A strategic network might therefore require multiple sites, steerable elements, broad beams, scheduled corridors or knowledge of approximate patrol areas. Each solution weakens another advantage. A broader beam reduces flux density. More sites increase infrastructure. More precise targeting requires better knowledge of a platform whose location is intentionally protected.

The problem begins to resemble a joke told by physics to operations research: the carrier can cross the planet, but only if the giant machine points the right way.

There is another strategic issue. A transmitter that exists for nuclear command must survive enough of the war it is meant to command. Fixed sites are targetable. Hardening an accelerator-scale facility is different from hardening a conventional radio transmitter, though some components might be distributed or redundant. A neutrino channel’s resistance to jamming does not make its source resistant to attack.

This distinction is routinely lost in discussions of “unblockable” signals. Propagation survivability is not system survivability.

The Fermilab result therefore proves something narrower and cleaner. Given an existing intense neutrino source and a suitable detector, digital information can be encoded and recovered through substantial matter.

That is enough to be extraordinary.

It is not enough to make the transmitter disappear.
