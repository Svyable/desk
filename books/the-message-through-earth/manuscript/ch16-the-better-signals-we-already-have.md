# The Better Signals We Already Have

Every exotic technology should be forced to compete against the systems that already exist, not against a blank page.

Submarine communication has accumulated layers because no single path solves every problem. Very-low-frequency transmitters reach submerged receivers under some conditions. Extremely-low-frequency systems historically traded almost everything for penetration. Airborne relay aircraft move the transmitter and complicate an adversary’s targeting problem. Satellites provide flexible high-capacity paths when the submarine accepts the geometry needed to use them. Buoys and masts permit other compromises. Procedures stitch these channels together.

A neutrino system would enter this architecture as another layer.

That framing immediately improves the analysis. The question is no longer whether neutrinos are better than radio. They are better at some propagation problems and dramatically worse at other system problems. The useful question is whether there is a failure mode in the existing architecture valuable enough to justify the new layer.

Jamming resistance is one candidate. Physical obstruction is another. Deep reception without a near-surface antenna is a third. A ground-based path that does not require an orbital relay could also matter in a war where satellites are degraded.

Against those advantages sit source scale, pointing, receiver burden, data rate, power, cost and strategic targetability of fixed infrastructure.

Existing systems also benefit from maturity. Operators know their failure modes. Supply chains exist. Exercises expose weaknesses. Hardware improvements accumulate. A new channel must outperform not the first version of ELF or VLF, but the evolving portfolio available when deployment is considered.

This is an underrated reason futuristic systems fail to arrive. The future keeps improving the incumbent too.

One can see this in the GAO’s 1979 review of Navy strategic communications. The debate over ELF did not occur in isolation. GAO compared it with TACAMO and other pathways and questioned assumptions about vulnerability. The system case depended on what the entire communications architecture already provided.

A modern neutrino proposal deserves the same treatment. How much additional probability of message delivery does it buy in the scenarios that matter? What failure modes are independent of existing channels? How much does that increment cost?

This is not a demand for a public answer to classified nuclear-command details. It is a design discipline. Even with abstracted scenarios, one can ask whether the neutrino channel has a unique region of the reliability space.

The answer may be yes.

A truly through-Earth, one-way, difficult-to-jam signal has distinctive properties. If a compact receiver and practical source became available, strategic planners would be irrational not to examine it.

But examination is not adoption. Military institutions study many technologies that never become systems because incumbents improve, requirements shift, or costs do not survive comparison.

This gives us another evidentiary warning. Proof that a defense organization funded a study of neutrino communication would be interesting. It would establish interest. It would not establish that the study found a useful system.

The better signals we already have are not perfect.

They are merely real, integrated, exercised and paid for.

That is a formidable advantage.
