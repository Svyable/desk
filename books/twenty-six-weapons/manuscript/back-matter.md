# Back Matter

## About the Book

*Twenty-Six Weapons* tells 250 years of American history through one weapon, platform, or weapon system for each decade from the 1770s through the 2020s.

The selections are arguments, not awards.

The weapon chosen for a decade is not necessarily the deadliest, the most advanced, the most widely produced, or the one a collector would place first in a display case. It is the object that best exposes something consequential about the United States at that moment: what the country could manufacture, what it had to import, what its institutions understood too late, what its myths preferred to remember, what its alliances made possible, what its adversaries forced it to learn, and what costs followed from the choices made around the weapon.

That is why the book begins with a French musket.

The American Revolution depended on foreign supply before the country later associated with the phrase arsenal of democracy could reliably arm itself. The next chapters follow the machinery that grew around that dependence: federal armories, machine tools, contracts, gauges, ammunition plants, shipyards, laboratories, satellites, software, multinational supply chains, and the less visible work required to turn a weapon from a drawing into something a soldier can actually use.

The history does not move cleanly from primitive to sophisticated.

Some of the most revealing weapons arrive late. Some fail before succeeding. Some become famous for reasons the battlefield evidence does not justify. Some matter chiefly because they are not used. Some are cheap additions to old objects. Some are so dependent on external networks that the physical object makes little sense by itself.

Across the chapters, the definition of the weapon therefore widens.

The Charleville-pattern musket is inseparable from French supply.

The Hall rifle is inseparable from machine tools and gauges.

The Model 1861 rifle-musket is inseparable from wartime contracting.

The atomic bomb is inseparable from a national industrial project.

Paveway and JDAM are inseparable from targeting information.

Predator is inseparable from communications links and remote crews.

The F-35 is inseparable from software, data, sustainment, and allies.

HIMARS is inseparable from the factory that can keep producing rockets after the first magazines are empty.

The object remains useful because it gives the reader something to hold onto.

The argument is everything surrounding it.

## How the Selections Were Made

Each decade had more than one plausible choice.

That competition is part of the method.

The 1860s could have belonged to the ironclad, the Spencer repeating rifle, or the Gatling gun. The rifle-musket wins because mass contracting and standard small-arms production reveal the scale of the Union's industrial state more directly.

The 1930s could have belonged to the B-17 or the aircraft carrier. The M1 Garand wins because it puts a peacetime institutional choice into the hands of millions of wartime users.

The 1950s could have belonged to the ICBM or the nuclear submarine. The thermonuclear weapon wins because the delivery systems make strategic sense only after destructive scale has changed what war itself could mean.

The 1990s could have belonged to the B-2 or the Tomahawk. JDAM wins because a relatively inexpensive guidance kit made a rare property — precision — into a mass property.

The 2020s choice is different from all the others because the decade is unfinished. HIMARS/GMLRS is the selection as of 2026, not a prediction carved into stone. Cheap drones, autonomous systems, long-range antiship weapons, electronic warfare, counter-space systems, or another technology may prove more consequential by 2029. If the evidence changes, the selection should change with it.

The earlier chapters have hindsight.

The last one has a timestamp.

## Notes and Sources

The manuscript was researched against a chapter-level source ledger maintained alongside the book.

The source hierarchy favors primary and institutional records where they can answer the question directly: National Archives material, official service histories, armory and museum collections, congressional investigations, Department of Energy and Department of Defense records, Government Accountability Office reports, manufacturer records for narrow production facts, declassified documents, and original technical or historical studies.

Secondary scholarship is necessary where the primary record is incomplete, disputed, inaccessible, or liable to produce a misleadingly narrow view. It is especially important in chapters involving Indigenous peoples, civilian casualties, labor, public memory, nuclear decision-making, covert force, and wars in which official American accounts describe only one side of the event.

A technical specification is never treated as proof of battlefield importance.

Adoption is not the same as fielding.

Fielding is not the same as reliability.

Accuracy is not the same as correct targeting.

A weapon's existence is not proof that it caused a political outcome.

A famous slogan is not proof that a weapon did what the slogan claims.

The chapter research therefore records competing selections, counterevidence, and a question that should survive every draft: what would make this choice wrong?

That discipline matters because weapon history is unusually vulnerable to mythology. The objects are collectible. Their mechanisms are fascinating. Their names acquire emotional weight. Advertising, movies, memoirs, service pride, corporate history, and national memory all simplify what happened.

The book uses those myths when they are themselves historically consequential.

It does not use them as evidence without checking them.

## A Note on Harm

A history organized around weapons can accidentally turn war into a sequence of engineering milestones.

That would be a failure of the project.

Every weapon has at least two histories.

One is the history seen from the people who design, buy, carry, maintain, and command it.

The other is the history seen from the people the weapon is meant to frighten, wound, displace, incapacitate, or kill.

The second history is often harder to preserve because military archives are built by institutions that operate the weapons. The imbalance in documentation does not make the imbalance in experience acceptable.

The chapters therefore resist two opposite temptations.

The first is celebration: treating greater range, accuracy, explosive power, stealth, or autonomy as progress without asking what the capability enabled.

The second is technological determinism: treating the weapon as though it made the decision to use itself.

A weapon creates an option.

People and institutions choose what to do with it.

That distinction does not reduce responsibility. It locates responsibility where it belongs.

## About the Author

Sven Hardy Benson writes about technology, markets, institutions, and human judgment. His work is interested in the machinery behind apparently simple outcomes: the supply chain behind a strategic resource, the capital cycle behind a technological boom, the information system behind a prediction, or, in this book, the country behind a weapon.

He is also the author of *Leveraging Luck: An Optimistic Guide to Life*, *Predictable: AI, Prediction Markets, and the Next Great Bubble*, and *Chokepoint: The Hidden War for the Metals That Run the World*.
