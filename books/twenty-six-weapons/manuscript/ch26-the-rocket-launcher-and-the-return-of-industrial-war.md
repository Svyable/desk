# The Rocket Launcher and the Return of Industrial War

The most important American weapon of the 2020s may turn out to be a truck that taught the United States to count rockets again.

That sentence is provisional.

It has to be.

The decade is not finished.

By 2029, cheap autonomous drones may look more consequential. Long-range antiship missiles may define American deterrence in the Pacific. Counter-space weapons, electronic warfare, artificial intelligence, directed energy, or systems not yet public may change the ranking completely.

A book about historical hindsight should not pretend to possess hindsight in advance.

As of 2026, however, the M142 High Mobility Artillery Rocket System — HIMARS — deserves the position because the launcher became a visible symbol of something larger than precision rockets.

It made industrial depth visible again.

The launcher was not new.

That is part of the argument.

HIMARS development reaches back to the 1990s, and the U.S. Army fielded the system years before Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. The launcher is mounted on a wheeled truck chassis and carries one pod that can fire families of guided rockets and missiles. Its tracked relative, the M270 Multiple Launch Rocket System, carries two pods.

For years, HIMARS was one capable artillery system among many.

Ukraine made it famous.

The United States announced the transfer of HIMARS and Guided Multiple Launch Rocket System ammunition to Ukraine in June 2022. The early launchers arrived in a war already defined by artillery, drones, trenches, electronic warfare, armor, missiles, and enormous consumption of ammunition.

The launcher did not transform that war by itself.

The ammunition did.

The targeting did.

The training did.

The intelligence did.

The logistics did.

The fact that this list is longer than the word HIMARS is the reason it belongs at the end of the book.

Ukraine used GMLRS rockets to strike ammunition depots, command posts, bridges, logistics nodes, and other targets behind Russian front lines at ranges that forced Russian forces to adapt. Warehouses moved farther from the front. Ammunition was dispersed. Command posts adjusted. Electronic warfare efforts evolved. Decoys and concealment mattered. The advantage did not remain static because no battlefield advantage does.

HIMARS became effective partly because a target had to be worth a rocket.

That sounds obvious.

It is the entire economics of precision war.

A guided rocket costing far more than an artillery shell should not be wasted on something cheaper weapons can destroy. The system works best when intelligence identifies targets whose destruction produces effects disproportionate to the munition expended.

A depot holding thousands of shells.

A bridge carrying supplies.

A command facility coordinating forces.

A radar.

A launcher.

Precision becomes a way to trade one expensive round for an even more expensive enemy problem.

This was the same logic Paveway introduced in the 1970s.

The difference is that Ukraine exposed how quickly a successful precision weapon becomes an inventory problem.

A launcher can survive for years.

A rocket disappears in seconds.

That arithmetic had been easy to forget during decades of American wars against opponents unable to consume American munitions at peer-war rates.

The wars in Iraq and Afghanistan were long, costly, and lethal. They required enormous logistics. But the United States often possessed uncontested industrial and air advantages, and the pattern of ammunition expenditure did not resemble the worst years of twentieth-century great-power war.

Ukraine restored a different image.

Warehouses empty.

Barrels wear out.

Factories matter.

Production lines have maximum rates.

Rocket motors have suppliers.

Explosives need plants.

Castings, electronics, seekers, fuzes, cases, and propellants have lead times.

Workers need training.

Machine tools need floor space.

A contract signed today may not become a munition for months or years.

The weapon returns to the factory.

This book began with the American Revolution depending on French muskets because the new republic could not produce enough of its own.

Two hundred and fifty years later, the United States is the arsenal supplier.

The structural problem is surprisingly similar.

A country can have money and allies and still discover that physical output cannot be increased instantly.

Production capacity is not a bank account.

It is machinery, people, suppliers, permits, energetics, test equipment, quality control, and accumulated process knowledge.

The United States relearned this after 2022 across multiple munitions categories.

Guided rockets are one example.

The Department of Defense reported that production of GMLRS had increased from 833 rockets per month in 2022 to 1,167 per month by early 2025, equivalent to about 14,000 a year. HIMARS launcher production capacity also rose, with industry expanding from roughly four launchers per month before the surge toward eight per month, or ninety-six a year.

Those numbers matter more than the glossy launcher photograph.

They describe the rate at which strategic promises can become physical objects.

In 2026, the industrial story widened further. Australia successfully test-fired the first GMLRS missiles manufactured domestically in South Australia to U.S. standards, part of an effort to create allied production capacity rather than rely entirely on American factories.

That is alliance strategy in factory form.

A treaty can promise support.

A production line determines how much support can actually be delivered.

The United States and its partners began treating munitions manufacturing as an alliance capability in its own right.

The move resembles the F-35's multinational industrial architecture but with a different urgency.

The F-35 network distributes production because the program was designed as a long-term multinational system.

The GMLRS production network is being expanded because war exposed the risk of insufficient magazines.

Both turn allies into industrial nodes.

The 2020s have also changed the meaning of scale.

For much of the post-Cold War era, American defense discussion celebrated exquisite systems: stealth aircraft, precision missiles, submarines, advanced sensors, and small professional forces able to achieve remarkable effects.

Ukraine did not make exquisite systems irrelevant.

It showed that exquisite systems can be consumed.

A precision rocket is still one rocket.

A Patriot interceptor is still one interceptor.

A Javelin is still one missile.

A drone is still one airframe.

Quality and quantity are not opposites.

Quality has to exist in quantity appropriate to the war.

That is the return of industrial war.

The phrase can be misunderstood as nostalgia for World War II factories.

The modern industrial base is different. A GMLRS rocket contains electronics and precision components that a 1940s artillery shell did not. Production can depend on specialized suppliers scattered across regions and countries. Semiconductor supply, energetics, rocket motors, rare materials, test equipment, software, and security requirements complicate rapid expansion.

Modern weapons are harder to produce precisely because they are more capable.

That creates an uncomfortable strategic trade.

A weapon can require fewer shots per target because it is accurate.

The same weapon can be slower to replenish because it is complex.

Precision saves ammunition tactically and creates production bottlenecks industrially.

HIMARS sits at that intersection.

The launcher itself also demonstrates the value of mobility.

A firing unit can move, launch, and displace. Mobility complicates counterbattery attack. A wheeled chassis eases road movement and strategic transport compared with heavier tracked systems in some circumstances.

But mobility is not invisibility.

Drones, satellites, signals intelligence, radar, human observers, and persistent surveillance make the modern battlefield hostile to anything that emits, moves, or remains in one place too long.

The launcher survives through behavior as much as armor.

Shoot.

Move.

Hide.

Use decoys.

Control emissions.

Protect the resupply chain.

The operational system therefore includes discipline.

The weapon can be given away through careless phone use, predictable routes, or visible support vehicles.

The information environment has become part of camouflage.

This is where HIMARS intersects with the strongest challenger for the decade: the cheap drone.

First-person-view drones, one-way attack drones, commercial quadcopters, reconnaissance drones, and rapidly improvised autonomous or semi-autonomous systems have changed battlefield observation and strike at extraordinary speed.

They are cheap compared with many traditional guided weapons.

They can be assembled from commercial components.

They expose vehicles and soldiers that once relied on terrain or distance for protection.

They make movement observable.

They force electronic warfare down to tactical units.

They blur the line between ammunition and aircraft.

If this book were written in 2029, the drone might win.

Why does HIMARS win in 2026?

Because the launcher exposes the whole American system more clearly.

The drone revolution demonstrates cheap adaptation.

HIMARS demonstrates the interaction among expensive precision, satellite navigation, intelligence, allied training, export policy, munitions stockpiles, factory capacity, and strategic resupply.

It is the culmination of the book's argument.

The physical launcher is the least interesting part of the weapon.

Remove GPS-quality navigation or equivalent guidance support and accuracy changes.

Remove targeting intelligence and the rocket may be wasted.

Remove training and the crew cannot exploit the system.

Remove maintenance and the launcher eventually stops.

Remove rockets and it stops immediately.

Remove political authorization and certain targets remain off limits.

Remove allied logistics and the whole campaign changes.

The weapon is the chain.

The chain is industrial and political as much as technical.

The 2020s also made adaptation speed a central weapon characteristic.

When HIMARS began producing strong effects in 2022, Russian forces adjusted. Depots moved. Command nodes dispersed. Bridges were repaired or bypassed. Electronic-warfare pressure against satellite-guided weapons increased. Targets became more fleeting.

A weapon that is decisive for several months may become merely useful after the enemy learns.

This is not failure.

Forcing the enemy to change behavior is itself an effect.

A depot moved farther back increases trucking distance.

A command post dispersed may communicate less efficiently.

A bridge protected by air defenses consumes defensive resources.

A launcher hunted by drones has to spend more time concealed.

The battlefield is full of costs that do not appear in destroyed-equipment counts.

HIMARS mattered because it imposed geometry on Russian logistics.

Then Russia changed the geometry.

That exchange is what real military innovation looks like.

Not revolution.

Iteration.

The same dynamic makes the chapter's provisional nature essential.

The GMLRS rocket itself is evolving. Extended-range variants are intended to reach roughly twice the range of earlier rounds. The Precision Strike Missile gives the same launcher a different class of long-range weapon. ATACMS, an older missile family, was also supplied to Ukraine. One launcher can therefore participate in several range bands and target sets depending on the pod loaded.

The launcher is an interface.

That makes it durable.

The truck does not have to embody the final missile.

It carries containers that allow the ammunition family to evolve.

Again the architecture matters more than the object.

This is the same principle that made the AR-15/M16 family durable and made JDAM scalable.

Standardize the interface.

Change what plugs into it.

The industrial advantage comes when a country can improve capability without replacing every launcher.

The industrial disadvantage comes when the new ammunition is sophisticated enough that production remains slow.

By August 2026, the scale of American concern had become unusually explicit. The Army issued a request asking industry how it might support production of more than 133,000 GMLRS rockets through 2034, with planned annual output in later years far above the production capacity reached earlier in the decade.

A request for information is not a signed production contract and should not be described as one.

Its significance is still considerable.

The Army was putting magazine depth into industrial planning language.

The launcher had taught the institution to count the future.

This is a striking reversal from the post-Cold War assumption that technological overmatch could often compensate for smaller inventories.

Overmatch still matters.

But inventories are part of overmatch.

A weapon unavailable on day thirty is not superior on day thirty.

That sentence sounds banal.

It is one of the hardest truths for peacetime militaries to finance.

Factories appear inefficient when demand is low.

Surge capacity costs money while idle.

Long-term munitions contracts tie up budgets.

Stockpiles age.

Politicians prefer visible platforms to invisible inventory.

Companies hesitate to build capacity without durable orders.

Then war begins and everyone asks why production cannot triple by Tuesday.

The economics of readiness remain awkward because success often looks like unused capacity.

The United States encountered the same problem with rare-earth processing, shipyards, semiconductors, and other strategic supply chains.

Resilience looks wasteful until concentration fails.

The weapon chapter and the economic chapter become the same chapter.

HIMARS also reveals the role of restraint inside weapon effectiveness.

Throughout the war in Ukraine, the United States and other allies debated which munitions to provide, what ranges were appropriate, and under what conditions weapons could be used against targets in Russian territory. Those policies evolved.

The launcher did not possess one fixed strategic range.

Its effective political range was determined partly by the ammunition supplied and the permissions attached to its use.

That is another way the weapon extends beyond steel.

Policy can function like a mechanical stop.

A missile may physically reach a target that the operator is not authorized to strike.

Changes in policy can therefore alter battlefield capability without modifying the launcher at all.

This is not unique to Ukraine. Rules of engagement have always shaped weapons use.

Networked precision makes the relationship unusually visible because range, coordinates, software, intelligence, and authorization can be controlled at multiple layers.

The weapon's map is political.

The 2020s have also weakened the assumption that the United States can separate the defense industrial base from civilian industry.

Drones depend on commercial electronics, batteries, cameras, radios, processors, and manufacturing techniques. Advanced missiles depend on semiconductors and specialized materials. Satellite communications use commercial constellations as well as government systems. Cybersecurity affects factories. Skilled labor moves between sectors.

The arsenal is embedded in the economy.

It always was.

The difference is that supply chains are now global enough that a national weapon can contain components, minerals, machine tools, and intellectual property crossing multiple borders.

The French musket returns in another form.

America can be the world's largest defense spender and still depend on foreign inputs.

The idea of complete military self-sufficiency was always partly mythical.

The question is which dependencies are tolerable, which are resilient, and which become chokepoints under pressure.

HIMARS therefore closes the 250-year arc without completing it.

The first weapon in this book arrived from an ally because America lacked enough arms.

The last weapon is supplied by America to an ally and reveals that America can lack enough ammunition for the scale of conflict it is preparing to deter.

The direction of dependence changed.

The fact of dependence did not.

That may be the strongest conclusion available in an unfinished decade.

A weapon is never only the thing that fires.

It is the mine and refinery.

The factory and contract.

The training range and satellite.

The maintenance depot and software update.

The alliance and export license.

The analyst who finds the target.

The truck that brings the pod.

The worker who casts the motor case.

The budget that keeps the line open before anyone knows whether the round will be needed.

The launcher makes all of that visible because the launcher is useless the moment the chain stops.

The most important weapon of the 2020s may yet be something else.

If so, the book should change.

That is not a weakness in the selection.

It is the final historical lesson.

People living inside a decade do not know which object will define it.

They know what is scarce.

They know what is failing.

They know what everyone is suddenly trying to build faster.

In 2026, the United States is trying to build more rockets.

That is evidence worth listening to.
