# The Fighter That Became a Network

The most important American weapon of the 2010s was an airplane that made the word airplane insufficient.

The F-35 Lightning II has wings, an engine, landing gear, a cockpit, a gun on one variant, internal weapon bays, and the familiar silhouette of a military aircraft.

Those facts are almost misleading.

Its historical claim is not that it flies farther, faster, or higher than every fighter before it.

Its claim is that the aircraft tries to turn sensing, computing, low observability, weapons, electronic warfare, logistics, and allied interoperability into one distributed combat system.

The fighter became a network node.

That is why the F-35 is the weapon of the 2010s.

The program had begun long before the decade. The Joint Strike Fighter competition belonged to the 1990s. Lockheed Martin's X-35 won the competition in 2001. The first production-oriented F-35 flew in 2006.

The 2010s were the decade in which the promise had to become an operational force.

That transition was painful.

The Marine Corps declared initial operational capability for the F-35B in 2015. The Air Force declared the F-35A combat ready in 2016. The Navy's carrier-capable F-35C reached initial operational capability in 2019.

By then, the program had already become a political object larger than the aircraft.

It was expensive.

It was late.

It had technical deficiencies.

It was assembled through a multinational industrial network.

It had supporters who described it as the necessary foundation of future air power and critics who treated it as evidence that the American acquisition system had lost the ability to build affordable weapons.

Both camps sometimes talked past the actual airplane.

The F-35 is valuable precisely because the argument around it reveals what a modern weapon system has become.

Start with the sensors.

A traditional fighter pilot looks through the canopy, checks radar displays, listens to radio calls, reads instruments, and mentally assembles a picture from different information sources.

The F-35 was designed to do more of that assembly inside the machine.

Its radar, electro-optical systems, electronic-support sensors, distributed aperture cameras, navigation, mission data, and external information feeds contribute to a fused picture presented to the pilot.

Sensor fusion is an unromantic phrase for a profound ambition.

Do not merely give the pilot more data.

Reduce the pilot's need to reconcile the data manually.

The difference resembles the transition from an unguided bomb to a guided bomb.

The older system leaves more correction to the human.

The newer system puts more correction into the architecture.

That does not eliminate the pilot.

It changes the pilot's work.

The ideal is not a person wrestling with separate sensors.

It is a person making tactical decisions from an integrated picture.

Whether the implementation fully achieves that ideal in every situation is a separate question.

The aspiration itself marks the decade.

The fighter is becoming a computer with an aircraft wrapped around it.

That phrase can be exaggerated. The F-35 is still governed by aerodynamics, propulsion, heat, structural loads, fuel, weapons, and maintenance. A software bug does not make gravity negotiable.

But software increasingly determines what the physical platform can do.

Mission systems identify threats.

Software fuses tracks.

Electronic warfare functions depend on code and data libraries.

Weapons integration depends on software blocks.

Maintenance and mission planning depend on information systems.

Modernization can add capability without changing the airframe's external shape.

The platform's operational identity therefore changes over time.

A 2015 F-35 and a later upgraded F-35 may share the same basic airframe while differing substantially in software, sensors, weapons, processing, and threat data.

The weapon has versions that are not visible in a museum photograph.

This creates a new historical problem.

How do you date a software-defined weapon?

The Garand was adopted in 1936. Mechanical modifications followed, but the rifle remained legible as an object.

The F-35's capability arrives in increments, software blocks, hardware refreshes, mission-data updates, weapons clearances, and sustainment changes.

There is no single moment when the finished weapon appears because the weapon is never finally finished.

That is ordinary in software.

It is disruptive in military procurement.

A government traditionally wants to define requirements, test a design, buy the design, and maintain it.

Software creates pressure for continuous development.

Threats change.

Processors age.

Libraries need updating.

Bugs are discovered.

New weapons must be integrated.

Networking standards evolve.

The acquisition system has to manage a product that behaves increasingly like a service.

The F-35's troubled logistics software made this visible in an almost comic way.

The Autonomic Logistics Information System, or ALIS, was intended to help manage maintenance, parts, mission planning, and other functions across the fleet. The idea fit the aircraft's networked philosophy: use data to make sustainment smarter.

The implementation became one of the program's most criticized features.

Government Accountability Office reports documented inaccurate or incomplete data, manual workarounds, connectivity concerns, parts-management problems, and risks that could affect aircraft availability. In some cases, the system could indicate that an aircraft should not fly even when maintainers knew a properly installed part was safe.

The smart fighter had acquired a dumb database problem.

That is not a side story.

It proves the thesis.

If a logistics information system can ground a flight-ready aircraft, then the logistics information system is part of the weapon.

The F-35's physical performance can be excellent and operational availability can still suffer because the digital supply chain is wrong.

The same system logic that creates capability creates dependency.

Networks widen the weapon and widen the failure surface.

This book began with a French musket whose dependency was visible in a crate.

The F-35's dependencies are harder to see.

Software repositories.

Mission-data files.

Specialized test equipment.

Global spare-parts pools.

Engine depots.

Coatings.

Secure networks.

Training simulators.

Contractor technical data.

International suppliers.

Runways, ships, fuel, munitions, and tankers.

The weapon is global before it takes off.

That is not an accident of procurement.

It was part of the political design.

The Joint Strike Fighter program was built with international partners and customers. Components and industrial work were distributed across allied countries. The aircraft was intended to replace multiple legacy fleets while increasing interoperability among the United States and partner air forces.

A fighter purchase became an alliance choice.

That gives the F-35 strategic value beyond its individual combat performance.

If allied air forces fly compatible aircraft, share training, weapons, sustainment structures, tactics, and data standards, cooperation becomes easier in some ways. A coalition can develop a common operational language.

The industrial relationship can also make withdrawal harder.

Factories, jobs, maintenance facilities, pilot training, and national force plans become tied to the program for decades.

The weapon helps lock in an alliance architecture.

A rifle rarely does that.

A multinational aircraft program can.

This is why judging the F-35 only by dogfighting metrics misses much of the program's purpose.

Critics have often asked whether a single aircraft can adequately replace or supplement specialized fighters, attack aircraft, and short-takeoff platforms. The concern is legitimate. Multi-role programs can become compromises. Commonality can save money in theory and produce integration complexity in practice. Requirements from three U.S. services and multiple partners can pull a design in conflicting directions.

The F-35's three variants make those tensions physical.

The Air Force's F-35A uses conventional takeoff and landing.

The Marine Corps F-35B can conduct short takeoffs and vertical landings through an elaborate lift system.

The Navy's F-35C has larger wings and carrier-specific structures.

They share an identity without being identical airplanes.

Commonality is an engineering and political target, not a magical condition.

The program's cost makes every shortfall visible.

GAO repeatedly described the F-35 as the Department of Defense's most ambitious and costly weapon system. By the end of the 2010s, hundreds of aircraft were operating across the United States and international partners, while long-term acquisition and sustainment commitments stretched into enormous sums.

The number is so large that it can become rhetorical rather than analytical.

The useful question is not whether the program is expensive.

It obviously is.

The useful question is what the United States is buying with the expense and whether the architecture delivers those effects more effectively than plausible alternatives.

That answer cannot be reduced to unit price.

A stealth aircraft may allow a different strike package.

A sensor-fused aircraft may contribute targeting information to other forces.

A common allied fleet may reduce some interoperability costs while creating new dependencies.

A large production run may spread fixed development costs while locking users into a sustainment ecosystem.

Software modernization may extend usefulness while creating permanent upgrade expense.

The denominator matters.

Cost per airplane is easy to understand.

Cost per coalition effect is harder.

So is the opportunity cost.

Money committed to F-35 fleets cannot be spent on tankers, munitions, bases, drones, electronic warfare, ships, air defenses, readiness, or another fighter design.

A weapon can be effective and still be too expensive at the margin.

That ambiguity should survive the chapter.

The F-35 also inherited the stealth lesson of the F-117 without inheriting its narrow mission.

The Nighthawk sacrificed heavily to penetrate defended airspace and drop precision bombs. It had no radar, modest speed, and a specialized role.

The F-35 tries to combine low observability with radar, electronic warfare, air-to-air weapons, air-to-ground weapons, network connectivity, and day-to-day multi-role operations.

Stealth moved from special operation to baseline design philosophy.

That normalization is historically important.

A capability that once justified a tiny black fleet became something the United States planned to buy in the thousands across multiple services and partners.

This is the same distribution logic seen with the Garand and JDAM.

American power often turns an elite advantage into a standard one.

The difficult part is paying to sustain the standard.

By 2019, GAO reported that the growing F-35 fleet still faced serious readiness problems, including spare-parts shortages and logistics-system difficulties. Those facts should not be treated as embarrassing annotations to a success story.

They are the story.

The F-35's thesis is that the network makes the airplane more capable.

Its failures show that the network can also make the airplane unavailable.

The weapon becomes as strong as its least visible dependency.

This is exactly the problem the 2020s would rediscover at larger scale.

A HIMARS launcher can be excellent.

Without rockets, it is a truck.

An F-35 can be excellent.

Without mission-capable engines, spare parts, software, data, weapons, and trained maintainers, it is expensive parking.

The industrial base returns.

The 2010s seemed, in some American defense debates, to be the era when exquisite capability could compensate for quantity. The United States had dominated the air against opponents unable to field comparable integrated systems. Stealth, precision, sensors, networking, and professional crews encouraged the belief that quality could substitute for mass.

The F-35 embodied that confidence.

It also complicated it because the program itself depended on mass.

Thousands of planned aircraft.

Large allied fleets.

Global production.

Enormous sustainment networks.

The exquisite platform required industrial scale.

That contradiction is worth preserving.

The fighter is not the opposite of mass production.

It is mass production at a higher level of complexity.

The strongest runner-up for the 2010s might be the MQ-9 Reaper, which expanded the remotely piloted strike architecture developed with Predator. Cyber capabilities also became weapons in a more explicit sense, as state operations could damage systems, steal data, or disrupt infrastructure without conventional attack. Long-range antiship missiles and increasingly capable air defenses pointed toward a Pacific contest in which large American platforms would face growing risk.

The F-35 wins because it sits at the convergence.

Stealth.

Sensors.

Software.

Data links.

Precision weapons.

Electronic warfare.

Alliances.

Global supply chains.

And the uncomfortable truth that none of those advantages function independently.

The aircraft's most important output may sometimes be information rather than a bomb.

An F-35 can detect, classify, and share information about threats. In networked operations, another platform may fire the weapon. The fighter may contribute to a kill chain without being the shooter.

That breaks the old image of a weapon platform.

A battleship is judged partly by its guns.

A fighter is traditionally judged partly by what it can shoot down.

A networked sensor platform can create combat value by helping something else shoot.

The weapon's boundary dissolves.

This creates difficult questions of attribution.

Which system killed the target?

The sensor that detected it?

The aircraft that fused the track?

The satellite that relayed data?

The commander who authorized the shot?

The missile that physically hit?

Industrial-age weapons encouraged object-level answers.

Information-age warfare increasingly does not.

The F-35 is the fighter that became a network because the United States increasingly wants combat power to emerge from connection rather than from isolated platform performance.

That approach can be devastating when the connections work.

It can be brittle when they do not.

The 2010s ended without settling the question.

The program had crossed from development into operational reality, but reliability, software, sustainment, modernization, and affordability problems remained. More countries were buying the aircraft. More squadrons were training. More capability was being added.

The weapon was simultaneously deployed and unfinished.

That may be the most accurate description of modern military technology.

There is no final version.

There is only the version connected today.
