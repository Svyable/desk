*An American History, One Decade at a Time.*

The United States is approaching its 250th anniversary with a familiar habit: telling its history through presidents, wars, elections, inventions, and declarations. *Twenty-Six Weapons* tells the same history through twenty-six physical objects and systems — one for every decade from the 1770s through the 2020s — chosen not because each was the deadliest or most technically impressive weapon of its moment, but because each reveals something unusually important about the America that made, bought, borrowed, feared, exported, mythologized, or depended on it.

The first weapon is French. That matters. The revolutionary republic that would become the world's largest arms producer began by surviving on muskets it could not manufacture in sufficient numbers for itself. The story then moves through federal armories, interchangeable parts, frontier mythology, the factory war of the 1860s, repeating rifles, submarines, machine guns, nuclear weapons, precision guidance, stealth, drones, software, and satellite-linked rockets. The trajectory is not a simple march from primitive to advanced. Weapons fail. Procurement systems fail. Myths outlive battlefield evidence. Cheap adaptations sometimes matter more than exquisite platforms. A weapon can be strategically decisive without firing very often, and culturally decisive without deserving its legend.

The book treats a weapon as a compressed history of state capacity. Inside it are factories, metallurgy, chemistry, finance, labor, doctrine, communications, logistics, alliances, bureaucracy, politics, and moral choice. Choosing one per decade forces an argument: why this one, why then, and what did it change that a more famous candidate did not? The selections are deliberately contestable. The point is not to settle a collector's ranking. It is to make two and a half centuries of American power visible in objects a reader can picture.

This is not a celebration of weaponry, and it is not an argument that weapons alone made the United States. The people on the receiving end remain part of the story. So do the workers who built the weapons, the allies who supplied them, the taxpayers who financed them, the officers who mistrusted them, the politicians who oversold them, the engineers who fixed them, and the civilians who lived with the consequences. Technical change matters most when it changes institutions and choices around it.

The final chapter is necessarily provisional. The 2020s are not over. That uncertainty belongs in the book rather than being edited away. A weapon important in 2026 may look different in 2029. The last selection therefore asks a second question the earlier chapters can answer with hindsight: how do we know when a weapon has actually changed an era, rather than merely dominated its headlines?
