# Chapter 15 — The Classroom

The classroom removes one of diplomacy's most useful protections: classification.

Inside government, uncertainty can be contained in restricted memoranda, private meetings, intelligence channels, and the understood discretion of colleagues. A professor has to put the argument on the table.

What do you believe?

Why?

What evidence would change your mind?

Nicholas Burns arrived at Harvard Kennedy School carrying the accumulated habits of a career in which those questions had consequences beyond the room. He had represented the United States in negotiations, defended policy publicly, advised presidents and secretaries, managed alliances, and worked problems in which a wrong judgment could damage relationships among governments.

Teaching changed the direction of accountability.

A diplomat explains the world upward to officials with authority and outward to foreign governments. A teacher explains it to people who are entitled to interrupt.

That interruption matters.

Students can ask the question that bureaucracy has learned not to ask because the premise became institutional years ago. Why does this alliance exist? Why is this country an adversary? Why should another government accept an American definition of order? What would this policy look like from the other capital? When does credibility become vanity? When does patience become drift?

The questions are basic.

Basic questions are dangerous to mature institutions.

Burns's academic work centered on diplomacy itself. At Harvard he became professor of the practice of diplomacy and international politics and helped build the Future of Diplomacy Project. The project treated diplomacy not as ceremony at the edge of foreign policy but as a profession that could be studied, taught, criticized, and renewed.

The word practice was important.

Diplomacy has theory, but its failures often arrive through particulars.

A sentence is translated too literally. A promise is made by an official who cannot deliver it. A summit is scheduled before the lower-level negotiation is mature. A concession is offered without understanding what the other government needs in order to reciprocate. A negotiator mistakes silence for agreement. A capital reads a tactical delay as strategic refusal.

None of these errors requires ignorance of international relations theory.

They require misreading people and institutions.

The classroom can slow those moments down.

Consider the India civil nuclear negotiations. From a distance, the policy can be described in a paragraph: the United States sought a strategic transformation in its relationship with India and agreed to alter a nuclear regime that had constrained cooperation for decades. India accepted a separation plan and safeguards for designated civilian facilities. Washington then had to secure domestic and international changes necessary to make civil nuclear commerce possible.

That summary is accurate enough to conceal the work.

The work was sequencing.

India had domestic constraints. The United States had Congress. The international system had the Nuclear Suppliers Group and the IAEA. Each actor needed confidence that another actor would eventually do something that actor had not yet done.

Diplomacy in such a case is partly the construction of a bridge from commitments that cannot all be delivered at once.

A seminar can take that bridge apart.

What had to happen first? Which commitment was reversible? Which actor bore the greatest political risk? What language allowed two governments to describe the same bargain differently to domestic audiences without making the bargain fraudulent? At what point would delay have destroyed confidence?

These questions turn history into a laboratory without pretending history can be rerun.

Burns's years outside government also widened the audience for his argument about diplomacy. He wrote, lectured, advised, and spoke publicly about American leadership, alliances, Russia, the Middle East, India, and eventually China. He served on boards and worked with institutions that sat between government, business, philanthropy, and the academy.

That position is common in American foreign policy and deserves scrutiny rather than romance.

Washington has a large ecosystem of former officials who move among universities, think tanks, consultancies, companies, commissions, campaigns, and administrations. The circulation can preserve expertise. It can also narrow imagination if the same people, assumptions, and networks continually reproduce themselves.

A serious account of public service has to hold both possibilities.

Experience matters.

So does renewal.

Burns's career offers an unusually clear view because he had already served administrations of both parties. His professional identity was built less around a single ideological project than around an institution: the diplomatic service of the United States.

That institution itself was changing.

The foreign-policy environment in the years after Burns left government became harder to describe with the categories that had organized much of his career. Russia moved from difficult partner to increasingly direct antagonist. China became simultaneously a central trading partner, technological competitor, military rival, climate actor, and indispensable participant in global problems. Domestic polarization reached into foreign policy. Social media accelerated public diplomacy while making it more brittle. Cyber operations blurred distinctions between peace and conflict.

The diplomatic profession had to operate in a world where governments were no longer the only actors capable of moving international events at speed.

A platform company could affect political speech across borders.

A semiconductor manufacturer could become strategically important to deterrence.

A private satellite network could matter in war.

A laboratory could become part of a geopolitical competition over artificial intelligence.

A virus could close borders faster than diplomats could negotiate their reopening.

The traditional embassy remained, but the number of systems it had to understand multiplied.

This made Burns's insistence on diplomacy seem at once conventional and newly relevant.

The strongest argument for diplomacy is not that talking produces agreement.

Often it does not.

The argument is that adversaries need mechanisms for discovering what they disagree about, how strongly, under what conditions, and with what risk of accidental escalation.

That is especially true when relations deteriorate.

Friendly governments have redundant channels. Officials travel. Businesses talk. Militaries exercise together. Legislatures visit. Scientists collaborate. Journalists move. Universities exchange students. Misunderstandings can be corrected through many routes.

Rivals lose redundancy.

Visas tighten. Military contacts are suspended. Academic exchange becomes politically suspect. Technology ecosystems separate. Journalists are restricted. Commercial relationships become securitized. Public rhetoric hardens.

The fewer channels remain, the more weight each surviving channel must carry.

This would become central to Burns's next public assignment.

By 2021, the United States and China were not simply managing a difficult bilateral relationship. They were trying to determine the rules of a strategic competition whose boundaries neither side fully controlled.

Trade, Taiwan, technology, the South China Sea, human rights, nuclear weapons, climate, pandemics, supply chains, alliances, cyber operations: the agenda was too broad for a single bargain and too consequential for diplomatic absence.

The classroom had allowed Burns to study diplomacy as a craft.

Beijing would test whether the craft still worked when agreement was scarce.

There is a line running from the young officer in Cairo to the professor at Harvard that is easy to miss because the jobs look so different.

Both are apprenticeships in attention.

In Cairo, the question was how to understand a country before speaking for one's own.

At Harvard, it was how to teach future officials to interrogate their assumptions before those assumptions became policy.

The classroom does not reproduce the pressure of government. It cannot.

No student's grade changes the position of an aircraft carrier. A case discussion cannot recreate the political cost borne by a prime minister who accepts a compromise. A simulation cannot reproduce the uncertainty of an intelligence warning whose reliability is unknown and whose consequences may be irreversible.

But the classroom can cultivate a habit that government often loses under pressure.

It can make room for the second question.

What if our first interpretation is wrong?

That question is not indecision.

It is one of diplomacy's oldest instruments.
