# The India Bargain

The most consequential negotiations often begin with an admission nobody wants to make publicly: the old policy no longer produces the future you want.

For three decades, nuclear policy had been one of the great impediments in relations between the United States and India. India had tested a nuclear device in 1974 and remained outside the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. The United States had helped construct and enforce a nonproliferation system whose restrictions fell heavily on New Delhi. India, for its part, had developed a strategic culture in which nuclear autonomy was tied to sovereignty, security, and the refusal to accept a permanently unequal global order.

Neither side had forgotten the argument.

By the middle of the 2000s, however, Washington was asking a different question. India was a democracy of more than a billion people, an increasingly important economy, a consequential Asian power, and a country with which the United States shared a growing range of interests. If the nuclear dispute remained the organizing principle of the relationship, the past would continue to veto the future.

President George W. Bush and Prime Minister Manmohan Singh announced the Civil Nuclear Cooperation Initiative in July 2005. The political idea was enormous. The implementation problem was worse.

Nicholas Burns became one of the central American negotiators charged with turning the idea into something that could survive law, politics, nonproliferation doctrine, Indian sovereignty, Congress, the International Atomic Energy Agency, the Nuclear Suppliers Group, and the accumulated suspicion of both countries.

A declaration between leaders can change direction. It cannot by itself change a system.

The system had to be negotiated.

The bargain asked India to separate civilian and military nuclear facilities and place designated civilian facilities under international safeguards. It included commitments touching export controls, testing, and nonproliferation practices. In return, the Bush administration undertook to seek changes in American law and international policy that would permit civil nuclear cooperation with India.

Each sentence contained a constituency capable of killing the agreement.

For American nonproliferation specialists, the danger was obvious: India had developed nuclear weapons outside the NPT. Making an exception could look like rewarding refusal to join the treaty and weakening rules Washington expected others to obey. For Indian critics, the danger ran in the opposite direction: cooperation with the United States might become a mechanism for constraining India's strategic independence, separating its nuclear program on terms Washington dictated, or creating future leverage over Indian decisions.

The agreement therefore had to do something diplomacy is often required to do and rarely praised for doing. It had to allow two governments to enter the same structure for different reasons without pretending those reasons were identical.

Washington wanted a strategic opening with India and argued that bringing more of India's civilian nuclear infrastructure under safeguards improved the nonproliferation environment relative to the status quo. New Delhi wanted recognition, access to civil nuclear commerce, energy opportunities, and an end to a form of nuclear isolation it regarded as discriminatory.

The overlap was real. So were the differences.

Burns's task was not to erase them. It was to find the boundary inside which they could coexist.

This required a kind of diplomacy that is difficult to narrate because its drama is cumulative. There was no single room in which the entire bargain was made. There were trips to New Delhi, meetings with Indian officials, consultations in Washington, congressional conversations, technical negotiations, draft language, disagreements that seemed capable of stopping the process, and repeated attempts to convert political intent into legal text.

In March 2006, during President Bush's visit to India, the two governments announced a major step on the separation plan. Burns publicly described the agreement as a way to remove what he called a basic irritant of roughly thirty years in the bilateral relationship. The phrase was almost comically modest for the amount of political weight being moved.

But the understatement captured something important.

Long diplomatic estrangements are often sustained not only by incompatible interests but by institutional habit. A restriction imposed for a reason becomes a policy. The policy becomes a constituency. The constituency develops arguments for preserving it. Years pass. Eventually officials inherit the structure without having participated in the event that created it.

Breaking such a structure requires more than announcing that circumstances have changed. It requires making change administratively possible.

That is where the 123 Agreement came in.

Under Section 123 of the Atomic Energy Act, peaceful nuclear cooperation required a bilateral framework. The negotiations over that framework became a test of whether the political breakthrough could survive precision. Nuclear agreements are not inspirational documents. Their nouns matter. Their verbs matter. Their definitions matter. Fuel supply, safeguards, termination, reprocessing, testing, consent rights: each issue can carry strategic consequences far beyond the sentence in which it appears.

This was diplomacy at its least glamorous and most consequential.

A word that looks technical to the public may determine whether a government can defend the agreement before parliament or Congress. A phrase inserted for legal reassurance can be read by the other side as a future trap. Ambiguity can be constructive until it becomes dangerous. Precision can be reassuring until it makes agreement impossible.

Burns and the American team worked opposite Indian officials who were operating under the same pressures in reverse. Foreign Secretary Shivshankar Menon became one of Burns's principal counterparts. Above them stood political leaders who had invested prestige in the initiative. Around them stood bureaucracies trained by decades of disagreement.

This is where relationships between negotiators matter without becoming mystical.

Personal trust cannot repeal a statute. It cannot erase national interest. It cannot authorize a concession a government has forbidden. What it can do is reduce uncertainty about process. A negotiator who believes his counterpart will accurately convey a position, warn when a proposal is politically impossible, protect a confidence, and distinguish a tactical maneuver from a final line can negotiate closer to the edge without constantly assuming deception.

That margin can be the difference between a deadlock and another draft.

By May 2007, Burns was again in New Delhi for talks on the 123 Agreement. The official description was careful: productive meetings, some progress, more work remaining. Such language is familiar enough to disappear when read. In negotiations it can mean the difference between a process that is alive and one that is being prepared for burial.

The remaining differences were serious.

Yet in July 2007 the United States and India announced completion of negotiations on the bilateral 123 Agreement. Burns gave an on-the-record briefing that morning. He placed the accord in the larger history of the relationship and described the effort as part of breaking through decades of separation.

Completion of the bilateral text did not complete the diplomacy.

India still needed an IAEA safeguards agreement. The Nuclear Suppliers Group had to change international policy to permit nuclear commerce with India. The United States had to navigate Congress. Indian domestic politics remained volatile. Every additional institution created another point at which the bargain could fail.

This is the part of international agreements that photographs obscure. Sovereignty is distributed.

A president may want an agreement and still need legislators. Two governments may agree and still need an international organization. An international body may approve and still leave domestic law unchanged. Diplomacy in a system of institutions is not a handshake between two sovereigns. It is a chain of permissions.

The negotiator must keep the chain from breaking.

Critics of the deal did not disappear as the chain advanced. Nor should a serious account pretend they had no case. The central objection remained: if the nonproliferation system made special accommodation for India, what principle prevented other states from demanding exceptions? Did strategic enthusiasm for India outweigh the long-term value of consistent rules? Would the agreement strengthen safeguards in practice or weaken norms in precedent?

Supporters answered that the relevant comparison was not between the agreement and an imaginary world in which India joined the NPT as a non-nuclear-weapon state. India was already a nuclear-armed state outside the treaty. The question was whether a new arrangement could place more civilian facilities under safeguards, improve export-control behavior, and remove a structural barrier to a relationship Washington increasingly considered strategically important.

This was not a dispute between people who believed in rules and people who did not.

It was a dispute over which rule best served the system under conditions the rule had failed to prevent.

That is a harder argument.

By 2008, the process had moved through the IAEA and toward the Nuclear Suppliers Group and Congress. Burns had announced his departure from government earlier that year, but the initiative continued toward completion. The U.S. Congress approved the 123 Agreement in October. Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice and Indian External Affairs Minister Pranab Mukherjee signed it on October 10.

The signing belonged to many people: Bush and Singh, Rice and Mukherjee, legislators, Indian ministers and civil servants, American officials, diplomats in other governments, IAEA officials, negotiators on both sides. To isolate one man as the author would misunderstand how an agreement of this complexity comes into existence.

Burns's importance lies somewhere more interesting.

He had become one of the people a president and secretary of state could put into the middle of a strategically ambitious idea and ask to make it negotiable.

That is a distinct form of public service.

The India bargain also revealed the limits of the tidy moral categories applied to diplomacy from a distance. The agreement was neither pure principle nor pure expediency. It was a judgment that an inherited structure should be altered because the world around it had changed.

Such judgments are dangerous. They are also unavoidable.

Every international order is partly a collection of exceptions accumulated around rules. The statesman insists the exception is necessary. The lawyer asks what precedent it creates. The diplomat has to construct language that can survive both questions.

For Burns, India became proof that patient negotiation could alter a relationship larger than the transaction on the table.

It also created a contrast that would define the next problem.

With India, the United States was trying to loosen a nuclear barrier in order to build a strategic partnership.

With Iran, it was trying to tighten one before the barrier failed.
