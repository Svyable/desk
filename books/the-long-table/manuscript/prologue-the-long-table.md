# Prologue — The Long Table

Diplomacy is photographed at the end.

The flags are already in place. The communique has been cleaned of its brackets. The principals know where to stand. Hands meet over a table whose shape has been considered by people whose names will not appear in the caption.

The photograph is evidence that something happened. It is poor evidence of how.

A diplomatic career is built in the part outside the frame.

It is built in briefing books read too late at night, in the distinction between what another government says for the record and what it is trying to make you understand, in the cable whose adjective matters, in the ally who must be called before the announcement, in the official who remembers why a sentence was written that way fifteen years ago. It is built in airports, secure rooms, residence dinners, motorcades, hotel corridors, windowless operations centers, and the recurring humiliation of discovering that the other side has a domestic politics too.

Nicholas Burns spent a career in those places.

He entered American diplomacy near the end of one strategic age and eventually returned to government as ambassador to China in another. Between those points were Cairo and Jerusalem; the State Department Operations Center; Soviet and Russian affairs at the National Security Council as the Soviet Union disappeared; the State Department podium; Athens; NATO during the years after September 11; the third-ranking position at State; negotiations involving India, Iran, Israel, Afghanistan and others; a long interval teaching diplomacy; and then Beijing.

The itinerary is impressive. The more interesting thing is what repeated exposure to those rooms does to a person.

The United States likes the language of beginnings. New administration. New doctrine. New era. New strategy. Every four or eight years Washington fills with people explaining what has changed.

Diplomacy is one of the professions that has to remember what has not.

Countries retain grievances longer than administrations retain appointees. Allies remember assurances. Adversaries remember humiliations. A phrase used casually in Washington can arrive in another capital carrying thirty years of history. Governments have institutional memories even when voters reasonably demand political change.

The career diplomat stands awkwardly inside that tension.

He is not elected. He should not make policy in place of elected officials. Yet elected officials depend on him to know what happened before they arrived, what the other government is likely to hear rather than what Washington intends to say, and which apparent opportunities have been tried under three previous names.

This makes the profession easy to caricature. Caution can look like timidity. Memory can look like resistance. Protocol can look like vanity. Negotiation can look like concession. Expertise can become its own form of blindness. Institutions can protect wisdom and also protect habits long after their usefulness has expired.

A serious biography of a diplomat therefore cannot be a sequence of rooms in which the protagonist was retrospectively correct.

The craft is more interesting than that.

Diplomacy is practiced under uncertainty. The people in the room do not know which dinner will matter, which cable will survive, which alliance will be tested, which leader will fall, which war will begin, or which apparently permanent order is already ending. They work forward while biography works backward.

Burns's career offers an unusual way to restore that uncertainty because it crossed so many changes in the American position.

He began as the United States was still living inside the Cold War. He worked Soviet affairs while the Soviet state came apart. He represented the country in Greece during a difficult period in a difficult alliance. He represented the United States at NATO when the alliance invoked Article 5 after the September 11 attacks and then struggled with the consequences of a war far from the territory for which NATO had been designed. As Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs, he worked negotiations in a moment when American primacy still seemed unusually deep. Years later he went to Beijing when the central question was no longer how to integrate China into an American-led order, but how two powerful, distrustful states might compete without making catastrophe more likely.

Those are different strategic worlds.

The profession connecting them is the story.

There is an American idea hidden inside that continuity. It is less glamorous than the idea of a brilliant statesman bending history through force of personality. A republic can build institutions in which people learn the world slowly and then lend that accumulated judgment to presidents they did not choose.

That arrangement requires discipline on both sides.

The diplomat must accept democratic authority. The political leader must understand the value of expertise that is not personal loyalty. Neither condition is automatic.

The American Dream is usually told as ascent: arrive, strive, build, succeed. Public service offers a stranger version. The achievement is partly submission to an institution larger than the individual. The diplomat's name may disappear from the policy he helped make. The administration changes. The flag does not.

That is why the long table matters.

A diplomat sits at one end for a few years. Across from him sits another representative carrying another country's memories, constraints, ambitions, vanities and fears. Behind each person are governments full of people not in the room. Beyond them are publics who may never know the conversation occurred.

The table is where abstractions acquire human voices.

National interest. Alliance. Deterrence. Sovereignty. Credibility. Peace.

Someone eventually has to say the words to someone else.

And then listen to the answer.
