# Political Affairs

There are jobs in diplomacy that give an officer a country. There are jobs that give an officer an alliance. The office Nicholas Burns entered in 2005 gave him neither. It gave him the argument.

As Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs, Burns became the department's senior career diplomat and one of the people expected to move across the seams of American foreign policy: Europe and Russia, Iran and the Middle East, India and the changing balance of Asia, the United Nations, sanctions, alliances, crises that did not respect the State Department's organizational chart. The title was bureaucratic. The work was not.

He had spent much of his career learning how governments speak. Political Affairs required another skill: deciding which conversations had to be connected.

This is one of the least cinematic truths about diplomacy. The world presents itself as separate problems. Iran is an Iran problem. India is an India problem. NATO is a European problem. Russia is a Russian problem. Congress is domestic politics. The IAEA is technical. The United Nations is multilateral. A working diplomat quickly discovers that the categories are administrative conveniences. The actual system is entangled.

A concession made to one government can become a precedent feared by another. A nuclear arrangement negotiated in South Asia can alter arguments about nonproliferation in the Middle East. An agreement reached abroad can die in Congress. An alliance position that looks coherent in a communiqué can conceal several different national calculations. The senior diplomat's job is therefore not merely to negotiate across the table. It is to keep multiple tables from contradicting one another.

Burns arrived at Political Affairs with an unusual preparation for that problem. He had worked in small posts and large ones, in the Middle East, at the National Security Council, behind the State Department lectern, as ambassador in Athens, and at NATO. He had seen American policy as reporting, as presidential decision, as public language, as bilateral representation, and as alliance management.

Now those forms converged.

The United States in 2005 possessed enormous power and unusually little strategic quiet. The wars in Afghanistan and Iraq had altered Washington's relationships abroad. Iran's nuclear program was becoming a central international dispute. Russia's political direction was producing new friction with the West. China and India were rising on different trajectories. The European alliance Burns had just represented was still absorbing new members and adapting to missions far beyond the geography for which it had been created.

Political Affairs sat where these pressures became choices.

The office also exposed a distinction that runs through Burns's career: the difference between policy and diplomacy. Policy can be stated as an objective. Diplomacy begins when another sovereign government is required for the objective to become real.

That second part is where clean sentences become difficult.

Washington could decide that Iran should not acquire a nuclear weapon. It could not, by decision alone, create a common position among European allies, Russia, China, the IAEA, the United Nations Security Council, and Iran itself. Washington could decide that a transformed relationship with India served American interests. It could not erase three decades of nuclear estrangement by announcement. Congress had laws. India had domestic politics and strategic traditions. Other governments had nonproliferation concerns. International institutions had rules. Each actor possessed a veto over some part of the desired outcome.

Diplomacy was the work of assembling enough consent that the policy could survive contact with sovereignty.

This made Burns's position a kind of switching station. He traveled, briefed, negotiated, testified, returned to Washington, and left again. The visible meetings were only the surface. Beneath them ran cables, legal language, interagency disputes, congressional consultations, allied anxieties, draft texts, red lines, and the accumulated memory of what had already been promised.

The temptation in writing about such work is to turn the diplomat into the author of everything that happens around him. That would be false. Condoleezza Rice was Secretary of State. George W. Bush was President. National security policy was made through a large administration containing powerful and frequently competing institutions. Foreign governments had their own leaders and negotiators. Congress was not a ratifying machine. History was not waiting for one official to move it.

The more revealing measure of Burns's role is narrower and more demanding: when an administration chose a difficult diplomatic objective, could he help construct the path by which other governments and institutions might say yes?

India became the largest test.

The proposition looked, at first glance, almost self-contradictory. The United States had spent decades helping build a global nuclear order around rules that India had never fully joined. India possessed nuclear weapons outside the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. Yet Washington increasingly saw India not as a problem to be isolated but as a democratic power whose rise could serve a broader American interest.

The inherited policy and the emerging strategy no longer fit together.

That mismatch is where diplomacy often begins.

It is easy, after a major agreement has been signed, to describe it as inevitable. The photographs encourage this. Leaders stand behind polished tables. Flags alternate in exact symmetry. Pens are placed at the correct angle. A negotiated outcome acquires the visual grammar of something that had always been heading toward completion.

But before the photograph there is usually a period when the idea appears politically impossible, legally awkward, strategically disputed, or all three.

The civil nuclear initiative with India was all three.

It asked the United States to distinguish between defending a nonproliferation system and mechanically preserving every policy inherited from that system. It asked India to accept safeguards and commitments that touched questions of sovereignty and national pride. It asked Congress to alter American law. It eventually required action through the International Atomic Energy Agency and the Nuclear Suppliers Group. It demanded that two democracies with long memories of mistrust persuade themselves that an exception could strengthen a relationship without simply dissolving the rule.

For a diplomat, this is a more interesting problem than agreement versus disagreement. It is architecture.

Which commitment has to come first? Which government must move before another government can move? Which language gives each side enough political space to claim that it protected the principle it could not abandon? Which objections are substantive, which are procedural, and which are really requests for reassurance? What can the executive branch promise? What requires Congress? What requires an international body? What must be left deliberately unresolved until the next stage?

The senior diplomat lives inside sequencing.

Burns had encountered versions of this problem at NATO, where thirty minutes of ceremony could rest on months of allied bargaining. Political Affairs expanded the scale. An agreement with India would require not only bilateral negotiation but the management of an international system.

That made patience a strategic resource.

American political culture often treats patience as the absence of action. Diplomatic practice suggests almost the opposite. To remain in a negotiation through repeated failure, to preserve a channel without surrendering a position, to keep congressional and allied constituencies informed, to know when a near-agreement is worse than another month of argument—these are active judgments.

They also rarely produce a dramatic public record.

Burns's own speeches from the period reveal how the administration wanted the India initiative understood. It was not presented merely as a nuclear transaction. The larger claim was strategic: the United States and India were attempting to remove a central obstacle that had constrained their relationship for decades. The nuclear issue mattered partly because it had become a symbol and mechanism of estrangement.

That framing did not settle the nonproliferation objections. It made the argument explicit.

A diplomat does not get to choose between principle and compromise as abstract nouns. He gets a text. A counterpart. A legal constraint. A political deadline. A president who wants an outcome. Legislators who may reject it. Allies who need reassurance. Critics who may be right about some of the risks. The craft lies in discovering whether a structure exists in which the competing truths can coexist long enough to become policy.

Political Affairs was where Burns began operating at that level every day.

There was another change, less visible but equally important. The young officer in Mauritania had been paid to notice. The political officer in Jerusalem had been paid to interpret. The NSC staffer had been paid to compress. The spokesman had been paid to state. The ambassador had been paid to represent. At NATO he had been paid to assemble.

Now he was being paid to negotiate consequences.

The distinction matters because negotiation creates ownership. An analyst can explain why a policy failed. A negotiator may have his fingerprints on the failure. Advice can be caveated. A negotiated text eventually has to be defended as a choice.

This is one reason senior diplomacy changes the people who practice it. The distance between judgment and consequence narrows.

By 2005, Burns had been in government long enough to know that no agreement survives exactly as its architects imagine. Governments change. Legislatures reinterpret. Crises intrude. Counterparts leave office. Strategic conditions move. The diplomat is not designing permanence. He is trying to create an arrangement strong enough to remain useful after the meeting ends.

The India initiative would demand that kind of construction.

So would Iran, in a very different register.

One negotiation sought to bring a rising power closer to the United States by changing inherited nuclear rules. The other sought to prevent a hostile government from moving closer to a nuclear weapon by defending and enforcing nuclear constraints. Taken together, they exposed the central difficulty of American statecraft in the period: rules mattered, but strategy required judgments about how rules should apply to different states under different conditions.

There was no formula that could make those judgments for the people in the room.

That is the part of diplomacy bureaucratic titles conceal.

The Under Secretary for Political Affairs did not command an army or control a treasury. Burns's principal instruments were access, argument, credibility, text, coalition, institutional memory, and the authority temporarily delegated to him by elected leaders.

They sound weak beside aircraft carriers.

They are weak when badly used.

Used well, they can change what the aircraft carriers are needed for.

The next several years would test whether that proposition still held.
