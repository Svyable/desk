# Iran

Diplomacy becomes most difficult when talking itself is treated as a concession.

The United States and Iran entered the middle of the 2000s carrying more than a quarter-century of accumulated grievance: revolution, hostages, sanctions, regional conflict, mutual suspicion, and political systems that had learned to use the other as evidence about the world. Iran's nuclear program placed that history inside a technical dispute with potentially catastrophic consequences.

For Nicholas Burns, the problem arrived while he was Under Secretary for Political Affairs. It was almost the inverse of India.

With India, Washington was asking whether an inherited nuclear restriction should be modified to make room for a strategic relationship. With Iran, Washington and its European partners were asking how international restrictions could be made credible enough to change the behavior of a government they feared might acquire the capacity to produce a nuclear weapon.

The common instrument was diplomacy.

The political psychology was completely different.

Iran had negotiated with Britain, France, and Germany over its nuclear activities. The International Atomic Energy Agency occupied a central technical and legal role. Russia and China mattered because any durable sanctions strategy through the United Nations Security Council required their participation. Israel and Arab states watched the program through the lens of their own security. Washington possessed enormous leverage but carried the burden of a relationship in which direct official contact itself was politically charged.

A diplomat looking at this landscape did not see one negotiation.

He saw several negotiations stacked on top of one another.

There was a negotiation among the United States and European allies over common policy. There was a negotiation with Russia and China over what the Security Council could support. There was the IAEA process. There was the indirect contest with Iran. There was an internal American argument over incentives, sanctions, pressure, and the conditions under which the United States should participate directly in talks.

The first task was coalition.

This can sound secondary. It was not. Pressure on Iran would mean little if the principal powers disagreed about the objective or if Tehran could move from one capital to another seeking a softer position. A sanctions regime required enough agreement among states with different commercial interests, strategic priorities, and relationships with Iran that the restrictions would be more than an American declaration.

Coalition diplomacy is the conversion of national concern into shared cost.

Every government may agree that a problem exists. The harder question is what each will give up to address it.

For European states, Iran was geographically closer and economically connected. Russia had its own strategic and commercial relationship with Tehran. China had energy interests and a broader resistance to some forms of Western pressure. The United States brought power but also the baggage of Iraq and a history of hostility with Iran. Agreement could not be assumed simply because Washington considered the stakes high.

Burns worked this problem in the language he had learned at NATO: consultation before expectation.

The principle was straightforward. If another government is expected to bear the cost of a policy, it must have some ownership of the policy's design.

That meant the United States could not treat allies and other major powers merely as delivery mechanisms for an American position. The package had to become genuinely multilateral—an arrangement in which incentives, demands, and consequences could be defended in several capitals at once.

In 2006, the United States joined Britain, France, Germany, Russia, and China in a framework that paired an offer of negotiations and incentives with the prospect of increasing pressure if Iran refused to suspend sensitive nuclear activities. The architecture mattered. Diplomacy and coercion were not presented as opposites. They were designed to reinforce one another.

The word "pressure" often produces a false picture of statecraft, as if sanctions mechanically squeeze a government until it changes policy. In practice, economic restrictions are political messages carried through financial and commercial systems. Their effectiveness depends on participation, enforcement, time, domestic resilience, and the target government's calculation of what relief would be worth.

Sanctions without a credible diplomatic exit can become punishment without leverage.

Diplomacy without consequences can become conversation without incentive.

The difficult work lies between them.

Burns's public statements during these years repeatedly framed the objective around preventing Iran from acquiring a nuclear weapons capability while leaving a path open to negotiation if Tehran met specified conditions. That position contained an unresolved American debate. Should the United States speak directly to Iran before suspension? Would direct talks reward defiance? Could Washington discover Iran's actual bargaining position without sitting across from Iranian officials? Did refusing contact strengthen pressure or merely reduce information?

These were not semantic questions.

They went to the heart of what diplomacy is for.

Talking to an adversary is often described in domestic politics as a gesture of approval. Professional diplomacy begins from a colder premise: communication is an instrument. Governments speak to adversaries because adversaries possess information, power, and choices that matter. A meeting can fail. It can be exploited. It can provide propaganda. It can also clarify intent, test a proposition, expose internal divisions, transmit a threat accurately, or discover that a supposedly impossible compromise has a shape.

The diplomatic question is not whether talking is virtuous.

It is whether the information and leverage gained by contact exceed the political and strategic costs of the contact.

During Burns's tenure, the Bush administration remained cautious about direct participation. The United States supported the multilateral framework and increased pressure through the Security Council. The diplomacy produced several UN sanctions resolutions. It did not produce a final nuclear settlement.

That outcome matters because biographies tend to overvalue agreements. A diplomat's career contains many negotiations that do not conclude while he is in the room.

The absence of a signed settlement does not mean nothing was built.

Coalitions persist. Sanctions architectures persist. Positions evolve. Channels opened in one administration can become the starting point of another. The international framework constructed around Iran during the Bush years would later be inherited, modified, intensified, and used by officials negotiating the agreement that became the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action in 2015.

Burns was not the negotiator of that later accord. To imply otherwise would collapse distinct administrations and diplomatic phases into a single heroic narrative.

His chapter belongs earlier, in the construction of the multilateral problem.

This is an important difference.

The diplomat who signs an agreement is visible. The diplomats who spend years persuading other governments that there should be a common negotiating position are often not. Yet without the latter, the former may have no table at which to sit.

Iran also sharpened another lesson Burns had encountered repeatedly: intelligence does not remove uncertainty from policy.

Nuclear programs generate data—centrifuges, enrichment levels, facilities, inspections, procurement networks. But policy decisions require judgments about intention, threshold, time, risk tolerance, domestic politics, and the behavior of other states. Technical information can narrow uncertainty. It cannot answer the political question of what a government will do next.

This is why verification matters so much in nuclear diplomacy.

Trust is too weak a foundation for agreements between adversaries. The objective is not to produce faith. It is to construct arrangements in which important claims can be checked and violations can carry consequences.

That distinction—between trusting a counterpart and building a system that does not require trust—sits near the center of modern diplomacy.

Burns had spent his career learning the value of personal credibility. Iran demonstrated its limit.

No relationship between negotiators could substitute for inspectors. No eloquence could replace monitoring. No handshake could answer a technical question about nuclear material. Human trust might keep a conversation alive, but institutional verification had to carry the agreement if one was ever reached.

The same diplomat therefore had to believe in both things at once: relationships matter, and relationships are not enough.

By the time Burns prepared to leave government in 2008, Iran remained unresolved. The United States and its partners had created a multilateral structure of incentives, demands, and sanctions. Tehran had not accepted the central terms Washington sought. The danger had been managed, not eliminated.

There is a tendency in political memoir to sort such episodes into victories and defeats. Diplomacy resists the sorting.

Sometimes the achievement is an agreement.

Sometimes it is preventing the coalition from collapsing.

Sometimes it is buying time.

Sometimes it is discovering that the other side will not yet move.

Sometimes it is leaving your successor a stronger position than the one you inherited.

Those are unsatisfying categories for a culture that wants outcomes on election-night schedules. International politics is rarely so punctual.

Burns left the Foreign Service after twenty-seven years with the India initiative approaching its final institutional approvals and the Iran nuclear dispute still open. One relationship had been transformed. Another remained trapped between pressure and negotiation.

Together they formed an argument about the profession he had practiced since Mauritania.

Diplomacy is not the art of being agreeable.

It is the disciplined management of disagreement when the alternative to managing it is worse.
