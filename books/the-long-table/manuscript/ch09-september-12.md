# September 12

Nicholas Burns had been the American ambassador to NATO for barely a month when the United States was attacked.

The date that entered history was September 11, 2001. For an alliance, the institutional date was also September 12.

That was when the North Atlantic Council invoked Article 5 of the Washington Treaty for the first time in NATO’s history.

The clause had been written for collective defense in a Europe shadowed by Soviet power. Generations of diplomats and military planners had imagined the circumstances under which it might be used. The expected geography was Europe. The expected attacker was a state. The expected direction of rescue ran westward across the Atlantic: America coming to Europe.

History reversed the picture.

The attack was against the United States.

The allies came to America.

For Burns, the moment joined nearly every strand of his professional education. He had studied Europe. He had worked the Soviet collapse. He had represented the United States in Greece. He had watched NATO adapt after the Cold War. Now the alliance’s central promise was being tested in circumstances its founders had not designed it to meet.

The first requirement was not eloquence.

It was classification.

What had happened? Who had done it? Did an attack by a terrorist network qualify as an armed attack for purposes of collective defense? Had it been directed from abroad? What did solidarity require in practical terms? What should the alliance promise before the facts were sufficiently established?

These questions sound legalistic only from a distance.

In a crisis, classification is action.

Call something an accident and one machinery begins. Call it terrorism and another begins. Call it an armed attack covered by a collective-defense treaty and obligations, expectations, military planning, domestic politics, and adversary perceptions change at once.

NATO’s response was therefore both emotional and procedural.

An alliance is made of people, and people had watched the towers burn. The shock was immediate. So was the solidarity. Contemporary accounts from NATO describe American personnel leaving headquarters amid uncertainty about whether Europe itself might be under threat. Allied representatives were already thinking about Article 5 on September 11. The North Atlantic Council acted the next day, conditionally invoking the collective-defense principle while awaiting confirmation that the attacks had been directed from abroad.

That confirmation came in early October after the United States briefed the Council on the investigation linking the attacks to al-Qaeda operating from Afghanistan under Taliban protection.

The distinction mattered.

NATO had not simply reacted. It had created a chain from evidence to judgment to treaty consequence.

That chain is diplomacy.

It is easy, particularly after the fact, to describe alliances as collections of commitments. The more interesting truth is that commitments require interpretation. Treaties do not read themselves. Governments do. Every historic clause eventually meets a circumstance its authors did not fully anticipate.

Then people in rooms have to decide what the words mean now.

Burns occupied the American seat in one of those rooms.

His role should not be inflated. Article 5 was not his personal achievement. The decision belonged to the nineteen allies acting through NATO’s institutions, under Secretary General George Robertson and with governments across the alliance making their own sovereign judgments. The historical record is more impressive when left collective.

But the American ambassador had a particular burden.

He represented the country that had been attacked.

That position can produce two opposite diplomatic errors. One is entitlement: assuming allies owe support and treating questions as evidence of disloyalty. The other is false modesty: becoming so careful not to impose that the country fails to say clearly what it needs.

Alliance diplomacy lives between them.

The United States had to accept solidarity without converting solidarity into obedience.

It also had to determine what assistance was useful.

In the months that followed, NATO allies and NATO assets supported the United States in concrete ways: intelligence sharing, overflight rights, access to bases, maritime operations, and the deployment of NATO AWACS aircraft to patrol American skies. The alliance’s response was not identical to a single NATO war plan. It was a mixture of collective decisions, allied national contributions, and broader coalitions.

That complexity led some observers to dismiss Article 5 as symbolic.

The criticism misunderstood symbols.

A symbol in international politics can alter behavior if governments believe it carries commitments behind it. Article 5 did not need to turn every military action after September 11 into a NATO operation to matter. Its invocation told adversaries, publics, markets, militaries, and allied governments that the attack had crossed a boundary larger than the United States.

It also told Americans something about the architecture they had spent half a century building.

The alliance was not a warehouse from which Washington withdrew military contributions when convenient. It was stored political capital.

For Burns, this was a vindication of a professional idea that had run through his career: relationships built before a crisis determine what is possible inside one.

Diplomats are often asked to prove their value at precisely the wrong moment. A successful negotiation produces a document people can point to. A successful military operation produces visible movement. Much diplomatic work produces neither. It creates familiarity, procedural trust, habits of consultation, secure channels, personal credibility, and institutional memory.

For years these can look like overhead.

Then a morning comes when they become infrastructure.

September 11 was such a morning.

September 12 revealed the infrastructure.

There was another lesson, less comforting.

Solidarity at the beginning of a crisis does not settle the politics that follow it.

The attacks created extraordinary unity, but unity is not a permanent political condition. Governments would soon disagree over strategy, threat, Iraq, the use of force, and the relationship between American power and allied consent. The same NATO table that demonstrated solidarity would become a place where the United States encountered limits.

That did not mean the alliance had failed.

It meant the alliance was real.

A coalition in which no one can disagree is not necessarily strong. It may simply be subordinate. NATO’s difficulty was that its members were democracies with different electorates, geographies, histories, military capacities, and threat perceptions. Collective action required more than announcing leadership. It required building consent among governments that retained the ability to say no.

Burns had learned the basic version of this in Athens. He now faced it at continental scale.

The ambassador to NATO does not represent the United States to one foreign government. He represents it inside a permanent negotiation among allies. The table itself is the posting.

Every seat has instructions from a capital. Every capital has domestic politics. Every military commitment has logistical and parliamentary consequences. Every phrase in a communiqué may have been fought over because language is one of the ways alliances distribute risk.

This is why diplomatic skill inside alliances is not ornamental to power.

It is one of the ways power is assembled.

After September 11, the United States possessed overwhelming military capability. What it did not possess was the sovereign authority to command other democracies. Their participation had to be requested, argued for, negotiated, and maintained.

The distinction would become increasingly important.

The post-Cold War period had encouraged a dangerous simplification in some American thinking: that unmatched capability reduced the need for diplomacy. The logic seemed plausible. If the United States could act, why spend so much time persuading?

September 12 suggested the opposite.

The more extensive America’s interests became, the more it depended on access, intelligence, legitimacy, bases, airspace, logistics, sanctions, financial cooperation, political cover, and allied military contributions that it could not simply manufacture at home.

Power created diplomatic demand.

It did not abolish it.

Burns’s NATO years would become a laboratory for that proposition.

The alliance had answered the first question after the attacks with unusual clarity: an attack on one could still be treated as an attack on all.

The harder question came next.

All for what?
