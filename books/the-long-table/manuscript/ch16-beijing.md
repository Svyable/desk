# Chapter 16 — Beijing

When Nicholas Burns returned to government, the country across the table was China.

The symmetry with the beginning of his career was imperfect but difficult to ignore. As a young officer he had entered government near the end of a long confrontation with the Soviet Union. Four decades later he was asked to represent the United States in a relationship increasingly described through the language of strategic competition.

History had not repeated itself.

That was precisely the problem.

China was not the Soviet Union. The economic relationship alone made the analogy unstable. American and Chinese companies, consumers, universities, supply chains, financial markets, and technologies had become entangled over decades. At the same time, military competition intensified in the Indo-Pacific, Washington strengthened alliances and partnerships, Beijing pressed its claims around Taiwan and in surrounding seas, and both governments increasingly treated technology as an arena of national security.

The United States needed an ambassador capable of representing competition without reducing the relationship to a slogan.

President Joe Biden nominated Burns in 2021. The Senate confirmed him, and he served as U.S. ambassador to the People's Republic of China from 2021 to 2025.

He arrived with an unusual credential for the post: not a career centered on China, but a career centered on diplomacy.

That distinction could be a liability. China is a civilization, state, party system, economy, and strategic actor that punishes superficial understanding. No general diplomatic skill substitutes for language, history, institutional knowledge, or regional expertise.

But an ambassador is not sent merely to know the host country.

An ambassador is sent to carry one government into a relationship with another.

That requires knowing Washington too.

Burns had spent decades learning the political and bureaucratic anatomy of American foreign policy. He knew the State Department, White House, Congress, alliances, public diplomacy, negotiation, and the complicated boundary between a president's policy and a career official's professional advice. In Beijing, those skills would be applied under conditions in which almost every major issue was contested.

The job was not to find a grand bargain.

The job was to keep competition from becoming incomprehension.

That sounds modest until one considers the alternatives.

Strategic rivalry produces incentives to interpret ambiguous actions pessimistically. A military exercise becomes rehearsal. An export control becomes containment. An alliance statement becomes encirclement. A diplomatic visit becomes provocation. A delay becomes refusal. A domestic political speech becomes official doctrine.

Some of those interpretations may be correct.

The danger is not pessimism itself. The danger is acting on an interpretation that has never been tested against the other government's actual intent.

Embassies exist partly for that test.

By the time Burns reached Beijing, the U.S.-China relationship contained several problems that could not be solved by bilateral diplomacy alone. Taiwan's security involved U.S. policy, Chinese claims, Taiwan's democracy, regional alliances, military posture, technology supply chains, and domestic politics on all sides. Trade disputes implicated national security. Technology controls implicated military modernization. Human-rights disputes were moral questions and diplomatic conflicts simultaneously.

The agenda did not converge.

It accumulated.

This changed the function of the ambassador.

In a relationship moving toward partnership, diplomacy searches for agreements.

In a relationship moving toward rivalry, diplomacy may have to search first for boundaries.

What will trigger a response? Which military activity is routine and which is escalatory? Which communication channel will remain open during a crisis? What does one government believe the other has misunderstood? Where is cooperation possible without pretending the strategic competition has disappeared?

These are questions of architecture rather than friendship.

Burns's earlier career had prepared him for parts of the problem. NATO taught alliance management. The Soviet collapse taught the danger of using old categories after the political reality changes. India taught that durable agreements require multiple institutions to move. Iran taught that diplomacy can be necessary even when the central disagreement remains.

China combined all of those lessons without reproducing any of those cases.

There was another complication.

An ambassador in Beijing operated inside a communication environment radically different from the one Burns had entered as a young officer.

Information was abundant. Government statements could be translated instantly. Satellite imagery circulated publicly. Commercial data illuminated trade and shipping. Social media accelerated nationalist reaction. Think tanks, intelligence agencies, journalists, companies, and open-source analysts produced continuous assessments.

Scarcity had moved.

The difficult commodity was no longer information about what had happened.

It was confidence about what the event meant.

This is where diplomacy becomes easy to underestimate. If every government can observe more, why maintain expensive embassies and long diplomatic conversations?

Because observation does not authenticate intention.

A satellite can show forces moving.

It cannot reliably explain why a political leader ordered the movement or what would cause the leader to reverse it.

An export-control regulation can be read online.

It cannot tell Beijing whether Washington considers the regulation the beginning of a broader campaign unless Washington communicates that policy with sufficient authority and clarity.

A speech can be translated perfectly and understood badly.

The ambassador's work therefore sits in the space between visibility and meaning.

Burns had spent a career in that space.

In Beijing, the margin for error was unusually small because the two governments possessed the power to impose enormous costs on one another and on the rest of the world. A breakdown in communication would not remain bilateral. Markets, allies, shipping routes, semiconductor supply chains, climate diplomacy, and global institutions could all feel the consequences.

This did not make diplomacy supreme.

Military deterrence mattered. Economic policy mattered. Technology policy mattered. Alliances mattered. Domestic political legitimacy mattered.

Diplomacy's role was narrower and indispensable: to connect those instruments to an intelligible political purpose.

Without that connection, power can communicate the wrong thing.

A deployment intended to reassure an ally can appear offensive to an adversary. A sanction intended to create bargaining leverage can be interpreted as evidence that bargaining is pointless. A public statement designed for domestic politics can close a private negotiating path.

None of these problems can be eliminated.

They can sometimes be identified before they become irreversible.

That is what the long table is for.

The table may be literal: delegations facing one another beneath flags, interpreters and note-takers recording phrases that will be examined later for movement.

Or it may be institutional: embassy to foreign ministry, military channel to military channel, president to president.

What matters is not furniture.

It is continuity.

The existence of a place where authoritative meaning can be tested.

Burns arrived in Beijing after decades spent inside different versions of that place.

Now the question was whether it could remain open between the two most consequential powers of the age.
