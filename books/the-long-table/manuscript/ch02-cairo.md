# Cairo

The first permanent posting in a diplomatic career is an education in scale.

Washington trains people in subjects. A capital teaches them simultaneity. A young officer can begin the morning with a visa problem, move into an ambassadorial staff meeting, read reporting about a government whose public language differs from its private calculations, and end the day discovering that the most consequential fact was something he had not known to ask about at breakfast.

Nicholas Burns arrived in Cairo in 1983 as a young Foreign Service officer. The official record places him there until 1985, serving as vice consul and staff assistant to the ambassador. The titles sound modest because they were modest. That is part of their importance.

A career in diplomacy is usually remembered backward. Once someone has sat across from ministers, briefed presidents, represented the United States at NATO, or carried an embassy in Beijing, the earlier jobs acquire the false glow of preparation. They begin to look like stations on a route that was already known.

It was not known.

Cairo was work.

The embassy did not exist to educate Nick Burns. It existed to represent the United States in one of the most consequential countries in the Arab world. Egypt had made peace with Israel only a few years earlier. President Anwar Sadat had been assassinated in 1981. Hosni Mubarak inherited a country whose relationship with Washington had strategic weight far beyond the bilateral relationship itself. The United States was deeply engaged in the region, the Lebanese civil war was still burning, and the Cold War supplied its own layer of competition beneath local politics.

A junior officer entered that machinery near the bottom.

That location can be clarifying.

Consular work brings a state into contact with individual lives. Policy arrives in Washington as categories—migration, security, bilateral relations, assistance. At a consular window, categories become people with documents, omissions, stories, deadlines and motives that must be assessed under rules the officer did not write. The authority is real, but bounded. A visa decision can alter a person's immediate future. It does not permit the officer to improvise American law.

This is one of the first disciplines of public service: consequential judgment inside delegated authority.

The second half of Burns's Cairo assignment put him close to the ambassador's office as a staff assistant. The change in altitude did not eliminate the discipline. It changed what he could observe.

An embassy is an institution for converting a foreign country into information that a government can use without pretending the conversion is complete. Political officers report. Economic officers report. Defense attachés see another portion of the state. Intelligence officers operate under different authorities. USAID personnel may understand institutions and communities through years of programs. Local employees know things no rotating American officer can know. Washington sends instructions back. The ambassador has to decide what matters enough to elevate, what requires action, what requires patience, and what the capital is misunderstanding.

The young staff assistant sees the traffic.

He also sees hierarchy without the romance that later attaches to it. Senior officials need paper. Meetings need preparation. Visitors arrive with compressed schedules and expansive expectations. Cables have to be accurate. The ambassador cannot spend an hour rediscovering what someone in the building already knows. A functioning embassy therefore depends on a quality that rarely appears in speeches about foreign policy: organized memory.

Burns would spend much of his later career in positions where organized memory became strategic power. On the National Security Council, he would work on the Soviet Union as it ceased to exist and on the fifteen states that followed it. At NATO he would represent the United States as the alliance invoked Article 5 after September 11. As Under Secretary for Political Affairs he would negotiate across files whose histories could not be reconstructed each morning. In Beijing decades later, he would again operate in a relationship where words carried accumulated meanings and where forgetting the previous round could be expensive.

Cairo came before all of that.

The useful lesson is not that a staff assistant was secretly rehearsing to become an ambassador. It is that the profession makes senior judgment out of repeated exposure to other people's work.

You learn what a good cable does because you read cables that save the reader time and cables that waste it. You learn how an ambassador asks questions by hearing which questions cause a room to become more precise. You learn that a visitor from Washington may arrive with more rank than context. You learn that a local employee can possess less formal authority and more usable knowledge. You learn that the embassy's view of the country is necessarily partial and that confidence is not the same thing as accuracy.

These are small recognitions. Accumulated, they become craft.

There is another reason Cairo matters in Burns's story. It placed the young American officer in a region where American power was immense and insufficient at the same time.

The United States could supply assistance, weapons, diplomatic attention and political pressure. It could not erase history. It could not make Egyptian politics identical to American preferences. It could not cause the Israeli-Palestinian conflict to yield because an American administration wished it to. It could not reduce the Arab world to a contest between pro-American and anti-American governments without losing information necessary to act intelligently.

A diplomat learns the limits of power differently from a theorist. The theorist can describe constraint. The diplomat receives instructions on Tuesday and discovers on Wednesday which part of reality has declined to cooperate.

That does not make diplomacy cynical. Done well, it makes it empirical.

Burns had studied history at Boston College and international relations at Johns Hopkins SAIS. Cairo offered a harsher examination. The question was no longer whether he understood American foreign policy as a field of study. It was whether he could be useful to an institution practicing it.

Usefulness at that stage meant reliability. The memo has to be right. The schedule has to work. The name has to be spelled correctly. The official has to know what happened in the previous meeting. The officer has to distinguish what he observed from what he inferred. If he does not know, he has to know that he does not know.

This can sound pedestrian beside summitry. It is the foundation of summitry.

Diplomacy is unusually vulnerable to the mythology of the room: the belief that history changes because the right person says the right sentence at the right table. Sometimes a sentence matters enormously. But the sentence usually rests on an invisible architecture of preparation. Someone established the facts. Someone reconstructed the other side's position. Someone noticed a change in wording. Someone knew which concession had already been offered and withdrawn. Someone understood the domestic constraint behind the foreign minister's public posture. Someone prevented the principal from walking into the room with an obsolete assumption.

The room is the visible tip of an institution.

Cairo taught the institution.

It also placed Burns inside a diplomatic culture in which careers were expected to move between regions, functions and levels of responsibility. The Foreign Service wager was that breadth did not have to mean superficiality if each assignment trained a different faculty. Consular work trained judgment under rules. Staff work trained institutional awareness. Political reporting would train interpretation. Washington would train coordination. Senior office would eventually train decision.

The wager can fail. Bureaucracies can reward caution until caution becomes passivity. Rotation can produce officials who know a little about everywhere and not enough about anywhere. Hierarchy can teach deference instead of judgment. Institutional memory can harden into institutional prejudice.

But the alternative—a foreign policy apparatus composed only of political principals and outside experts—has its own weakness. Every administration would begin closer to zero.

The career service exists partly so the republic does not have to forget the world every four years.

Burns would eventually serve presidents of both parties. That fact is often presented as a compliment to his bipartisanship. More interesting is what it says about the office. A career diplomat serves an elected government without becoming its personal property. The administration has the democratic authority to set policy. The officer has a professional obligation to execute lawful policy, offer candid advice through proper channels, and preserve the capacity of the institution for whoever is elected next.

That bargain is easier to praise in the abstract than to live through consequential disagreements. Burns's career would eventually encounter them. Cairo was earlier, when the obligation was being learned in ordinary forms.

The young officer did not need to agree with every feature of American policy to understand whose policy it was.

He needed to make it better informed.

After Cairo came Jerusalem.

The move was geographically short and professionally decisive. Burns would serve as a political officer at the American Consulate General and work on U.S. economic assistance to Palestinians in the West Bank and East Jerusalem. He would move from watching the machinery of an embassy to reporting from inside one of the world's most contested political landscapes.

Cairo had taught him that diplomacy is an institution.

Jerusalem would teach him that almost every important noun in diplomacy has more than one meaning.
