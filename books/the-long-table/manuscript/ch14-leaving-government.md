# Chapter 14 — Leaving Government

A diplomatic career is built around departures.

The junior officer leaves one post for another. The ambassador leaves a residence that was never really his. Administrations change. Secretaries leave. Governments fall. Negotiations end badly, or well, or merely stop. The profession teaches attachment to work alongside detachment from position.

Nicholas Burns had spent most of his adult life inside that rhythm by the time he announced that he would leave the State Department in 2008.

The official biography is neat about it. Twenty-seven years in government. Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs from 2005 to 2008. Ambassador to NATO before that. Ambassador to Greece. State Department spokesman. National Security Council. Jerusalem. Cairo. The Secretariat. The list has the polished quality of a career already being converted into institutional memory.

But departure from government is not merely subtraction. For a career diplomat, it creates an unusual problem: what happens to accumulated statecraft when the state no longer owns the calendar?

Burns had worked for Republican and Democratic administrations. That fact mattered. The American Foreign Service rests on a proposition that is easy to praise and difficult to practice: expertise can be politically consequential without becoming partisan property. Career officials serve elected presidents whose policies they may help design, defend, negotiate, or implement. Then another president arrives.

The bargain is not political neutrality in the sense of having no judgment. Diplomats are paid to have judgment. It is professional subordination of that judgment to constitutional authority, combined with an obligation to make the judgment useful before the decision is made.

Burns's career had repeatedly placed him near the seam.

He had worked Soviet affairs during the first Bush administration and Russia, Ukraine, and Eurasia under President Clinton. He had spoken for Warren Christopher and Madeleine Albright. He had represented George W. Bush at NATO and then served as the State Department's senior career diplomat for political affairs under Condoleezza Rice.

The policies changed. The obligation to understand the foreign government across the table did not.

That distinction helps explain why leaving government could become another form of service rather than a repudiation of it.

Burns moved to Harvard.

The transition sounds larger than it was in one respect and larger still in another. He exchanged an institution of action for an institution of argument. He no longer carried instructions from a president or secretary of state. He could criticize policy publicly. He could say what government service had taught him without first clearing the language through a bureaucracy.

But the underlying material remained recognizable: alliances, negotiation, Russia, Europe, the Middle East, India, China, American power, and the problem of making judgment under conditions of incomplete information.

There is a temptation to imagine government and the university as opposite worlds. In diplomacy, they can be consecutive rooms.

A government meeting asks what must be done by Friday. A seminar can ask whether the premise behind Friday's decision is wrong.

Government compresses time. Teaching restores it.

The difference matters because diplomatic institutions are unusually vulnerable to the loss of tacit knowledge. Cables preserve facts. Memoranda preserve options. Treaties preserve bargains. None of them fully preserve the craft by which a negotiator recognizes that a counterpart has moved, that an ally needs political cover, that a seemingly procedural objection is substantive, or that a beautifully drafted agreement cannot survive the politics required to ratify it.

Those are not mystical skills. They are patterns learned through repeated exposure to consequences.

The experienced diplomat has seen language fail.

He has watched governments misunderstand one another while speaking through professional interpreters. He has watched a capital demand certainty from an embassy that cannot honestly provide it. He has seen negotiations in which the decisive obstacle is not the issue printed on the agenda but the coalition waiting behind one of the negotiators. He has learned that a foreign minister who agrees privately may still be unable to agree publicly.

The classroom offers a place to make those hidden mechanics explicit.

Burns joined Harvard Kennedy School and eventually directed the Future of Diplomacy Project. The name carried a useful tension. Diplomacy is one of the oldest instruments of organized political life, yet every generation believes it has invented conditions that make the old practice obsolete.

The telegraph was supposed to change diplomacy. So were the telephone, radio, television, jet travel, satellite communications, twenty-four-hour news, social media, ubiquitous surveillance, cyber operations, and artificial intelligence.

They did change it.

They did not remove the need for it.

What changed most dramatically was the amount of time between event and expected response. The diplomat who once waited weeks for instructions eventually waited hours, then minutes. By the time Burns entered academic life, presidents and foreign ministers could communicate directly, intelligence could arrive continuously, and a political crisis could become global before an embassy had finished its first reporting cable.

That compression creates an odd possibility: the more technologically immediate international politics becomes, the more valuable practiced human judgment may become.

Information is not the same as interpretation.

Access is not the same as trust.

Contact is not the same as diplomacy.

Burns's move to Harvard gave him room to teach that distinction. It also placed him among a generation of students for whom the Cold War was history rather than memory. The Berlin Wall was an image. The Soviet Union was a map in an old textbook. NATO's Article 5 invocation after September 11 belonged to a political world formed before many of them could vote.

For Burns, those abstractions had been assignments.

This is one of the peculiar functions of teaching after public service. A person becomes evidence.

Not authority in the final sense. Experience can mislead as easily as theory. Veterans of one crisis may see its pattern everywhere. Officials can confuse access with understanding and survival with success. Institutional memory can preserve old errors alongside old wisdom.

But a practitioner can make policy feel less clean.

The India nuclear negotiations could be taught not simply as an agreement but as a chain of political permissions. NATO enlargement could be discussed not merely as a map changing color but as a series of arguments among sovereign governments. Iran could be understood not only as a nuclear file but as a problem of leverage, verification, coalition management, and time.

The career that had trained Burns to speak on behalf of the United States now allowed him to speak in his own name.

That freedom carries its own discipline.

Former officials possess a kind of borrowed luminosity. Audiences listen because they were once in rooms the audience could not enter. The danger is obvious. Proximity can become a substitute for proof. Anecdote can become doctrine. The former official can turn a contingent decision into a universal rule simply because he remembers how consequential it felt.

The best use of experience is narrower.

It is to expose the machinery.

Who had authority? What information was missing? Which ally had to be persuaded? What could not be said publicly? What did the negotiators misunderstand? Which part of the agreement was hardest to sell at home? Where did personality matter, and where would institutions have produced roughly the same result with someone else in the chair?

Those questions turn memoir into method.

They also prepare the ground for return.

Burns left government in a period when American foreign policy was still dominated by the wars that followed September 11 and by arguments over the exercise of U.S. primacy. During the years that followed, another problem grew larger: the relationship between the United States and China.

China was not a country Burns had been professionally formed around in the way he had been formed around the Soviet Union and Europe. That would matter later. He would eventually be asked to represent the United States in Beijing not because he had spent a lifetime as a China specialist, but because he had spent a lifetime practicing diplomacy.

There is a distinction there worth holding.

Regional expertise and diplomatic craft overlap. They are not identical.

The specialist knows the country deeply: language, history, institutions, personalities, political vocabulary, memory, grievance, habit. The diplomat must know enough of those things to act intelligently while also understanding Washington, alliances, domestic politics, negotiation, bureaucratic authority, and the limits of his own instructions.

Great powers need both.

By leaving government, Burns entered the long interval in which those two kinds of knowledge could be studied from outside the daily machinery of state.

He had spent twenty-seven years learning how the United States spoke to the world.

Now he had time to ask what the United States should say.
