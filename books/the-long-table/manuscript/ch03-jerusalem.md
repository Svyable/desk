# Jerusalem

Jerusalem punishes imprecise language.

A road is not only a road. A neighborhood may be a neighborhood, a settlement, a political claim, a security problem, a municipal fact, or several of these at once depending on who is speaking. A map can describe terrain and still function as an argument. Even the name attached to a place may tell you something about the speaker before the speaker has made a case.

Nicholas Burns served at the American Consulate General in Jerusalem from 1985 to 1987. The official biographies describe him as a political officer and record a concrete responsibility: coordinating American economic assistance to Palestinians in the West Bank and East Jerusalem.

The assignment moved him closer to the central work of diplomacy: deciding what another political community means when its vocabulary does not fit neatly inside your own.

Political reporting is sometimes caricatured as the collection of elite opinion. At its best it is closer to disciplined translation. The officer has to determine what happened, what people say happened, what they believe happened, what they want Washington to believe happened, and which differences among those categories matter.

Jerusalem offered no shortage of differences.

The mid-1980s preceded the first intifada, which began late in 1987, but the underlying conflict was already present in daily political life. Israeli security, Palestinian political aspirations, occupation, terrorism, settlements, municipal authority, economic dependence, Jordanian interests, Arab politics and American policy all overlapped. Washington had relationships and commitments that did not dissolve merely because they pulled against one another.

Burns was not sent there to solve the conflict. Junior political officers are rarely entrusted with solving history.

He was sent to understand enough of it to report usefully and administer a piece of American policy inside it.

Economic assistance sounds technocratic until geography becomes political.

A development project has a location. A road connects particular communities. A school serves particular families. A water system crosses lines that may be administratively clear and politically contested. Money passes through institutions whose legitimacy is not viewed identically by all sides. An assistance program therefore produces two outputs at once: whatever material thing it funds, and a political interpretation of why the United States funded it.

The diplomat has to see both.

This is where the word empathy becomes dangerous if used carelessly. Diplomacy does require the ability to understand how the world looks from another position. But understanding is not agreement, and empathy is not the suspension of judgment. The professional requirement is more exacting: represent another side's reasoning accurately enough that your own government can make a better decision.

That requires resisting caricature even when caricature is politically convenient.

An Israeli official's security concern cannot be understood if it is automatically reduced to a pretext. A Palestinian account of life under occupation cannot be understood if it is automatically reduced to propaganda. The officer does not have to accept every claim. He has to know what kind of claim he is hearing.

This distinction would recur throughout Burns's career.

In negotiations with Russia, an American official needed to understand Moscow's stated interests without granting Moscow a veto over its neighbors. In work on Iran, understanding the regime's incentives did not require accepting its nuclear position. In China, listening carefully to Beijing's conception of sovereignty and national rejuvenation did not require Washington to adopt it.

Diplomacy fails when understanding is confused with endorsement. It also fails when refusal to endorse becomes refusal to understand.

Jerusalem makes that failure visible quickly.

The city also teaches another professional habit: ask who is absent.

Official meetings create an optical illusion. The people at the table appear to constitute the problem because they constitute the meeting. But policy affects people who are not accredited, invited, organized, adult, enfranchised, armed, wealthy, English-speaking, or capable of reaching an American officer.

Assistance work can puncture the illusion because it forces policy outward from ministries and negotiating rooms. The questions become less abstract. Who receives the benefit? Who controls access? Which institution can actually deliver? What happens when a policy designed in Washington meets a checkpoint, a municipal rule, a local patronage network, or a family whose political identity does not map cleanly onto an American category?

There is no evidence that Burns left Jerusalem with a grand doctrine derived from these questions. It would be artificial to assign one to him.

What the record does allow is a narrower observation. His early career placed him repeatedly where American foreign policy had to be implemented among people who did not experience American policy as an abstraction.

That is useful preparation for power.

Senior officials are surrounded by compression. Briefing books compress years into pages. Talking points compress policy into sentences. intelligence products compress uncertainty into judgments. Diplomatic cables compress countries into reportable developments. Compression is unavoidable; government cannot function without it.

The danger is forgetting what has been compressed away.

A political officer in the field has the opportunity to remember.

The opportunity is not automatically taken. Diplomats can become captive to familiar interlocutors. Embassy reporting can reproduce the assumptions of the capital. Language ability can be weaker than the complexity of the society requires. Access itself can distort perception: the people most willing to meet an American official may not be representative of the people the official is trying to understand.

The profession's answer is not perfect objectivity. It is method.

Broaden the contacts. Separate observation from interpretation. Compare accounts. Read history. Learn which source has an incentive to mislead and which source may be sincere but wrong. Report disagreement inside the embassy rather than smoothing it away for aesthetic reasons. State confidence carefully. Return to the same question after events change.

Most of all, do not confuse access with knowledge.

Burns would later become exceptionally well connected. That can obscure the apprenticeship required to use access rather than merely possess it. A contact is not valuable because a famous person answers the telephone. A contact is valuable when the relationship produces information, candor, warning, clarification, or a channel through which a government can test intent without making a public commitment.

Those relationships are built before the crisis in which they become useful.

The Jerusalem posting also complicates any easy version of an American-dream biography. Burns's career can certainly be told as a story of public service and upward responsibility. But the work itself constantly confronted him with people whose relationship to American power was more ambivalent.

The American diplomat abroad represents a country that can be admired, needed, feared, blamed and courted by the same government in the same week. American ideals travel with American interests, and the two do not always arrive in perfect alignment.

A serious biography cannot resolve that tension on the subject's behalf.

It has to show him working inside it.

By 1987 Burns returned to Washington. He entered the State Department's Operations Center and Executive Secretariat—the nervous system through which urgent information and senior-level paper move. Soon afterward he would work on Soviet and Eastern European affairs as the communist order began to crack.

The scale of his career was about to change.

But Jerusalem left behind a durable problem.

What does the other side mean?

Not what did it say. Not what do we wish it meant. Not what would make our policy easiest to defend.

What does it mean?

The question sounds elementary. Much of diplomacy consists of governments failing to answer it before acting.
