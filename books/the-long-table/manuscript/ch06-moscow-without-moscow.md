# Moscow Without Moscow

The Soviet desk lost its country.

That is too simple, but it captures the administrative absurdity of a geopolitical earthquake. In 1990 Nicholas Burns joined the National Security Council staff in the George H. W. Bush White House. He worked on Soviet affairs and then Russian affairs. During his five years at the NSC, the Soviet Union ceased to exist and the United States found itself dealing not with one familiar superpower but with fifteen independent states emerging from its remains.

The job was not to admire the transformation. The job was to manage consequences that did not wait for the historical argument to settle.

Nuclear weapons were among them. So were the Baltic states, Ukraine, Russian military forces outside Russia, economic collapse, democratic experiments, territorial questions and the uncertain authority of governments that had acquired sovereignty faster than institutions capable of exercising it.

Burns attended U.S.-Soviet summits and other high-level meetings during this period. Under President Bush he worked on economic assistance, relations with Russia and Ukraine and ties with the Baltic countries. Later, under President Bill Clinton, he became Special Assistant to the President and Senior Director for Russia, Ukraine and Eurasia Affairs, with responsibility for advising the President, Vice President and National Security Advisor on relations with all fifteen states of the former Soviet Union.

The scale of the assignment reveals something about diplomacy after an empire breaks.

One relationship becomes many. One chain of command becomes several. One intelligence problem becomes a family of them. Every newly sovereign government acquires its own domestic politics, historical memory and definition of independence. The old center still matters, but it no longer possesses the legal or political authority that once allowed outsiders to treat the periphery as an extension of Moscow.

A map can change overnight. A diplomatic system cannot.

## December

The Soviet Union's dissolution in December 1991 is often narrated as an ending. From the desk of a policymaker it was also an administrative beginning.

The United States had to decide how to recognize new states, establish relations, assign diplomatic coverage and think about security commitments in a region where the old order had disappeared before a stable replacement existed. It had to manage its relationship with Boris Yeltsin's Russia without treating Russia as though it still possessed an empire. It had to engage Ukraine as a sovereign country rather than merely as a strategic territory between Russia and Europe. It had to understand why the Baltic states viewed restoration of independence differently from republics that had emerged through the Soviet Union's dissolution.

These distinctions were not semantic. They determined what respect looked like.

Great powers are prone to a particular diplomatic error: they see smaller states through the lens of the larger state next door. Geography encourages it. Efficiency rewards it. History sometimes appears to justify it.

But sovereignty is partly the refusal to be interpreted entirely through someone else's capital.

For Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, independence was not simply a new foreign-policy option. It was the restoration of political existence after decades of Soviet occupation. Burns's later receipt of Estonia's Order of the Cross of Terra Mariana for his work connected to the withdrawal of Russian military forces from Estonia offers a small biographical marker of a much larger problem. A Russian troop withdrawal could look, from Moscow, like strategic contraction. From Tallinn, it could mean the practical completion of restored sovereignty.

The same event carries different histories depending on which side of the table is speaking.

Diplomacy begins when a government can hear both without pretending they are identical.

## Ukraine

Ukraine forced the new map into strategic focus.

An independent Ukraine was not merely a former Soviet administrative unit with a different flag. It was a large European state with its own population, history, economy, political conflicts and inherited military infrastructure. Its emergence altered the strategic geometry of Europe.

For Washington, the nuclear inheritance was impossible to separate from the political one. The breakup of the Soviet Union left nuclear weapons on the territories of Russia, Ukraine, Belarus and Kazakhstan. The United States had a profound interest in reducing nuclear danger. Newly independent governments had profound interests in establishing that their sovereignty was not provisional.

Those interests could overlap without being the same.

This is the kind of problem that exposes the limits of slogans. 'Denuclearization' describes an objective. It does not tell a diplomat how a newly sovereign state evaluates the security value of what it is being asked to relinquish, what assurances it considers meaningful, how domestic politics constrain its leaders, or how historical experience shapes its view of a more powerful neighbor.

Burns's portfolio required him to live inside those distinctions.

The larger lesson is uncomfortable because later history changes how earlier bargains are judged. Diplomatic biography should not pretend that people in the early 1990s possessed knowledge of the decades that followed. Nor should it seal the period inside the optimism of its own moment. The useful task is to reconstruct what officials could reasonably know, what they believed, what alternatives they considered, and which assumptions proved durable or fragile.

That is harder than retrospective verdict. It is also more honest.

## The Baltic question

The Baltic states taught another version of the same lesson.

To say that the Cold War had ended was not to say that all consequences of Soviet power had ended with it. Russian military forces remained outside the Russian Federation. Withdrawal therefore became both a practical negotiation and a test of the new order.

A military unit has barracks, equipment, officers, families, supply chains and political meaning. Removing it is not a sentence in a communiqué. It is a sequence of agreements and implementation problems. The difference between a promise to withdraw and the last troops actually leaving is the difference between diplomatic language and diplomatic completion.

This helps explain why Burns's later career would place such weight on alliances and implementation. Agreements are not self-executing. Political intention has to survive contact with institutions.

The work is often less cinematic than the summit that announces it. That does not make it less consequential.

## Two presidents

Burns's five years at the NSC crossed a presidential transition from George H. W. Bush to Bill Clinton.

Career public service makes transitions visible in a way electoral politics often does not. The administration changes. The constitutional government continues. New political leaders arrive with different priorities, assumptions, constituencies and language. Career officials owe them the same professional obligation they owed their predecessors: accurate information, candid analysis, disciplined execution and loyalty to lawful policy.

Burns rose under Clinton to Special Assistant to the President and Senior Director for Russia, Ukraine and Eurasia Affairs. The portfolio gave him lead responsibility at the White House for relationships across the former Soviet space.

The promotion also illuminates a quality that would define his public career: political leaders of different parties repeatedly trusted him with sensitive work.

That is not political neutrality in the sense of having no views. Diplomats have judgments. They must. It is institutional nonpartisanship: the ability to separate professional service from personal ownership of power.

The distinction sounds quaint until a republic loses it.

## The seduction of the moment

The early post-Soviet years encouraged large conclusions. Liberal democracy appeared ascendant. Russia was experimenting with elections and market reform. The Baltic states were free. Eastern European states were turning westward. The nuclear confrontation that had structured generations of strategic thought had receded.

Optimism was not irrational.

But optimism can become analytical laziness when a favorable direction is mistaken for an irreversible one.

Diplomacy has to preserve contingency. Institutions can weaken. Leaders can change. Public expectations can sour. Economic shocks can discredit political systems. National humiliation can be converted into political capital. A relationship that appears to be converging can diverge again.

Burns would eventually return to these countries in very different circumstances. Russia would cease to look like the hoped-for democratic partner of the early 1990s. Ukraine would become the victim of Russian aggression. The Baltic states would enter NATO. The strategic vocabulary would change again.

None of that means the choices of the early 1990s can be judged simply by reading later events backward. It means that statecraft must make decisions under uncertainty without confusing uncertainty with ignorance.

The professional habit Burns was developing was therefore not prediction. It was preparation for revision.

## Fifteen relationships

The phrase 'the former Soviet Union' is administratively useful and diplomatically dangerous.

It describes what fifteen states had been. It does not describe what they intended to become.

For an American official responsible for the region, the temptation was to continue seeing the new states as pieces of a single inherited problem. The better practice was harder: learn each government's interests, constraints, fears and political vocabulary while still understanding the regional system connecting them.

That balance between the whole and its parts would recur throughout Burns's career. NATO is an alliance, but it is made of governments. The European Union can take positions, but its members retain politics. The international coalition on Iran could agree on a negotiating objective while differing on risk. The United States and China could speak of a bilateral relationship while dozens of agencies, companies, militaries and political constituencies pulled it in competing directions.

There is no single long table in diplomacy.

There are tables nested inside tables.

The Soviet collapse made that visible early. Burns entered the White House working on one superpower and left his Russia, Ukraine and Eurasia portfolio responsible for a landscape of sovereign states that had not existed in that form when he arrived.

The profession had taught him to learn countries.

History was now teaching him how quickly a country could become countries.
