# Athens

An embassy is a promise made visible.

The flag, the chancery, the Marine at the door, the motorcade, the residence dinner, the visa window, the political officer taking notes in a provincial office: together they tell a foreign country that the United States has decided to be present. Not merely interested. Present.

For Nicholas Burns, Athens was the first time the promise belonged to him in the peculiar way it belongs to an ambassador.

He arrived as a career Foreign Service officer who had spent much of his professional life near power without possessing its ceremonial form. He had worked in Cairo and Jerusalem, in the State Department’s Operations Center and Secretariat, beside the counselor of the Department as Eastern Europe changed, on the National Security Council as the Soviet Union disappeared, and at the State Department lectern as spokesman for Warren Christopher and Madeleine Albright. He knew how policy was made, translated, defended, revised, and sometimes rescued from its own rhetoric.

In November 1997 President Bill Clinton appointed him ambassador to Greece. He presented his credentials in December. The posting returned him to a country he had already encountered from the White House, when he had advised President George H. W. Bush on Greece, Turkey, and Cyprus and accompanied Bush on the 1991 state visit to Greece and Turkey.

But advising on a country and representing America inside it are different professions.

Washington teaches compression. The ambassador’s job teaches consequence.

A sentence drafted in Washington may arrive in a capital as policy, pressure, insult, reassurance, threat, or opportunity. The ambassador has to know which. He has to understand not only what Washington intended but what the receiving country heard. Then he must explain each side to the other without becoming captive to either.

Greece made that problem unusually vivid.

The United States and Greece were allies. They were also countries carrying a complicated twentieth century between them. Greece had endured occupation, civil war, dictatorship, political violence, and bitter arguments about American influence. The Cold War had made the relationship strategically important without making it emotionally simple. Turkey and Cyprus ensured that alliance management was never merely ceremonial. The Balkans remained unstable. Questions involving Macedonia carried identity, history, and domestic politics into diplomacy with unusual force.

Burns therefore inherited a relationship in which the word ally could not be treated as a synonym for agreement.

That distinction matters.

Americans sometimes speak about alliances as though friendship were their operating system. It is not. Alliances are arrangements among sovereign governments whose interests overlap enough, often enough, to make durable cooperation preferable to permanent improvisation. Their strength comes partly from affection and common values, but also from habit, procedure, military interoperability, political investment, and the accumulated credibility of commitments kept.

An ambassador works inside all of those layers at once.

He also lives in public.

The spokesman’s podium had prepared Burns for scrutiny, but an ambassadorship changed its direction. In Washington he had represented the Department to a press corps largely asking what America intended to do elsewhere. In Athens he represented America to people for whom American decisions were not abstractions. They had consequences in their region, their politics, their security, and their memory.

This required a different kind of listening.

The temptation for a powerful country is to treat explanation as persuasion. Explain the policy clearly enough and the other side will understand why it is right. Diplomacy repeatedly punishes this assumption. People can understand an American position perfectly and still oppose it. Governments can share strategic interests while disagreeing about timing, tactics, history, status, or acceptable risk.

The ambassador’s task is not to eliminate disagreement. It is to keep disagreement from destroying the machinery needed for the next agreement.

That is slow work.

It happens in official meetings, but also in the accumulated contact surrounding them: parliamentarians, ministers, opposition leaders, military officers, journalists, academics, businesspeople, mayors, religious leaders, students. The embassy is not simply a transmitter of instructions from Washington. At its best it is an instrument for building a sufficiently detailed model of another society that Washington can act without relying entirely on caricature.

This was an old diplomatic function. It was becoming more important, not less, in an era of instant news.

The paradox was already visible. Information about Greece was increasingly abundant. Understanding Greece remained scarce.

Burns had spent the early part of his career learning that distinction. Athens required him to institutionalize it.

An ambassador cannot personally know everything an embassy knows. The job depends on creating a culture in which political reporting, economic analysis, consular work, security judgments, public diplomacy, military relationships, and commercial questions can reach the chief of mission without being flattened into a single story. The ambassador then has to decide what Washington actually needs to hear.

Sometimes that is the message Washington wants.

Sometimes it is not.

The latter is where the profession earns its keep.

A diplomatic service that reports only what political leaders wish to hear is not a diplomatic service. It is a theater company. Career officers serve elected presidents precisely because democratic legitimacy belongs to the elected government. But professional loyalty does not require analytical obedience. The officer owes the administration execution of lawful policy and, before decisions are made, the clearest account possible of facts, alternatives, local reactions, and likely consequences.

Athens put Burns on the receiving end of that system. Younger officers now brought him the inconvenient reporting.

He had to decide what to do with it.

There is a revealing progression in the career. In Mauritania he had learned to notice. In Cairo, to support. In Jerusalem, to report and interpret. In the Secretariat, to move information through the institution. At the NSC, to convert analysis into presidential choices. At the State Department podium, to convert policy into public language.

In Athens he had to do all of these at once.

And he had to attach his name to the result.

That is one of the quiet transformations of becoming an ambassador. Authority becomes personal without becoming private. The ambassador may be the President’s representative, but the embassy does not become his property. Every reception, démarche, press statement, cable, security decision, and meeting exists inside an institutional chain running back to an elected government and outward through thousands of public servants.

The best ambassadors therefore learn an odd combination of confidence and impersonality.

They must enter a room as though the United States itself has arrived while remembering that they are temporary custodians of the chair.

Burns served in Athens until 2001.

By the time he left, the diplomatic world was about to change again. He had spent the 1990s inside the great post-Cold War question: what should the United States do with a period of extraordinary relative power? NATO had enlarged. The Balkans had forced Europe and America to confront war on the continent after the Cold War. Relations with Russia had moved from hope to complication. The European project was deepening. Greece remained an essential ally in a difficult neighborhood.

Yet the organizing problem of American foreign policy still largely looked outward: unstable regions, unfinished European security, proliferation, authoritarian states, humanitarian crises, trade, enlargement.

Burns’s next assignment was Brussels.

He would become the United States Permanent Representative to NATO in August 2001.

The timing was almost cruel in its precision.

He had spent a career learning how alliances managed disagreement.

Within weeks, he would be sitting inside the alliance when the United States was attacked.

The question would no longer be how an American ambassador represented a powerful country to an ally.

It would be what an alliance meant when one of its members needed it.
