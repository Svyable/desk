# NATO at War

The first invocation of Article 5 answered a question about solidarity. It did not answer the question of strategy.

That distinction became the work.

Nicholas Burns remained the United States Permanent Representative to NATO from 2001 until 2005, a period in which the alliance moved from the shock of September 11 into Afghanistan, enlargement, military transformation, and the bitter transatlantic arguments surrounding Iraq.

The job placed him inside a contradiction at the center of American power.

The United States was stronger than any of its allies individually. It was not strong enough to make their politics disappear.

This is where alliance diplomacy becomes visible as a craft rather than a sentiment.

A president can decide what the United States will do. He cannot decide what Germany will do, what France will do, what Turkey will permit, what a parliament will authorize, what an allied public will sustain, or how another government will interpret a threat. American leadership operates through these independent decisions or it operates around them.

The difference can determine whether a coalition lasts.

After September 11, NATO contributed to the campaign against terrorism in ways that crossed the old boundaries of alliance geography. NATO aircraft helped protect American airspace. Allied ships operated in the Mediterranean. European and Canadian forces deployed to Afghanistan. NATO eventually took command of the International Security Assistance Force in 2003, its first major operation beyond the traditional Euro-Atlantic area.

The alliance built to deter Soviet armies in Europe was now helping stabilize a country in Central Asia.

Institutions survive by changing without becoming unrecognizable.

Too little adaptation and they become museums. Too much and members no longer know what obligation they joined.

Burns had entered NATO just as that balance became urgent.

The argument over NATO’s relevance had followed the Soviet Union into history. If the threat that produced the alliance was gone, critics asked, why should the alliance remain? The Balkan wars had already provided one answer. September 11 provided another. Security threats did not respect the geography on which NATO’s Cold War planning had been built.

But saying NATO needed to adapt was easier than agreeing on the adaptation.

The practical questions were brutal in their specificity. Which forces could actually deploy? How quickly? With what transport? Under whose command? Could national units communicate securely with one another? Who would pay? Which governments would accept casualties? What restrictions would they place on their forces? How far beyond Europe could the alliance reasonably operate before political consensus fractured?

These are not footnotes to grand strategy.

They are grand strategy after contact with reality.

Burns had spent enough time in government to understand the seduction of declarations. A summit can announce a transformation in an afternoon. Militaries and bureaucracies may spend years making the nouns in the declaration real.

Capability is political credibility with logistics attached.

The United States pushed NATO toward forces that could move faster, operate farther from home, and confront terrorism and other post-Cold War threats. The Prague summit of 2002 became a major point in that transformation, combining enlargement with commitments to new capabilities and a NATO Response Force.

Enlargement carried its own historical weight.

Burns had worked on Eastern Europe as communist rule collapsed. He had worked on Russia, Ukraine, and the former Soviet republics as an imperial system became a set of sovereign states. Now, at NATO, countries that had lived inside the Warsaw Pact or Soviet sphere were entering the Western alliance.

For them, membership was not an abstraction about institutional architecture.

It was a choice about where history would be allowed to return.

For Russia, the same process could look very different.

Diplomacy rarely grants the comfort of a single valid perspective. A policy can be stabilizing to one country and threatening to another. The diplomat’s responsibility is not to pretend these perceptions are morally equivalent. It is to understand that perceptions have strategic consequences whether Washington considers them justified or not.

That distinction—understanding without conceding—had been part of Burns’s education since Jerusalem.

NATO enlargement required it.

So did Iraq.

The transatlantic dispute over the 2003 invasion exposed the limits of the extraordinary unity that had followed September 11. Major allies disagreed with the United States over the threat, the evidence, the legitimacy and timing of force, and the role of the United Nations. The arguments became public and, at times, acrimonious.

For an American ambassador at NATO, this was not an academic disagreement about alliance theory. It was the room.

The temptation in Washington was to divide allies into the helpful and the obstructive. The temptation in European capitals was to interpret American impatience as proof that consultation was merely decorative. Both stories contained enough truth to become dangerous.

An alliance can survive disagreement. It has greater difficulty surviving contempt.

Burns’s task was therefore larger than winning individual votes. It involved preserving the ability to work together after losing them.

This is one of diplomacy’s least celebrated skills.

Politics rewards the decisive moment: the agreement, the veto, the speech, the breakthrough. Diplomacy often has to optimize for the morning after defeat. If an ally refuses the policy you wanted today, will it still share intelligence tomorrow? Will its officers still plan with yours? Will its government support the next mission? Can its ambassador trust that disagreement will not be treated as permanent exile?

The answer depends partly on institutions and partly on conduct.

Burns’s career had given him an unusual preparation for this. He knew the White House and the State Department. He had served Republican and Democratic presidents. He had represented policy publicly and privately. He had been an ambassador in a country where alliance did not eliminate grievance. He understood that foreign governments listened not only to the formal American position but to tone, sequence, consultation, and whether Washington appeared to have heard objections before rejecting them.

None of this meant yielding American judgment to allied consensus.

Leadership sometimes requires acting despite disagreement.

But there is a difference between deciding independently and behaving as though other countries do not possess independent judgment. The first may be necessary. The second accumulates costs.

Those costs are often invisible until the next crisis.

The NATO years therefore sharpened a principle that would matter when Burns returned to Washington as Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs: diplomacy is not the alternative to leverage. Diplomacy is the process by which different forms of leverage are combined without pretending they are the same thing.

Military capability is leverage. Market access is leverage. Intelligence is leverage. Geography is leverage. Legitimacy is leverage. Trust is leverage. The ability to convene is leverage. So is the ability to say no and still be invited back into the room.

Alliances multiply these assets when they work.

They also multiply veto points.

That is not a design flaw. It is the price of assembling sovereign power rather than imperial obedience.

By 2005 Burns had spent four years learning this at the long NATO table.

He had watched the alliance invoke collective defense for the first time. He had helped represent the United States as NATO moved beyond its old geography. He had participated in debates over enlargement and transformation. He had lived through one of the deepest transatlantic disputes of the post-Cold War period.

Then Condoleezza Rice asked him to return to Washington as Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs.

It was the Department’s third-ranking position and, in another sense, the culmination of the career Foreign Service track. Burns would become one of the senior professional diplomats responsible for turning presidential strategy into negotiations across regions and issues.

The scale changed again.

At NATO, the table had been long because many allies sat around it.

In Washington, the table would become longer still.

India would sit at one end.

Iran at another.

Israel, Russia, Europe, Congress, the White House, the bureaucracy, and decades of inherited policy would crowd the remaining chairs.

Burns had learned how to keep an alliance together when its members disagreed.

He was about to learn what happened when the agreement America wanted required changing America’s own rules.
