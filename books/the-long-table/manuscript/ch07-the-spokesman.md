# The Spokesman

Diplomats are trained to listen. In 1995 Nicholas Burns was given a job that required him to speak almost every day.

He became the State Department spokesman and acting assistant secretary for public affairs, serving Secretary of State Warren Christopher and later Madeleine Albright. The assignment placed him behind a lectern in a briefing room where foreign policy was translated into public language under adversarial questioning.

It was a different kind of negotiation.

The reporters in front of him were not foreign governments. They did not owe the Department discretion, sympathy or patience. Their job was to find the contradiction, the withheld fact, the policy shift, the gap between yesterday's formulation and today's. A spokesman who treated the briefing as theater would eventually be exposed by the record. A spokesman who treated it as merely the recitation of approved language would fail to explain anything.

The job required another form of diplomatic discipline: saying exactly enough.

Burns gave the Department's daily press briefings, traveled with the secretaries of state and coordinated public outreach. His official biography later noted the visibility of the role and the recognition he received for government communication. But the significance of the assignment in his formation is deeper than visibility. It forced a career diplomat who had spent years handling private analysis at State and the White House to confront the public half of foreign policy.

A democracy cannot conduct statecraft entirely in private.

## The sentence

Government language is often mocked for being bloodless. Sometimes it deserves the mockery. Sometimes the apparent dullness is engineered.

A sentence delivered by the State Department can be heard simultaneously by a domestic political audience, an allied government, an adversary's foreign ministry, financial markets, military planners, journalists and people whose lives are directly affected by the policy. A phrase that seems trivial in Washington can be parsed elsewhere as a signal of commitment or retreat.

The spokesman therefore lives inside the difference between ordinary language and official language.

An ordinary speaker can correct himself. A government can correct itself too, but the correction becomes evidence.

That changes how words are chosen.

A briefing answer has to be true enough to survive the next document, precise enough not to create an accidental commitment, comprehensible enough to communicate policy, and flexible enough not to negotiate publicly what diplomats are still trying to settle privately.

Those demands pull against one another.

Too much precision can close options. Too little becomes evasion. Too much confidence can outrun the facts. Too much caution can make a government sound as though it has no policy at all.

The craft is not eloquence. It is calibrated clarity.

## Public and private

Diplomacy depends on private communication because governments sometimes need room to move before they can explain movement publicly. Democracy depends on public accountability because foreign policy is conducted in the name of citizens who bear its costs.

The spokesman stands on the seam.

Burns had already seen how private channels worked inside government. He had handled sensitive Soviet and post-Soviet policy at the NSC. Now he had to defend and explain American policy without exposing every internal disagreement or negotiating position that produced it.

This is not automatically deception. Every functioning government distinguishes deliberation from announcement. The ethical problem begins when necessary confidentiality becomes a habit of avoiding accountability, or when public language is used to create an impression officials know the underlying facts cannot sustain.

A good spokesman therefore needs something more difficult than message discipline. He needs a relationship with truth sturdy enough to withstand institutional pressure.

That relationship is tested most severely when the answer is inconvenient.

'I don't know' is sometimes the most accurate statement a government can make. 'I can't discuss that' can be more honest than an elaborate non-answer. 'Our policy has changed' can be more credible than pretending two incompatible formulations mean the same thing.

The briefing room punishes these admissions politically. Over time it can reward them institutionally.

Credibility is accumulated in small units.

## The traveling brief

Burns accompanied Christopher and Albright on foreign trips. Travel changes the spokesman's job because the distance between diplomacy and explanation collapses.

A secretary meets a foreign leader. Reporters want to know what happened. The other government may issue its own account. Officials may disagree about what a phrase meant. Markets or militaries may react before the delegation reaches the next capital. The spokesman has to communicate while the policy process is still warm.

There is no clean separation between action and narration.

This is particularly important in alliance politics. Governments are constantly interpreting not only what the United States does but how the United States describes what it has done. Public words help allies assess reliability. They help adversaries assess resolve. They also create constraints. Once a government has defined an event publicly in a certain way, changing that definition can carry political cost.

The spokesman therefore participates in policy even when he does not make it.

Explanation is an instrument of statecraft.

## Madeleine

When Madeleine Albright became Secretary of State in 1997, Burns continued as spokesman for a time under a secretary whose relationship to public language was unusually direct.

Albright understood symbols. She understood television. She understood that diplomacy was conducted not only through formal notes and closed meetings but through the political meaning audiences attached to gestures, phrases and images. Her own career had crossed academia, the National Security Council, the United Nations and electoral politics. She knew that foreign policy in a democracy had to be argued, not merely administered.

For Burns, serving both Christopher and Albright offered a lesson in the variety of legitimate diplomatic styles.

There is no single temperament required for statecraft. Some officials lower the temperature of a room. Others clarify choices by sharpening them. Some persuade through detail, others through moral language, others through relationships built over years. Institutions become stronger when they can use different temperaments without confusing temperament with policy.

A career officer learns to serve the office as well as the occupant.

## The risk of fluency

Public speaking creates its own danger: fluency can become a substitute for thought.

A person who answers questions every day gets good at answering questions every day. The skill is real. So is the temptation to believe that an answer delivered smoothly is therefore a good answer.

Diplomacy offers a permanent corrective. The foreign government does not care how elegantly Washington explained a policy if the policy misunderstands its interests. An ally does not become reassured because a spokesman sounds reassuring. Facts outside the briefing room retain veto power.

Burns's later career would repeatedly combine the public and private halves of diplomacy. As ambassador, under secretary and eventually ambassador to China, he would explain American policy publicly while conducting relationships whose most sensitive work could not be done at a microphone.

The spokesman years taught the connection between the two.

Private diplomacy without public legitimacy becomes brittle. Public diplomacy without private candor becomes performance.

A serious state needs both.

## The record

Press briefings disappear quickly from the news cycle and remain stubbornly in the archive.

That is another discipline of official speech. The answer given under pressure today can be compared with the document released years later. Governments eventually acquire a record longer than the political moment in which any one statement was useful.

Career diplomats understand this almost physically. They work inside institutions whose files outlive administrations.

The knowledge should produce humility. It does not always. But it can.

Burns had begun his career learning to report what he saw from posts far from Washington. He had then worked inside the machinery that moved information upward toward secretaries and presidents. At the White House he had watched the international system change faster than its inherited categories.

Now the flow reversed.

He stood before the press and sent the government's words outward.

Listening and speaking are often treated as opposite diplomatic skills. They are not. The quality of the second depends on the first.

A government earns the right to be understood only after it has done the work of understanding.
