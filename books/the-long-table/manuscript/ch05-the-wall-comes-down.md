# The Wall Comes Down

In 1989 Nicholas Burns moved close to the problem that had organized American foreign policy for most of his life.

He was not yet a famous diplomat. He was not an ambassador. He was a young Foreign Service officer working as special assistant to the Counselor of the Department of State, Robert Zoellick, with responsibility for Soviet and Eastern European affairs. The distinction matters. History is often remembered through the people standing at microphones after it has happened. Diplomacy is also made by the people behind them who have to decide, while events are still moving, which changes are temporary, which are structural, and which old assumptions have quietly become dangerous.

The Cold War did not end in a single evening. That is one of the conveniences memory imposes on history. The Berlin Wall gives the story an image: concrete, crowds, television lights, people climbing onto a structure that had divided a city and represented a continent's captivity. But governments could not govern by image alone. Washington had to understand what was happening in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, the Baltic republics and Moscow at the same time. It had to consider German unification, NATO, Soviet security, nuclear weapons, economic instability, nationalism and the possibility that a system apparently opening could still close violently.

For a working diplomat, the drama was partly an analytical one. How quickly could the United States allow its mental map to change?

Institutions are built to remember. That is one of their virtues. It is also one of their hazards. A bureaucracy accumulates categories because categories make action possible. Ally. Adversary. Bloc. Republic. Satellite. Stable government. Recognized border. Established interest. A category that was accurate yesterday can continue to organize paperwork long after the world that produced it has begun to disappear.

Burns's generation encountered that problem at unusual scale. They had been educated for a bipolar world and were now being asked to help manage its dissolution.

There is a temptation in retrospect to imagine that the democratic revolutions of 1989 made the outcome obvious. They did not. A government watching another great power weaken has choices, and triumph is among the least disciplined of them. The United States could celebrate. It also had to avoid turning Soviet retreat into Soviet humiliation so quickly that cooperation became impossible. It could encourage the liberation of Eastern Europe while thinking about the security arrangements that would follow. It could welcome German reunification while confronting the fears reunification produced elsewhere. It could hope that Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms succeeded while preparing for circumstances in which Gorbachev himself might not.

This is where biography and statecraft meet. Burns did not end the Cold War. No serious account should inflate a staff officer into the author of transformations made by millions of people and leaders on several continents. The more interesting fact is that he entered senior policy work at precisely the moment when the diplomatic profession was required to distinguish celebration from strategy.

The lesson would remain with him.

A diplomat does not get to choose whether history arrives at a convenient time. The work is to recognize that the operating assumptions have changed before the institution has fully caught up.

## The counselor's office

The Counselor of the State Department occupies an unusual position. The office is close enough to the Secretary to participate in large strategic questions but flexible enough to move across the Department's geographic and functional boundaries. For a young officer working there on Soviet and Eastern European affairs, the assignment offered a view of policy as something larger than a country desk.

The cables still mattered. So did meetings, memoranda, intelligence, allied reporting, presidential priorities and the personalities of foreign leaders. But the question was no longer simply what Moscow wanted. It was what a rapidly changing Soviet system was capable of doing, what the states around it were becoming, and what architecture could replace a division of Europe that had seemed permanent.

That difference between intention and capability is elementary in theory and difficult in practice. A state can want an outcome it no longer has the means to impose. A leader can make a promise his own political system cannot sustain. A government can appear powerful because its symbols remain intact after its authority has begun to erode.

Diplomacy therefore requires a kind of double vision. One eye remains on the formal world: governments, treaties, recognized authorities, official statements. The other watches the informal movement beneath it: legitimacy, fear, public expectation, elite fracture, economic exhaustion, the widening distance between what a government says and what its people will still accept.

In Eastern Europe that distance was becoming impossible to miss.

For Burns, this was an education in historical velocity. The map on Monday could be politically obsolete by Friday even if the borders printed on it had not moved.

## No victory lap

The George H. W. Bush administration became known for caution during the revolutions of 1989. Caution can look unimpressive beside televised liberation. It is nevertheless one of the central virtues of statecraft when the alternative is to make another government's loss of control more dangerous than it needs to be.

The problem was not whether Americans should be pleased that communist rule was collapsing in Eastern Europe. The problem was what the United States should do with its pleasure.

Public humiliation is a strategic act. So is restraint.

A superpower whose adversary is retreating can make concessions easier by allowing the adversary's leadership to describe outcomes in language it can survive. It can also make concessions harder by insisting that every change be narrated as surrender. Diplomacy operates in that gap between the material substance of an agreement and the political story leaders must tell about it at home.

Burns would encounter versions of this problem repeatedly: in alliances, in nuclear negotiations, in dealings with Iran, and eventually in the increasingly adversarial relationship with China. A government may know what bargain it can accept before it knows how to explain acceptance.

The end of the Cold War provided an early master class.

## The professional temptation

There was another danger. Specialists can become emotionally attached to the permanence of their specialty.

A Soviet expert had invested years in language, history, ideology, institutions, personalities and doctrine. If the Soviet Union itself disappeared, the expertise did not become worthless. Much of it became more valuable. But its object changed. The professional challenge was to avoid defending the old conceptual map simply because one had become skilled at reading it.

This is an underappreciated discipline in public service: the willingness to let one's expertise be revised by events.

The best specialists are not those who know the most facts about yesterday's system. They are the ones whose knowledge helps them notice when yesterday's system no longer explains today.

By 1990 Burns was moving from State to the National Security Council staff at the White House. The assignment would put him still closer to President George H. W. Bush and the decisions surrounding the Soviet Union's final years. Official records identify him first as a director dealing with European and Soviet affairs and, in the accounts of his later career, as Director for Soviet and then Russian Affairs.

The title itself would soon contain history.

Soviet affairs would become Russian affairs because the country named in the first title would cease to exist.

There are few bureaucratic changes more consequential than that.

## The map after the map

The disappearance of an adversary does not eliminate foreign policy. It multiplies it.

A relationship once organized principally through Moscow would become relationships with Russia, Ukraine, the Baltic states and the other former Soviet republics. Nuclear weapons were distributed across more than one newly independent state. Borders that had been administrative lines inside a union became international frontiers. Economic systems fractured. Militaries required new chains of authority. Questions of citizenship, language and historical grievance acquired sovereign consequences.

The United States had spent decades studying how to manage the Soviet Union. It now had to learn how to deal with its absence.

That may be the deeper meaning of 1989 in Burns's career. It was not simply the moment when one side won. It was the moment when the habits required to oppose a durable system became insufficient for managing the uncertainty that followed it.

Victory creates obligations of imagination.

The young officer who had learned in Cairo to respect the distance between Washington's map and another society's reality, and in Jerusalem to listen for meanings hidden beneath formal language, was now watching the largest geopolitical category in American government begin to break apart.

The lesson was becoming larger.

Countries do not conduct diplomacy with abstractions. They conduct it with other political systems at particular moments of confidence, weakness, transition and fear. The names remain printed on folders long after the conditions underneath them have changed.

A diplomat's responsibility is to notice.
