# Chapter 17 — The Narrow Channel

The most important diplomatic channel is often the one that still exists after the others have narrowed.

By the time Nicholas Burns served in Beijing, the United States and China had no shortage of ways to observe one another. Satellites watched military movement. Customs data recorded trade. Companies mapped supply chains. Intelligence services collected. Universities studied. Journalists reported. Governments issued statements at a pace that would have been unimaginable when Burns entered the Foreign Service.

The shortage was elsewhere.

It was in confidence that a message had reached the person who mattered, had been understood as intended, and carried enough authority to survive the next news cycle.

This is the narrow channel.

It is not friendship. It is not accommodation. It is not a euphemism for weakness.

It is the minimum diplomatic infrastructure required for two powerful governments to remain intelligible to one another while disagreeing about matters they consider fundamental.

Burns had spent much of his career learning that diplomacy becomes more important as agreement becomes harder. In Jerusalem, he had worked inside a conflict where language carried history. At the National Security Council, he watched the Soviet system disappear faster than the vocabulary built to describe it. At NATO, he saw an alliance move from solidarity after September 11 into bitter disagreement over Iraq. With India, he helped construct a bargain from institutions that could not all move at once. With Iran, he worked a problem in which negotiation and coercion existed simultaneously.

China brought those lessons together under greater pressure.

A strategic competitor is not merely an adversary with more power. It is a government whose choices shape the environment in which your own choices have meaning.

If Beijing changes military posture around Taiwan, Washington responds not only to the movement but to its interpretation of the movement. If Washington changes export controls, Beijing responds not only to the regulation but to its interpretation of American purpose. Each side watches what the other does and then acts partly on what it believes the action means.

That creates a feedback loop.

The loop can stabilize competition.

It can also manufacture escalation.

Suppose one side takes a step intended as deterrence. The other reads preparation for attack. It responds with a measure intended to restore deterrence. The first side reads confirmation of its original fear. Neither government has to desire war for the sequence to become dangerous.

Diplomacy cannot eliminate that structure.

It can insert questions into it.

Is this exercise temporary? Is this sanction conditional? Is this statement new policy or political rhetoric? What would de-escalation require? Which official can speak authoritatively for the government? Which channel will remain open if the public relationship collapses for a week?

These are not glamorous questions.

They are the questions that keep ambiguity from automatically becoming action.

The channel becomes especially important around Taiwan because the issue contains several layers of political meaning that do not fit comfortably together. Beijing's claims, Taiwan's democratic government, the United States' one-China policy and unofficial relationship with Taiwan, regional military posture, alliance commitments elsewhere in Asia, and the semiconductor economy all intersect without collapsing into one negotiable dispute.

There is no sentence an ambassador can deliver that dissolves those contradictions.

The work is therefore partly custodial.

Keep the relationship capable of communication while the contradiction remains.

That idea can sound unsatisfying in a political culture that rewards solutions. But statecraft often consists of managing conditions that cannot be solved on the timetable of an administration.

Nuclear deterrence is not solved. Rival territorial claims are not solved merely because a crisis ends. Alliance anxieties return. Historical grievances survive communiqués. The diplomat's achievement may be to leave a dangerous problem dangerous rather than catastrophic.

This is difficult to celebrate.

It is also one of the oldest purposes of the profession.

Burns's Beijing assignment occurred during a period when the bilateral relationship repeatedly demonstrated how quickly channels could constrict. Political disputes affected military contacts, official meetings, travel, technology policy, and public rhetoric. High-level diplomacy therefore had a paradoxical quality: meetings were evidence not that the relationship was healthy but that it was strained enough to require deliberate maintenance.

A functioning channel does not prove trust.

Sometimes it proves the absence of trust.

Governments that trust one another have many ways to correct misunderstanding. Competitors may have only a few. That makes protocol, access, and continuity more important, not less.

The ambassador becomes part interpreter, part transmitter, part institutional memory.

He explains Beijing to Washington without becoming Beijing's advocate. He explains Washington to Beijing without pretending to control Washington. He carries messages he did not write and reports reactions he did not cause. He must know when a counterpart is speaking for the system and when the counterpart is testing language that has not yet become policy.

The position rewards ego publicly and punishes it privately.

The flag, residence, motorcade, title, and ceremonial access suggest personal importance. The work continually demonstrates the opposite. An ambassador's authority is delegated. His usefulness depends on remembering that fact.

The same principle applies to the other side of the table.

Diplomacy becomes dangerous when officials mistake access to a foreign counterpart for control over that counterpart. A minister may understand the American position perfectly and still reject it. A Chinese official may accurately convey Washington's warning upward and still be unable to change policy. Communication can succeed while persuasion fails.

That distinction matters because diplomatic failure is often diagnosed too crudely.

If the other government does not do what we asked, diplomacy failed.

Not necessarily.

Diplomacy may have clarified the cost of refusal. It may have discovered the boundary of compromise. It may have prevented one government from misreading the other's red line. It may have created time for military, economic, or political conditions to change. It may simply have preserved a route back to conversation after the crisis.

The narrow channel is valuable precisely because it makes no promise of agreement.

It promises contact with authority.

That promise becomes more consequential as technology accelerates everything around it.

Artificial intelligence can produce policy options, translate documents, summarize intelligence, model economic consequences, generate propaganda, detect patterns, and eventually participate more deeply in military decision systems. None of those capabilities automatically answers the central diplomatic question: what does the other government intend, and how certain are we?

Machines can help answer.

They can also make certainty feel cheaper than it is.

A model that produces a confident interpretation of an adversary's behavior may compress ambiguity into a probability. That can be useful. It can also obscure the political fact that the adversary itself may not have settled on a course of action.

Governments are not unitary minds.

Policy emerges from leaders, institutions, factions, bureaucratic incentives, military plans, domestic politics, accidents, and changing information. Sometimes the correct assessment is not that the other side has a hidden intention waiting to be discovered. It is that the intention is still being formed.

Diplomacy can enter that interval.

A message can change the calculation before it hardens into policy.

That is the deeper value of the channel.

It does not merely reveal intention.

Sometimes it changes intention.

Burns's career had begun in a diplomatic world where information moved slowly enough that embassies were essential collectors. It approached its final government chapter in a world where collection was nearly continuous.

Yet the old institution remained.

A representative sits across from another representative.

Each carries a government larger than the room.

Each tries to determine which words matter.

The table has become narrower.

The stakes have not.
