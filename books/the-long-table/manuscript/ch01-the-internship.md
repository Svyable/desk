# The Internship

Before Nicholas Burns represented the United States in Beijing, before NATO, before the White House and the State Department podium, there was Mauritania.

The scale matters.

Diplomatic biographies are vulnerable to destiny. Once the reader knows that a young person will become an ambassador, every early train ride begins to look like preparation for history. The effect is flattering and usually false. Careers do not feel inevitable while they are being lived.

Burns had studied European history at Boston College and international affairs at Johns Hopkins SAIS. He had spent time in France and learned French. Before entering the Foreign Service, he served as an intern at the American embassy in Nouakchott, Mauritania.

An internship is almost the opposite of high diplomacy. Its educational value comes from proximity without authority.

You see the machinery before anyone asks you to operate it.

An embassy is a peculiar American institution. It is at once an office building, listening post, political symbol, service center, security problem, workplace, reporting station and piece of sovereign theater. The ambassador may be the public face, but the institution runs on people whose work is rarely visible outside it: political and economic officers, consular staff, security personnel, administrative specialists, communicators, locally employed colleagues, analysts, assistants and drivers. Washington exists there as a collection of tasks.

For a young American considering the Foreign Service, the revelation is not necessarily grandeur.

It is detail.

A government has to know things.

It has to know who actually has influence in the host country, which official can deliver a promise, whether a public statement signals a policy change or domestic positioning, whether an economic statistic is credible, what Americans in trouble need from the consular section, what Washington is misunderstanding, and what the host government misunderstands about Washington.

The work turns foreign policy from an argument into a practice.

Mauritania was a useful place to learn this because it offered none of the psychological ease of assuming that the world naturally organizes itself around American attention. A relatively small West African state could force the beginner to confront a permanent diplomatic fact: every country is the center of its own map.

Washington may classify states by region, strategic importance, aid relationship or current crisis. People living inside those states do not experience themselves as a category in somebody else's briefing book.

The diplomat has to move between those scales without confusing them.

This sounds elementary. It is not.

Great powers are especially vulnerable to failures of perspective because they can sometimes survive them. A small state cannot easily ignore a large one. A large state can ignore a small one for years and then be surprised when the accumulated ignorance becomes expensive.

The first discipline of diplomacy is therefore attention.

Not agreement. Not affection. Attention.

The distinction would recur throughout Burns's career.

In Cairo, he would work inside one of America's most consequential Arab relationships. In Jerusalem, he would encounter the political and human density of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict. In Washington, Soviet affairs would place him near the transformation of the international system. Later postings would involve allies capable of saying no, adversaries capable of imposing enormous costs, and negotiations in which every word could be inspected by several governments at once.

But the basic act would remain recognizable from the small embassy: learn what the other place thinks is happening.

## Entering the profession

Burns entered the Foreign Service in the early 1980s.

The institution he joined had been shaped by the Cold War, Vietnam, the professionalization of American diplomacy, and the continuing tension between political leadership and career expertise. Its officers could spend a life moving among cultures while working for a government whose policy changed through elections.

That creates a particular professional ethic.

A career diplomat is not supposed to possess a private foreign policy.

The elected government decides. The officer advises, reports, warns, negotiates, implements and, when necessary, argues internally. After a decision is made, the task changes from winning the argument to carrying the policy faithfully unless conscience requires resignation.

This is harder than it sounds because expertise creates conviction.

The officer who has spent years on a country knows more about it than most political appointees arriving with a new administration. But knowledge does not confer democratic authority. The profession depends on knowing the difference.

It also depends on political leaders knowing the reciprocal difference: expertise is not disloyalty merely because it complicates the preferred answer.

The healthiest version of the relationship is friction without sabotage.

The diplomat makes policy harder to misunderstand before helping make it real.

## Learning to report

One of the Foreign Service's foundational crafts is reporting.

This can sound archaic in an age when leaders can read foreign newspapers instantly, watch events live, receive satellite imagery, search social media, query intelligence systems and ask machines to summarize thousands of pages before breakfast.

Yet information abundance does not eliminate the need for diplomatic reporting. It changes its purpose.

The useful cable is not valuable because nobody in Washington could otherwise learn that a minister gave a speech. It is valuable when the officer can explain why this minister used this phrase, what changed from the last formulation, who in the government is likely to object, what private conversation preceded the speech, and what action may follow.

Facts are plentiful.

Meaning remains scarce.

That scarcity would eventually become one of the defining problems of Burns's final major posting in China. By then the United States would possess extraordinary technical means of observing the world. It would also face a Chinese political system whose intentions, internal debates and red lines were matters of immense consequence and imperfect knowledge.

The tools changed.

The requirement to understand another government did not.

## The junior officer's advantage

Junior officers occupy an interesting position in an embassy.

They have little formal power, which can make them better observers.

Senior officials often encounter the host country through scheduled meetings with other senior officials. Junior diplomats spend more time closer to the institutional seams. They process cases, draft notes, prepare visits, talk to working-level counterparts, assemble briefing material, and discover which part of the official story does not quite fit the administrative reality.

This is apprenticeship through errands.

The errands teach sequence.

Before a secretary of state walks into a meeting, someone has decided what the United States wants from it. Someone has written the background. Someone has checked the names. Someone has anticipated the objection. Someone has coordinated with another bureau. Someone has asked the embassy what the host government is likely to say. Someone has determined what can be promised. Someone has prepared the press line for afterward.

High-level diplomacy rests on low-level accuracy.

A person who spends enough years inside that machinery begins to see institutions differently. The public event is no longer the event. It is the visible edge of preparation.

## Cairo next

Burns's first Foreign Service posting would take him to Cairo, where he served as vice consul and staff assistant to the ambassador.

The move was an escalation in complexity.

Egypt sat at the center of American strategy in the Arab world after the Camp David agreements and the Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty. The embassy was not a distant listening post. It was part of a major bilateral relationship involving security assistance, regional politics, Israel, the Palestinians, the Soviet-American contest and the internal pressures of Egyptian politics.

For a young officer, the lesson would become more demanding.

Attention was not enough.

Now attention had to be organized for someone with authority.

The staff assistant's craft is compression. A senior official cannot know everything. The staff has to decide what deserves the scarce minutes before a meeting, what can wait, which warning belongs on top, and what detail that looks minor may become decisive once the door closes.

This is an early encounter with one of diplomacy's enduring truths: the world produces more information than any decision-maker can absorb.

Someone always edits reality before power sees it.

The ethical responsibility in that sentence is enormous.

A good diplomat does not merely acquire information. He learns what not to omit.

That lesson begins long before the long table.
