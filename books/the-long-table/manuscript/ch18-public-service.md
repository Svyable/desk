# Chapter 18 — Public Service

At the end of a diplomatic career, the photographs remain.

A young officer becomes an ambassador. Presidents change. Secretaries change. Flags behind podiums change. The rooms grow larger, then smaller again. The archive accumulates handshakes, hearings, credentials, summits, briefings, motorcades, official portraits.

The photographs make a career look like a sequence of important people meeting other important people.

The work was mostly everything between them.

Nicholas Burns entered American diplomacy before the final decade of the Cold War and eventually represented the United States in Beijing during a new period of great-power competition. Between those points lay Cairo, Jerusalem, the State Department Secretariat, the National Security Council, the collapse of the Soviet Union, the department podium, Athens, September 11, NATO, India, Iran, Harvard, and China.

It is possible to tell that story as ascent.

That would miss the institution.

The Foreign Service is built on the premise that no individual owns the office he occupies. Ambassadors leave. Career officers rotate. Political appointees arrive. Presidents replace presidents. The seal stays on the wall.

This can make government feel impersonal.

It is also one of democracy's protections.

Authority is temporary.

The career official serves a constitutional system rather than a permanent ruler. That sounds obvious in the abstract. In practice it requires repeated acts of professional discipline. An officer may have expertise the elected official lacks. He may have served longer, read more cables, known more foreign leaders, watched earlier versions of the crisis unfold. None of that gives him democratic authority to substitute his policy for the president's.

Expertise advises power.

It does not inherit it.

The reciprocal obligation is equally important. Political leaders need institutions willing to tell them what they do not want to hear. A diplomatic service that merely decorates political preference with professional language is not serving the president well. It is withholding the thing experience is supposed to provide: friction before consequence.

The relationship is uncomfortable by design.

That discomfort runs through Burns's career.

The young officer learns to report accurately rather than impress Washington. The NSC official must distinguish the president's decision from the bureaucracy's assumptions. The spokesman has to defend policy without inventing certainty. The ambassador represents instructions while reporting honestly about how those instructions are received. The negotiator must know the difference between what Washington wants and what another sovereign government can actually accept.

None of these roles is heroic in isolation.

Together they form a theory of public service.

The theory begins with attention.

Pay attention to the country before explaining it.

Pay attention to language because a small word can carry a large political burden.

Pay attention to institutions because the person across the table may not possess the authority his title suggests.

Pay attention to domestic politics, including your own, because foreign policy is not conducted outside democracy.

Pay attention to what the other government fears, not because its fear is justified but because fear acts on policy whether justified or not.

Then comes restraint.

The diplomat lives near power but rarely owns it. This can be psychologically difficult. Access produces the sensation of agency. The official sits in meetings, travels with leaders, receives classified information, speaks with ministers, and helps draft decisions. The world appears to move through rooms he can enter.

Then history reminds him how much is outside the room.

The Berlin Wall falls because political forces vastly larger than any American official have reached a breaking point. The Soviet Union dissolves. Terrorists attack. Voters replace governments. Wars escape their planners. Alliances disagree. Negotiations fail because another country's parliament, party, military, or public refuses the bargain.

Statecraft is conducted inside limits.

A mature diplomat does not confuse acknowledging those limits with accepting helplessness.

The profession exists because choices remain inside constraint.

A government can choose whether to communicate during a crisis. It can choose whether to leave an adversary a face-saving exit. It can choose whether to consult an ally before announcing a policy that affects the ally. It can choose whether to distinguish punishment from strategy. It can choose whether to verify an assumption before acting on it.

Those choices are often small relative to the forces surrounding them.

Small choices accumulate.

This is why diplomacy resists the mythology of the single decisive meeting. The photograph at the signing ceremony compresses hundreds of prior decisions into a moment. Someone identified the possible bargain. Someone persuaded a skeptical capital. Someone revised language at midnight. Someone discovered that the foreign delegation could accept the substance but not the phrase. Someone called an ally. Someone briefed Congress. Someone kept the conversation alive after a meeting that appeared to fail.

The agreement, when it comes, belongs to the system as much as to the people whose names appear beneath it.

So does failure.

This is an important corrective to biography itself.

A life story naturally enlarges its subject. The reader follows one person through events and begins to experience history from that person's position. The temptation is to make the subject causal wherever he was present.

That would be a poor lesson from Burns's career.

He did not end the Cold War. He did not create NATO. He did not cause India's rise. He did not resolve the Iranian nuclear problem. He did not determine the course of U.S.-China competition.

He practiced diplomacy inside those histories.

That is enough.

Perhaps more than enough, because democratic societies need a language for consequential work that is neither celebrity nor command.

Public service is full of people whose contribution is real and partial.

The partiality is not an embarrassment.

It is the nature of institutions.

Burns's career also spans a change in the American idea of power. When he entered government, the United States faced a Soviet superpower and worked through alliances built for containment. By the 1990s, American primacy seemed unusually broad. After September 11, military power moved to the center of foreign policy. The wars that followed exposed its limits. By the time Burns returned to government for Beijing, the United States was again confronting a peer-scale competitor, but in a world of economic interdependence, digital networks, nuclear deterrence, and global problems that rivalry could not make disappear.

Across those eras, diplomacy repeatedly suffered from being judged against impossible standards.

If diplomacy works, the crisis that does not happen leaves no monument.

If it fails, the failure is visible.

If it produces compromise, each side can accuse its negotiators of surrendering something.

If it preserves a relationship without solving the dispute, critics can call it empty talk.

Yet the alternative to diplomacy is not firmness.

Firmness is a policy characteristic.

The alternative to diplomacy is making decisions about another government with fewer reliable ways to understand and influence it.

Sometimes that is unavoidable.

It should not be confused with strength.

The strongest states can afford channels precisely because they do not depend on conversation alone. Military power, economic leverage, technology, alliances, intelligence, geography, and domestic resilience all shape the bargaining environment. Diplomacy connects those instruments to political purpose.

Without political purpose, instruments become activity.

A sanction is imposed because sanctions are what governments impose. A deployment is ordered because movement demonstrates resolve. A speech is hardened because softness is politically dangerous. Each action may be defensible. Together they can produce a strategy no one consciously chose.

The diplomat's question is irritatingly simple.

Toward what end?

That question followed Burns from the Cold War into the age of strategic competition with China.

It also returns us to the long table.

The table is not a place where national interests disappear. It is where they become explicit enough to test.

Across it sits another government with its own history, politics, fears, ambitions, bureaucracies, errors, and internal arguments. The diplomat's obligation is not to admire those interests or concede to them.

It is to understand them accurately enough that American power acts on the world that exists.

That is harder than it sounds.

Countries tell stories about themselves. Great powers tell especially persuasive ones. They describe their actions as defensive and the other side's as provocative. They remember concessions they made and forget concessions they received. They treat their own domestic constraints as legitimate and the other side's as excuses.

Diplomacy introduces another country's reality into the room.

Not moral equivalence.

Reality.

The distinction matters.

A democracy can believe in its principles and still need to know how those principles are perceived abroad. It can oppose another government's conduct and still need to understand the political logic producing it. It can deter an adversary and speak to it at the same time.

Indeed, it may need to speak because it is deterring.

The world Burns entered as a young diplomat has vanished. The tools available to governments have multiplied. Artificial intelligence will accelerate analysis, translation, surveillance, drafting, simulation, and persuasion. Leaders will receive more information more quickly. Embassies will compete with open-source intelligence and direct digital communication. Some traditional diplomatic tasks will become easier or disappear.

One scarcity will remain.

Trusted judgment under authority.

Someone will still have to decide whether the message is real.

Someone will have to know whether the person delivering it can commit the government.

Someone will have to recognize that the elegant option on the briefing paper cannot survive the politics of the ally expected to implement it.

Someone will have to tell a president that the other side may mean what it says.

Someone will have to tell the other side that the president means what he says.

That work can be assisted.

It cannot be reduced to information processing because its object is not information alone.

Its object is political intention among human institutions.

This is why the career of a diplomat can illuminate more than the events he attended.

It shows a democratic state trying to remember itself across administrations.

The people change.

The obligations remain.

Listen before assuming. Report before flattering. Advise before deciding. Execute after decision. Keep a channel open when possible. Know whose authority you carry. Leave the office when your time is over.

Then someone else sits down.

The table remains.
