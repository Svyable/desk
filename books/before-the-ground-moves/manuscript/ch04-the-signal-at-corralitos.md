# The Signal at Corralitos

Seven kilometers is close enough to make a magnetometer feel like a witness.

When the Loma Prieta earthquake struck on October 17, 1989, a magnetic station at Corralitos, California, sat only a few kilometers from the epicentral region. The earthquake was large enough to kill people, collapse part of the Cypress Street Viaduct in Oakland, interrupt a World Series and reorder the public imagination of Bay Area seismic risk. In the scientific aftermath, one instrument record acquired a different kind of fame.

Before the earthquake, the Corralitos station had recorded unusual increases in low-frequency magnetic noise.

A. C. Fraser-Smith and colleagues published the result in 1990. The signal appeared in the ultra-low-frequency range. There was enhanced activity in the weeks before the earthquake and a particularly dramatic increase in the hours preceding it. Here, at last, seemed to be what earthquake prediction research had been waiting for: an instrument, close to a major event, showing something strange before rupture.

The case had almost everything a precursor needs to become legendary.

It was recorded, not remembered. It had a timestamp. The station was near the earthquake. The anomaly was physical rather than behavioral. It appeared before a damaging event. And the proposed frequency band offered an intuitive story: perhaps processes deep in stressed rock generate low-frequency electromagnetic energy that can propagate to the surface when higher-frequency signals would be attenuated.

For years, the Loma Prieta signal remained one of the most cited examples in seismo-electromagnetics.

Then the instrument log became part of the experiment.

In 2009, Jeffrey Thomas, Jeffrey Love and Malcolm Johnston published a reanalysis of the Corralitos case using the longer station record, operational notes and comparison data from other magnetometers. Their conclusion was severe: the famous anomaly was not a precursor. It was an artifact of sensor-system malfunction.

The reversal is worth moving through slowly because it shows how an observation can be genuine and the interpretation wrong.

Nobody needed to allege that the published waveform was fabricated. The magnetometer did record something unusual. The question was whether the thing it recorded existed in the geophysical environment outside the instrument.

That distinction is easy to forget because scientific plots look like direct windows onto nature. They are not. A plot is the end of a physical chain: source, propagation, sensor, electronics, calibration, timing, filtering, storage, processing. Any link can create structure. The more extraordinary the event at the end of the time series, the more likely we are to interpret a nearby instrument anomaly as external and meaningful.

Thomas and colleagues had an advantage the original paper could not fully exploit: time.

They examined approximately twenty-one months of Corralitos data spanning long before and after Loma Prieta. This expanded denominator revealed baseline changes at other times. They consulted the station log. They compared the behavior with magnetic records elsewhere, including Japanese data and the Fresno observatory, and with indices of global geomagnetic activity. The extended context made the precursor-shaped object look increasingly like an instrument-shaped object.

This is the quiet power of negative time.

The hours before an earthquake are visually loud on a plot because we know what is coming. Months in which nothing important happens seem like background. But the background tells us what the instrument is capable of doing by itself. An anomaly that looks unique in a one-week window can become routine once the window expands.

The Corralitos reanalysis is not a general refutation of every possible electromagnetic precursor. It is something more precise and therefore more valuable: a strong refutation of a strong case.

That difference matters. Science often gets discussed as if evidence accumulates by voting. Ten positive papers on “electromagnetic precursors” against eight negative papers leaves the field roughly tied. Real evidentiary weight does not work that way. A later analysis that identifies a specific sensor failure in the flagship case can matter more than several new correlations. It changes how the whole archive is read.

After Corralitos, instrument health could no longer be a footnote.

Every future ULF anomaly had to answer questions that seem mundane until you remember what happened. Did nearby stations see it? Was the electrode or magnetometer response stable? Did gain change? Were there gaps? Did temperature shift? Did a cable, battery or amplifier behave differently? Was the signal local, regional or global? Was there lightning, power infrastructure, rail activity or geomagnetic disturbance? Did similar pulses occur during periods without earthquakes?

Those are not skeptical decorations. They define the physical object.

The history becomes even more interesting because earthquake-generated magnetic effects are not imaginary. In 1987, Malcolm Johnston and Robert Mueller reported seismomagnetic offsets associated with the 1986 magnitude 5.9 North Palm Springs earthquake. Permanent magnetic changes appeared at stations close to the event. Coseismic changes of this sort are physically intelligible: stress changes, movement and changes in magnetization can alter measured fields.

This creates a recurring trap. If an earthquake can produce electromagnetic effects, it feels reasonable that the approach to an earthquake should produce smaller versions beforehand.

Maybe it does sometimes. The inference is not automatic.

A system can undergo an abrupt state change with strong outputs at failure while producing no scalable, externally measurable precursor. A light bulb emits light after the switch closes; that does not imply a tiny glow before closure. A brittle material can release acoustic energy when a crack runs; whether it emits a diagnostic pattern hours before catastrophic fracture depends on its microphysics and load path. Coseismic electromagnetism establishes coupling, not lead time.

This is one reason the phrase “earthquakes generate electromagnetic signals” can mislead. The sentence compresses timing. After, during and before are different scientific claims.

The Corralitos episode also exposes another temptation: proximity.

Seven kilometers from an epicenter feels causally intimate. But proximity can increase both the value and the vulnerability of a measurement. A station near the fault has a better chance of detecting a weak tectonic source. It is also embedded in a local environment with roads, power, fences, pipelines, groundwater systems and station-specific noise. A single nearby sensor cannot by itself distinguish a localized geophysical source from a localized instrumental problem.

Networks help by giving anomalies geometry.

If three stations see a coherent field change with amplitudes and phases consistent with a buried source, the problem becomes different. If one station sees it while a slightly farther station remains quiet, that could indicate rapid spatial attenuation—or a broken station. Mechanism and array design have to decide which interpretation is plausible.

The lesson sounds obvious in 2026 because distributed sensor networks are normal. It was harder when high-quality continuous instrumentation near faults was sparse and expensive. Many historical precursor claims live in this technological transition: enough instrumentation to see something interesting, not enough to triangulate it.

That makes old datasets uniquely treacherous. They are too good to ignore and too thin to resolve ambiguity.

A precursor enthusiast can look at Corralitos and say: one failed sensor does not eliminate electromagnetic precursors. Correct.

A skeptic can look at the same case and say: the most famous signal disappeared under scrutiny. Also correct.

What should change is not the belief dial but the experimental design.

After Corralitos, no serious claim should rest on a single station if a network can be built. The anomaly definition should be tested against long non-earthquake periods. Instrument metadata should be archived with the signal. Raw data should be available where possible. The analysis should include “negative earthquakes”—events near the station that did not produce the claimed precursor—and “negative anomalies”—signals that looked precursor-like but were followed by nothing.

This last category is particularly important.

A field organized around earthquakes naturally indexes its archive by earthquakes. Researchers ask what happened before Loma Prieta, before Tohoku, before Hector Mine. Prediction, however, is encountered by a system moving forward in time. The system sees anomalies first. To evaluate it honestly, we need an archive indexed by anomalies.

Take every ULF pulse above the proposed threshold, regardless of what happened later. Then ask what fraction were followed by target earthquakes. That reversal sounds trivial. It can invert the apparent result.

The same principle will later matter for radon. If you start with major earthquakes, you can usually find a well or station that changed somehow beforehand. If you start with every radon excursion and ask how many preceded major earthquakes, the predictive picture can collapse.

Corralitos taught this lesson in hardware.

There is something almost literary about the fact that a broken instrument could become a canonical precursor. The machine was built to remove human memory from the problem. It did. The waveform was objectively there. The error migrated one layer down, from witness to apparatus.

Objectivity never means the instrument cannot fool you.

It means the ways it can fool you are different and, with luck, diagnosable.

This is why the station log matters as much as the graph. Science likes clean data products. The truth often lives in the note that somebody replaced a component, observed a baseline shift, lost power or suspected a fault. Maintenance records feel external to theory until they destroy one.

Modern precursor research inherits a much richer version of this metadata problem. Satellite data carry orbital geometry, local time, instrument mode and space-weather context. GNSS-derived ionospheric measurements depend on satellite-receiver paths and background models. Machine-learning pipelines add preprocessing, class balancing and feature selection. The instrument has become an instrument-plus-software stack.

The principle remains Corralitos-simple: before calling the Earth unusual, prove the measuring system is behaving normally.

There is a danger in turning the case into a morality play about gullibility. Fraser-Smith and colleagues were not foolish to publish an unprecedented signal near a major earthquake. Extraordinary anomalies deserve investigation. The scientific failure would have been refusing to revisit the interpretation when a longer record and better diagnostics became available.

The 2009 reanalysis is therefore not an embarrassment to erase from the history of earthquake prediction. It is one of the best things that happened to the field.

It demonstrates what a successful falsification looks like when the original claim was genuinely interesting.

The signal did not vanish from the archive. It changed meaning.

Before 2009, the Corralitos trace was evidence that the crust might announce rupture magnetically.

After 2009, it became evidence that a precursor discipline must monitor its monitors.

That is less romantic.

It is also more useful.
