# The Greek Wires

An earthquake prediction looks different when it is written down before the earthquake.

That was always the strongest fact in favor of the Greek VAN program. The acronym came from the surnames of Panayiotis Varotsos, Kessar Alexopoulos and Konstantine Nomicos, physicists who began investigating transient electrical signals in Greece in the early 1980s. They called the signals SES: Seismic Electric Signals. The claim was not merely that earthquakes sometimes coincided with odd voltages. The group argued that particular transient electrical disturbances appeared before significant earthquakes and could be used, together with station selectivity and empirical relationships, to estimate where and how large an event might be.

The controversy that followed became so intense that it is difficult to reconstruct now without inheriting somebody's tone.

To admirers, VAN was a case of an unconventional method performing better than a skeptical establishment was willing to admit. Predictions were issued. Earthquakes followed some of them. The researchers developed a physical vocabulary and continued publishing for decades. To critics, the predictions were spatially and temporally loose, the statistical evaluation was inadequate, the signals were vulnerable to cultural noise, and the rules for associating an SES with a later earthquake allowed too much retrospective flexibility.

The argument mattered enough that *Geophysical Research Letters* devoted unusual attention to it in the 1990s. This was not a fringe website versus a national academy. It was an argument inside geophysics over what should count as a successful prediction.

That is why VAN belongs in this book even if one begins skeptical of the method.

The wires force the scoring problem into the open.

The basic measurement is conceptually simple. Put electrodes in the ground, separated by distance, and monitor electric potential differences. The Earth is electrically noisy. Telluric currents vary. Magnetospheric and ionospheric activity induces fields. Rain changes near-surface conductivity. Industrial systems leak current. Trains, power networks, cathodic protection systems, pumps and other human infrastructure can create transients. Electrodes age. Soil moisture changes contact conditions.

Inside that mess, VAN researchers identified particular pulses or pulse trains as SES.

Their proposed interpretation involved stress-related electrical processes developing before rupture. The group also reported that individual stations had “selectivity”: a given station tended to respond to earthquakes in particular source regions rather than simply to the nearest event. In VAN's framework, this pattern was not a nuisance. It became part of the location method, potentially related to conductive pathways or crustal structure.

Selectivity is either a deep clue or a statistician's nightmare.

If the crust really channels weak electrical signals along preferential conductive structures, then a station could be unusually sensitive to a distant source and insensitive to a closer one. That would be fascinating geology. But selectivity also expands the number of empirical relationships that can be fitted. A station that does not respond to nearby earthquakes can be said not to be selective to that region. A signal followed by an event elsewhere can help define a new selectivity map. Unless those relationships are frozen prospectively, the map can learn the catalog it is supposed to predict.

This is the recurring precursor problem in a particularly pure form: complexity can be mechanism or freedom.

VAN researchers understood that prediction methods had to be evaluated. In a 1996 *Geophysical Research Letters* paper, Varotsos and colleagues explicitly discussed principles for testing prediction, pointing to a previously published multi-year sample. Whatever one thinks of the results, the insistence that predictions be public and evaluated was scientifically important.

Critics focused on how those predictions were specified and scored.

Suppose a prediction includes a broad region containing several active faults, a magnitude range with uncertainty, and a time window of weeks. Greece is seismically active. Some earthquakes will fall into the box by chance. The correct comparison is not the fraction of alarms followed by an earthquake; it is performance relative to a null model given the alarm volume and underlying earthquake rate.

There are multiple ways to do this, and the choice matters. One can measure the fraction of target earthquakes successfully predicted, the fraction of time under alarm, probability gain relative to background, false-alarm rate, or a loss function assigning different costs to misses and alarms. A method can look impressive under one metric and mediocre under another.

Prediction evaluation therefore becomes part of the scientific mechanism.

That sounds perverse. Surely electricity in rocks should be decided by physics, not scoring rules. But VAN combined two claims: that SES are a real pre-seismic phenomenon and that they support earthquake prediction. Physics can validate the first without validating the second. Prediction scoring tests the second directly.

The distinction allows a calmer reading of the controversy.

There may be transient electric signals in the crust related to tectonic stress or fluid movement. Ground electrical measurements unquestionably respond to geophysical processes. The question is whether the signals VAN classified as SES have a stable, independently discriminable identity and enough specific relationship to future earthquakes to carry predictive information.

Cultural noise is particularly damaging here because it can imitate exactly the kind of transient being sought. A man-made current does not have to look sinusoidal, regular or obviously industrial. Switching events and grounding systems can create sharp pulses. The geography of infrastructure can also create apparent station selectivity. If a rail or power system couples into the ground differently at different stations, a repeated source can look like a stable geophysical response pattern.

Researchers working around VAN stations therefore spent effort characterizing local resistivity and field polarization. That work is scientifically useful even if prediction fails. It turns “the ground signal” into a problem of actual crustal conductivity and source geometry.

One can see why the debate became personal. If you have spent years operating electrodes in difficult terrain, repeatedly see transients before earthquakes and publish alarms in advance, a critic who says the method has no demonstrated skill can seem to be ignoring the physical labor. If you have spent years developing statistical tests for rare-event prediction, a method whose windows and associations seem elastic can look impossible to falsify.

Both experiences can be sincere.

The only resolution is a test neither side controls after it begins.

Imagine rebuilding VAN now.

We would deploy multiple electrode arrays at each site, with redundant sensors, independent power, continuous calibration and high-resolution metadata. Nearby magnetometers would help identify externally induced fields. Utility operators would provide switching logs where possible. Lightning networks, rainfall, soil moisture, groundwater and temperature would be recorded. Raw data would be streamed to an immutable public archive with a delay short enough to prevent post-event editing.

Before the first scoring period, the researchers would define an SES classifier. It could be physical, statistical or machine learned. But the version used for evaluation would be frozen. Every detected SES would generate an alarm according to a fixed rule for location, magnitude and time. The alarm would be published automatically.

Then we would wait years.

Most of the scientific value would come from the signals that fail.

An SES with no earthquake tests specificity. An earthquake with no SES tests sensitivity. A pulse seen at only one redundant electrode pair tests instrumentation. A pulse coherent across distant stations tests external electromagnetic forcing. A signal synchronized with a known industrial event tests cultural-noise rejection. The network would gradually reveal which categories the original observational program had to infer case by case.

This experiment would be expensive and unglamorous. That is probably why the precursor literature contains fewer decisive tests than arguments.

Rare-event validation requires infrastructure that can spend years proving nothing happened.

There is also a philosophical difficulty. Suppose the modern VAN network produced weak but genuine skill. Perhaps the probability of a target earthquake increases threefold after a certain SES pattern, but from a very low base rate. Would that vindicate VAN?

Scientifically, it would vindicate an important piece of the underlying claim. Operationally, maybe not. A threefold increase from one chance in a million to three in a million does not justify evacuating Athens. Yet it would demonstrate that stressed crust carries information before rupture.

The debate has often been framed too coarsely to admit that outcome.

Either VAN predicts earthquakes or it does not. Either the signals are seismic or noise. Real physical systems often give messier answers. A signal can be partly tectonic and poorly predictive. A classifier can have statistical skill and insufficient utility. A mechanism can operate only for some faults.

The most provocative aspect of VAN may therefore be its refusal to disappear cleanly. The method did not become an accepted public warning system. It also did not dissolve as neatly as the Corralitos magnetic precursor. There is no single instrument log that ends the argument. Instead there are decades of measurements, predictions, criticisms, revisions and later developments such as “natural time” analysis.

That kind of persistence can mean two opposite things.

It can mean a weak real effect is difficult to isolate.

Or it can mean a flexible research program is difficult to falsify.

Those possibilities are not distinguishable by longevity.

The history of anomalous research is full of claims defended by survival: people have studied this for forty years, therefore there must be something there. Mainstream science contains the same mistake in more respectable clothing. A field can persist because the question is hard, the signals are ambiguous, institutions exist, careers accumulate and each new instrument reopens the possibility.

Persistence is evidence of interest.

It is not evidence of truth.

Still, VAN deserves credit for forcing precursor researchers toward prospective claims. A vague story about strange voltages before old earthquakes is easy to protect. A prediction posted before the event can be scored by enemies.

That is a healthier scientific object.

The hard part is making sure the score is not renegotiated after every game.

When we leave the Greek electrodes and move into rock mechanics, the temptation will be to solve the dispute with mechanism. If we can show that stressed rock generates electrical charge, perhaps the wires were right.

Not so fast.

A laboratory mechanism can make an SES plausible. It cannot tell us which historical pulse was tectonic. It cannot recover a false alarm. It cannot narrow an alarm window that was too broad. And it cannot convert an electrical response to stress into a reliable statement about whether a fault will cascade into a damaging earthquake.

Prediction lives one level above mechanism.

The wires can be real.

The voltage can be real.

The earthquake can arrive.

We still have to prove that the middle fact knew anything about the last one.
