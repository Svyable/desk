# A Rock That Becomes a Battery

There is a version of earthquake prediction in which the hard part is finding the right sensor.

In that version, stressed rock begins to behave differently before failure. Charges move. Fluids are squeezed through pores. Resistivity changes. Magnetic properties shift. The fault, in effect, becomes an enormous laboratory specimen whose approach to rupture alters measurable physical quantities. Put the instrument close enough, make it sensitive enough, and the crust will tell you what it is doing.

This version is appealing because it turns prophecy into materials science.

It is also incomplete.

Rock really can generate electrical phenomena under stress. There is no need to invoke exotic physics to get some of them. Quartz-bearing materials can exhibit piezoelectric effects under suitable conditions. Fluid moving through charged pore surfaces can produce electrokinetic potentials. Fracturing can separate charge. Friction creates triboelectric effects. Changes in stress can alter magnetization. Chemical reactions and defect states can carry charge. The crust is not electrically dead.

The disagreement begins when these mechanisms are asked to scale.

A centimeter-sized sample can be placed between electrodes, stressed at a known rate, kept at a controlled temperature and broken while every channel is recorded. A fault nucleation zone sits kilometers underground inside a conductive, heterogeneous medium whose exact stress state is unknown. It may be partly saturated, fractured, mineralized and intersected by fluids under high pressure. Signals generated there must propagate through all of that before reaching an instrument at the surface.

The phrase “stressed rock emits electricity” skips this journey.

Friedemann Freund's positive-hole hypothesis is one of the more ambitious attempts to bridge it. In simplified form, the proposal begins with defects in oxygen-bearing minerals. Under stress, certain dormant defect structures can activate mobile electronic charge carriers associated with oxygen anions—described in this literature as positive holes. These carriers may propagate through rock, potentially over distances much larger than an individual crack. If enough reach the surface, the theory proposes consequences including surface potentials, ionization of air and perhaps infrared or luminous phenomena.

The attraction is obvious. One mechanism appears capable of connecting electrical precursors, earthquake lights and even some atmospheric or ionospheric claims.

A unifying mechanism is exactly where we should become more demanding.

The first question is replication. Can independent laboratories produce the same charge carriers under controlled stress in relevant rocks? Under which mineralogies? How does water change the effect? What temperatures and confining pressures matter? How repeatable is the current magnitude? Does the material have to be freshly cut or otherwise prepared in ways that natural rock at depth is not?

The second question is budget.

If a future magnitude 6 earthquake has a nucleation region of some characteristic size, how much rock enters the required stress regime before rupture? How many charge carriers per unit volume are activated? How quickly? What current density follows? How much is lost before the signal reaches a surface station? A qualitative effect that is easy to measure across ten centimeters can become physically negligible after ten kilometers of attenuation and leakage.

The third question is timing.

The precursor problem needs the effect before dynamic rupture. A mechanism triggered primarily by rapid cracking during rupture may explain coseismic electromagnetic emissions or light without helping prediction. If stress activation occurs during slow loading, how early does it begin? Does the signal grow monotonically? Does it saturate? Does it appear every time the rock reaches a certain stress, including events that stop before becoming large earthquakes?

The last question is the one mechanism enthusiasts often postpone: specificity.

A fault can be highly stressed for years. Rocks crack all the time. Fluids move for reasons unrelated to imminent rupture. Tides, rainfall, pumping and seasonal hydrology alter pore pressure. If a proposed electrical process responds to generic stress and microfracture, it may be an excellent stress sensor and a terrible earthquake predictor.

This is not a semantic distinction. Imagine a borehole electrode that reliably detects increased microcracking on a fault. The signal doubles during periods of elevated stress. Most of those periods end with small events or aseismic relaxation. Once every twenty years one develops into a damaging rupture. The electrode is measuring something real about the system and still cannot tell a city to evacuate.

The central difficulty is the difference between preparation and commitment.

Does a large earthquake pass through a physical state that small earthquakes and non-earthquake deformation do not? If so, a precursor may exist in principle. If not—if large rupture emerges from a cascade after nucleation begins—then sensors can detect loading and damage without knowing the eventual magnitude.

This problem is not solved by adding more sensitive instruments.

Sensitivity can make it worse. The more sensitive the sensor, the more small processes it sees. A high-gain electric field instrument does not reveal only earthquake preparation. It reveals weather, groundwater, infrastructure, contact potentials and every minor crustal disturbance the older instrument ignored.

Scientific instruments do not remove noise. They reveal new kinds of it.

The same is true of laboratory rock physics. Better instrumentation has shown that brittle failure is preceded by rich microstructural activity under some controlled conditions: acoustic emissions, crack growth, changes in velocity and electrical properties. It is tempting to map this directly onto faults. But a laboratory failure has one enormous advantage over an earthquake: the experimenter knows the boundary conditions.

The load is applied by a machine. The sample geometry is known. The confining pressure is controlled. Failure is often inevitable because the machine keeps increasing stress until the specimen breaks.

Earth's crust has no obligation to continue loading a particular patch in the same clean way. Stress redistributes. Faults creep. Fluids change effective normal stress. Nearby events intervene. The system is open.

This is why laboratory success can create false confidence in field prediction. A rock cylinder that gives a lovely accelerating precursor under monotonic loading tells us that failure physics can produce advance signals. It does not tell us that a natural fault follows the same trajectory or that we know where to place the cylinder's equivalent sensors.

The best use of the laboratory is narrower and more powerful: force proposed mechanisms to make quantitative promises.

If electrokinetic flow is invoked, measure the streaming potential coefficient under relevant salinity and pressure. Estimate fluid velocities required to produce the field signal. If piezoelectricity is invoked, account for crystal orientations and the ability of opposing domains in bulk rock to cancel. If positive holes are invoked, measure charge mobility, lifetime and propagation through realistic fractured media. If triboelectric fracture is invoked, determine whether the source spectrum can survive conductive attenuation to the station.

Every mechanism should eventually collide with an order-of-magnitude calculation.

This is the part of anomalous research that is often missing because it threatens the romance on both sides. Enthusiasts prefer qualitative plausibility. Skeptics sometimes prefer to dismiss the entire category because the field evidence is messy. A budget can embarrass both. It may show that one popular mechanism is impossible while another neglected one is large enough to deserve a network.

Earthquake lights are an example. If stress-activated charge reaching the surface is supposed to ionize air, then the model should imply an electric field, current and optical emission. Those quantities can be compared with breakdown thresholds and camera sensitivity. A mechanism strong enough to make a visible glow should probably leave other signatures. If those signatures are absent, the interpretation weakens.

Conversely, if a multi-sensor station records a luminous event, a transient surface electric field and a local atmospheric-ion change with timing inconsistent with grid arcing, the case becomes much stronger. The point of mechanism is not to explain a mystery after the fact. It is to tell you what else to measure.

The crust offers another class of effects that are less dramatic but more firmly grounded: resistivity changes associated with fluids, cracks and stress. Electrical resistivity tomography and magnetotellurics routinely reveal subsurface structure. Laboratory resistivity can change when cracks open or fluid connectivity changes. Again, the leap from “resistivity responds to fault-zone state” to “resistivity predicts earthquakes” is the difficult one.

The fault is always changing state.

This may be the most important correction to the battery metaphor. A battery has terminals and a designed chemical reaction. A fault does not. The sensor sees superposed processes from volumes it cannot isolate cleanly. A voltage measured between two electrodes can represent local soil chemistry, induced regional fields, infrastructure or a distant geological source traveling through conductive structure. There is no label on the electron saying PRECURSOR.

Source localization is therefore as important as detection.

Networks with multiple baselines can estimate field direction. Magnetotelluric arrays can distinguish external from internal sources under some conditions. Borehole sensors can get below near-surface noise. Combining electric and magnetic measurements constrains source impedance. Hydrological sensors can identify fluid-related events. Modern precursor work becomes credible as it starts resembling ordinary geophysical inversion rather than anomaly collection.

There is a subtle shift here.

The more rigorous the field becomes, the less likely it is to produce a single magic signal.

Instead of “earthquake electricity,” one gets a set of conditional processes: electrokinetic potentials under certain fluid movements, stress-magnetic changes in certain rocks, charge generation during fracture, surface effects under particular geology. This fragmentation can feel like retreat. It is actually progress. Universal categories are being replaced by mechanisms with domains of validity.

The prediction dream may not survive that process intact.

If different tectonic environments produce different precursors, a global predictor becomes unlikely. A shallow continental strike-slip event in dry crystalline rock may not resemble a subduction megathrust beneath ocean sediments. A volcanic earthquake with active fluids is another system again. The demand for one signal before “an earthquake” may be as misguided as demanding one medical biomarker before every kind of organ failure.

This heterogeneous view helps explain a puzzling feature of the literature: occasional strong cases amid poor replication.

The standard skeptical interpretation is that the strong cases are chance, artifact or publication bias. Often they may be. But another possibility is that precursor production is conditional. A particular fluid pathway, fault geometry or rock type can generate a large electrical or chemical signal that most earthquakes do not. If researchers average across all tectonic settings, the effect disappears. If they study one favorable case retrospectively, it looks universal.

This possibility is scientifically interesting and operationally awkward.

A predictor that works only when you already know the hidden subsurface conditions has limited value. But identifying those conditions could still reveal how faults prepare.

That is why this chapter refuses both easy endings.

We cannot say that rock physics validates the old precursor claims. Laboratory electrical effects do not authenticate historical SES pulses, lights or magnetic anomalies.

We also cannot say that because prediction has failed, stressed-rock electromagnetism is irrelevant. The crust contains real electromechanical and electrochemical coupling. Earthquakes demonstrably produce electromagnetic consequences. The open question is what portion of those processes develops early, escapes the source, and carries information specific to impending dynamic rupture.

A useful experiment would not begin by asking whether a rock can make a current.

We know many can.

It would ask whether the current contains information unavailable from ordinary measures of stress and seismicity, whether that information survives field propagation, and whether the same current appears during the countless episodes of deformation that do not become damaging earthquakes.

The battery metaphor fails because a fault does not store a clean voltage waiting for us to attach leads.

But sometimes, under stress, the rock really does become electrical.

That fact is not the solution.

It is the beginning of a much harder measurement problem.
