# L'Aquila and the Cost of Saying Something

The earthquake prediction problem becomes morally different when somebody has to speak into a microphone.

For months before April 6, 2009, the central Italian city of L'Aquila and its surroundings had experienced a seismic swarm. Small earthquakes were common enough to frighten residents and unusual enough to create demand for an answer. Was a larger earthquake coming?

There was another ingredient: a technician named Giampaolo Giuliani had been promoting claims that radon measurements could anticipate earthquakes. His public warnings and predictions became part of the atmosphere around the swarm. Authorities and scientists faced two risks at once. An unsupported alarm could cause disruption and panic. A falsely reassuring message could leave people less prepared for the real event.

At 3:32 in the morning on April 6, a magnitude 6.3 earthquake struck. More than three hundred people died.

The prosecutions that followed became one of the most distorted stories in modern science.

A common retelling says Italian scientists were convicted because they failed to predict the earthquake. That is not an adequate description of the case. The legal proceedings focused on risk communication surrounding a March 31 meeting of the Major Risks Commission and related public statements. The first-instance convictions were later largely overturned on appeal; the legal history is complicated and should not be reduced to “scientists jailed for not predicting an earthquake.”

The simplification persists because it converts a difficult institutional failure into an attack on scientific uncertainty.

The real problem is harder: what should officials say when the probability is elevated but still small, public anxiety is high, a prediction claim is circulating, and the science cannot provide a deterministic answer?

This is where the distinction between prediction and forecast leaves the textbook.

A swarm changes information. Earthquake occurrence is clustered. After one earthquake, the probability of another is not the same as it was during an arbitrary quiet week. A sequence of small events can therefore justify a time-dependent forecast. But an elevated probability can remain low in absolute terms, and most swarms do not culminate in destructive earthquakes.

This is difficult to communicate because human language dislikes low-probability high-consequence states.

“No danger” is too strong.

“A big earthquake is coming” is unsupported.

“There is an elevated probability, but a damaging event remains unlikely in the short term” is scientifically closer and psychologically unsatisfying.

People want a recommendation.

Science often has only a distribution.

After L'Aquila, an international commission chaired by Thomas Jordan developed recommendations for Operational Earthquake Forecasting. The resulting work is one of the most useful documents in this entire subject because it begins from the assumption that earthquake probabilities can change and asks how those changes should be used without pretending they are deterministic predictions.

Operational forecasting is deliberately modest. It updates probabilities using seismicity and other validated information. It insists on prospective testing. It recognizes that communication is part of the system. It separates scientific probability from the decisions authorities make about that probability.

This separation is essential.

A probability does not contain its own policy.

A one-percent chance of a damaging earthquake in the next day could be extraordinarily high relative to background and still mean a ninety-nine-percent chance that no such earthquake occurs. Whether to close a bridge, evacuate a school or tell people to sleep outside depends on the cost of the action, vulnerability, duration of exposure and the consequences of false alarms.

Different actions should have different thresholds.

This is where the dream of a binary precursor can be dangerous. “Radon anomaly: yes or no” sounds like a decision. In reality, even a validated precursor would need calibration. How much does the anomaly change the probability? How reliable is that estimate in this region? Does the current seismic swarm add independent information or duplicate the same underlying process? What action becomes rational at the resulting probability?

Without those steps, a precursor becomes a siren without a scale.

Giuliani's radon claims illustrate the problem. A prediction system can be sincerely motivated and physically plausible at the level of mechanism, yet public warning requires a much higher evidentiary standard. If a method issues alarms that move in space or time after events unfold, or if the full false-alarm history is unavailable, authorities cannot responsibly treat it as an operational predictor merely because one later event occurs near a publicized concern.

The burden is not ideological. It comes from the cost of being wrong.

But institutional caution has costs too.

A public agency that repeatedly emphasizes uncertainty can slide into language that people hear as reassurance. Officials may fear panic, economic disruption or the reputational cost of false alarms. A low-probability warning can be softened until its practical meaning disappears. If a damaging event then occurs, the earlier communication looks complacent in retrospect.

This asymmetry can encourage the worst possible language: confident enough to calm people, vague enough to deny being a prediction.

Risk communication needs a different standard.

Do not say what people should feel. Say what changed.

The current earthquake rate is above normal. Most swarms end without a damaging event. A damaging event is more likely during this period than during ordinary background conditions. Existing building vulnerability remains the dominant risk. Here are the actions that are useful regardless of whether a larger earthquake occurs.

That last category is crucial.

Move heavy objects away from beds. Know how to shut off gas if necessary. Keep exits clear. Review family contact plans. Inspect vulnerable buildings. Emergency services can check staffing and communications. None of these actions requires pretending a mainshock has been predicted.

The best risk response is often the one least sensitive to the forecast error.

This is a general rule for uncertain hazards. When prediction is weak, favor reversible, low-cost actions with value across outcomes. As probability and confidence rise, more disruptive actions become rational.

Earthquake precursor enthusiasts sometimes interpret this framework as institutional cowardice. If the signal is real, why not warn?

Because “real” is not the decision variable.

A signal can be real and weakly informative. A radon excursion might double the odds of an earthquake and still leave the absolute probability tiny. A probabilistic forecast can be scientifically excellent while supporting no extraordinary public action. Conversely, a modest probability increase can matter greatly for a highly vulnerable structure where the precaution is cheap.

The action lives in the loss function.

L'Aquila forced seismology to confront this in public. It also produced a chilling concern: will scientists become less willing to communicate uncertain risks if they fear legal consequences?

The answer cannot be to retreat into silence. Silence is also communication.

Nor can it be to speak with artificial certainty to avoid misunderstanding. Uncertainty is the substance of the problem.

What institutions can do is make roles explicit. Scientists estimate probabilities and describe uncertainties. Civil-protection authorities decide actions using those probabilities and other considerations. Communications staff translate without erasing the numbers. Records preserve what was known at the time.

This last point matters because disasters create hindsight pressure.

After a destructive earthquake, every earlier statement is reread as if the speaker should have known the outcome. A 5 percent probability sounds obviously alarming once the 5 percent occurs. A 95 percent non-occurrence probability sounds obviously reassuring before it does. The same number changes emotional meaning after the event.

Prospective protocols protect institutions from that distortion in the same way preregistration protects precursor science.

Decide in advance what probability thresholds trigger which internal reviews, public messages and actions. Then an event cannot retroactively rewrite the standard.

There is a symmetry here with scientific prediction.

For a sensor, freeze the anomaly rule before the earthquake.

For an agency, freeze the communication rule before the crisis.

Both are defenses against hindsight.

L'Aquila also demonstrates why the failure of deterministic prediction does not make public communication easier. In some ways it makes it harder. If science could say “magnitude 6.3 at 03:32 tomorrow,” the action would be obvious. Instead, officials inhabit a continuum of changing but incomplete risk.

This is the environment where charlatans, overconfident outsiders and overconfident institutions all gain an advantage. Certainty is easier to communicate than calibration.

The public, understandably, may reward the person willing to say yes or no.

Science has to compete with a less satisfying sentence.

We do not know whether the larger earthquake will occur. The probability has changed. Here is how much. Here is why. Here is what remains useful to do.

That sentence is not weakness.

It is the form responsible knowledge takes when the ground has not moved yet.
