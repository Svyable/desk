# Contradiction and incentive audit

## The field's productive contradictions

### Earthquakes clearly make electromagnetic signals / convincing electromagnetic precursors remain unproven

These statements are not inconsistent. A fault that slips can change stress, strain, fluid pressure, magnetization, electric currents and surface conditions. The difficult proposition is that a measurable version of one of those processes necessarily escapes the source *before* dynamic rupture in a form that can be distinguished from ordinary background activity.

### The Corralitos anomaly was striking / the instrument record undermines it

The pre-Loma Prieta ULF signal is visually and historically compelling. Its later reanalysis is therefore more important than a weak null result. Thomas, Love and Johnston did not merely say the signal was “probably noise”; they used a longer time series, station log and external magnetic data to identify baseline changes and instrument problems. The chapter must let the original case feel convincing before showing why the expanded denominator matters.

### Radon can respond to crustal and hydrological changes / radon does not therefore predict earthquakes

Radon is a real gas with real transport physics. Stress, cracking, water movement, barometric pressure, soil moisture and temperature can all change measured concentrations. The same sensitivity that makes radon an interesting crustal probe makes it a difficult diagnostic alarm. Recent strong case reports should be presented with their actual statistics and then tested against station-history false positives, environmental corrections and prospective rules.

### Satellite statistics can show an association / association can still be useless for prediction

A superposed-epoch signal can be scientifically important even when event-by-event classification is poor. If hundreds of earthquakes produce a small average ion-density change, that might reveal coupling physics without providing a useful warning. Conversely, a case study with a dramatic anomaly can have no predictive value if similar anomalies happen routinely when no earthquake follows.

### Earthquake lights may include a real tectonic subset / many earthquake lights are plainly not tectonic

Grid arcing, transformers, lightning, aurora, meteors, vehicle lights and camera artifacts populate the same visual category. The correct question is not whether “earthquake lights are real” as a single binary. It is whether some tightly defined luminous observations co-located in time and space with faulting survive mundane explanations and can be attached to a quantitative mechanism.

### Animals can respond before people / animals have not been shown to forecast rupture days in advance

A dog noticing a P wave seconds before its owner is not paranormal and is not prediction. That ordinary mechanism can inflate the folklore by creating genuine “the animal knew first” experiences. Longer-horizon claims require baseline behavior and prospective recording because every animal does strange things sometimes.

## Incentives that can distort the evidence ecology

### The spectacular-case incentive

A successful-looking anomaly before a catastrophic earthquake is publishable, memorable and easy to narrate. The same waveform on a Tuesday when nothing happens is usually not. This asymmetry selects for hits unless continuous archives are analyzed.

### The rescue-theory incentive

When a method misses, it is tempting to change the magnitude threshold, radius, time window, anomaly definition or environmental exclusion after the fact. Some changes are scientifically justified. The problem is that each extra degree of freedom can rescue a story while destroying the meaning of its nominal significance.

### The institutional-skepticism incentive

Mainstream institutions also have incentives. A public earthquake alarm has enormous costs. Agencies can rationally demand a much higher evidentiary bar for a warning system than a journal demands for an interesting physical correlation. That asymmetry should not be misdescribed as suppression. It does mean “not operationally useful” and “physically impossible” are different claims.

### The commercial-warning incentive

Private sensor networks and prediction services can monetize fear, exclusivity and claims of proprietary signals. Company press material can document what a service claims but cannot substitute for an independently scored forecast ledger.

### The national-program incentive

China, Japan, France and other states have funded unconventional precursor monitoring at different times. Program existence proves institutional interest, not efficacy. Conversely, termination of a program does not prove every measured effect was fictitious.

### The hindsight incentive

After a disaster, witnesses remember odd lights, animal behavior, headaches, smells, weather, water changes and electronic disturbances. Some recollections may be accurate. But the earthquake itself changes which pre-event details become salient and reportable. Contemporary timestamps therefore carry much more evidentiary weight than later memory.

## Claims this manuscript must not make

- That earthquake prediction has been secretly solved.
- That any government satellite program demonstrates operational prediction.
- That the VAN method has been validated merely because some alarms preceded earthquakes.
- That positive-hole theory is accepted consensus or explains all earthquake lights.
- That radon anomalies are worthless because no universal predictor exists.
- That the Tohoku TEC debate is settled simply by citing the first or last paper.
- That L'Aquila defendants were prosecuted merely for not predicting an earthquake.
- That animal behavior is evidence of an unknown sense when P-wave response or ordinary behavioral variability is sufficient.
- That the absence of an operational predictor proves there is no earthquake preparation physics.

## Editorial pressure test

Every chapter should be able to answer four questions without retreating into adjectives:

1. What exactly was measured?
2. Was it measured before the earthquake, and was that timing fixed independently of the analysis?
3. How often did comparable measurements occur when the target earthquake did not?
4. What physical or statistical observation would make the favored interpretation fail?

If a chapter cannot answer the third question, it should say so plainly. The missing denominator is itself part of the story.
