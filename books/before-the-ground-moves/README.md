## Reader promise

Earthquake prediction has accumulated a peculiar archive: lights over faults, voltage steps in Greek hills, radon excursions in wells, magnetic pulses, animals leaving burrows, and changes in the ionosphere hundreds of kilometers above the ground. Some observations are mistakes. Some are artifacts. Some happen after rupture and get remembered as before. Some are physically real and still fail the harder test of becoming a prediction.

This book follows that distinction all the way down. It asks what the Earth actually does before it breaks, which proposed precursors survived better instrumentation, why retrospective anomalies are so much easier to find than prospective alarms, and whether the failure of prediction means there is no preparatory physics or merely that the relevant physics is intermittent, local, nonunique, and drowned in a very loud planet.

## Distinct contribution

The book treats earthquake-precursor research neither as a museum of pseudoscience nor as a suppressed breakthrough. It puts several neglected evidence streams in the same frame: Parkfield's instrumented prediction experiment, the Corralitos magnetic-precursor reversal, Greece's VAN seismic-electric-signal controversy, radon networks, earthquake lights, laboratory rock electrification, DEMETER and later satellite observations, the Tohoku ionospheric-TEC dispute, animal claims, and the post-L'Aquila shift toward operational probabilistic forecasting. The collision matters because each field contains phenomena that can be real without being diagnostic.

The book's working thesis is that the deepest obstacle is **specificity rather than mere detectability**. Stressed rock, moving groundwater, coseismic deformation, atmospheric waves, geomagnetic weather, infrastructure, and instrument drift all create measurable signals. A useful precursor must do much more: appear before rupture often enough, remain absent often enough when no damaging rupture follows, scale in a physically defensible way, survive prospective analysis choices, and improve decisions after the cost of false alarms is counted.

## Strongest competing explanation

The strongest skeptical answer is that damaging earthquakes do not have a practically observable deterministic preparation phase at all. Fault rupture may be a cascade whose eventual size is undecidable from the available pre-rupture state. On that view, the long precursor literature is mainly selection bias, flexible analysis, environmental noise, instrumental failure, ordinary tectonic or hydrological variability, and post hoc storytelling. This book takes that possibility seriously. It also asks a narrower question the categorical skeptical position does not settle by itself: why some repeatable electrical, chemical, luminous, and ionospheric effects cluster around earthquakes even when they fail as predictors.

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified / documented:** earthquake occurrence and instrumental records; coseismic electromagnetic and ionospheric effects; the Parkfield experiment; the Corralitos sensor record and later reanalysis; DEMETER mission data; published radon measurements; documented earthquake-light reports; laboratory rock-electrification results; official forecasting and prediction assessments.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** some pre-event electrical, radon, ion-density, and luminous anomalies that survive basic controls but lack enough prospective denominator data or independent replication to establish predictive value.
3. **Disputed / unconfirmed:** VAN's claimed operational success; robust pre-earthquake TEC anomalies; reliable animal warning behavior; specific earthquake-light mechanisms; claims that individual radon excursions constitute actionable predictions.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** different precursor classes may be intermittent products of particular geology, fluid pathways, stress states, and atmospheric conditions rather than a universal countdown signal; multi-parameter monitoring may therefore be scientifically useful even if deterministic prediction remains impossible.
