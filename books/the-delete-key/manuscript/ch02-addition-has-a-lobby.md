# Addition Has a Lobby

Every new thing arrives with an advocate.

A proposed product has a product manager. A new law has a sponsor. A new office has a director waiting to be named. A new software service has an engineer who wants to ship it. A new report has somebody who asked for it. A new meeting has an organizer. A new field in a database has a team that needs the value. A new grant program has applicants before the first dollar is spent.

Creation comes with a constituency attached.

Removal usually begins with a stranger.

The person who notices that the report has not changed a decision in three years may not be the person who requested it. The engineer who discovers that a service appears unused may have joined long after the service was built. The official reviewing an old rule may have had nothing to do with the emergency that produced it. The employee wondering why a weekly meeting still exists is often the least powerful person in the room.

This creates a political asymmetry before anyone has an argument.

The addition has a story about what it will do.

The deletion has to tell a story about what will not happen.

That second story is harder to sell.

Suppose a company has a monthly executive report assembled by six people. It was created after a bad quarter years ago. At the time, the report answered an urgent question. Every month since, people have gathered numbers, reconciled definitions, copied charts, checked formatting, circulated a draft, corrected the draft, and sent the final document.

Nobody can remember the last decision the report changed.

Still, cancelling it is surprisingly difficult.

The analyst who builds one chart does not own the report. The finance team owns another section but cannot cancel the whole package. The executive whose predecessor requested it may skim it and assume somebody else relies on the detail. Nobody wants to be the person who ends a reporting process two weeks before the one month when it might have mattered.

So the report survives.

Its labor is distributed. Its cancellation risk is concentrated.

This pattern appears everywhere because organizations do not merely accumulate objects. They accumulate reasons.

A thing is created because of a reason that is vivid at the moment of creation. A customer complained. An audit found a gap. A system failed. A regulator asked a question. A manager wanted visibility. A competitor launched a feature. A security incident exposed a weakness.

The response becomes durable.

The reason fades.

Years later, the institution remembers the response better than the condition that produced it.

That is how a workaround becomes a process.

It is how a temporary permission becomes an entitlement.

It is how a crisis committee becomes an office.

It is how a dashboard becomes a ritual.

The mechanism is not stupidity. It is incentives.

Imagine two managers.

The first proposes a new initiative. If it succeeds, there will be a launch, a metric, perhaps a promotion. If it fails modestly, the organization may call it an experiment.

The second proposes deleting an old process. If the deletion succeeds perfectly, nothing happens. If the deletion causes one important failure, everybody can identify the decision that removed the safeguard.

The upside of addition is legible.

The upside of subtraction is counterfactual.

This is why institutions often require more certainty to stop something than they required to start it.

A new control may be approved because it could prevent a recurrence. Years later, removing the control may require proof that the recurrence cannot happen.

A new data field may be added because it might be useful. Removing it may require checking every report, integration, export, model, and user that might still depend on it.

A new committee may be formed because coordination seems inadequate. Dissolving it may require persuading the committee itself that coordination no longer needs the committee.

Addition can be speculative.

Deletion is expected to be forensic.

That difference compounds.

Each new object creates more surfaces for future dependency. The report becomes an input to another report. The database field enters a model. The policy is cited by a procedure. The procedure becomes part of training. The training becomes part of certification. The certification becomes a contractual requirement.

After enough time, the original reason no longer matters. The thing is supported by the dependencies that grew around it.

The institution has manufactured a lobby.

Not necessarily a lobby in the formal political sense. There may be no money changing hands, no public campaign, no deliberate resistance. A lobby can be as simple as a collection of people for whom continued existence is easier than change.

Familiarity is a constituency.

Expertise is a constituency.

A budget line is a constituency.

A workflow is a constituency.

A contract renewal date is a constituency.

An API integration is a constituency.

Every dependency votes for continuation because continuation asks nothing new of it.

This helps explain why organizations can spend years discussing simplification while becoming more complicated.

Simplification is usually treated as a project layered on top of the existing system.

A task force is created to reduce task forces.

A dashboard is built to measure dashboard reduction.

A new approval process governs changes to old approval processes.

The organization attempts subtraction through addition because addition is the operation it knows how to perform.

There is something almost comic about this until you see how rational it is.

A new simplification program has an owner. It has a budget. It has milestones. It can be announced. The absence of twelve obsolete procedures has none of those properties unless the organization deliberately creates a way to value absence.

That is the deeper problem.

We do not have enough institutions whose product is subtraction.

Garbage collectors in programming languages are built to reclaim memory. Identity systems can be designed to revoke credentials. Records schedules can specify when information moves from active use to archive or destruction. Asset-management systems can include decommissioning. Legislatures can require review. Cloud platforms can attach expiration to temporary resources.

In each case, removal becomes more reliable when it is not left to individual heroics.

The system has a role for endings.

Most organizational life does not.

Projects have kickoff meetings. Few have deletion meetings five years later.

Contracts have effective dates. Renewal is often easier to operationalize than retirement.

Employees receive access through onboarding checklists. Offboarding is better than it used to be in many organizations, but the difference reveals the principle: permissions disappear reliably only when somebody designed a process specifically for making them disappear.

The default does not remove them for us.

Defaults matter because attention is scarce.

Anything that requires an affirmative act to continue competes for attention at the moment of renewal. Anything that continues automatically can survive without winning that competition.

This is why automatic renewal is so commercially powerful. It converts inattention into persistence.

Institutions do the same thing to themselves.

A recurring meeting is an automatic renewal contract with everyone's calendar.

A standing report is an automatic renewal contract with the analysts who produce it.

A legacy system is an automatic renewal contract with the operations budget.

A policy without a review date is an automatic renewal contract with the future.

Nobody signs again.

Continuation happens because nobody stopped it.

The obvious answer is to attach expiration dates to everything.

That answer is too simple.

A hospital should not discover that a critical protocol vanished because its renewal email went to a former employee. A bridge does not become safe to ignore when an inspection schedule expires. Constitutional rights should not depend on a calendar reminder. Historical records should not disappear because their original administrator retired.

Some things deserve strong persistence.

The point is not that every object should expire.

The point is that every object should have a persistence model.

That model can say permanent. It can say review every five years. It can say expire after ninety days unless renewed. It can say archive after seven years. It can say retain while a legal obligation remains. It can say destroy when the encryption key is retired. It can say keep indefinitely because this record is part of the public memory.

What matters is that continuation is a design decision rather than residue.

This changes how we think about ownership.

When organizations ask who owns a system, they usually mean who is responsible for keeping it working.

There is another question.

Who owns deciding when it should stop?

Those are not necessarily the same person.

The operator of a system can have every incentive to preserve it. The business owner may understand whether its purpose still exists but not its technical dependencies. The risk team may understand what could go wrong but not bear the cost of indefinite retention. The archivist may know what evidence must survive. The security team may know which credentials and data copies remain exposed.

A mature retirement decision often requires all of them.

That sounds expensive because it is.

Ending things safely can cost more than starting them.

This is the paradox at the center of the book. Creation feels like the expensive operation because it produces the visible object. But once an object is woven into a system, removing it can require understanding the system more deeply than its creator ever did.

The creator only needed to know enough to connect the new thing.

The remover must know what every connection now means.

That is why the most dangerous moment for accumulation may be the moment creation becomes extremely cheap.

Artificial intelligence is reducing the cost of producing many kinds of organizational artifacts. Code can be generated. Reports can be drafted. dashboards can be proposed. Policies can be rewritten. Workflows can be assembled. Agents can create tickets, branches, documents, experiments, accounts, and services.

The marginal act of addition can approach zero while the marginal act of institutional understanding does not.

A company may soon be able to create ten thousand useful little automations in the time it once took to build ten.

That sounds like a productivity miracle.

It is also a maintenance problem with a delay fuse.

Who knows which automations are still running six months later?

Who knows which credentials they hold?

Who knows which decisions depend on their output?

Who knows which generated reports have become unofficial policy?

Who knows which temporary databases contain copies of customer information?

Who knows which agent-created service is now called by another agent-created service that nobody explicitly designed?

Cheap creation increases the number of candidates for persistence.

It does not increase the amount of human attention available to reconsider them.

Unless removal becomes cheaper too, the asymmetry widens.

This suggests a different design goal for the age of abundant intelligence.

Do not merely ask whether an agent can create something.

Ask whether the system can later explain why that thing still exists.

A generated object should carry more than a creation timestamp. It should carry purpose, owner, dependencies, permissions, retention class, review date, and perhaps a predicted cost of continued existence.

Creation should leave behind the information required for eventual deletion.

That is a radical change in mindset because it treats the end of a thing as part of its beginning.

Buildings sometimes have decommissioning plans before construction. Financial instruments have maturity dates. Cryptographic certificates expire. Temporary cloud credentials can be short-lived by design. Good database migrations consider rollback. Nuclear facilities are forced to think about decommissioning because the consequences of pretending the end will never arrive are too large.

Most of civilization does not work this way.

We build first and leave the future to discover how to stop.

The future then inherits a system full of artifacts whose creators are gone and whose reasons are partially forgotten.

That future is us.

The next time somebody proposes adding one more process, one more service, one more field, one more report, one more permission, one more committee, or one more rule, the right question is not only whether the addition is useful.

Ask what its lobby will look like in ten years.

Ask who will be able to turn it off.

Ask what evidence they will need.

Ask whether the thing is being born with an ending.

Because addition always has an advocate.

If subtraction is going to have one too, we will have to design the job.
