# Laws That Outlive Their Reasons

A law can remain perfectly valid after the world that produced it has changed.

That is not a bug in law. Stability is part of what makes law useful. People need to know that obligations, rights, contracts, and procedures will not vanish because the weather changed or a new administration dislikes an old decision.

But stability creates a second problem.

A rule can outlive the factual conditions that once made it sensible.

The easiest response is to add another rule around it.

An exception handles a new technology. Guidance handles the exception. A reporting requirement handles the guidance. Software encodes the reporting requirement. Training explains the software. Soon the original provision has become the center of a small administrative solar system.

Repeal now looks more expensive than continued accommodation.

This is how legal accumulation becomes operational accumulation.

The text of a law is only the beginning. Rules propagate into forms, databases, licensing systems, contracts, enforcement routines, court decisions, audit practices, vendor products, professional training, and public expectations.

That is why repeal can be both legally simple and institutionally difficult.

Strike the paragraph and the descendants remain.

A serious retrospective review therefore asks more than whether the original policy still sounds reasonable. It asks what the policy currently does.

Who bears its costs?

Which harms does it prevent?

What changed since enactment?

Which other rules now cover the same ground?

What infrastructure assumes the rule exists?

What transition obligations would repeal create?

This is not anti-regulation. It is maintenance.

Every complex rule system needs maintenance because rules interact over time. A regulation that was coherent alone can become redundant after ten later regulations arrive. A threshold can drift under inflation. A definition can become obsolete after an industry reorganizes. A reporting requirement can persist after another system begins collecting better data.

The mere fact that no one has repealed a rule tells us little about whether anyone has recently justified it.

That difference matters.

Persistence can mean continued democratic endorsement.

It can also mean nobody owned the review.

Sunset clauses attempt to force that distinction into the open by making some rules expire unless renewed. Regulatory-stock reviews take another approach by examining existing rules as a portfolio rather than treating each one as permanent background.

Both mechanisms have limits.

A legislature or agency can be flooded with expirations and renew everything mechanically. Review can become a ceremonial box-check. A sunset can destabilize regulated parties that made long-lived investments in reliance on the rule. Powerful groups can dominate review while less organized beneficiaries remain invisible.

Deletion governance is still governance.

The remedy for accumulated rules cannot be a machine that automatically removes them when a timer reaches zero.

The useful principle is narrower: rules need lifecycle states.

New.

Temporary.

Under review.

Permanent by design.

Superseded.

Repealed but operationally unwinding.

Archived as historical authority.

These states already exist in fragments across legal systems. Making them more explicit would help institutions distinguish legal memory from current instruction.

Technology can help, especially with propagation.

Imagine a rule registry that does not merely store legal text but links provisions to the forms, software validations, reporting fields, guidance documents, and controls that implement them. When the rule changes, the institution receives a dependency map rather than a scavenger hunt.

This is requirements traceability applied to governance.

It would not decide whether repeal is wise. It would make the consequences of repeal more legible.

Artificial intelligence could accelerate this work by comparing rules, finding obsolete references, tracing citations, and locating duplicated obligations across large bodies of text. That is a better use of generative systems than creating unlimited new policy every time an incident occurs.

The risk is obvious.

If AI makes drafting rules almost free, organizations may respond to every failure by generating another procedure.

The first ten controls may improve safety.

The next hundred may create a system nobody can fully understand.

At that point, formal compliance can become less reliable because the rule set exceeds the institution’s capacity to know which instructions matter most.

The solution is not fewer rules as an ideology.

It is a rule system that can learn.

Learning requires both addition and revision. Sometimes it requires repeal.

A law that outlives its original reason may discover a new reason to survive. That is possible and should be respected. Institutions often repurpose old structures successfully.

But the new reason should be stated.

Otherwise age quietly becomes argument.

The standard should be simple enough to say even when difficult to apply:

A rule deserves persistence because its present purpose and effects justify it, not merely because deleting it would be work.
