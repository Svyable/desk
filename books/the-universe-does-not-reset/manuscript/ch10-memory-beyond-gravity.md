# Memory Beyond Gravity

Once a good word enters physics, it reproduces.

There is gravitational displacement memory. Electromagnetic kick memory. Spin memory. Center-of-mass memory. Color memory in non-Abelian gauge theory. Cosmological memory effects. Velocity memory in certain gravitational settings. Memory in condensed matter means other things entirely. Memory kernels appear in statistical mechanics. Quantum memories are devices. Shape-memory alloys remember through phase structure. Biology uses immune memory. Computers use memory as storage.

A book with “memory” in its title can become dishonest simply by letting these meanings blur.

So this chapter is a fence.

The memory effects relevant to the infrared triangle share a recognizable structure: radiation or flux passes, an observable integrated over the event has a persistent change, and the change is connected to symmetry or conservation information. The exact observable may be displacement, velocity, relative time delay, phase, or a gauge-theoretic quantity. The specific mechanism depends on the theory.

Spin memory provides a good example of both the power and danger of extension. In gravitational physics, spin memory is associated with angular momentum flux and can be described through relative time delays for counter-orbiting light rays or related observables. It connects to subleading soft graviton structure and extended asymptotic symmetries. It is not simply “the detector rotates permanently.” The operational picture is subtler than displacement memory.

Center-of-mass memory is subtler again, linked to boost or center-of-mass aspects of asymptotic charges. The nomenclature reflects a growing catalog of balance laws and persistent observables rather than a single scalar called Memory that nature updates after every event.

Non-Abelian gauge theories add another layer. Electromagnetism has a simple Abelian gauge group: charges commute. Yang-Mills theories such as the gauge theory underlying the strong interaction are non-Abelian. Their charges carry group structure and interact with the gauge fields themselves. Researchers have studied color memory effects in which a radiation pulse can change the relative color orientation of test charges or Wilson-line data.

This is theoretically elegant and experimentally remote. Quarks are confined. We do not place isolated color charges on a laboratory table and watch their orientation kick after a gluon pulse. Color memory is valuable as a clue about infrared gauge structure, not as an upcoming consumer sensor.

The distinction between conceptual and observable frontiers matters.

Frontier physics is often sold as a queue of discoveries waiting for larger machines. That is not how the field actually works. Some frontiers are mathematical: determine the correct asymptotic symmetry group under different boundary conditions. Some are computational: extract memory accurately from numerical simulations. Some are observational: reach enough sensitivity to distinguish memory in detector data. Some are conceptual: understand how infrared charges relate to information, dressings, or black-hole states. Their clocks are different.

The book's thesis survives only if it respects those clocks.

The well-established core is narrow. General relativity predicts gravitational-wave memory. The relationship between leading displacement memory, BMS supertranslations, and leading soft graviton theorems is a mature part of the modern infrared story. Electromagnetic analogues are similarly well grounded. Numerical relativity can compute memory contributions. Searches and forecasts for detector observation are active.

Outside that core, certainty drops by degrees.

The extension of asymptotic methods to realistic cosmology is active because our universe is not exactly asymptotically flat. Celestial holography seeks a description of four-dimensional scattering in terms of a theory living on the celestial sphere; asymptotic symmetries and soft theorems are central there, but a complete holographic dual for realistic flat-space quantum gravity is not established in the way AdS/CFT is established as a framework. Black-hole soft hair is an influential proposal, not a solved information paradox. Claims that soft modes encode all information about infalling matter are contested.

Those sentences are not hedging for politeness. They are the actual map.

A frontier book has a choice. It can sand down the differences until every live research program sounds like discovered fact, which produces excitement at the cost of truth. Or it can keep the uncertainties visible and risk sounding less cinematic.

The uncertainties are the cinema.

Physics is most alive where a theorem is solid but its interpretation is not, where an effect is predicted but not yet measured, where two formulations are equivalent under assumptions and researchers are testing how far the equivalence extends. The world does not become less interesting when the labels read “open.”

Consider cosmological memory. Gravitational waves propagate through an expanding universe. Cosmic expansion changes amplitudes and waveforms. In de Sitter or FLRW-like backgrounds, the asymptotic structure differs from Minkowski spacetime. Researchers have derived memory effects in cosmological contexts and debated how they should be defined operationally. A sufficiently distant source is not merely a flat-space binary with a luminosity-distance factor pasted on afterward.

This matters observationally because future detectors will see mergers across cosmological distances. Precision will eventually make propagation effects and cosmological modeling part of the memory problem.

The universe itself becomes part of the detector transfer function.

That sentence is less metaphorical than it sounds. Every astronomical signal is filtered by propagation: expansion redshifts light and gravitational waves; plasma disperses radio pulses; dust extinguishes starlight; gravitational potentials lens paths; intervening matter scatters or absorbs. We infer sources by modeling the channel.

Memory complicates the channel because its most interesting structure lives at low frequency and long time, exactly where cosmological boundary conditions matter most.

There is another extension I care about more for this book: the idea of memory as a test of whether “vacuum” should be treated as one state or a family labeled by soft data.

In broken-symmetry systems, distinct vacua are ordinary. A ferromagnet can settle into different magnetization directions. In gauge/gravity infrared physics, the vacuum degeneracy is more abstract, tied to large transformations and soft charges. A radiation event can move the system between sectors.

The analogy to a magnet is limited, but it cures a conceptual mistake. Empty does not necessarily mean unique.

If the low-energy state space has many physically distinguishable sectors, then an event can alter the sector without leaving a hard particle sitting around as a marker. The aftermath is encoded in the long-range field configuration.

This is the version of “the universe does not reset” I trust most.

Not that everything is remembered.

That the quiet state after an event need not be the same quiet state as before.
