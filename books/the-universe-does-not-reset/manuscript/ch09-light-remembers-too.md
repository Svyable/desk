# Light Remembers Too

Gravity did not invent memory.

Electromagnetism has its own family of memory effects. A burst of electromagnetic radiation associated with changing charge motion can leave a lasting change in the velocity of a distant charged test particle, or an integrated electromagnetic field effect that plays the same conceptual role as gravitational displacement memory. Large gauge transformations at null infinity and soft-photon theorems complete an electromagnetic version of the infrared triangle.

This matters because it removes one excuse for treating gravitational memory as an exotic artifact of curved spacetime.

The underlying pattern is broader: gauge fields with long-range interactions have infrared sectors in which asymptotic symmetries, soft radiation, and integrated classical effects are tied together.

Take a charged particle initially at rest far from a scattering event. A pulse of electromagnetic radiation passes. The electric field exerts a force. Integrate the force over time and the charge acquires a net change in velocity if the time-integrated field is nonzero. That kick is electromagnetic memory.

Nothing mystical happened. The Lorentz force acted. The oddity is that the integral of a radiation pulse contains a universal piece connected to the changing asymptotic charge-current configuration.

The soft-photon theorem supplies the scattering face. As a photon energy becomes very small, amplitudes factorize in a universal manner determined by the charges and momenta of the hard particles. Large electromagnetic gauge transformations at infinity supply the symmetry face. The classical kick supplies the memory face.

The family resemblance to gravity is obvious once somebody points at it.

The differences are just as important. Electromagnetic test particles carry charge and can respond directly to the field with an acceleration. Gravitational test masses all respond through spacetime geometry according to the equivalence principle. Gravity is nonlinear in a way Maxwell electromagnetism is not, giving rise to the gravitational-wave energy contribution to memory. The symmetry groups differ. The detector technologies differ. The analogy is structural rather than identical.

That structural analogy asks a useful question: what is the minimum architecture required for a field to leave this kind of aftermath?

Long-range interactions are part of the answer. Massless gauge fields propagate to infinity. Their soft modes do not decouple innocently from scattering because arbitrarily low-energy radiation is connected to long-time behavior and asymptotic charges. The boundary conditions are not empty bookkeeping. They encode how the field behaves after the hard event is over.

In a world where every interaction were short-ranged and every field had a large mass gap, the infrared would look different. Correlations would die exponentially with distance. The special role of asymptotic null boundaries would change. Memory in the gauge-theory sense belongs to a universe where some influences refuse to become local furniture.

Photons and gravitons, at least in our effective descriptions, are the great long-distance accountants.

Again I use “accountant” with caution. The soft sector knows charges and momenta; it does not know the gossip. Universal soft behavior is powerful because it ignores most microscopic details. The charge that changes velocity does not receive a narrated summary of the collision. It receives an impulse constrained by what entered and left.

This is why memory can coexist with forgetting.

A physical system may lose almost all recoverable details of its microhistory while preserving a handful of conserved or asymptotic quantities. Thermodynamics is the obvious example. A gas reaches equilibrium and forgets, for practical purposes, which molecule began in which corner. Energy, particle number where conserved, angular momentum, and other macroscopic constraints remain. The final state is compatible with vast numbers of microscopic pasts.

Memory effects are not thermalization, but they sharpen the same conceptual distinction. Persistence of a constraint is not persistence of a story.

The distinction becomes important in quantum infrared physics because the ordinary Fock-space picture of isolated charged particles with no accompanying soft field is inadequate for exact scattering. Charged particles are accompanied by long-range electromagnetic fields. In formulations using dressed states, the asymptotic charged particle is inseparable from a cloud of soft photons encoding its long-range field configuration. Gravity has analogous gravitational dressings.

This can sound like the soft cloud is a hidden archive attached to every particle. It is better to think of it as the price of having long-range interactions and gauge constraints. A charged particle cannot be a perfectly local object independent of its field. The field reaches outward. The asymptotic state has to know enough about that field for infrared divergences and conservation laws to make sense.

The object has tails.

That may be the more general lesson.

Human perception is built around bounded things. The cup ends at its ceramic surface. The tree ends at bark. The body ends at skin. Physics keeps producing entities whose relevant description does not respect those folk boundaries. A charge comes with an electromagnetic field. A mass-energy distribution curves spacetime. A quantum field excitation is not a billiard ball. A black hole has a horizon, but its gravitational influence extends indefinitely. Even an apparently isolated laboratory sits inside electromagnetic, gravitational, neutrino, cosmic-ray, and quantum environments.

Memory effects are what happens when the tails have history.

A charged scattering event changes the long-range field. The far field eventually learns. A gravitational event changes asymptotic data. Distant detectors eventually learn. There is no instantaneous update; causality is obeyed. The new relation propagates outward at light speed.

That last point is worth dwelling on because “permanent” can make memory sound as though the entire universe changes at once.

It does not.

Before the radiation front reaches a distant detector, the detector remains in the old causal domain. During passage, it responds. After passage, it can occupy the new relational state. Memory spreads with the signal. Permanence is downstream of causality.

A memory front therefore divides spacetime not only into before and after in time but into regions that have and have not yet been causally updated by the event.

This is familiar from any radiation. We see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago. A civilization fifty light-years away would not know about a terrestrial broadcast until fifty years after transmission. Memory adds a twist: when the front arrives, it can alter more than an information register. It can impart a kick or displacement.

Information and dynamics travel together.

Electromagnetic memory is experimentally different from gravitational memory because ordinary charges are everywhere and electromagnetic environments are noisy beyond comparison. Static and slowly varying electric fields from local matter swamp tiny astrophysical soft effects in most settings. The theoretical clarity of the memory effect does not imply an easy detector program like LIGO's.

This is another useful antidote to the belief that every theoretical observable deserves a billion-dollar instrument. Some observables are most valuable as consistency relations linking parts of theory. Others become feasible only in special systems or future technology. Experimental accessibility is a contingent engineering fact, not a measure of conceptual importance.

The gravitational case happens to be unusually fortunate. We have astrophysical sources that radiate strongly, detectors designed for gravitational waves, and a theory that predicts both the hard waveform and the soft aftermath. That convergence turns a deep infrared structure into a realistic long-term measurement target.

Electromagnetism supplies the proof of family.

Gravity is not remembering alone.
