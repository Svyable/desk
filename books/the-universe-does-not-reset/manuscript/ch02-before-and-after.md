# Before and After Are Different Places

Physics likes baselines. Measure a quantity, perturb the system, measure again. The simplicity is deceptive because a baseline is not merely a number written down before something happens. It is a claim about which comparisons remain meaningful while the experiment changes.

That sounds like laboratory housekeeping until gravity enters the room.

In Newtonian mechanics we can imagine an external coordinate grid that survives the experiment untouched. Two masses move across the grid. We compare their positions before and after. General relativity takes away the innocent grid. Coordinates are labels; they are not themselves rods bolted to the universe. What becomes physical are relations that survive the freedom to relabel spacetime: proper times, geodesic separations in suitable regimes, curvature, causal relationships, scattering data, detector outputs.

Memory effects live exactly in this awkward space between “something changed” and “show me the comparison that proves it.”

Take the ideal ring of test masses again. Before a gravitational-wave burst, the masses are freely falling with some relative arrangement. During the burst, geodesic deviation changes their separations. Afterward, the spacetime in their neighborhood can again be approximately quiet. If the final relative separation differs from the initial one, the detector has retained a displacement memory.

The word final is an idealization. No laboratory waits until eternity. No astrophysical environment is empty forever. Earth moves, the Galaxy accelerates, seismic fields act, spacecraft orbit, mirrors hang from suspensions, pulsars spin down. The clean mathematical statement often refers to limits taken before and after a localized burst in a region where the assumptions are controlled. Experimental physics then tries to identify a finite-time proxy.

That gap between asymptotic and finite is where philosophy can either become useful or become annoying.

Useful philosophy asks what the observable means. Annoying philosophy treats a technical term as permission to leap immediately to consciousness, destiny, or cosmic archives. Memory requires us to be unusually strict because its plain-English name is more expansive than its measurement.

A good way to stay strict is to ask what could make the effect disappear.

If a passing gravitational wave produces only an oscillation with zero net integral in the relevant observable, a ring can return to its original configuration. If the waveform contains a memory contribution, it does not. If later forces move the objects again, the literal configuration can change, but that does not retroactively erase the gravitational contribution any more than moving a seismometer after an earthquake makes the earthquake not have displaced the ground. The effect is reconstructed by a model of the forces and the measurement history.

This is already familiar in precision science. A satellite's orbit is not a pristine Newtonian ellipse. Atmospheric drag, radiation pressure, Earth's multipole moments, tides, relativistic corrections, maneuvers, clock offsets, and tracking errors all coexist. We call one contribution real when a coherent model lets it be inferred across observations and alternative explanations fare worse. Gravitational-wave memory will not be exempt from this ordinary burden merely because the mathematics is beautiful.

The beauty comes partly from integration.

Oscillatory waves tend to advertise themselves through frequency. Memory advertises itself through accumulated imbalance. A symmetric positive and negative excursion can cancel. A net flux can leave a step. In this sense memory belongs to a broader class of physical ideas where what matters is not the instantaneous magnitude but what survives integration over an event.

Impulse is the integral of force. A small force applied long enough can change momentum. Dose integrates exposure. Fluence integrates radiant flux. The total radiated energy of an astronomical transient depends on its history, not the brightest instant. None of these analogies is exact, but they teach the right suspicion: a process can look temporary point by point while leaving a nonzero cumulative consequence.

Gravity complicates the picture because the thing being integrated is bound up with geometry and flux at infinity. In asymptotically flat treatments, the passage from one quiet era to another can involve a change in the shear data at null infinity. The memory observable can be related to this change. The vacuum before and the vacuum after need not be represented as the same member of the asymptotic family once the radiative flux has passed.

That sentence is where many popular accounts accelerate too hard. “Different vacua” sounds like the event has punched the universe into a wholly new reality. The technical statement is more disciplined. General relativity's asymptotic structure admits degeneracies related by BMS supertranslations. Radiation can connect configurations whose asymptotic data differ. The memory effect provides an observable imprint of that transition.

The important word is asymptotic.

The Bondi program developed a way to describe isolated radiating systems by looking at spacetime far from sources and organizing the gravitational field near null infinity—the ideal boundary reached by outgoing light rays. This is not a wall somewhere beyond the galaxies. It is a mathematical structure used to define radiation, mass loss, symmetries, and conserved quantities cleanly. The framework is extraordinarily powerful precisely because it strips away much local mess.

Our universe, meanwhile, is not globally asymptotically flat in the simplest textbook sense. It contains matter on large scales and is expanding. Real observations occur at finite distance. That does not make asymptotically flat results irrelevant to compact-binary waveforms; local systems can often be modeled to excellent approximation within appropriate regimes. It does mean the grandest interpretations need their domain labels attached.

A recurring problem in frontier physics is domain laundering. A statement proved under one set of assumptions migrates into ordinary prose, loses the assumptions, and comes back dressed as a universal ontology. Memory is a good place to refuse that habit.

Under controlled assumptions, outgoing radiation changes relational data. That is enough.

Why should anyone beyond relativity care about such a small technical aftereffect?

Because our default model of causation is strangely event-centered. We identify the collision, eruption, election, bankruptcy, birth, firing, war, discovery, or failure. We draw a box around it. The box creates a before and after in narrative space, but the after is usually treated as an ordinary world plus consequences. Physics offers a sharper possibility: some events change the very reference relations by which later states are compared.

This is true outside gravity too, although not in the same mathematical sense. A tectonic rupture changes stress fields. A biological infection can alter immune memory. A financial crisis changes collateral conventions and risk models. A software exploit changes what defenders count as a safe baseline. The aftermath is not simply a list of outputs; it can alter the measuring apparatus, institutions, or state variables that define subsequent decisions.

I am not claiming a common law of “memory.” That would be cheap metaphor. The useful transfer is narrower: do not assume that removal of the forcing restores the prior baseline.

Engineers know this as hysteresis in systems where output depends on history. Materials can retain magnetization. Mechanical systems can have backlash and plastic deformation. Ecological systems can cross thresholds. Again, gravitational memory is not ordinary material hysteresis. The analogy matters because human intuition is otherwise drawn toward elasticity: push, release, return.

Some systems do not return.

A black-hole merger is about as far from an elastic perturbation as nature allows. Two horizons become one. Mass-energy leaves in gravitational waves. Momentum can be carried anisotropically, giving the remnant a recoil. Angular momentum changes. The radiation field departs. The final black hole settles into a Kerr state characterized, in the classical idealization, by mass and spin plus motion. Nobody expects the material configuration to “reset.” What is less intuitive is that distant freely falling observers can retain an additional integrated signature of the radiative transition itself.

There is a practical asymmetry here. Astronomers are excellent at cataloging objects before and after a transient when both are visible. We compare a supernova progenitor image with the remnant field. We track a variable star. We measure an orbit before and after an encounter. Gravitational memory asks for a subtler comparison because there may be no luminous object at the detector that looks changed. The changed quantity is in relative positions, timings, or phases so small that ordinary matter and instrumentation overwhelm them.

This makes the experiment a war against baseline drift.

Every precision instrument has drift. Lasers wander. Clocks age. mirrors charge. temperatures change. Earth's crust moves. electronics acquire offsets. software calibrations evolve. A claim of memory must distinguish a physically predicted persistent component from all the mundane ways an instrument can fail to come back to zero.

The irony is almost perfect. To test whether spacetime fails to reset, we need machines whose own failure to reset is understood far better than any machine has a right to be.

That is why detector design belongs in a philosophical book about memory. The epistemic problem is not solved by saying “permanent displacement.” We need an account of how permanence appears in a controlled channel. Ground-based interferometers are high-pass instruments in important practical senses; suspension and control systems suppress slow drifts. Pulsar timing arrays infer gravitational effects from deviations accumulated across years. LISA will use drag-free spacecraft and laser links over millions of kilometers, with its own low-frequency limitations and time-delay interferometry. Each architecture defines a different way of asking whether before and after differ.

There is no God's-eye ruler.

The absence of a God's-eye ruler does not collapse physics into subjectivity. Quite the opposite. It forces us to construct comparisons that can be shared, repeated, modeled, and challenged. Relativity became one of the most precise theories ever tested by giving up absolute frames, not by giving up objectivity.

Memory fits that tradition. It says: choose your observable carefully. Specify the asymptotic or finite-distance setup. Separate coordinate effects from measurable relations. Account for the detector. Then look for the nonzero residue.

If it is found, the interpretation will be modest and profound at once. A wave crossed the apparatus. The loud oscillations ended. After every modeled nuisance was removed, a relation remained changed in exactly the way the theory predicted.

Before and after would not be merely times on either side of an event.

They would be measurably different geometries.
