# A Galaxy-Sized Memory Detector

A pulsar is a dead star used as a clock by people who will never visit it.

That sentence contains most of what I love about pulsar timing arrays.

A neutron star can rotate hundreds of times per second. If its magnetic beam sweeps Earth, radio telescopes receive regular pulses. Millisecond pulsars are especially stable. Their arrival times are not perfectly periodic in a naive sense: the pulsar moves, Earth moves, planets tug, interstellar plasma disperses radio waves, clocks have errors, the pulsar can be in a binary, and its intrinsic spin slowly changes. Pulsar timing turns all of these into a model.

After the model, small residuals remain.

Spread dozens of suitable pulsars across the sky and those residuals become a gravitational-wave observatory with baselines measured in kiloparsecs. NANOGrav in North America, the European Pulsar Timing Array, the Parkes Pulsar Timing Array and its successors in Australia, the Indian Pulsar Timing Array, and the International Pulsar Timing Array collectively use radio astronomy to search the nanohertz gravitational-wave universe.

The main target has been the stochastic gravitational-wave background expected from populations of supermassive black-hole binaries. In 2023, several pulsar-timing collaborations reported evidence for a common low-frequency process with the spatial correlations expected from a gravitational-wave background, an important milestone after years of limits. The interpretation continues to be refined with more data and modeling.

Memory is a different signal.

A sufficiently strong gravitational-wave burst with memory crossing Earth would induce a persistent change in the apparent pulse arrival behavior from pulsars across the sky. In idealized descriptions, the timing residual develops a ramp after the burst: the gravitational wave changes the effective redshift relation, and because timing residuals integrate frequency shifts, a step in one quantity becomes a growing offset in another.

The exact angular pattern depends on source direction and pulsar geometry. The Earth term would appear coherently across the array at the same epoch, projected according to the antenna response. A corresponding pulsar term occurs when the wavefront crossed the pulsar, typically thousands of years separated in effective timing because the pulsars are so distant.

A single astrophysical event therefore writes at two ends of a baseline separated by the Galaxy.

That is worth sitting with.

The radio pulse left the pulsar hundreds or thousands of years ago. A gravitational-wave burst may cross Earth now. The timing analysis compares a rotating neutron star's old photons with terrestrial clocks and models, searching for a kink produced by a spacetime event in between. The “detector” is not one machine. It is a causal arrangement of ancient stellar rotation, interstellar propagation, radio telescopes, atomic time, planetary ephemerides, and decades of human records.

Gravitational memory is almost designed to embarrass the noun detector.

NANOGrav searched its 11-year data set for burst-with-memory signals and published upper limits in 2020. The search did not report a detection. That is the correct scientific state to carry forward: the method is real, the data constrain event rates and amplitudes, and the absence of a detected burst is compatible with expectations for rare sufficiently loud events.

Upper limits are often treated as disappointing because they do not produce a discovery noun. They are actually how a field learns the shape of possibility.

A limit says that if events above a given amplitude occurred more frequently than some rate, we likely would have seen them under the model. As data spans lengthen, timing precision improves, pulsar counts grow, and analysis methods mature, the excluded region expands. The universe has less room to hide that particular phenomenon.

Memory searches with pulsar timing arrays have unusual timescales. A burst may be effectively instantaneous compared with a decades-long data set, but the ramp afterward accumulates. In that sense the signal can become more obvious with time after the event.

This is the reverse of most astronomy.

A supernova fades. A gamma-ray burst disappears in seconds. A fast radio burst is over before the telescope operator knows it happened. Afterglows weaken. Memory can leave a timing signature whose integrated residual grows, at least until parameter fitting and other effects complicate the picture.

The aftermath gains leverage.

But long data sets create their own degeneracies. A ramp can resemble an error in pulsar spin frequency if seen in only one pulsar. A global clock error affects all pulsars in a monopolar pattern. An error in the solar-system ephemeris produces a dipolar sky pattern. Gravitational waves produce a different correlated angular structure. The array separates them statistically.

This is astronomy as geometry.

No one pulsar is trusted to be the universe. The pattern across the sky adjudicates the cause.

A burst with memory from the merger of two supermassive black holes would be an extraordinary event. These systems emit at low frequencies because their dynamical timescales are long. The final merger can create a memory amplitude potentially accessible to timing arrays if sufficiently nearby and massive. Rate estimates are uncertain because the population of close supermassive black-hole binaries depends on galaxy merger histories, stellar and gas dynamics, environmental coupling, and the notorious “final parsec” evolution toward gravitational-wave domination.

The same astrophysics that shapes the stochastic background shapes the burst prospects.

A future memory detection in pulsar timing would therefore do more than confirm a relativistic effect. It would identify or constrain a rare massive-black-hole merger in the local-ish universe, potentially without the kind of luminous electromagnetic display people expect from a cosmic catastrophe.

The black holes could change our clock network while remaining optically obscure.

This is a recurring humiliation for the visual species. The universe does not organize significance around light.

Neutrinos crossed Earth from SN 1987A and gave direct information about the collapsing stellar core. Gravitational waves from GW170817 arrived before much of the electromagnetic counterpart was characterized. Dark matter, if the standard picture is broadly right, dominates galactic mass while being invisible. Pulsar timing uses radio flashes as clock hands to detect distortions that the radio source did not cause.

We keep borrowing one part of nature to measure another.

That is perhaps the most honest version of humanity's role in fundamental physics. We do not stand outside the system with perfect instruments. We recruit physical systems into comparisons. Quartz clocks replaced pendulums for some purposes; atomic transitions replaced quartz; millisecond pulsars extend the comparison across the Galaxy. Laser cavities measure spacetime strain. Cold atoms measure acceleration. Cosmic microwave background photons measure an early thermal surface we cannot revisit.

A detector is a treaty between phenomena.

Pulsar timing arrays make the treaty explicit. We trust a neutron star's rotational regularity enough to use it against spacetime, but only after modeling the neutron star. We trust terrestrial atomic time enough to compare arrival epochs, but only after building international time standards. We trust the solar-system ephemeris enough to subtract Earth's motion, but only after spacecraft tracking and planetary astronomy refine it.

The “memory” in gravitational-wave memory arrives on top of several layers of human memory.

If an observatory loses old timing data, or metadata becomes unreadable, or clock standards cannot be reconstructed, the cosmological signal does not vanish from history. Our ability to establish it does.

A physical trace can outlive epistemic access.

This may be the central sentence of the whole book.

The universe does not have to reset for us to forget.
