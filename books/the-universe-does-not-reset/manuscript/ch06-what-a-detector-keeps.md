# What a Detector Actually Keeps

A permanent displacement is easy to picture if the detector is six perfect beads floating beyond everything.

LIGO is not six beads. It is two four-kilometer vacuum systems arranged in an L, with suspended mirrors, high-power lasers, feedback controls, seismic isolation, calibration lines, thermal effects, electronics, environmental monitors, and software reconstructing a strain time series from an apparatus that would stop functioning if its mirrors were simply allowed to drift wherever spacetime and Earth pleased.

The practical question is therefore not: does a mirror remain permanently somewhere else?

The practical question is: does the measured differential arm response contain the predicted nonoscillatory component after the full instrument response and control system have been accounted for?

That is less poetic and much closer to science.

A laser interferometer measures phase differences. Light travels down two perpendicular arms, reflects from test masses, returns, and interferes. A gravitational wave changes the proper relations along the arms in a direction-dependent way, producing a differential optical response. The detector is calibrated so the output can be interpreted as dimensionless strain over a defined frequency band.

The phrase frequency band is the trapdoor beneath memory.

Ground-based interferometers cannot observe arbitrarily low frequencies. Below roughly the tens-of-hertz regime, seismic noise, gravity-gradient noise, suspension dynamics, and control challenges grow severe. The exact useful low-frequency boundary changes with detector generation and observing run, but no current terrestrial gravitational-wave instrument behaves like a perfect DC ruler. It is built to detect variations, not hold a geodesic baseline untouched for eternity.

A memory signal resembles a step in strain: it grows during the source event and approaches a new level in an idealized description. A step has substantial low-frequency content. The faster rise associated with a compact merger leaks enough signal into the detector's band that memory is not literally invisible, but much of the intuitive “permanent” part lives where the instrument is least comfortable.

This creates a useful distinction between physical permanence and instrumental persistence.

An earthquake can permanently shift a benchmark while an accelerometer, designed to respond to acceleration rather than static position, returns to zero. Nothing contradictory happened. The device measured the time derivative of a quantity rather than the final offset. An interferometer's response is more complicated, but the analogy teaches the right lesson: a lasting physical change need not appear as a lasting nonzero number in the raw channel.

The signal can survive as a characteristic transient response to a step.

This matters when memory is described to non-specialists. Saying “LIGO's mirrors stay displaced” is too literal. The test masses are suspended and controlled. Earth-bound apparatus does not remain in ideal free fall. What general relativity predicts is a memory component in the gravitational strain. The detector's measured output is that strain filtered through the actual transfer function and control system.

The distinction between a phenomenon and a detector's representation of it is universal. A CCD does not preserve incoming photons; it converts their interactions into charge and then into digital values. A microphone does not keep sound pressure; it converts pressure variations to electrical signals. A calorimeter can absorb a particle and reconstruct its energy from the shower. Scientific instruments are translation machines.

Memory adds the question of whether the translation preserves a zero-frequency or integrated component.

For ground-based detectors, one strategy is matched filtering or model-based inference. If numerical relativity and analytical models predict the memory contribution for a binary merger, the data can be tested against waveforms including and excluding memory. The problem is not to stare at the strain plot until a little step appears. It is to ask which model better explains the measured time series under the noise distribution and calibration uncertainty.

Another strategy is stacking. Individual memory signals can be too weak, but repeated events provide repeated experiments. The oscillatory waveform gives estimates of masses, spins, distance, inclination, sky position, and polarization. Those estimates predict the expected memory shape and amplitude probabilistically. Combining many events can accumulate evidence.

This is not free sensitivity. Badly aligned signals cancel. Parameter degeneracies can destroy the known sign. Selection effects can bias a sample. Non-Gaussian detector noise can fake low-frequency structure. Waveform systematics matter. Stacking works when the analysis retains enough phase and orientation information to combine the same physical quantity rather than merely adding vaguely similar bumps.

The history of gravitational-wave data analysis has trained the field to distrust pretty bumps.

GW150914 looked dramatic after the fact, but the discovery claim rested on independent detectors, data-quality studies, background estimation, waveform consistency, instrumental checks, and statistical analysis. Memory will need the same culture of suspicion, probably more so because slow transients are closer to the habits of instrumental drift.

A detector remembers its own history too.

Suspension states change. Calibration models are updated. hardware injections test response. Glitches appear from scattered light, electronics, weather, earthquakes, human activity, and couplings not anticipated when the instrument was designed. Long-duration low-frequency structures are exactly where “something happened to the machine” competes most directly with “something permanent happened in the gravitational field.”

This produces a kind of epistemic symmetry. The phenomenon and the nuisance both fail to return cleanly to baseline.

The way out is correlation and prediction.

A real astrophysical memory signal should appear across detectors with relative amplitudes and delays fixed by sky position and antenna patterns. It should occur at the merger time of a source already observed in the oscillatory channel. Its sign and shape should be constrained by the source parameters. Environmental monitors should not show a terrestrial culprit correlated across sites. A population should accumulate according to astrophysical expectations.

Noise can imitate one feature. It has a harder time imitating all of them coherently.

This is one of science's deepest practical techniques: do not demand that a single measurement channel be pure. Demand that independent consequences agree.

The same logic is why multimessenger astronomy is so powerful. GW170817 produced gravitational waves, a gamma-ray burst, kilonova light, afterglow observations, host-galaxy identification, and nucleosynthesis evidence. Each channel had its own uncertainties. Together they constrained the story far more sharply than any one instrument.

Memory is potentially a second gravitational channel nested inside the first.

The oscillatory waveform tells us a violent event occurred. The memory tests the integrated radiative aftermath. A successful detection would be satisfying precisely because the strong signal makes a prediction for the weak one.

There is an analogy to shadows. Once you know the location of a lamp and object, a shadow should fall in a particular direction. Detecting the object is not the same as detecting the shadow, and failure to see the shadow could mean bad lighting, bad geometry, or a wrong model. But the object constrains where to look.

The main gravitational waveform is the lamp.

Future detectors will change the problem. Third-generation terrestrial observatories such as Cosmic Explorer and the Einstein Telescope are designed for much greater sensitivity and lower-frequency reach than current instruments. LISA will operate in space with million-kilometer-scale laser links and sensitivity to millihertz gravitational waves. These instruments could observe heavier black-hole systems, earlier inspiral phases, enormous event populations, and memory under different noise conditions.

A new detector does more than turn up the volume. It changes what kind of permanence can be operationally defined.

Pulsar timing arrays make this obvious. Their “detector” is a network of millisecond pulsars spread across the sky, with Earth as one end of every timing baseline. Radio observatories measure pulse times of arrival over years. A gravitational-wave burst with memory can induce a persistent change in the timing residual slope after the wave crosses Earth or a pulsar. The signal is not a mirror offset at all. It is a kink in an accumulated clock comparison.

Same theoretical family. Different physical record.

This is why I resist the phrase “memory is permanent displacement” as a complete definition. It is a good entry point for displacement memory in ideal detectors, but the deeper observable depends on the measurement architecture. Spin memory relates to time delays around loops. Other memory effects can appear as velocity changes or phase shifts. What persists is not always a coordinate distance.

What persists is a relational mismatch between the state before radiation and the state after it.

Human beings do something peculiar with mismatches. We institutionalize them. We define a meter, maintain atomic time scales, survey benchmarks, calibrate interferometers, archive pulsar observations, preserve instrument logs, and write software that can reprocess old data with new models. The apparatus becomes a social object stretched across generations.

LIGO's first detection depended on work done by people who did not live to see it. Pulsar timing arrays depend on observations accumulated longer than a graduate student's career stage. LISA's current engineers are building an instrument whose most interesting discoveries may belong to researchers who are children now.

A memory experiment therefore contains two kinds of persistence.

Nature may leave a physical residue.

Civilization has to leave a trustworthy baseline long enough to notice it.
