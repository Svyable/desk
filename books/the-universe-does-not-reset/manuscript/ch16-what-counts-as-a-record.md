# What Counts as a Record

A record is not simply something that changed because of the past.

Everything changed because of the past.

The chair in front of me exists because a tree grew, a saw cut, a factory ran, a truck moved, a person bought it, and gravity kept the pieces where carpentry expected. If causation alone made a record, the universe would be one undifferentiated archive and the word would explain nothing.

A useful record needs at least three things: a persistent difference, a mapping between that difference and possible past causes, and enough stability that some later process can read the mapping.

Gravitational memory satisfies the first condition in the idealized theory. A detector relation changes persistently. It partially satisfies the second: the pattern and amplitude are constrained by integrated flux and source geometry. The third belongs to experimental design. Without a detector or natural system capable of preserving and comparing the relation, the physical consequence may remain unread.

This is why I have resisted saying the universe remembers everything.

Most consequences are terrible records.

Drop a glass on a stone floor. The shards preserve information about the impact, material flaws, and forces. In principle an impossibly powerful model with complete microscopic data might infer much of the event. In practice the glass is a many-to-one encoding. Countless impact histories produce overlapping shard patterns. Thermal motion spreads information. Air currents carry energy away. Sound radiates. Dust moves. Humans sweep the floor. The recoverable past contracts rapidly.

The event changed the world permanently without remaining reconstructible.

That difference between irreversibility and recordability matters in physics. Entropy gives time an arrow in macroscopic processes even though microscopic laws can be reversible or unitary in important formulations. Decoherence spreads phase information into environmental correlations. Scrambling distributes information nonlocally across many degrees of freedom. Information may remain in a formal quantum state while becoming practically unrecoverable.

“Not erased” and “available” are different claims.

Gravitational memory is unusually legible because theory tells us which integrated observable to measure. The ring of masses is not just altered; it is altered in a tensor pattern determined by the gravitational wave. The signal has geometry.

Geometry makes a scar readable.

A random dent in a car door says less than a dent with paint transfer, impact height, directional deformation, and matching damage on another vehicle. A tree ring is useful because growth layers have ordered time. Ice cores are useful because gases and isotopes are trapped in stratified deposition. DNA is useful because replication maps sequences through ancestry, although mutation, recombination, selection, and drift complicate the reconstruction.

Nature produces records when a process creates structured correlations that persist.

Quantum mechanics makes correlation central. A measurement entangles a system with an apparatus and environment. Decoherence makes certain pointer states robust because their information is redundantly copied into environmental degrees of freedom. Quantum Darwinism, developed by Wojciech Zurek and others, describes how multiple observers can independently access information about stable system observables through fragments of the environment, helping explain the emergence of objective classical facts.

This is another literature where the word memory is tempting, but I will not merge it with gravitational memory. The connection is conceptual: objectivity depends on durable, redundantly accessible correlations.

A photon scattered from a table carries information about the table. Many photons scatter. Your eyes intercept a tiny fraction and infer an object. The environment has become an information channel. Classical reality, in this account, is partly the subset of quantum information that gets broadcast redundantly enough for observers to agree without destroying the source.

Gravitational memory is not quantum Darwinism. Yet both trouble the picture of observation as a passive mind looking at an independent present. The present is full of records propagated from elsewhere.

Astronomy is almost entirely record-reading.

We never observe the Sun's core directly at the moment it is producing the photons that later reach our eyes. Photons scatter outward for long periods before escape. Neutrinos leave more directly. Helioseismology infers internal structure from surface oscillations. The cosmic microwave background is a fossil radiation field from when the universe became transparent to photons, filtered by later expansion, lensing, reionization, and foregrounds. Heavy-element abundances record nucleosynthesis histories. Meteorites preserve isotopic clocks. Gravitational waves record compact-object dynamics.

A universe with no persistent correlations would be scientifically opaque.

We would have only the immediate state and no reliable way to infer how it came to be. Causation could still operate, but history would be inaccessible.

Our universe is not like that. It is messy in the opposite way. It overproduces traces and underproduces indexes.

The cosmic microwave background contains primordial information and foreground contamination. Geological strata preserve events and later deformation. Genomes preserve ancestry and horizontal transfer, mutation, selection, demographic bottlenecks. Financial records preserve transactions and reporting conventions. Human testimony preserves experience and reconstruction. Every archive is mixed with processes that happened after the thing we care about.

Reading a record is model subtraction.

Gravitational-wave analysis fits this perfectly. The detector output contains astrophysical signal plus instrument noise. Pulsar timings contain gravitational effects plus spin noise, plasma, clock errors, ephemeris errors, binary motion, and telescope systematics. Memory is not identified because a number persists. It is identified because the persistence has the predicted causal structure after alternatives are modeled.

This gives us a better definition of physical memory: a persistent correlation whose causal map survives challenge.

That definition is deliberately observer-relative in the practical sense without making reality subjective. A correlation can exist before anyone reads it. Whether it functions as a record depends on whether some process can distinguish its alternatives. Fossils were records before paleontology. Gravitational memory existed in theory's universe before interferometers. Cosmic microwave photons crossed space before radio astronomy.

The human contribution is not creation of the trace.

It is creation of the decoder.

This is why archives and instruments belong in the same conceptual family. Both stabilize a mapping across time. An instrument converts a physical interaction into a calibrated channel. An archive preserves the channel and its context. Without calibration metadata, old detector data can become nearly uninterpretable. Without coordinate standards, a century-old survey can be ambiguous. Without file formats and software, digital storage becomes mineralized bits.

We imagine digital records as immortal because copying is cheap. They are often more dependent on conventions than paper.

A printed equation can be read with light. A proprietary simulation file may require a specific library, compiler behavior, endian convention, schema, and metadata. The bits can persist while the meaning disappears.

Physics has its own version of this failure. An asymptotic charge without a specified boundary condition is not a complete statement. A waveform without a frame is ambiguous. A memory amplitude without source orientation and detector response is incomplete. A “permanent displacement” without an operational definition can become rhetoric.

Meaning is part of the record architecture.

This pushes against a common fantasy about cosmic information. People hear that quantum evolution may be unitary and conclude that the past is, in principle, always recoverable. Unitarity says the quantum state evolves without fundamental loss of information in the formalism. It does not hand a finite observer a decoding algorithm, access to all environmental degrees of freedom, reversal of cosmological expansion, or control of black-hole evaporation.

The distinction between information existence and information accessibility may be one of the ignored universal realities this book is actually about.

The universe can carry consequences that no later observer can read.

It can also carry weak, awkward consequences that become readable only after a species invents a detector built for the aftermath.

That is memory's narrow miracle.
