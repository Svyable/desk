# LISA and the Slow Kick

Put the interferometer in space and the word low changes meaning.

The Laser Interferometer Space Antenna, LISA, is planned as a constellation of three spacecraft separated by millions of kilometers, exchanging laser beams while following heliocentric orbits. It is designed to detect gravitational waves at frequencies far below the ground-based band: roughly the millihertz world of massive black-hole binaries, compact Galactic binaries, extreme mass-ratio inspirals, and other slow gravitational systems.

For memory, that frequency shift is not cosmetic.

The memory contribution from a massive black-hole merger can be much larger in absolute strain than memory from a stellar-mass merger because the system radiates vastly more energy. The characteristic timescale is also longer, moving the rise into LISA's band. A merger of black holes with millions of solar masses can produce a substantial nonoscillatory component over seconds to hours depending on redshifted mass, rather than the millisecond-scale rise of a ground-based stellar merger.

LISA therefore changes both amplitude and instrument response.

Space does not abolish low-frequency problems. LISA spacecraft are not inertial points floating perfectly. They experience solar radiation pressure and other non-gravitational forces. Test masses inside the spacecraft are shielded and used as reference bodies, while microthrusters keep the spacecraft centered around them in drag-free control. Laser frequency noise is vastly larger than the gravitational-wave signal and must be canceled through time-delay interferometry, combining phase measurements along unequal, changing arms.

A permanent step in the gravitational field has to be reconstructed through that machinery.

This is why “space is quieter” is an inadequate description. Space removes seismic ground motion, allows enormous baselines, and opens low frequencies inaccessible on Earth. It replaces those problems with orbital dynamics, spacecraft disturbances, clock and ranging requirements, laser-link losses, time-dependent geometry, and a data-analysis problem no ground interferometer has exactly prepared us for.

Every new window has its weather.

LISA's memory prospects are especially interesting for massive black-hole mergers because the main event can be spectacular. Two supermassive black holes formed through galaxy mergers may spiral together over cosmic time, enter LISA's band months or years before coalescence, and generate enormous signal-to-noise ratios for favorable systems. The inspiral can provide advance warning. The merger and ringdown can be localized progressively. Electromagnetic counterparts may exist in gas-rich environments, though their presence and timing are uncertain.

Memory arrives as aftermath inside an event we may have watched approaching.

That reverses astronomy's usual chronology. Most catastrophic transients are discovered at or after explosion. A LISA massive-black-hole binary could be tracked long before merger, allowing telescopes to prepare. The detector would establish an exquisite before-state. When the final burst occurs, the memory component can be compared with months of prior waveform information.

If any experiment is conceptually designed for before-and-after physics, this is close.

The asymptotic idealization still needs translation. LISA's arms are finite. The constellation moves. Light travel times across the arms are long enough that the gravitational wave cannot always be approximated as changing the whole detector simultaneously. Time-delay interferometry creates synthesized observables whose responses depend on frequency and sky direction. A DC-like metric change can be partly absorbed into orbital or ranging parameters if the analysis is careless.

A memory search therefore becomes partly a question about parameter identifiability.

Can a predicted step be distinguished from a small error in spacecraft ephemerides, laser-link calibration, clock synchronization, or slowly varying acceleration? The answer depends on how the signal projects across multiple time-delay interferometry channels and how well the main merger constrains its timing and angular structure.

Again the loud event helps.

A memory step at a random time could be nuisance drift. A step locked to the coalescence of a massive binary, with amplitude and angular dependence predicted from the main waveform and consistent across LISA channels, has a much narrower alternative set.

This is the same epistemic architecture as ground-based stacking, but one event may carry much more weight.

LISA also opens extreme mass-ratio inspirals: stellar-mass compact objects orbiting massive black holes for tens of thousands of cycles. These systems map spacetime geometry with extraordinary precision. Their memory signatures differ from comparable-mass mergers and can accumulate in ways that probe long-duration fluxes. Whether individual memory components are measurable depends on source parameters and mission sensitivity, but the theoretical opportunity is rich because the hard waveform is a prolonged audit of the central spacetime.

There is a broader principle hiding here. Measurement of aftermath improves when the event's approach is observable.

Medicine has better before-and-after inference when baseline health data exist before an intervention. Engineering diagnoses failure better when sensors recorded the machine during normal operation. Ecology attributes regime shifts better with long pre-disturbance time series. Economics estimates shocks better with stable prior trends.

LISA can know the gravitational system before it becomes a transient.

That is unusually powerful because gravitational memory is defined through a difference. The better the prior state, the less freedom the after-state has to masquerade as calibration.

There is another human-scale strangeness in LISA: the instrument cannot be physically serviced in the ordinary sense. It will operate millions of kilometers from Earth. Its data products will depend on a ground segment, navigation, mission operations, calibration pipelines, and software written over decades. A slow instrumental bias discovered after launch may have to be modeled rather than repaired.

The detector's memory will be software.

That is not a weakness. The Hubble Space Telescope's famous early spherical aberration was physically corrected with servicing missions, but data-analysis techniques can also recover information from imperfect instruments when the response is known. Radio interferometers routinely synthesize images from complex calibration. Cosmic microwave background missions extract microkelvin anisotropies from instruments with foregrounds and systematics far larger than the target signal.

What matters is not purity. It is identifiability.

This is a theme I keep returning to because “permanent” tempts us to expect visual obviousness. In real measurement, permanence is a model comparison across long timescales. The residue can be invisible to the instrument's raw intuition and still recoverable statistically.

LISA will also force memory theory into cosmology. Massive black-hole mergers at high redshift propagate through an expanding universe. Their strain is redshifted; luminosity distance and cosmological parameters enter. Gravitational lensing can magnify or demagnify signals. Peculiar velocities and large-scale structure affect inference. Memory formulas derived in asymptotically flat spacetime must be translated carefully.

The farther we listen, the less the universe resembles the empty boundary condition that made the theorem clean.

That is not a reason to retreat. It is where the next physics lives.

A detection of gravitational-wave memory by LISA would likely arrive packaged with extraordinary source information: black-hole masses and spins, distance constraints, sky localization, perhaps an electromagnetic environment, and a detailed inspiral-merger-ringdown waveform. The memory would be one small component in one of the richest relativistic observations ever made.

Which is fitting.

The permanent part may be easiest to see when almost everything else is known.
