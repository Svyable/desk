# Christodoulou's Surprise

A theory in which energy gravitates should make us suspicious of any calculation that lets radiation escape without asking what the radiation itself does gravitationally.

General relativity is nonlinear for reasons deeper than the slogan “gravity gravitates,” but the slogan earns its keep here. The Einstein field equations do not allow the gravitational field to behave as a passive messenger laid over a fixed stage. The field contributes to the dynamics. Gravitational waves carry energy and momentum. Their flux changes the system. In the right calculation, the outgoing waves contribute to a lasting gravitational-wave memory.

Demetrios Christodoulou's 1991 work established a nonlinear memory effect in full general relativity. The result became known as Christodoulou memory. Kip Thorne quickly emphasized its relationship to gravitational-wave detection, and later work placed it within a broader category often called null memory: memory sourced by energy flux reaching null infinity, whether that flux is gravitational radiation or other effectively massless radiation.

There are several ways to make the result sound more magical than it is. “Gravity remembers gravity” is one. “Waves leave permanent scars in spacetime” is another. I will use the scar image sometimes because it is useful, but the mathematics deserves first claim on the idea.

A compact binary radiates gravitational waves because its mass distribution has a changing quadrupole and higher multipoles. The dominant oscillatory waveform alternates sign as the bodies orbit. Now consider the energy carried by that radiation. Energy flux is positive. Its angular distribution changes in time, but it does not oscillate between positive and negative energy the way the strain oscillates. Integrating the flux over the event produces a net contribution. Through the nonlinear field equations, this integrated radiative energy generates a nonoscillatory change in the gravitational field observed far away.

That is the rough physical route to nonlinear memory.

The word rough should remain visible. Actual waveform calculations use multipolar expansions, radiative moments, post-Newtonian theory, numerical relativity, perturbation theory, or combinations of these depending on the source phase. The memory contribution depends on inclination and source geometry. Different modes contribute. Gauge and asymptotic-frame choices have to be handled correctly. A sentence about positive energy flux is intuition, not a substitute for the calculation.

Still, the intuition explains why a merger is such a natural source. Near coalescence, a binary black hole can radiate several percent of its total mass-energy in gravitational waves. GW150914's remnant mass was about three solar masses less than the sum of the initial black-hole masses, with that difference radiated predominantly as gravitational-wave energy. The event briefly emitted gravitational power beyond ordinary astronomical comparison. If gravitational radiation itself sources memory, the most violent gravitational-wave sources are obvious places to look.

Obvious does not mean easy.

The oscillatory chirp from a binary black hole sits in the band terrestrial interferometers were built to hear. Memory builds like a step around merger. Its characteristic frequency content reaches toward the low-frequency wall where ground motion and suspension/control behavior make interferometers less sensitive. The peak memory strain is also much smaller than the main oscillatory signal for typical events.

A wonderfully perverse measurement problem follows. The same event can be loud enough to announce itself clearly while its most permanent predicted gravitational signature hides below the detector's practical baseline stability.

We hear the shout and struggle to prove the room moved.

This is where statistical accumulation enters. If memory has a predictable sign and angular dependence relative to parameters inferred from the main waveform, multiple events can in principle be combined. The loud oscillatory signals tell us enough about source masses, orientation, distance, and phase to predict aspects of the weaker memory contribution. Rather than demand that one event rise alone above the noise, researchers can perform coherent stacking or population-level inference.

There is a subtlety: the sign of the memory in a detector is not automatically known from every ordinary gravitational-wave observation because binary orientation and polarization can have degeneracies. Methods have been developed to use higher-order waveform information or other features to break the degeneracy and stack consistently. This is an example of why “just add the events together” is not analysis.

The broader lesson is more interesting. A weak aftermath can become observable because the transient that caused it supplies a template.

This is not unique to gravitational waves. In particle physics, a strong tag can define a sample in which a rare decay is sought. In astronomy, a bright transient can trigger searches for faint counterparts at other wavelengths. In geophysics, the timing of an earthquake lets scientists average post-seismic effects against a known event. Cause and residue can be measured on different channels.

The gravitational-memory case is unusually clean conceptually because the primary and residual signals emerge from the same relativistic event and can be modeled together.

What would observing Christodoulou memory tell us?

At the most conservative level, it would confirm a specific nonlinear prediction of general relativity in the radiative regime. Direct gravitational-wave detections have already tested nonlinear strong-field gravity impressively through inspiral-merger-ringdown consistency, population analyses, propagation tests, black-hole spectroscopy efforts, and agreement with numerical-relativity waveforms. Memory would add a qualitatively different observable: an infrared, nonoscillatory component tied to energy flux.

It would also test waveform completeness. For precision astronomy, neglected memory can eventually bias parameter estimation or waveform comparisons as detector sensitivity improves. Even before memory becomes a standalone trophy detection, it may matter as part of accurate gravitational-wave modeling.

And it would make the asymptotic-symmetry triangle experimentally less abstract. The classical displacement memory is tied, under the relevant assumptions, to the same structure that appears as a BMS supertranslation and a soft-graviton theorem. Observing the detector residue would not “detect a symmetry” in the simple sense, but it would test one face of a correspondence whose other faces live in mathematical descriptions far removed from interferometer photodiodes.

That is an appealing thought because physics often advances when structures invented for consistency become instruments for observation.

Noether's theorem connects continuous symmetries to conserved currents and charges. Gauge symmetry organizes the Standard Model. General covariance shapes gravity. These are not decorative principles. They control which processes can occur, how amplitudes behave, what charges exist, and which quantities can be measured.

BMS symmetry initially looked like specialized mathematics at the boundary of asymptotically flat spacetime. Memory helped pull it toward phenomenology.

There is a temptation to say that Christodoulou discovered the universe keeps a receipt for gravitational radiation. I like receipts, but this one overpromises. A receipt lists line items. Memory is compressed. It depends on integrated fluxes and angular patterns. It loses enormous detail. Two distinct merger histories can yield similar net memory in one detector. A network and full waveform recover more information, but the mapping is not a perfect inverse.

A better analogy is recoil.

Fire a gun and the shooter's shoulder moves. From the recoil you learn that momentum went somewhere. You do not reconstruct the exact pressure field in the barrel. A rocket changes velocity when exhaust leaves; the final velocity integrates a history of thrust but does not preserve every combustion fluctuation. Christodoulou memory is far subtler, but it belongs to this family of physical consequences that witness flux without replaying it.

The universe keeps aftermath, not minutes.

This matters when people drag memory effects into debates about information loss. A lasting classical observable does not by itself prove microscopic information preservation or solve the black-hole information paradox. The information paradox asks whether quantum evolution through black-hole formation and evaporation is unitary and how information about infalling states is represented in outgoing radiation or other degrees of freedom. Soft modes and asymptotic charges may constrain the story, but memory alone is not a magic hard drive containing all lost microstate information.

Hawking, Perry, and Strominger proposed that black holes carry “soft hair” associated with asymptotic symmetries, stimulating an enormous literature. Critics and follow-up work have clarified both what these charges capture and what they do not. The strongest claims remain debated. We will get there later.

For now, Christodoulou memory deserves to stand without borrowing grandeur from the information paradox.

Its grandeur is already sufficient. General relativity predicts that radiation's own energy contributes to a permanent relative displacement. The wave is part of its own aftermath.

That sentence violates a useful Newtonian habit. We like to divide the world into source, signal, and receiver. The source emits. The signal transports. The receiver records. Nonlinear gravity blurs the compartments. The signal carries energy that gravitates; that energy participates in producing the receiver's lasting relational change.

Source, signal, and aftermath are coupled by the field equations.

This is not consciousness. It is not cosmic purpose. It is a nonlinear conservation story written in geometry.

Which is stranger, and better.
