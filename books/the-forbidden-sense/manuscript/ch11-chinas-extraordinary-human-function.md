# China’s Extraordinary Human Function

China’s late-twentieth-century encounter with psi is useful because it breaks the lazy assumption that anomalous human-capacity research is merely an American Cold War eccentricity.

From the late 1970s through the 1990s, China experienced what scholars have called qigong fever and, at times, enthusiasm for “extraordinary human functions.” Claims included unusual perception, psychokinesis, healing effects, distant influence and exceptional physiological control. Researchers, popular magazines, officials, scientists, military-linked institutions and charismatic qigong masters occupied overlapping parts of the story.

The period became large enough to create its own scientific-pseudoscientific boundary war.

That boundary is exactly what makes the Chinese case valuable.

The claim was not simply “ancient wisdom versus Western science.” Chinese researchers often tried to instrument the phenomena. They discussed controls. They argued over tamper-proof targets. Some experiments used sealed objects, videotaping and multiple observers. Chinese journals published studies. U.S. defense and intelligence organizations collected and translated portions of the literature because they wanted to understand what was being claimed.

CIA’s declassified STARGATE holdings include DIA translations of Chinese material on qigong and paranormal abilities. One translation titled “Trends of Qigong Research at Home and Abroad in the Past Ten Years” was produced by DIA in 1991 from Chinese material and is available at https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp96-00792r000200650023-0 . Another document discussing Chinese somatic-science work notes proposed safeguards such as tamper-proof sealing of targets and continuous multi-angle observation: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp96-00792r000300410014-5 .

These documents are evidence of Chinese claims and American interest in them. They are not independent replication of the claims.

That sentence should be printed on the cover of every declassified intelligence collection.

A translated foreign paper does not become more true because DIA translated it. An intelligence archive can preserve bad science as faithfully as good science.

Still, the details matter.

Chinese researchers were confronting the same methodological problem Western parapsychologists faced: if a subject claims to move or identify a hidden object, how do you exclude ordinary access and trickery?

Tamper-evident sealing is a clue that fraud was already part of the experimental imagination.

This matters because qigong fever created incentives for performance. Famous masters drew followers. Extraordinary abilities could confer social status and authority. In any environment where a human subject benefits from astonishing observers, adversarial controls become mandatory.

Magicians understand this instinctively.

Scientists often do not.

A laboratory is designed to measure cooperative nature, not a strategic human opponent. Nature does not palm the coin. Nature does not peek at the envelope. Nature does not recruit an assistant. A claimant can.

This is why anomalous-human-performance research should borrow security engineering.

The experiment should assume that information wants to leak.

Containers should be uniquely numbered, weighed, photographed and independently prepared. Randomization should happen outside the claimant’s access. Continuous video should include blind spots and handoffs. Materials should be controlled before and after. Independent observers should include people experienced in deception methods. If the phenomenon survives, the claimant benefits.

The cultural difficulty is obvious. Treating a respected qigong master as a potential adversary may be considered insulting. Treating a psychic subject that way can destroy rapport. Researchers sometimes worry that suspicion itself reduces performance.

This is a genuine design problem.

A protocol can be adversarial without being hostile.

Casinos do it. Nuclear materials accounting does it. Drug trials do it through blinding rather than accusation. The system should make deception difficult regardless of anyone’s character.

Chinese “extraordinary human function” research also collided with nationalism.

A society emerging from the Cultural Revolution and reopening scientific institutions faced a complicated question: what counted as uniquely Chinese knowledge worthy of modern validation? Qigong, traditional medicine and extraordinary-function claims could be framed as indigenous resources neglected by Western materialism.

That framing raises the stakes of an experiment.

A positive result no longer validates only one subject. It can validate a civilization’s intellectual heritage. A negative result can feel like cultural betrayal.

Science performs poorly when every outcome carries identity.

The West has its own versions. Nutrition research becomes attached to lifestyle tribes. climate evidence becomes political identity. Psychedelic science attracts evangelists. AI safety arguments map onto institutional camps. Anomalous phenomena are simply more naked about the problem.

Qigong fever also demonstrates how health claims and psi claims can merge.

Breathing practices, slow movement, attention training and social exercise can have ordinary physiological effects. Relaxation can alter heart rate and stress. Exercise improves health. Expectation changes subjective experience. Group practice creates community. None of these effects requires an external qi energy capable of acting at distance.

When real benefits exist, they can lend credibility to more extraordinary claims.

This is another adjacency trap.

Meditation changes brain activity, therefore telepathy. Acupuncture produces some pain-modulation effects, therefore meridians are literal invisible channels. Breathing changes autonomic state, therefore qi emissions can alter matter. The first statement does not entail the second.

A mature investigation decomposes the bundle.

What can qigong practitioners do that matched controls cannot?

Can physiological effects be explained by breathing, posture, expectation or conditioning?

Are claimed external emissions measurable with ordinary instruments?

Do distant effects survive blinding?

Can extraordinary-perception tasks survive adversarial controls?

China’s scientific debate eventually became fierce. Critics publicly challenged extraordinary claims and exposed fraud. The political environment around qigong organizations also changed sharply by the end of the 1990s. The story cannot be reduced to a steady secret national program discovering psychic powers. It involved mass culture, scientific controversy, ideology and state response.

Scholarship on the period describes intense disputes over science and pseudoscience. One useful historical treatment is Li Jianhui’s 2015 article on qigong fever and the science-pseudoscience debate in China: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/zygo.12178 . The article is secondary scholarship, not proof or disproof of individual experiments. It helps reconstruct the social landscape.

The U.S. intelligence translations add another layer: foreign observers were mining that landscape for possible signal.

This mirrors the Soviet problem.

An adversary’s or competitor’s interest can make fringe research strategically relevant even if the underlying claim is weak. If Chinese laboratories were investigating unusual human capabilities, U.S. analysts wanted to know whether any result might matter.

Again, collection is not endorsement.

The Chinese case also challenges a common conspiracy narrative that governments uniformly suppress psi.

At different times, governments have suppressed, encouraged, funded, translated and investigated anomalous claims. There is no single “government” position because states contain competing institutions with different incentives.

A military organization may investigate. An academy may criticize. A security service may worry about mass movements. A health ministry may regulate claims. A political leadership may shift policy. The same phenomenon can be treated as science, threat, fraud and cultural heritage by different parts of the state.

This pluralism is important when people say, “China knows.”

Which institution?

Which year?

Knows what?

From what evidence?

A translated paper about a claimed qigong effect is not evidence that the Chinese government possesses a validated nonhuman or paranormal technology. It is evidence that people in China investigated and argued about extraordinary human-function claims.

That history is already substantial.

One especially interesting feature is the attempt to reconcile extraordinary abilities with materialism.

Like Soviet researchers, Chinese investigators often sought physical explanations. Qi could be treated not only as a traditional concept but as some measurable energy or information process. Reports discussed electromagnetic, infrared, particle or other emissions.

The appeal is understandable.

If a traditional concept maps onto a measurable physical variable, cultural continuity and modern science can be reconciled.

The danger is premature identification.

A thermal camera detects heat from a practitioner’s hands. That does not prove qi. Humans emit infrared radiation. An electric-field sensor responds near the body. That does not prove a novel energy. Bodies carry charge and environments contain mains noise. A gas-discharge photograph produces a glowing corona. That does not reveal an aura; high-voltage ionization can explain it.

Every “energy” claim needs a baseline model of ordinary physiology and instrumentation.

This is where frontier science often fails publicly. A sensor produces an image no one has seen before, and the image becomes the phenomenon. But instruments translate. They have transfer functions, noise floors, calibration errors and artifacts.

The correct question is not “did the instrument detect something?” It is “did the instrument detect something unexplained after known sources were modeled?”

That standard applies equally to UAP sensors, biofields, anomalous materials and psi.

The Chinese literature is also useful because it contains claims involving children and extraordinary perception. Such cases are culturally powerful and scientifically hazardous.

Children can be exceptional observers. They can also respond strongly to adult expectations. If a family, school, laboratory or national culture rewards extraordinary performance, demand characteristics become severe. Fraud can be conscious or unconscious. Researchers need child-protection protocols as well as experimental controls.

No scientific result is worth constructing an identity around a child that depends on continued paranormal success.

This ethical point rarely appears in heroic accounts of gifted subjects.

The better approach is ordinary: anonymize where possible, avoid public celebrity, preregister methods, provide independent oversight and treat null performance as normal rather than failure of the child.

China’s qigong era also offers a warning about mass adoption.

A weakly evidenced claim can become socially real even if physically false. Millions of people can organize routines, beliefs and institutions around it. Charismatic leaders can accumulate authority. Governments can respond to the social movement rather than the scientific claim.

This distinction matters for suppressed knowledge.

A government may restrict an anomalous practice because it fears organization, fraud, health consequences or political independence—not because the practice secretly works.

The fact of suppression does not validate the suppressed claim.

This is one of the most persistent reasoning errors in fringe history: “They tried to stop it, therefore it must be powerful.”

States suppress harmless things too.

States also fail to suppress dangerous things.

Political behavior is not a scientific instrument.

What, then, survives the Chinese case?

First, a documented period of broad Chinese interest in qigong and extraordinary human-function claims.

Second, real attempts by some researchers to develop experimental controls and physical measurements.

Third, intense scientific and public controversy, including fraud concerns and pseudoscience criticism.

Fourth, U.S. defense-intelligence interest sufficient to translate and archive portions of the literature.

Fifth, no publicly established, reproducible Chinese psi technology that crossed into ordinary global science.

That last point is important. If Chinese laboratories had developed a robust human capacity to perceive sealed targets or influence matter at distance, the most interesting fact would not be that DIA translated their papers. The interesting fact would be that the technique worked when anyone else tried it.

Real phenomena migrate.

They escape culture.

Gunpowder did not remain Chinese because Europeans had different metaphysics. Magnetic compasses did not require belief in qi. Acupuncture’s empirically testable components can be studied anywhere. A reliable anomalous ability should survive translation too.

If psi is real, it is not Chinese, Russian or American.

It is human.

That is the standard this book keeps returning to.

A nation can discover a phenomenon first. It cannot own the laws that make it reproducible.
