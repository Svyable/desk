# The Future Arrives Early

Precognition is the most offensive psi claim because it does not merely ask science to discover a new sensory channel. It asks causality to tolerate an insult.

Telepathy can at least be imagined as an unknown signal traveling between two brains. Clairvoyance can be imagined as some undiscovered coupling between observer and distant target. Psychokinesis can be imagined as an unknown force. Each hypothetical mechanism is unsupported, but the time order remains familiar: cause, then effect.

Precognition reverses the embarrassment.

A participant responds now. A target is randomly chosen later. The earlier response appears correlated with the later event.

If that correlation is real and ordinary leakage has been excluded, something in our account of time, causation, measurement or information is incomplete.

That is why precognition experiments deserve exceptionally severe controls.

In 2011 the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology published Daryl Bem’s “Feeling the Future,” a set of experiments that reversed the temporal order of familiar psychological effects. In one type of task, for example, participants responded before a randomly selected future stimulus that under ordinary causal assumptions should not yet have influenced the response. The paper was controversial precisely because it used recognizable mainstream experimental forms to make an unrecognizable claim.

The reaction helped accelerate a broader methodological reckoning in psychology.

Critics pointed to analytical flexibility, multiple comparisons, optional stopping, weak priors and the ease with which conventional significance testing could produce surprising results. The argument was not merely “precognition is impossible.” It became “if our standard methods can prove precognition, perhaps our standard methods are too permissive.”

That was an important moment.

A fringe claim became a diagnostic test for mainstream statistics.

Bem’s work also generated replication attempts and later meta-analysis. A 2016 meta-analysis of ninety experiments from thirty-three laboratories reported a small positive average effect and argued that independent replications remained above chance. The paper is openly available at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4706048/ . Critics disputed how strongly that aggregate should update belief, emphasizing prior probability, study quality, dependence, selective reporting and the vulnerability of tiny effects to analytical choices.

The effect sizes are the part casual readers usually skip. They should not.

The reported effects were small.

Small effects are where prior belief matters most. If a study reports that an ordinary memory intervention improves recall by a few percent, scientists may provisionally accept the result and wait for replication because the causal category is familiar. If the same few-percent shift implies information moving backward through time, the alternative explanations become far more probable than the extraordinary mechanism.

This is Bayesian common sense, not dogmatism.

But priors are not vetoes.

A low prior probability can be overcome by sufficiently strong, independent and reproducible evidence. The question is how much.

Jeffrey Rouder and Richard Morey examined Bem’s evidence using Bayesian methods and concluded that some subsets offered only slight support while one emotionally valenced subset produced more notable evidence, though still far below what they considered sufficient to overcome a skeptical prior about ESP. Their paper is indexed at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21573926/ .

This disagreement is useful because it forces an uncomfortable question into the open: how improbable should scientists consider psi before looking at the data?

The answer cannot be zero unless the claim is logically impossible.

Physics does not currently provide a mechanism for macroscopic precognition. That is not the same as a theorem that no empirical correlation across time can ever occur. Fundamental physics contains time-symmetric equations, delayed-choice experiments and correlations that offend ordinary intuition, but none of these establishes human precognition or allows easy backward communication. Popular psi writing often blurs that distinction.

The correct prior should therefore be very low, not metaphysically forbidden.

Then the data have a job.

The cleanest precognition experiment would make the future target genuinely unpredictable from all information available at response time. A high-quality hardware random number generator can choose the target only after the participant’s response is irreversibly recorded. The software and randomization process must be auditable. Timing must prevent buffering or pseudo-random predictability. Analysis must be fixed in advance. All trials must count.

If the effect survives those conditions across independent laboratories, the argument becomes serious.

Presentiment experiments push the idea below conscious choice.

In these studies, physiological measures such as skin conductance, heart rate or pupil response are recorded continuously. A computer later selects an emotionally arousing or neutral stimulus. Researchers ask whether physiology in the seconds before the random selection differs depending on what stimulus will subsequently appear.

A 2012 meta-analysis by Julia Mossbridge, Patrizio Tressoldi and Jessica Utts examined twenty-six reports and described a small anticipatory physiological effect: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23109927/ . The authors explicitly framed the result as unexplained anticipatory activity, not a settled mechanism.

Presentiment is experimentally appealing because physiology can be recorded automatically. There is less room for a person to narrate a vague impression after the fact.

It is also vulnerable to exquisitely technical artifacts.

Physiological signals have autocorrelation. Trial sequences may not be perfectly independent. Baseline correction can import post-stimulus information into pre-stimulus windows if done carelessly. Randomization algorithms can create local sequence structure. Filtering can smear signals backward in time. Researchers can choose analysis windows after observing data. Unequal numbers of stimulus classes can create expectation effects. Participants may learn sequence probabilities even when nominal selection is random.

Every one of these possibilities is more mundane than backward information flow.

This is why presentiment is a beautiful test case for adversarial analysis.

Give the same raw dataset to two teams. One team believes the effect exists. One believes it is artifact. Have both write preprocessing and analysis pipelines before unblinding condition labels. Then apply them to a new dataset. Better yet, have the teams agree on a single pipeline whose vulnerabilities both accept.

If the anticipatory effect survives, confidence rises.

If it vanishes, the old literature gains an explanation.

There is another way future information can appear without exotic causation: post-selection.

Human memory is a post-selection machine.

We dream constantly. We have intuitions constantly. We imagine accidents, phone calls, deaths, reunions and unlikely events. Most do not happen and are forgotten. When one later matches reality, it becomes luminous. The memory of the earlier experience may also sharpen after the event. Details that fit are strengthened. Details that do not fit fade.

This is why personal precognition stories can be psychologically overwhelming and scientifically weak at the same time.

A mother dreams of a plane crash and a relative flies the next day. A person thinks of an old friend moments before the friend calls. Someone feels dread before bad news arrives. These events can change a person’s worldview. Telling them that coincidence exists often feels insulting because the subjective improbability is enormous.

The scientific problem is denominator blindness.

How many dreams did the person have that year? How many relatives traveled? How many vague feelings of dread occurred before ordinary days? How many old friends were thought about without calling? How many details in the dream did not match?

Without prospective recording, the probability cannot be reconstructed.

Smartphones make a better experiment possible.

Thousands of participants could timestamp dreams or predictions before outcomes. Entries could be cryptographically committed so they cannot be edited. Specificity could be scored by blind raters. Base rates could be estimated. Participants could specify confidence. The study could distinguish ordinary coincidence from any residual anomaly.

This would be citizen science worth doing.

It would also probably disappoint both camps.

Most prophetic-feeling experiences would collapse under prospective accounting. A smaller residue might remain ambiguous. Human lives generate an enormous search space for coincidence, and rare correspondences become inevitable at population scale.

The same denominator problem applies to intelligence programs.

A viewer makes hundreds of statements across years. An officer remembers the one that resembles a later event. Unless all statements are prospectively scored, “too accurate to be chance” is intuition, not calculation.

Precognition claims demand more because the future itself creates selection opportunities.

There is a philosophical temptation to rescue the effect through block-universe physics.

Relativity undermines the idea of a universal present. In some interpretations, spacetime is treated as a four-dimensional structure in which past and future events are equally part of the manifold. Popular writers then ask: if the future “already exists,” why could consciousness not access it?

The answer is that ontology does not supply a channel.

A city may already exist north of you. That does not mean your brain receives information from it without a physical process. A block universe does not automatically permit controllable retrocausal signaling. Physics constrains correlations through equations and boundary conditions, not metaphors about what “exists.”

There are serious discussions of retrocausal interpretations in quantum foundations, but moving from those to human precognition requires a quantitative bridge that does not currently exist.

The absence of that bridge should be treated as a research gap, not filled with poetry.

There is, however, a subtler human-role thesis hiding here.

Brains are prediction machines.

Most of what feels like seeing the future is ordinary inference. We read posture, weather, tone, markets, disease trajectories, social tension and countless weak cues below conscious awareness. A firefighter senses that a room is wrong before the floor collapses because experience has compressed patterns. A clinician feels uneasy before lab results because subtle signs have accumulated. A parent knows a child is sick before temperature rises because tiny behavioral changes matter.

These intuitions can feel paranormal because the causal inputs were not consciously represented.

Any serious psi study must therefore compete against the extraordinary power of ordinary unconscious prediction.

This is a high bar.

A person who predicts an event before the explicit evidence is visible may still be using evidence.

The laboratory solves this by choosing future targets randomly after the response. If randomization is genuine, ordinary prediction should be impossible.

That is what makes those experiments worth attention despite their tiny effects.

The strongest skeptical explanation of precognition is not one thing. It is a family: methodological flexibility, publication bias, sequence artifacts, sensory leakage, expectation, regression, post-selection, memory distortion and chance.

The strongest proponent case is also cumulative: positive results reported across different paradigms, laboratories and decades, sometimes under automated conditions designed to remove known ordinary routes.

The argument cannot be settled by counting papers.

It has to be settled by building a better experiment than the literature that came before.

A global prospective replication network would be straightforward. The protocol could be open source. Target generation could be auditable. Data could stream in encrypted form to independent repositories. Analyses could be registered. Laboratories could run identical tasks for years. The project could be cheap enough for universities and citizen-science sites.

Why does this matter beyond psi?

Because a reliable zero would be informative about scientific self-correction, and a reliable nonzero would be one of the strangest biological facts ever measured.

There is almost no middle outcome worth more than the experiment costs.

Yet the field remains in the middle.

That is becoming a pattern.
