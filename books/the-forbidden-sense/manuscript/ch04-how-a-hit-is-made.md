# How a Hit Is Made

A remote-viewing transcript is not a result until somebody decides what it means.

That sentence sounds trivial. It is the hinge of the entire subject.

Imagine a page produced before a target is revealed. On it are the words “open,” “cold,” “metal,” “vertical,” “movement,” “water,” and a sketch that resembles a curved line beside two rectangles. The target turns out to be a dam. Once you know that, the page begins to organize itself. Water is obvious. Metal could be gates. Vertical is the face. Movement is flow. The curved line becomes a reservoir edge. The rectangles become towers.

Now replace the target with an aircraft carrier.

Water still fits. Metal fits better. Vertical can describe the island. Movement fits. The curved line becomes the flight deck edge. The rectangles become superstructure. Replace the target with a suspension bridge, hydroelectric plant, shipyard, refinery or marina and a human judge can continue finding correspondences.

This is not fraud. It is perception doing what perception does.

Human beings are correspondence engines. We recognize faces in clouds, animals in constellations and narrative structure in noisy sequences. More usefully, we can take incomplete information and map it onto a known situation. That ability is part of intelligence itself. It is also a contaminant when the scientific question is whether the incomplete information contained knowledge of one specific hidden target.

The solution is not to ban subjective description. The solution is to make the subjectivity compete.

A proper judging procedure can take one transcript and several possible targets, keep the judge blind to which target was actually used, and ask for a ranking. If the correct targets consistently receive better ranks than chance predicts, the result becomes harder to explain through ordinary retrospective matching.

Harder does not mean impossible.

Target pools matter. If one photograph shows a volcano and four show office buildings, a transcript containing “mountain” is unusually diagnostic. If targets differ systematically in complexity, emotional salience, color or geometry, generic descriptions may favor some. If the same target pool is reused, participants or judges may learn its structure. If target selection is not genuinely random, temporal patterns can leak. If the experimenter who interacts with the viewer knows the target, involuntary cueing can occur. If the judge knows which transcript belongs to which condition, expectations can shape ranking.

A psi experiment is therefore a cryptographic problem disguised as psychology.

Information must not leak through channels the experiment is not trying to test.

This is why the phrase “sensory leakage” is too narrow. Leakage can be procedural. It can arrive through file names, handling order, voice tone, target frequencies, experimenter behavior, feedback timing, metadata, room schedule or subtle differences in materials. A system can be physically sealed and informationally open.

The history of experimental psychology is full of these surprises. Clever Hans, the horse that appeared to perform arithmetic, was responding to tiny changes in human posture as the correct number approached. Experimenters had not intentionally signaled the answer. Their bodies leaked it. The lesson was not that animal cognition is fake. The lesson was that blinding matters whenever one organism reads another.

Remote viewing is even more vulnerable because the claimed channel is defined by exclusion. If ordinary information enters anywhere, the experiment no longer tests what it says it tests.

This creates an unusual burden. In many sciences a control group estimates nuisance effects. In psi, a single hidden route can generate the whole phenomenon.

Suppose a target is drawn from a pool of one hundred images using software. If the randomization seed depends on clock time and the viewer knows roughly when sessions occur, the target may not be as unpredictable as assumed. That sounds absurdly technical until one remembers that cryptographic systems have failed for exactly such reasons. Suppose the person preparing the target packet knows its contents and later hands materials to an experimenter. Tiny procedural differences may correlate with target classes. Suppose the outbound team’s travel time changes when a distant target is selected. Session duration may become a cue.

None of these examples proves that a specific remote-viewing study failed. They illustrate why extraordinary care is required.

The strongest psi researchers came to understand this. Later experimental designs used increasingly automated target selection, separate roles, blind judging and formal statistics. The move from ad hoc demonstrations to controlled series is the most important scientific development in the field.

It is also why casual summaries are misleading. Saying “the government tested psychics” compresses decades of methodological evolution into a carnival phrase. The actual question was how to produce a dataset in which a hidden target could be inferred from a participant’s response without ordinary information flow.

That is an information-science problem.

Consider rank-order judging.

If a viewer produces a response to one randomly selected target from a set of five and a blind judge later ranks the five targets by correspondence, chance predicts an average rank. Across enough trials, one can ask whether correct targets systematically rank better. The statistic is simple. The interpretation is not.

A positive average rank can arise because many trials are slightly informative or because a few are extremely strong. Those cases have different implications. A weak distributed effect might suggest a noisy channel. A handful of spectacular trials may suggest subject heterogeneity, procedural anomalies or outliers.

The distribution matters more than the headline p-value.

So does stopping.

If an experiment continues until a desirable threshold is reached, nominal significance becomes misleading. If researchers try multiple scoring rules and report the one that works, the probability of an apparent effect rises. If subgroup analyses are chosen after seeing the data, “gifted viewers” can emerge statistically. These problems are now familiar under labels such as optional stopping, researcher degrees of freedom and p-hacking. Psi research encountered versions of them before the language became common.

The remedy is prospective specification.

Define the target-selection procedure before the trial. Define the number of sessions. Define the scoring rule. Define exclusions. Timestamp data. Preserve failures. Ideally register the protocol publicly or with an independent party before results are known.

Parapsychology has increasingly adopted these practices, and that matters. A field should be judged by its strongest current methods, not only its historical weaknesses.

But history still affects the evidence base when meta-analysis combines old and new studies.

A meta-analysis can produce a precise estimate of a biased literature. Statistical aggregation does not magically clean methodology. If early studies had sensory leakage, flexible scoring or selective publication, combining them with later studies may preserve the bias. Researchers therefore argue over inclusion criteria, study quality and whether effect sizes decline as controls improve.

This argument is not unique to psi. Medicine, psychology and economics have all discovered that published literatures can exaggerate effects. Small studies tend to vary more. Positive findings are easier to publish. Researchers unconsciously make analytical choices that favor interesting results. Replication projects often shrink famous effects.

Psi is simply the harshest possible environment for these general scientific weaknesses because its prior credibility is low and its typical effects are small.

The field cannot afford ordinary sloppiness.

One reason remote-viewing anecdotes remain popular is that they bypass this statistical discomfort. A story has a target, a description and a moment of reveal. The human brain understands it instantly. A sampling distribution does not have the same emotional force.

But the story is precisely where confirmation bias is strongest.

Take a viewer who produces forty descriptors. Ten fit the target impressively, ten fit loosely, ten are generic and ten are wrong. A narrative retelling will usually compress the session around the first ten. The reader receives a much higher apparent hit rate than the contemporaneous record contained.

This is why source preservation matters.

The raw transcript should be available. The target pool should be available. The scoring procedure should be available. The misses should remain visible. If an anecdote is real, it gets stronger when the full record survives.

Declassified STARGATE files occasionally permit this kind of inspection. A session report can contain the transcript, sketches and administrative summary. The archive also contains explicit statements of poor correlation in some training sessions. Those failures are scientifically valuable because they prevent the archive from becoming pure legend.

The next question is whether the program’s successful cases were scored prospectively enough to support the claims later made about them.

That question is difficult because operational intelligence did not always operate like a laboratory.

In an operational tasking, the target may be unique. There is no tidy set of decoys. The viewer may provide narrative material to an analyst who already knows context. The analyst may extract a useful clue from a large volume of uncertain description. Later, the event outcome becomes known and the transcript is reread.

This environment is ideal for hindsight.

To measure whether the viewer added information, one would need independent judges who do not know the outcome to assess the transcript against plausible alternatives, or an explicit prediction coded before verification. Without that, operational stories remain suggestive rather than decisive.

The distinction is similar to forecasting.

A political forecaster who says “there may be instability, leadership tension and possible movement toward negotiation” can later appear prescient under many outcomes. A calibrated forecaster gives probabilities to specific events. The statement becomes capable of being wrong.

Psi needs the same cruelty.

“Industrial,” “water,” “large,” “secret,” “underground,” “military” are not enough. What discriminative claim was made before the answer was known? How many alternatives did it eliminate? How often were similar claims wrong?

Information can be measured as reduction in uncertainty.

This provides a useful way to think about remote viewing without arguing about consciousness. Suppose a target is one of sixteen equally likely sites. Before viewing, uncertainty is four bits. If a transcript allows a blind judge to reliably narrow the target to one of four sites, the transcript has conveyed about two bits of information even if it does not name the target. If it merely produces generic language that fits all sixteen, it conveys none.

This framing exposes why vividness is not the same as information.

A poetic transcript may feel specific but eliminate nothing. A boring descriptor like “bridge” may carry substantial information if bridges are rare in the target set.

It also exposes the operational problem. Intelligence users need bits that matter.

A viewer who reliably says whether a hostage is alive could be valuable even if all architectural description is vague. A viewer who produces beautiful site sketches but cannot distinguish the correct facility from alternatives may be useless. The relevant information depends on decision context.

The AIR evaluation in 1995 effectively confronted this gap. The reviewers separated laboratory evidence from operational utility. The publicly summarized conclusion was that remote-viewing information was too inconsistent and nonspecific for intelligence use. CIA’s own later public account says the independent review found enough accurate experiences to raise statistical interest but not enough reliability for an intelligence capability: https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/ask-molly-did-cia-really-study-psychic-powers/ .

This outcome is often presented as contradictory. It is not.

A weather model can be statistically better than chance and still be poor at predicting a rare tornado in one town. A medical test can have an average association and still generate too many false positives. A weakly predictive trading strategy can disappear after costs. Statistical reality does not guarantee practical value.

If anomalous cognition exists as a tiny effect, intelligence may have been almost the worst application imaginable.

Espionage demands specificity under adversarial conditions. Targets are obscure. Feedback may be delayed or impossible. The cost of false confidence is high. Analysts need provenance. A source that produces mostly noise with occasional accurate fragments can degrade decisions because the fragments are difficult to identify in advance.

That last phrase matters: in advance.

Afterward, everyone can see the hit.

The scientific value of a prediction lies in being able to distinguish it before the outcome.

This is also the answer to a common defense of psi anecdotes: “How could they possibly have guessed that?”

Sometimes the answer may genuinely be unclear. A low-probability correspondence can be remarkable. But without knowing the number of trials, the number of descriptors, the selection procedure and the number of forgotten failures, intuition about probability is unreliable.

Lotteries produce impossible-looking coincidences every week because millions of combinations are generated. Dreams appear prophetic because people dream constantly and remember the match. Large intelligence systems produce enormous volumes of paper; some sentences will align with later events.

The relevant denominator is usually invisible.

Psi research repeatedly fights over denominators.

How many viewers were tested before the good one was selected? How many sessions occurred before the famous hit? How many target descriptors were produced? How many analytical comparisons were tried? How many studies remained unpublished? How many laboratories attempted replication?

A field that can answer those questions becomes stronger even when the answers reduce effect size.

There is an ethical dimension too. Weak measurement encourages charisma.

If performance cannot be cleanly quantified, authority migrates to personalities. The gifted viewer, the famous researcher, the skeptical magician, the program insider. Each becomes a proxy for data. Communities divide into trusted people rather than shared procedures.

This is dangerous because anomalous fields attract strong identities. Some participants have experienced events they regard as life-changing. Some skeptics have built careers debunking claims. Government secrecy adds status. The result can become tribal.

A scoring protocol has no charisma.

That is its virtue.

The correct method should allow a hostile judge to reach the same conclusion as a believer. The viewer does not need to persuade anyone. The target list, ranking rule and predeclared analysis do the work.

This is the standard every frontier claim should want.

If the effect dies under it, the field has learned something. If it survives, the surviving effect is worth far more than a thousand anecdotes.

The most interesting possibility is that remote viewing is neither a robust faculty nor pure illusion, but a small effect entangled with ordinary cognitive noise. In that case better scoring would not produce spectacular demonstrations. It would produce modest but reproducible deviations.

This is exactly the kind of result that creates long scientific wars.

A large effect changes textbooks. A zero effect disappears. A small effect at the edge of detection can persist for generations because every methodological choice matters.

Psychology knows this territory well. Priming, social effects, subtle physiological responses and behavioral interventions have all produced literatures that looked substantial until large replications altered estimates. Some effects survived smaller. Some collapsed. Some turned out to depend on conditions not understood in the original work.

Psi may be another replication story.

Or it may be the most extreme version of one.

The path forward is therefore almost boring: registered multi-lab protocols, automated randomization, raw-data publication, adversarial collaboration, independent analysis, explicit model comparison and measurement of individual stability.

No spoon bending required.

The irony is that this kind of experiment is less culturally satisfying than the original mystery. If psi becomes real science, it will become tedious. There will be calibration plots. Exclusion criteria. Hardware failures. Power analyses. Arguments over mixed-effects models. Reviewers asking for another control.

That tedium is what truth looks like when it is being domesticated.

A real forbidden sense would eventually stop being forbidden.

It would become another column in the methods section.
