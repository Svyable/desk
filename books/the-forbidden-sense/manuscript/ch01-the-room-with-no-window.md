# The Room With No Window

The most useful place to begin is not with Uri Geller bending a spoon, not with a Himalayan adept, not with a Victorian séance, and not with a grainy photograph of a Soviet laboratory whose caption has been recopied so many times that nobody remembers where it came from. It is with a room in which almost nothing happens.

A person sits at a table. Somewhere else, a target has been chosen. The target might be a bridge, an airport, a water tower, a laboratory building, a coastline. The person at the table is not supposed to know which. An interviewer may know or may be blind. Sometimes an outbound team drives to the target while the person in the room writes words and draws shapes. Sometimes the target exists only as a photograph sealed away from the viewer. Later somebody compares the transcript to the target.

That is remote viewing in its least theatrical form. No incense. No trance costume. No claim, at least at the beginning, about souls leaving bodies or minds tunneling through spacetime. The phrase itself was chosen because it did not commit the investigators to a mechanism. Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ, working at Stanford Research Institute in the 1970s, wanted a neutral label for an alleged capacity to describe a distant or hidden target without ordinary sensory access. They could have called it clairvoyance. They did not.

The bureaucratic documents are stranger than the folklore because they are so ordinary. A declassified Army session report from 1981 begins by stating that the document records a remote-viewing session conducted in response to a request for information. It says the viewer’s impressions are being provided as “raw intelligence data” and that interpretation is the responsibility of the requester. The report then moves into transcript, sketches, timestamps and procedural notes. The page has classification markings. It looks like every other piece of Cold War paper that spent years in a safe. The target, the claimed perception and the institutional handling of it have been flattened into staff work. The document is available in the CIA’s STARGATE collection: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp96-00788r000700070001-8 .

Another report, from the Iranian hostage crisis, says the purpose of the session was to provide information relevant to the U.S. Embassy compound in Tehran. Again the language is administrative. Again the impressions are explicitly described as raw data, not established intelligence. Again the government tried it anyway: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp96-00788r000800060001-8 .

This is the first fact the subject has to survive. The United States government really did spend years testing remote viewing and related phenomena. CIA has said so plainly. In a public retrospective, the Agency says it began its own research in the summer of 1972, ended that phase in 1977, and that the work continued under Defense Department sponsorship before returning to CIA for evaluation in the mid-1990s. The collection was later declassified. You do not need an anonymous whistleblower for this part. You do not need to infer a program from a budget code or a secondhand memoir. The institutional history exists in the open: https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/ask-molly-did-cia-really-study-psychic-powers/ .

The second fact is harder for believers and skeptics alike. The existence of the program does not establish the existence of the ability.

Governments investigate things that turn out to be wrong. They fund ideas because an adversary might be pursuing them. They continue programs because an internal champion survives budget season. They are vulnerable to fashion, fear, bureaucratic inertia, contractor persuasion and the peculiar Cold War logic in which the possibility that the other side believes something can become a reason to investigate it yourself. A classified stamp does not transmute a hypothesis into a phenomenon. Sometimes secrecy protects a breakthrough. Sometimes it protects embarrassment. Often it protects uncertainty.

Remote viewing sits precisely in that uncomfortable middle. The records are real. The claimed faculty remains contested.

That makes it more interesting, not less.

The standard cultural treatment of psi forces a false choice. Either the world is secretly magical and institutions know it, or the entire history is a parade of gullibility and fraud. Both versions simplify away the best material. The actual record contains good experimental questions, bad experiments, sincere witnesses, subjective scoring, unusual statistical results, failures, methodological repairs, operational disappointments, institutional anxiety, Cold War threat inflation, and a long argument over what counts as replication when the effect under study is weak, noisy and allegedly dependent on human state.

The result is not a clean mystery. It is a scientific border dispute that lasted long enough to acquire intelligence officers, military tasking, congressional attention, Soviet threat assessments, Chinese translations, peer-reviewed papers, skeptics, meta-analysts and a warehouse of records.

The reason to take it seriously is not that the most extraordinary interpretation must be true. The reason is that the question was asked by institutions with enough money and motive to run it repeatedly, and the answer never became simple enough to kill the argument.

That is the thesis of this book.

Something happened in the remote-viewing literature that was persistent enough to keep serious people looking and unstable enough to keep the field from becoming ordinary science. The central task is to find out what survives after we remove the romance.

The most cautious version of the question is already unsettling: can a person produce information about a hidden target at rates that cannot be explained by ordinary sensory leakage, known inference, selective reporting, flexible scoring or chance?

Notice how little metaphysics that sentence contains. It does not ask whether consciousness is nonlocal. It does not ask whether a soul travels. It does not invoke quantum entanglement, because quantum words have been used for decades as decorative mortar around gaps in explanation. It asks whether an information effect remains after the ordinary routes are closed.

That is enough.

If the answer is no, then we have another problem worth understanding: how did a null effect survive inside laboratories and intelligence organizations for decades, and why did competent participants sometimes come away persuaded that something nonordinary was occurring? That question reaches into experimental design, intelligence analysis, cognitive bias, adversarial uncertainty and institutional sociology.

If the answer is yes—even weakly, intermittently, without a known mechanism—then the consequences are larger than the paranormal vocabulary that has grown around the field. A reliable anomaly in human information acquisition would be a fact about biology or mind before it was a fact about espionage. It would belong next to magnetoreception, echolocation, electroreception and the many other cases in which organisms turned out to couple to the environment through channels humans did not initially appreciate. The mechanism might be exotic or merely undiscovered. The first obligation would be measurement.

This distinction matters because humans have a terrible habit when confronting anomalies. We move from observation to mythology without spending enough time in the middle.

A person describes a distant structure. The description appears unexpectedly accurate. Before the scoring is settled, somebody has already written a theory of consciousness. A subject reports a vivid dream before an event. Before base rates and selection effects are established, the conversation has reached retrocausality. A laboratory claims an effect under sensory shielding. Before an independent team reproduces it, the result has become proof that materialism is dead.

Skepticism can commit the mirror-image error. A field attracts cranks, therefore every result is worthless. A protocol once leaked information, therefore no later protocol can matter. A famous claimant cheated, therefore all anomalous cognition is fraud. A proposed mechanism is foolish, therefore the observation it was meant to explain cannot exist.

Neither shortcut is science.

The room with no window is useful because it strips the problem down. What information was available? Who knew the target? How was it chosen? When was the transcript fixed? What counted as a hit? Who judged it? How many trials were run? What was discarded? Could the subject infer the target from procedure, experimenter behavior or the structure of the target pool? Was feedback given? Did the protocol change after failures? Were successful subjects selected after extensive testing? Can another laboratory produce the effect under comparable conditions? And, most important for intelligence work, did the information improve decisions before the target truth was known?

These questions sound mundane. They are the whole book.

One declassified training report is particularly instructive because it documents failure without ceremony. Remote Viewing Session 876, conducted in March 1982, says the post-session analysis showed “very little correlation” with the target. Another early training summary says a viewer showed “absolutely no target correlation.” These are not skeptical reconstructions produced decades later. They are documents from inside the program. They matter because they show that at least some failures were recorded in the same system that recorded apparent successes: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp96-00788r001400250001-0 and https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp96-00788r000900390001-1 .

That does not solve the file-drawer problem. We still have to ask whether failures and successes were preserved or discussed in equal proportion, whether operational anecdotes were selected, and whether judges were blind enough to prevent cueing. But it gives us a more complicated institution than the cartoon. The archive is not only a trophy case.

By the end of the program, the controversy had condensed into an evaluation commissioned by CIA and conducted through the American Institutes for Research. The two best-known outside reviewers, statistician Jessica Utts and psychologist Ray Hyman, looked at much of the same experimental record and did not arrive at the same interpretation. Utts argued that the statistical evidence for anomalous cognition was strong enough that chance was an inadequate explanation. Hyman argued that methodological history, dependence among experiments, questions about independent replication and the gap between statistical anomaly and paranormal mechanism made that conclusion premature. The operational reviewers were much less divided about usefulness: the output was too inconsistent, vague and interpretation-dependent to serve reliably as intelligence.

That split is one of the most important facts in the story. A phenomenon can be statistically interesting and operationally useless. A weak laboratory effect can survive aggregation while failing every person who needs a specific answer on Tuesday morning. Intelligence does not need a p-value. It needs the location of the hostage, the configuration of the site, the disposition of the unit, the thing that will happen next—and it needs the correct detail distinguished from the dozens of details that merely sound plausible.

The government’s ultimate judgment was not that every session was random noise. CIA’s own public account says the 1995 reviewers thought there were enough accurate experiences to defy randomness, but that the phenomenon was too inconsistent and sporadic for intelligence use. That phrasing is unusually awkward, which is why it is valuable. It refuses the clean ending both camps prefer.

A skeptic can reasonably say that “defy randomness” is not the same as demonstrate extrasensory perception. Hidden methodological structure, analytical flexibility, non-independence, sensory leakage and publication effects can all generate apparent departures from chance. A proponent can reasonably reply that repeatedly obtaining departures under improved protocols is exactly what experimental evidence looks like before mechanism arrives.

Now we have a real disagreement.

The room gets more crowded when the Soviet Union enters it.

American interest in psi did not develop in a vacuum. Intelligence assessments during the Cold War tracked Soviet and East European parapsychology and what was sometimes called psychotronics or paraphysics. A declassified U.S. reference list from the period includes DIA reports titled “Soviet and Czechoslovakian Parapsychology Research,” “Paraphysics R&D—Warsaw Pact,” and “Controlled Offensive Behavior,” along with CIA’s own “Parapsychology in Intelligence.” The documents show that U.S. officials were attempting to understand whether adversaries were investing in areas that might matter to national security. One such source is preserved here: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00788R001300040001-4.pdf .

This is where intelligence logic bends the scientific question.

In ordinary science, a weak effect may justify replication or abandonment according to evidence and expected value. In national security, the possibility that an adversary has discovered something can keep a line of inquiry alive long after a university department would walk away. The cost of being fooled has to be weighed against the cost of being surprised. If Soviet scientists are spending money on an implausible effect, perhaps they are wasting money. If they have found something you dismissed because it sounded ridiculous, the embarrassment is larger.

That asymmetry is one reason frontier and anomalous research repeatedly attracts defense institutions. Radar, rockets, stealth, cryptography, nuclear weapons, undersea surveillance, computer networking and machine translation all taught governments that technologies can move from improbable to strategic faster than comfortable bureaucracies expect. Those successes create a general appetite for weak signals. The appetite can find real revolutions. It can also fund nonsense.

Psi therefore became tangled with a problem broader than psi: how do institutions investigate a low-probability, high-consequence possibility without either dismissing it reflexively or becoming captured by it?

The answer is still not obvious.

Modern scientific institutions are better at handling questions whose variables can be isolated, mechanisms proposed, instruments standardized and independent teams recruited. Psi claims often arrive with the opposite profile. Human subjects vary. Believers say performance changes with boredom, expectation, emotional state, experimenter attitude or the meaningfulness of targets. Skeptics hear an expanding set of escape clauses. Proponents hear a biological system behaving like a biological system. Both may be partly right.

Consider vision. Human visual performance changes with illumination, attention, fatigue, expectation and training. We do not therefore say vision is unreal. But vision also produces strong, repeatable, instrumentally traceable effects. A person can read the eye chart. A camera can measure the stimulus. Lesions can remove specific capacities. Neural signals can be mapped. The mechanism is not perfect, but the causal chain is dense.

Psi has never acquired that density.

That absence is the central burden on the field. Extraordinary claims do not require theatrical skepticism; they require denser causality. If a remote viewer can obtain information, what variable tracks performance? Does shielding matter? Distance? Time displacement? Target entropy? Emotional salience? Geomagnetic conditions? Practice? Sleep? Genetics? Brain state? Can any predictor be measured prospectively and reproduced? Can performance be blocked, amplified, trained or lesion-mapped? Does a dose-response relation exist for anything?

A phenomenon that cannot accumulate causal handles remains vulnerable to reinterpretation forever.

This is why fifty years of argument can feel like circling. A statistical signal is reported. A methodological criticism arrives. A protocol changes. Another signal appears. A skeptical review says the database is still contaminated by earlier weaknesses or lacks independent replication. A proponent aggregates the improved studies and finds a small effect. The dispute migrates one level upward. The result is neither ordinary acceptance nor clean extinction.

The intelligence archive adds a different kind of evidence: persistence under institutional use.

Persistence is not proof. But it is data about belief. Somebody kept signing the tasking. Somebody thought there was enough possibility to continue. Somebody else eventually judged the returns inadequate. People inside the system recorded both hits and misses. The program changed names, sponsors and methods. Its existence became public. The argument continued outside government.

This book follows that persistence across borders and methods. It will look at the Soviet threat literature not as proof of Soviet psychic weapons but as evidence of what American analysts believed might matter. It will look at China’s period of “extraordinary human function” and qigong research, where scientific enthusiasm, nationalism, mass culture and fraud collided. It will look at laboratory paradigms designed to remove the narrative freedom of operational remote viewing. It will look at ganzfeld telepathy experiments, presentiment, precognition, random-number work and the replication fights around them. It will also look at neuroscience and sensory biology, because the history of human perception contains enough surprises that “we already know every channel” is not a scientific position.

And it will keep four bins on the table.

The first bin is documented fact: a program existed; a paper was published; a session was run; an evaluator wrote a conclusion; a government translated a foreign paper; a protocol used a specific method.

The second is credible but incomplete evidence: a statistical pattern that survives some controls but remains disputed, a witness whose account is detailed but not independently verifiable, an experimental result awaiting broad replication.

The third is allegation: claims of operational miracles, psychic spying successes, psychotronic weapons, biological effects or secret capabilities that do not carry enough public evidence to be treated as established.

The fourth is synthesis: the ideas we build after seeing the first three. Perhaps psi is a weak biological anomaly. Perhaps it is a measurement artifact that exploits recurring weaknesses in human experimentation. Perhaps several unrelated effects have been gathered under one word. Perhaps there is a real signal buried beneath decades of folklore. Perhaps intelligence institutions were responding mainly to adversary fear and organizational psychology.

The bins do not collapse into one another because a story is exciting.

That sounds severe. It is actually the only way to keep the strange material alive.

Credulity kills anomalies by making them cheap. Once every coincidence becomes evidence and every classification marking becomes confirmation, nothing can become more persuasive than anything else. Skepticism can kill them in a different way by deciding in advance which categories of fact are permitted to exist. Both approaches stop looking.

The room with no window asks for a harder discipline.

Close the ordinary channels. Fix the target. Fix the transcript. Blind the judge. Count the misses. Let another team try. Record what changes performance. Do not invent a mechanism because you dislike a vacuum. Do not discard a result because its possible mechanism makes you uncomfortable.

Then see what is left.

That remainder—whatever it is—is the forbidden sense.
