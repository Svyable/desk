# The Machine That Should Not Care

A random-number generator should be indifferent to you.

That indifference is the point.

Press a button, stare at the box, wish, pray, threaten, meditate, concentrate or leave the room. If the device is built correctly and its physical noise source is stable, the sequence should not change because a human mind wants more ones than zeros.

Psychokinesis research asks whether that obvious sentence is empirically complete.

The older version of the claim involved visible matter: dice, falling objects, spinning devices, metal, biological systems. Visible objects bring visible problems. A skilled person can touch, blow, vibrate, warm or mechanically influence them. Small biases in dice or throwing technique can masquerade as intention. The cleaner experimental move was to replace macroscopic objects with electronic random event generators.

If a person can influence an electronic process without physical contact, the effect can be counted across enormous numbers of trials.

This was the appeal of the Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research laboratory, usually called PEAR, founded by Robert Jahn at Princeton University and active for nearly three decades. Jahn was not a stage psychic. He was an engineer who had served as dean of Princeton’s School of Engineering and Applied Science. The laboratory investigated whether human intention correlated with outputs of random physical systems and whether people could obtain anomalous information from remote targets.

PEAR reported tiny statistical deviations accumulated across large datasets.

The effect, if real, was almost perversely small. No operator could walk into the lab and force a machine to output a million ones. The claim was that distributions shifted slightly in the intended direction over many trials.

This is the perfect shape for a scientific nightmare.

A tiny effect accumulated over huge samples can produce dramatic statistical significance while remaining exquisitely vulnerable to tiny systematic biases.

Suppose a random-number generator has an output bias of 0.01 percent because temperature drifts with time of day. Suppose participants tend to run “high intention” trials in the afternoon and control trials in the morning. With enough data the correlation can become highly significant. The mind never touched the machine. The schedule did.

Suppose operators choose when to stop sessions. Suppose device calibration changes slowly. Suppose data-cleaning decisions correlate with condition. Suppose multiple analytical partitions are explored. Again, small biases can accumulate.

This is why enormous N does not rescue a weak design.

Large samples reduce random error. They do not reduce systematic error.

The distinction matters far beyond psi. Modern data science repeatedly confuses precision with validity. Millions of records can estimate the wrong quantity with magnificent confidence. A sensor network can measure its own bias. An algorithm trained on huge datasets can reproduce the structure of the collection process instead of the world.

Psychokinesis research is a severe demonstration of this general principle.

A machine that should not care about human intention is useful because the null hypothesis is physically clean. Known physics does not predict that a person’s wish should alter electronic noise at a distance. If an effect survives, the mechanism problem becomes unavoidable.

Unlike remote viewing, there is no semantic judging stage.

The bit is one or zero.

That is a strength.

But the experiment contains other human choices: when trials occur, which device is used, how conditions are sequenced, how data are excluded, how intention is defined and how results are aggregated.

A truly decisive mind-machine experiment should remove almost all of them.

The machine should continuously generate and cryptographically commit data whether anyone is present or not. Human intention periods should be scheduled by an independent randomization system. Operators should not know the device’s current bias or output. The physical noise source should be monitored for temperature, voltage, electromagnetic interference and aging. Control periods should be interleaved. Analysis should be fixed before the data are unblinded. Multiple independent hardware designs should be used.

Then replicate.

If human intention correlates with several unrelated physical random sources under those conditions, the phenomenon becomes difficult to dismiss as one device artifact.

The effect should also scale in some measurable way.

Does distance matter? Number of operators? Duration? Practice? Emotional engagement? Target bit rate? Physical entropy source? If no variable systematically changes effect strength, theory has nothing to grab.

One of the strangest claims in mind-matter research is that effect magnitude may not scale in the ordinary physical ways one would expect. Proponents have sometimes reported similar statistical shifts across different devices and contexts, suggesting that the phenomenon behaves more like an information correlation than a force.

This is where the language gets dangerous.

“Information” can become a way to avoid physics.

Information is not magic substance. It is a formal property of states and distributions instantiated in physical systems. Saying consciousness interacts with information does not explain how a transistor switches differently, how thermal noise changes, or how a quantum event is biased.

A useful theory has to touch the apparatus.

The same caution applies to quantum explanations. Electronic random generators may use quantum noise sources, but quantum randomness does not imply that human intention can steer outcomes. Measurement in quantum mechanics is not equivalent to conscious wishing. Mainstream quantum theory predicts probabilities. If human intention modifies those probabilities, that is a new physical effect requiring a model and experiments.

One reason psychokinesis attracts quantum language is that both subjects involve probability.

The resemblance is superficial until equations connect them.

The correct scientific move is embarrassingly old-fashioned: vary the physical system.

Use avalanche diodes, radioactive decay, photon beam splitters, thermal noise and deterministic pseudo-random generators as controls. If the claimed effect appears equally on deterministic pseudo-random sequences generated before the participant’s intention, then physical influence becomes difficult to maintain. The result would instead suggest precognition, selection bias or analytical artifact. If the effect appears only on a specific real-time quantum source, that points elsewhere.

This is how categories should separate.

Parapsychology often reports that categories blur. A participant may appear to influence a future-selected target, which can be described as psychokinesis or precognition depending on framing. Some researchers therefore propose a generalized “psi” that produces correlations unconstrained by ordinary causal categories.

Scientifically, that is both attractive and dangerous.

It is attractive because one underlying anomaly could explain why telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis are difficult to distinguish experimentally.

It is dangerous because a theory that allows any correlation anywhere becomes impossible to falsify.

A good generalized theory must predict which correlations will not occur.

Without prohibitions, “psi” becomes a label for surprise.

PEAR’s long dataset also raises the issue of effect decline.

In many anomalous literatures, early experiments appear strong and later ones weaker. This can happen because initial results are lucky and regression to the mean follows. Publication bias can amplify early success. Better controls can remove artifacts. Researchers may lose enthusiasm. Subjects may change. A real effect may depend on conditions accidentally optimized at the beginning.

The decline effect is often romanticized by proponents as if psi resists observation.

That is the least useful interpretation.

If a phenomenon weakens with repeated study, the first task is to identify what changed. Instrument, protocol, participant population, analysis, environment, expectation. The decline itself can become data if measured prospectively.

A phenomenon that “goes away when watched too carefully” is not automatically mystical. It is more likely methodological until demonstrated otherwise.

Mind-machine work has another conceptual trap: enormous databases invite retrospective pattern mining.

After millions of trials, researchers can examine operator sex, experience, local sidereal time, device type, session length, intention direction, first sessions, last sessions, pairs of participants and dozens of environmental variables. Some subgroup will differ.

The more interesting the subgroup sounds, the easier it is to mistake discovery for confirmation.

Exploratory analysis is valuable. It should generate the next preregistered experiment, not become the final claim.

This separation between exploration and confirmation is one of the lessons science has painfully relearned in the last decade.

Psi needs it more than most fields.

There is a related problem called the “experimenter effect.” Some parapsychologists have reported that certain experimenters consistently obtain stronger psi results than others. The proposed explanations range from subtle interpersonal influence to experimenter psi itself.

The ordinary explanation is more immediate: laboratories differ.

Experimenters choose subjects differently, explain tasks differently, maintain equipment differently, handle exclusions differently and create different social environments. In psychology those differences can matter. Before inventing paranormal experimenter influence, the field has to exhaust procedural variance.

Yet if every procedural difference is measured and certain experimenters still predict outcome across standardized automated protocols, then the effect becomes interesting in a new way.

Again: the route to strangeness runs through boring controls.

Random-number experiments also force a philosophical question about agency.

What does “intention” mean operationally?

A participant is told to make the machine produce more high numbers. But human intention is not a binary switch. Attention wanders. Motivation changes. People become bored. Some intentionally try the opposite. Some care about winning. Some do not. The psychological state being correlated with machine output may be poorly measured.

This weakness suggests importing methods from cognitive neuroscience.

Track attention. Use experience sampling. Measure autonomic arousal. Compare active intention with matched visualization that carries no outcome goal. Include deception conditions where participants believe they are influencing a live generator but are actually viewing prerecorded data. This can separate psychological feedback effects from physical influence.

The prerecorded condition is especially revealing.

If participants produce the same apparent success with data generated and sealed before the session, their minds cannot be physically changing the past output under ordinary causation. Either the correlation is artifact, participants are somehow accessing future feedback, or the causal story is stranger than psychokinesis.

This illustrates a theme that runs through psi: the experiment’s metaphysics often depends on when the random choice occurs.

Timing is part of the apparatus.

The most culturally famous version of random-event psi is the Global Consciousness Project, which has examined networks of random number generators around major world events and reported departures from expectation associated with collective attention. Events such as terrorist attacks, celebrations and disasters have been analyzed.

The concept is irresistible. Millions of minds focus on one event; physical randomness shifts.

The inferential problems are equally large.

What defines an event window? Which events count? When does collective attention begin? How are overlapping events handled? What statistic is primary? Were events specified before analysis? Are network devices independent? How is temporal autocorrelation modeled?

If event definitions are flexible, world history supplies unlimited opportunities for pattern selection.

The project’s strongest possible version would need prospective event criteria and analysis locked before outcomes. Scheduled global events such as elections or New Year celebrations are easier to preregister than unexpected disasters. Unexpected events require a rule defined in advance for when windows begin and who declares them.

Without that, interpretation outruns measurement.

Still, the idea points toward a broader frontier: distributed human-environment coupling.

We already know that collective human behavior changes physical systems constantly. Electrical load shifts during major broadcasts. Network traffic surges. Transportation changes. Financial markets move. Mobile-phone patterns synchronize. Sound levels, light usage and radio emissions change. A sufficiently sensitive sensor network can detect global events without any paranormal mechanism.

This mundane fact matters because future anomalous experiments will operate in sensor-rich environments where human attention has physical correlates everywhere.

A random generator connected to a computer is not isolated from civilization.

Electromagnetic noise, power quality, network timing and temperature may all correlate with human activity. A claimed global consciousness effect must separate unknown coupling from the many known couplings of billions of devices and bodies.

This is a beautiful experimental challenge.

It also reveals why the phrase “mind over matter” may be conceptually primitive.

Humans are matter. Minds are processes implemented in biological systems. The scientifically interesting question is not whether mind can affect matter in the abstract; it obviously does every time you move a finger. The question is whether mental states correlate with external physical systems through channels not accounted for by known sensory, motor or technological pathways.

That framing removes mysticism without removing the anomaly.

If the answer is no, random-machine studies become a case study in how tiny systematic effects create large statistical stories.

If yes, physics has a new coupling to explain.

The machine should not care.

That is exactly why we should keep asking whether it does.
