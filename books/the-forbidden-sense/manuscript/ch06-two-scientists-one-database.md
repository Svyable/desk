# Two Scientists, One Database

In 1995, the remote-viewing program finally acquired the kind of ending science is supposed to like: an external evaluation.

Instead it produced a cleaner version of the dispute.

The Central Intelligence Agency commissioned the American Institutes for Research to review the program’s research and operational history. Two outside experts became central to the public debate. Jessica Utts, a statistician with experience analyzing parapsychology experiments, concluded that the experimental evidence showed anomalous functioning at rates not plausibly attributable to chance alone. Ray Hyman, a psychologist and longtime critic of parapsychology, agreed that some datasets were statistically unusual but rejected the leap from those results to established psychic functioning. He emphasized methodological history, replication, possible dependencies among studies and the need for independent confirmation.

It is tempting to turn them into characters: believer and skeptic, dueling over the paranormal.

That does both of them a disservice.

The interesting part is not that two experts disagreed. Experts disagree constantly. The interesting part is where they disagreed.

Neither man nor woman needed to decide whether the U.S. government had conducted remote-viewing research. That was fact. Neither needed to decide whether some experimental series had produced numerical outcomes that looked better than naïve chance expectations. The argument was about what those numbers meant.

That is a much narrower and more sophisticated fight.

It is also the exact fight that appears whenever a weak, surprising effect sits inside a complicated literature.

Utts’s argument was cumulative. If controlled experiments repeatedly produce above-chance outcomes under methods designed to remove ordinary sensory access, and if the probability of the aggregated results under a simple chance model is very small, then chance alone becomes an unsatisfying explanation. She argued that the evidence had reached the point where the existence of anomalous cognition should be accepted, even though practical application remained difficult.

Hyman’s objection was not that arithmetic stopped working in parapsychology. It was that statistical significance is conditional on the model used to generate it. If studies are not independent, if methodological weaknesses correlate with positive results, if experimental paradigms change, if subject selection is adaptive, if the database is not a clean prospective sequence, then a tiny p-value does not answer the causal question by itself.

This distinction is basic and often lost in public arguments.

Statistics can tell you how surprising data are under a specified null model. They do not automatically tell you which alternative explanation is true.

Suppose a coin lands heads seventy times in one hundred tosses. Under a fair-coin model that is unusual. But “psychokinesis” is not therefore the default alternative. The coin could be weighted. The tossing method could be biased. Results could have been selected. The counter could be wrong. Or something genuinely anomalous could be occurring. The data reject a simple model before they identify mechanism.

Remote viewing had exactly this problem.

The best experimental results, if taken at face value, suggested that a simple independent-chance model was incomplete. Hyman wanted the incompleteness explained without skipping over the history of the experimental system.

This is a stronger skeptical position than “impossible.”

A good skeptic asks what mundane process can generate the observed distribution.

A good proponent asks whether those mundane processes have actually been demonstrated in the strongest studies.

The debate becomes productive when each side has to make predictions.

If sensory leakage explains the effect, then eliminating leakage should reduce it. If subjective judging explains the effect, automated or blind rank-order scoring should weaken it. If publication bias explains the meta-analytic signal, registered replication should shrink the estimate. If a small psi effect is real, increasingly strict procedures should preserve some nonzero signal even as effect size changes.

This is how the field could, in principle, converge.

The problem is that the historical evidence was not generated as one giant prospective experiment. It accumulated across decades, institutions, protocols and personalities.

That makes the database itself an object of interpretation.

One recurring argument concerns independent replication. A result reproduced by the same research group is useful but weaker than a result reproduced by researchers with different incentives, equipment and assumptions. Hidden procedural habits travel within laboratories. An unnoticed cue can become part of the technique. Independent replication breaks that lineage.

Hyman emphasized this point. Utts emphasized that similar anomalous results had appeared across different experiments and that methodological sophistication had increased. Their disagreement was partly about how much independence was enough.

This remains a live issue in all controversial science.

A field can have many papers and few independent lineages.

Modern biomedical research learned this through cell lines and animal models. Psychology learned it through famous paradigms repeatedly studied by connected networks. Machine learning learns it when dozens of papers benchmark on the same datasets. Apparent replication can be less independent than publication count suggests.

Psi has an additional problem: researchers willing to work on the subject form a small community. The number of truly independent laboratories is naturally limited by stigma and funding scarcity.

This creates a vicious circle.

Mainstream scientists say the field lacks independent replication. Researchers reply that mainstream institutions will not fund or host replication. The absence of replication becomes evidence against the field, while the field’s low credibility makes replication professionally unattractive.

That social mechanism can suppress a real weak effect.

It can also shelter a false one.

A small insulated community may share methods, assumptions and analytical preferences that continually reproduce an artifact. Outsiders are needed precisely because they do not know how things are “normally done.”

The correct response is adversarial collaboration.

Design the experiment with skeptics and proponents together. Let each side specify the failure modes it considers important. Automate as much as possible. Register the analysis. Give both teams the raw data. Make the result interpretable under pre-agreed criteria.

Psi has occasionally moved in this direction, but not at the scale needed to end the dispute.

The Utts-Hyman episode also exposes the difference between evidentiary standards for existence and standards for mechanism.

Suppose repeated high-quality studies produced a tiny but stable above-chance effect. At what point should scientists say “there is an anomaly” without saying “ESP exists”?

This wording sounds cosmetic. It is not.

“Anomaly” names a mismatch between data and current model. “ESP” bundles the mismatch into a causal category: information arrived through extrasensory means. That conclusion requires ruling out every ordinary route relevant to the experiment.

A field can responsibly say the first before it can say the second.

Physics does this all the time. An unexplained residual is not immediately a new particle. Astronomers catalog transients before naming mechanisms. Medicine recognizes syndromes before etiology is known. The discipline comes from preserving the gap between observation and explanation.

Parapsychology has often suffered because the labels already contain explanation.

Telepathy means mind-to-mind information transfer. Clairvoyance means perception of hidden things. Precognition means information from a future event. Psychokinesis means mind influencing matter. If an experiment produces an unexplained correlation, assigning one of these words can outrun the evidence.

Remote viewing was a better term because it was descriptively modest. It said what the person was attempting to do, not how.

This book will use “anomalous cognition” when mechanism is not established.

The AIR evaluation’s operational conclusion was less philosophically delicate. Whatever the laboratory data meant, the information produced by the program was not reliable enough for intelligence work.

This split between scientific anomaly and application is exactly what makes the case durable.

If the evaluators had agreed that the laboratory results were random, the story would be easy. If they had agreed that viewers could reliably obtain actionable intelligence, the story would be world-changing. Instead the program ended in the least culturally satisfying state: interesting statistics, disputed interpretation, poor utility.

A weak effect is an epistemic trap.

People want categorical answers. Institutions need budget decisions. Science often produces distributions.

The program had to be continued or terminated even though the philosophical question remained unresolved. CIA chose termination. That administrative decision is sometimes cited as scientific disproof. It was not. A capability can fail a cost-benefit test without being metaphysically impossible.

The reverse mistake is more common in believer communities: because the program existed for decades, some hidden success must have justified it. That does not follow either. Low-cost exploratory programs can persist under uncertainty, especially when adversary interest provides strategic cover.

The 1995 decision therefore tells us something precise: the available performance did not justify maintaining remote viewing as an intelligence collection tool under CIA’s evaluation.

That is enough.

We do not need to rescue a secret continuation to make the history interesting.

The Utts-Hyman debate also belongs in the broader replication crisis that would become visible across psychology two decades later.

In the 1990s, conventional psychology often treated a p-value below .05 as a persuasive marker of discovery. Later work exposed how flexible analysis, small samples, publication bias and low statistical power could generate literatures full of exaggerated effects. Registered reports, open data and large multi-lab replications emerged partly in response.

Parapsychology’s critics had been raising analogous concerns for years.

Proponents sometimes respond that this cuts both ways: if mainstream psychology was methodologically weaker than it realized, psi research should not be uniquely dismissed for imperfections common elsewhere. That is fair as a sociological point and weak as an evidentiary one.

The correct lesson is that everyone needed better methods.

An extraordinary claim does not become stronger because ordinary psychology also had problems.

Yet psi can serve as a stress test for scientific method because the stakes of hidden bias are so visible. When the conclusion is “human beings may acquire information without known sensory contact,” reviewers suddenly become very interested in randomization details, optional stopping and blinding. The rigor that should be routine becomes emotionally urgent.

That urgency can improve science.

Imagine applying the psi standard to nutrition research, education interventions or management science. Was the hypothesis registered? Were analysts blind? How many outcomes were tested? Were negative studies published? Did an independent lab reproduce the effect? Is the individual-level prediction useful? Does a mechanism constrain conditions?

Many accepted claims would become less secure.

This does not validate psi. It validates methodological humility.

The Utts-Hyman split can be reframed as a disagreement about evidentiary compression.

Utts compressed a large set of experiments into an aggregate statistical conclusion: the results were unlikely under chance and therefore supported anomalous cognition.

Hyman resisted the compression because the studies had history. Methods changed. Laboratories were connected. Specific flaws might not be independent. The aggregate number risked hiding structure.

Both instincts are scientifically necessary.

Aggregation protects against anecdote. Historical disaggregation protects against averaging artifacts.

The strongest future evidence would satisfy both.

A large prospective multi-lab study with fixed protocol, independent sites, automated target generation, preregistered analysis and complete publication could produce an aggregate result whose internal structure is also visible. Subject-level stability could be measured. Site effects could be estimated. Null results would be included by design.

If that experiment produced nothing, the historical literature would look more like accumulated methodological noise.

If it produced a stable effect, the argument would change overnight.

Why has this not happened at decisive scale?

Partly money. High-quality multi-site experiments cost money, and mainstream funders rarely prioritize psi. Partly reputation. Early-career scientists have little incentive to enter a stigmatized field. Partly community structure. Proponents often believe the evidence is already strong enough and prefer targeted research. Skeptics may regard further replication as wasted effort or fear that participation grants legitimacy.

This is an institutional failure of curiosity.

A controversial low-cost claim can remain unresolved longer than it should because neither side wants to pay for the decisive experiment.

Governments once paid because intelligence risk changed the economics.

That is the irony of STARGATE. National-security anxiety funded a scientific question that ordinary science mostly rejected. When the operational application failed, the funding rationale collapsed even though the underlying experimental dispute did not.

The result was archival abundance and scientific incompletion.

The files are there. The argument is still there.

The next step is to leave government entirely and examine a laboratory paradigm that tried to make anomalous cognition easier to elicit by changing the subject’s sensory environment: the ganzfeld.

If remote viewing asks whether a person can describe a hidden target across distance, ganzfeld experiments ask whether reducing ordinary sensory structure makes weak anomalous information easier to detect.

The idea is either clever or a perfect machine for producing subjective noise.

Possibly both.
