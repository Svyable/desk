# Red Sox Diplomacy — Tweet Bank

*Working social copy for Sven Hardy Benson. Keep documentary claims inside the manuscript’s evidentiary rule; these are promotional/editorial drafts, not substitutes for sourcing.*

## Book / launch

1. I’m writing a biography of Nicholas Burns about something diplomacy usually edits out: the person doing it. Boston. Family. The Red Sox. Humor. Rivalry. Loyalty. The small local attachments that survive a life spent representing a country abroad. *Red Sox Diplomacy*.

2. What does a lifelong Red Sox fan carry into a negotiating room? Not a baseball metaphor for everything. Something more useful: a hometown, a sense of rivalry, rituals, jokes, losses, loyalties—a recognizable American self. That is the question behind *Red Sox Diplomacy*.

3. Diplomats are supposed to represent nations. But nations are abstractions and people aren’t. *Red Sox Diplomacy* is about what happened when Nicholas Burns carried Boston—and an unmistakably American baseball loyalty—into the much stranger work of representing the United States.

4. *Red Sox Diplomacy* is not a book arguing that baseball explains foreign policy. It is a book about the parts of a person that institutions fail to sand away—and why those parts can matter when the institution needs another human being to trust you.

5. There are two clocks in this book. One follows Nicholas Burns through Cairo, Jerusalem, Athens, NATO, India, Iran and Beijing. The other follows Boston and the Red Sox through losing, conflict, change and championships. Sometimes the clocks meet. Often they don’t. That’s the point.

6. I wanted to write a diplomatic biography without turning the diplomat into a résumé. *Red Sox Diplomacy* follows the career, but it keeps asking a less official question: who is the human being still standing there after the title, protocol and talking points arrive?

## Ideas from the book

7. Public diplomacy gets described as messaging. Sometimes it is recognition. A cap. A team. A joke. A rivalry. A thing another person can answer without first consulting the official position of their government.

8. The useful thing about sports in diplomacy is not that sports are secretly politics. It is that they can briefly make politics non-total. Two people can disagree about almost everything and still discover a smaller language in which neither has to surrender anything.

9. Rivalry is one of the more interesting political emotions because it contains a distinction diplomacy desperately needs: the person across from you can be an opponent without becoming an enemy.

10. Baseball did not make Nicholas Burns a diplomat. That claim would be tidy, memorable and almost certainly too neat for the evidence. The more interesting story is how a diplomat remained recognizably himself while performing one of the most institutional forms of public life.

11. Institutions make representatives by removing idiosyncrasy. Diplomacy sometimes works by putting a little of it back.

12. A country is too large to meet. A person isn’t. That may be one of diplomacy’s oldest technologies.

13. The State Department briefing room and the baseball bleachers look like opposites. Both are places where people keep score, remember errors, reward timing, punish overstatement and know immediately when someone is pretending not to care.

14. There is a difference between using culture as a tool and actually having a culture. People can usually tell which one you brought into the room.

15. The best diplomatic analogy for baseball may be that the analogy eventually breaks. Foreign policy has no final inning, no agreed scoreboard, no clean box score and no guarantee that patience wins.

16. One reason sports travel well is that they carry identity without requiring biography. You can learn something real about a stranger before either of you has explained yourselves.

17. Diplomacy is full of ceremonial language about relationships between countries. Countries do not have relationships. People do, inside systems that remember what those people did.

18. Soft power sounds abstract until it is a specific human being carrying something specific from home.

## Boston / baseball

19. Boston is not background scenery in *Red Sox Diplomacy*. It is one of the book’s recurring arguments: people represent America from somewhere. There is no generic American waiting underneath the regional one.

20. For most of the twentieth century, being a Red Sox fan meant learning that loyalty and victory were not synonyms. That is a dangerous lesson to romanticize—and an interesting one to carry into a profession built around outcomes you often cannot control.

21. The 2004 Red Sox championship is irresistible material for a biographer. It is also a trap. If the archive does not tell me exactly where Burns was, what he saw or what he felt, I don’t get to manufacture the scene. The gap stays a gap.

22. One rule of *Red Sox Diplomacy*: no invented baseball memories. If the record does not establish that Nicholas Burns remembers a famous season, game or moment in a particular way, the book does not borrow Boston’s collective memory and hand it to him.

23. Sports history is often written as communal memory: everybody remembers where they were. Biography requires a harsher standard. Which person? Where? According to whom? The famous game is documented. The private memory may not be.

24. Fenway Park is one of those places Americans can turn into shorthand for authenticity. A diplomatic biography has to resist shorthand. Boston is more complicated than Fenway; America is more complicated than Boston; the person carrying both abroad is more complicated still.

## Diplomacy / power

25. Diplomacy is often described as the art of persuasion. A lot of it is the art of remaining in the relationship after persuasion fails.

26. Patience is not automatically wisdom. Sometimes waiting creates room for agreement. Sometimes it gives the other side time. Sometimes the structure itself is wrong. A career across NATO, India, Iran and China is a useful way to watch the meaning of patience change.

27. “Human channel” can sound sentimental. It isn’t. A human channel is infrastructure: memory, credibility, permission to call, knowledge of what can be said privately, and some accumulated reason not to assume the worst first.

28. The interesting question in a negotiation is rarely just who won. It is what each side believed the game was.

29. Representation runs both directions. A diplomat explains America abroad, but also spends a career learning what America looks like from somewhere else. The second function may be less visible at home and just as important.

30. Great-power competition can make every exchange feel like a concession. That is exactly when communication becomes most valuable—and most politically expensive.

31. There is a strange contradiction in diplomacy: the more completely someone becomes the voice of the institution, the harder it may be for the person across the table to hear a human being. Professionalism needs a face.

32. Negotiation is not friendship. Communication is not agreement. Respect is not concession. Rivalry is not hatred. A surprising amount of diplomatic skill consists of keeping nouns from collapsing into one another.

## Research / writing process

33. The most tempting sentence in nonfiction is often the one the archive has not earned yet.

34. Writing biography from documentary evidence means accepting asymmetry. Public careers leave mountains of records. Private loyalties leave scraps. The answer is not to make the private life louder. It is to make the evidentiary boundary visible.

35. I would rather leave a famous night unresolved than give a real person a cinematic memory because the chapter wants one.

36. Research gets more interesting when a missing fact stops being an inconvenience and becomes information. What an archive cannot tell us about a public person is part of the shape of public life.

37. One of my rules for *Red Sox Diplomacy*: no baseball metaphor gets to survive merely because it is clever. The evidence has to want it there.

38. A documentary biography should let the reader see the seams: what is established, what a witness remembers, what an institution says, what is contested, and where the record simply stops.

39. Sources are not backstage machinery. For nonfiction, they are part of the reader experience. If a document is publicly readable, I want the book to make it easy to get there.

40. The goal is not to eliminate uncertainty from biography. It is to stop disguising uncertainty as prose.
