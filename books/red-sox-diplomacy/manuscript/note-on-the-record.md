# A Note on the Record

Diplomatic lives leave unusually large paper trails and unusually large blank spaces.

There are briefing transcripts, nomination hearings, treaty records, speeches, photographs, government biographies, oral histories, press accounts, institutional archives and, in this case, an improbable number of public references to the Boston Red Sox. None of that turns the past into a continuous recording.

A transcript can establish what Nicholas Burns said at a podium. It cannot tell us what he thought after leaving it. A photograph can place a battered Red Sox cap on his head at a particular place and time. It cannot, by itself, explain why he wore it. A retrospective interview can tell us what Burns or a colleague remembered years later. It does not become a contemporaneous document merely because the memory is vivid.

This book works inside those limits.

When contemporaneous primary records exist, they carry the chronology and the institutional action. Retrospective testimony is used for memory, interpretation and witness recollection, and is identified as such when the distinction matters. Official accounts are not automatically treated as neutral accounts. Institutional histories, team histories and later biographies can be useful, but they are not silently promoted into eyewitness evidence.

Contested claims remain contested. When Burns described restrictions on American public diplomacy in China, the manuscript preserves his allegation, independent reporting where available, and the Chinese government's denial rather than converting one side's account into narrator fact. The same rule applies elsewhere: a source can be important without being final.

The missing scenes stay missing.

We know Burns was assigned to Jerusalem during the 1986 World Series. The archive does not tell us where he watched it, whether he watched it live, or what he felt when Boston lost. We know the Red Sox won the World Series on October 27, 2004, while Burns was serving as under secretary of state. The perfect biographical scene—where he was, who was with him, what he said—is not in the reviewed record. Those absences are not problems to be solved with plausible invention. They are part of the shape of the evidence.

Baseball is treated the same way. The game can supply chronology, atmosphere, public identity and a second American clock. It does not become a secret training system for diplomacy. A lifetime of Red Sox fandom does not prove that Burns learned patience from losing, resilience from 2004, alliance management from a clubhouse or strategy from a box score. If the record supports a connection, the book uses it. If it does not, the baseball remains baseball.

Agency is also kept distributed. Diplomatic outcomes belong to governments, institutions, colleagues, allies, adversaries, spouses, staff members, legislators, civil servants, local organizers and counterparts as well as to the senior official whose name is easiest to remember. Burns is the central subject of this book, not the sole cause of the events around him.

The visible archive notes are therefore not decorative footnotes pasted onto a finished story. They are part of the story's load-bearing structure. They show where a claim comes from, where a source is retrospective, where two accounts disagree and where the record stops.

For readers who want to follow those paths, the chapter-keyed [Source Notes](source-notes.md) provide direct links to the main documentary spine. The underlying [research ledger](../research/source-ledger.csv) is broader and messier by design: it includes the larger working record behind the manuscript, not just the sources a reader needs to verify its major hinges.

The standard is not omniscience.

It is to know the difference between what the record establishes, what a witness remembers, what remains disputed and what nobody has yet found.
