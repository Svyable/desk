# Beijing

The seminar was over.

For thirteen years Nicholas Burns had taught diplomacy at Harvard. He could reconstruct negotiations after the fact, compare decisions, invite disagreement, distinguish what officials knew at the time from what hindsight made obvious later. A professor of practice could put experience on the table and let students examine it.

An ambassador could not.

When Burns returned to government as the United States ambassador to China, he returned to delegated authority. His public words once again belonged partly to an institution. He could have judgments. He could have doubts. He could have memories of how previous governments had handled previous crises. But he represented the administration that sent him.

The change was familiar.

The scale was not.

Burns had spent his career inside consequential relationships: Egypt after the peace treaty, Jerusalem before the first intifada, Russia after the Soviet collapse, NATO after September 11, India during the civil nuclear negotiation, Iran during the long nuclear confrontation.

China contained pieces of all of them and still was something else.

Security competition. Nuclear powers. Taiwan. Trade. Technology. Military signaling. Human rights. Climate. Fentanyl precursors. Russia's war against Ukraine. Students. Scientists. Business executives. Consular cases. Visas. Propaganda. Supply chains. Aircraft and ships operating close enough that an error could become a crisis before either capital had decided it wanted one.

Harvard later described Burns as having led Mission China personnel drawn from forty-eight agencies of the United States government.

That number is useful because it ruins the fantasy of the solitary ambassador.

The embassy was not Nick Burns with a flag behind him. It was an American government in miniature, carrying disagreements and specialties into a relationship too large for any one office to comprehend.

Burns's public formula for the relationship was not especially poetic.

Compete. Communicate. Cooperate where interests permitted.

Its plainness was a virtue.

Competition and communication are often described as opposite choices, as though governments speak because they are friendly and stop speaking because they are serious. Great-power diplomacy makes that intuition dangerous.

The less two governments trust each other, the more they need reliable ways to understand what the other is doing.

Communication does not create agreement.

Sometimes it only keeps disagreement from becoming accident.

That was the China problem Burns returned to government to inhabit.

He arrived after the relationship had already hardened. American policy had shifted toward strategic competition. Beijing increasingly described Washington as attempting to contain China's rise. The United States criticized Chinese military pressure around Taiwan, economic practices, human-rights abuses and support that benefited Russia's war effort. China objected to American technology controls, Taiwan policy, alliances and what it described as interference in its internal affairs.

Neither government needed an ambassador to discover that it disagreed with the other.

The ambassador's usefulness lay elsewhere.

He had to represent American policy clearly enough that Beijing could not plausibly mistake the message, report Chinese positions accurately enough that Washington did not merely hear its own assumptions repeated back, and preserve working relationships even when the substance of those relationships was hostile.

This was the human channel under pressure.

Not friendship.

Transmission.

The distinction became especially important during crisis.

In early 2023, a Chinese high-altitude balloon crossed the United States. Washington described it as a surveillance platform. Beijing said it was a civilian airship used mainly for meteorological purposes that had deviated from its planned course. The United States shot it down after it crossed the continent. Secretary of State Antony Blinken postponed a planned visit to Beijing.

The episode was almost a laboratory demonstration of the problem.

An object moved through physical space. Two governments assigned different explanations to it. Domestic politics accelerated. Military action followed. A diplomatic visit disappeared from the calendar.

Whatever communication had existed was suddenly more difficult at exactly the moment communication mattered more.

The relationship did not remain frozen.

Blinken went to Beijing in June 2023. Other senior American officials traveled to China. National Security Adviser Jake Sullivan and Wang Yi developed a senior-level channel. President Joe Biden and President Xi Jinping met at Woodside, California, in November.

Burns did not create those decisions.

The White House, State Department, Chinese leadership and officials on both sides owned them. An ambassador works inside that machinery rather than above it.

But this was the machinery he had spent a career learning to value: repeated contact, clearer signaling, enough institutional continuity that the next conversation did not have to begin from zero.

Woodside did not end strategic competition.

It produced something less cinematic and more useful.

The governments moved to resume important military-to-military communications. They advanced counternarcotics cooperation. They agreed to maintain high-level engagement. They created additional work for officials below the presidents.

A summit is often photographed as a climax.

Professionally, it is frequently an assignment sheet.

The work afterward tests whether the photograph meant anything.

Burns also pushed a less dramatic form of contact.

People.

The word can become abstract quickly. In Burns's own China life it had a more physical form, and it was not his alone.

He and Libby arrived in March 2022 and spent their first twenty-one days quarantined in the residence. Later Burns described the posting repeatedly in the plural: the two of them trying to learn Mandarin, traveling when they could, attempting to see a country that had been difficult for outsiders to enter during zero-COVID.

In one of their first months, Burns recalled, he and Libby walked from the residence toward Tiananmen Square. They passed through checkpoint after checkpoint—he counted roughly thirteen—and at one point were held while officials tried to determine what the American ambassador was doing walking there.

The story is funny only because the restriction is real.

Later, when movement became easier, the couple took second-class train rides and talked with people around them. They went to a Beijing soccer match, wore the home team's green scarves, and received high-fives from nearby spectators. They both studied Chinese.

None of this was negotiation.

That is exactly why it belongs in the people-to-people part of the story.

A diplomatic household can encounter a country in ways that do not carry delegated authority. The ambassador remains the official representative. The spouse does not become a second ambassador by proximity. Yet the life of the post is shared, and the documentary record from Beijing gives Libby a more visible role than the generic phrase *ambassador's wife* allows.

By October 2024 that distinction had become clearer. An American expatriate who attended a residence event called *A Celebration of Contemporary Chinese Art* reported that Libby had conceived the idea of bringing Chinese and China-inspired artists into the residence, where American art normally dominated the display. The attendee describes her introducing the artists and presenting art as a bridge between cultures.

That account is eyewitness reporting, not an embassy transcript, and should be treated at that level. But the broader pattern is independently visible: an American artist whose work hung in the residence says Burns and Libby personally selected pieces for the State Department's Art in the Embassies program. A local conservation organization later recorded both of them hosting Chinese academics and foreign diplomats at an Ambassadors for Nature event in the same residence.

The residence had become, again, a place where the shared life of the posting touched public representation.

Athens had done this too.

The parallel is not that Greece and China were alike. They were not.

The parallel is domestic architecture becoming diplomatic space. A house becomes a place where art, philanthropy, history, nature, visiting students, officials and neighbors can enter a relationship that is otherwise described in ministries and strategy papers.

The pandemic years and political deterioration had reduced ordinary movement between the two societies. American students were far less present in China than they had been. Academic and cultural exchange had become harder. Political suspicion attached itself to institutions that once would have been treated as routine bridges.

Burns argued publicly for rebuilding those connections.

The argument can be overstated very easily.

Students do not solve Taiwan.

A university exchange does not remove export controls. A cultural event does not settle maritime claims. A visiting scholar does not create military confidence.

The value is smaller and therefore more credible.

Direct contact makes the other society harder to reduce entirely to an abstraction.

That matters because strategic competition rewards abstraction. Governments become nouns. China does this. America wants that. Beijing believes. Washington fears.

Diplomacy requires those nouns. Human beings complicate them.

The complication is useful.

In 2024, even that modest proposition became contested terrain.

Burns publicly accused Chinese authorities of interfering with American Embassy public diplomacy and educational and cultural programming. He described repeated obstruction after the Biden-Xi meeting at Woodside and argued that Chinese actions were making the promised expansion of people-to-people ties more difficult.

The State Department backed his complaint.

China rejected the characterization.

Its Foreign Ministry said China supported people-to-people exchange while objecting to what it regarded as American interference and politicization. From Beijing's stated perspective, Washington could not demand openness in one domain while pursuing policies China regarded as hostile in others and then treat Chinese resistance as evidence that China opposed exchange itself.

The dispute is useful precisely because it cannot be resolved by repeating one government's press line.

Both governments said people-to-people contact mattered.

They disagreed about what was preventing it.

That is the relationship in miniature.

Even the human channel required negotiation.

There is a temptation in a book like this to make Burns's interest in ordinary contact sound like the answer to great-power competition. It was not.

His tenure in Beijing did not dissolve the structural conflict between the United States and China. Taiwan remained dangerous. Technology restrictions expanded. Military competition continued. Human-rights disputes remained. China's relationship with Russia remained a major American concern. Beijing continued to object to American alliances and what it viewed as containment.

The human channel did not replace any of that.

It existed beside it.

This was the mature version of a lesson that had appeared much earlier in Burns's career.

In Cairo, the young officer learned that representation happened inside another country's political world.

In Jerusalem, even an address carried policy.

At the State Department podium, a noun could cross a border if the spokesman was careless.

At NATO, allied solidarity did not eliminate allied disagreement.

In India, patient negotiation worked because governments eventually found a bargain they could accept.

With Iran, patience did not manufacture that bargain.

China gathered those lessons into a relationship in which almost every channel carried both cooperation and conflict.

And somewhere inside all of that, absurdly and appropriately, there was still baseball.

A 2024 public post from Burns showed young people in China and referred to future members of Red Sox Nation.

Nothing important in U.S.-China relations changed because of it.

That is why the moment belongs here.

The Red Sox had followed Burns through the State briefing room, Athens, NATO, Harvard and now Beijing. The fandom had been useful as humor, ritual, recognition and public personality. It had never been a diplomatic doctrine.

In China it remained what it had been at its best: a small piece of evidence that the American representative had arrived as a particular American rather than as a walking policy paper.

The difference mattered, but only at human scale.

That scale is easy to dismiss in a relationship measured in aircraft carriers, semiconductor controls and hundreds of billions of dollars in trade.

It is also the scale at which diplomacy physically occurs.

Someone enters a room.

Someone speaks.

Someone decides whether the words sound authorized, credible, threatening, evasive, serious or merely rehearsed.

Someone reports the conversation home.

Governments are abstractions with people inside them.

Burns had spent thirteen years teaching students to examine how those people made choices. Beijing returned him to the harder position.

He had to make choices while being examined by everyone else.

There was no final agreement that could summarize the assignment. No treaty with a date beneath it. No equivalent of Article 5 after September 11 or the India civil nuclear agreement. The United States and China were still competitors when Burns left Beijing. They were still talking. They were still accusing each other of destabilizing the relationship. They were still trading. Students still crossed the Pacific. Militaries still watched one another.

That unfinished quality was not evidence that diplomacy had failed.

It was the condition diplomacy had been asked to manage.

The human channel is easiest to celebrate when relationships are improving.

Its harder test comes when they are not.

Then the purpose of communication becomes less romantic.

Make the signal legible.

Keep the channel open.

Know who is speaking.

Understand what is actually being said.

Do not confuse contact with trust.

Do not confuse competition with permission to stop listening.

The relationship may remain dangerous anyway.

That is not an argument against diplomacy.

It is the reason for it.
