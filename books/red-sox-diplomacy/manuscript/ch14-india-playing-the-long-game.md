# India: Playing the Long Game

The finish line kept moving.

That was not because the negotiators were confused about what they wanted. It was because an agreement between India and the United States had to become several different agreements before it could become real.

First came a political decision between a president and a prime minister.

Then a separation plan.

Then a change in American law.

Then a bilateral nuclear agreement.

Then safeguards with the International Atomic Energy Agency.

Then an exception from the Nuclear Suppliers Group.

Then another vote in the United States Congress.

At almost every stage, somebody who had not been in the previous room acquired the power to stop the project.

This is one meaning of playing the long game.

Not waiting.

Surviving jurisdiction.

George W. Bush and Manmohan Singh made the original wager in Washington in July 2005. The United States would work to end India's exclusion from international civilian nuclear commerce. India would separate its civilian and military nuclear facilities, place the civilian side under international safeguards and undertake a set of nonproliferation commitments.

The bargain was strategically ambitious because it violated the habits of the previous three decades.

India had never joined the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty as a non-nuclear-weapon state. It had tested nuclear weapons. American law and the rules of the international nuclear-supply system had grown around that fact. The relationship was not going to be normalized by pretending the history had not happened.

The Bush administration instead proposed an exception.

That word mattered.

Exceptions are difficult diplomatic objects. They can be defended as recognition of reality or attacked as destruction of a rule. They can create trust with the country receiving them and suspicion among countries that complied with the old system. They can be strategically sensible and institutionally destabilizing at the same time.

The civil-nuclear initiative carried all of those arguments inside it.

Singh described the bargain from India's side shortly after the July announcement. India, he said, would gain a way out of its nuclear isolation and greater access to civilian nuclear cooperation. At the same time, he insisted that the agreement would not diminish India's strategic nuclear capability.

That was not a minor qualification.

It was the Indian political premise.

The United States was not negotiating India's nuclear disarmament. India was not accepting the status the nonproliferation system had long tried to impose on it. The two governments were trying to construct a relationship around a fact neither could erase: India possessed nuclear weapons outside the NPT framework and was not going to give them up as the price of civilian cooperation.

Nicholas Burns became one of the people responsible for turning that political decision into something institutions could execute.

He had become Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs in 2005. The job put him near the center of American diplomacy across regions, but India became one of his largest personal negotiations. His first principal counterpart was Foreign Secretary Shyam Saran.

Their task was not to admire the leaders' breakthrough.

It was to discover what the words meant.

By October 2005 Burns was in New Delhi talking with Saran about implementation. He described the arrangement publicly as both historic and complex. The two countries had spent roughly three decades without normal civilian nuclear cooperation. Now each side had obligations to perform.

Reciprocity became one of the controlling words.

It meant different things in Washington and New Delhi.

India had to decide which nuclear facilities belonged on the civilian list and which remained part of the strategic program. The United States had to persuade Congress to change law that had not been written for this exception. India would have to accept international safeguards over its declared civilian facilities. Washington would have to persuade other nuclear suppliers to change rules that required full-scope safeguards.

Each government was asking the other to move first enough to make its own next move politically possible.

This is a familiar diplomatic problem disguised as a technical one.

Trust is often discussed as if two people either possess it or do not. Large agreements work differently. Governments construct sequences in which neither side has to trust the other all at once.

A commitment makes the next commitment possible.

A law makes the next negotiation possible.

A negotiated text makes the next international vote possible.

The sequence becomes a machine for manufacturing enough confidence to continue.

It can also become a machine for manufacturing new objections.

The first difficult problem was India's separation plan.

Which facilities would become civilian and safeguarded? Which would remain outside? India insisted that its strategic program would not be impaired. American nonproliferation specialists worried that the proposed exception could free domestic Indian uranium for weapons use and weaken a system built around discouraging nuclear commerce with states outside the NPT.

Those objections were substantive.

It would be easy, in a biography of one of the agreement's American negotiators, to cast critics as people who did not understand the strategic opportunity.

Many understood it perfectly well.

They disagreed about the price.

That distinction matters because the India agreement was not a technical inevitability waiting for diplomats to clear away irrational resistance. It was a choice about which objective should dominate when important objectives collided: stronger relations with a rising democratic power, energy cooperation, commercial opportunity and strategic alignment on one side; the coherence and signaling power of the nonproliferation regime on the other.

Diplomacy did not abolish the tradeoff.

It operationalized the choice.

By March 2006, during President Bush's visit to India, the two governments had reached agreement on the separation plan.

That looked like a breakthrough because it was one.

It was not the end.

Washington still needed Congress.

The administration spent much of 2006 persuading legislators to authorize exceptional nuclear cooperation with India. Burns and Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice testified repeatedly. Congress eventually passed the Hyde Act.

The law opened a path.

It also created a new negotiating problem.

From Washington's perspective, any bilateral agreement now had to comply with American law. From New Delhi's perspective, American legislation could not be allowed to rewrite the political commitments Singh had made and defended before Parliament.

India's government repeatedly emphasized that the July 2005 understanding and Singh's parliamentary assurances remained its standard. It would not accept additional obligations merely because the American political system had produced them domestically.

This was not obstinacy.

Nor was the American insistence on compliance with U.S. law a negotiating trick.

Two democracies had discovered one of the less romantic features of democratic diplomacy: neither negotiating team owned its own country.

The American executive could not erase Congress.

The Indian executive could not erase Parliament, coalition politics or the strategic establishment.

By 2007 Shyam Saran had been succeeded as foreign secretary by Shivshankar Menon. Burns remained on the American side. The negotiation had moved from the broad political bargain into the bilateral agreement required under Section 123 of the U.S. Atomic Energy Act.

Burns publicly said in May that the two sides were about ninety percent of the way there.

The last ten percent was where the negotiation lived.

Fuel assurances mattered because India remembered previous disruptions in nuclear supply, particularly the history surrounding Tarapur. Termination and right-of-return provisions mattered because the United States was not willing to surrender legal protections for the worst cases. Reprocessing mattered because India wanted the ability to deal with spent fuel and did not want cooperation that left it permanently dependent on American discretion.

These were not details in the ordinary sense.

They were the places where each country's memory of the other had been converted into legal language.

In late May, Burns traveled to New Delhi with Richard Stratford, the State Department's nuclear-cooperation specialist. They met Menon and a wider Indian leadership that included Prime Minister Singh, External Affairs Minister Pranab Mukherjee and National Security Adviser M.K. Narayanan.

Burns later identified those days as the turning point.

The Indian government proposed establishing a new reprocessing facility under International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards. The proposal gave the United States a way to confer reprocessing consent while attaching it to a safeguarded facility and to arrangements and procedures the two governments would subsequently negotiate.

The direction of that initiative is worth preserving.

India proposed the facility.

American negotiators examined it.

The two sides used it to solve a problem neither had been able to solve by repeating its original position.

That is negotiation in its most useful form. Not splitting the difference. Changing the shape of the problem.

Even then, the agreement was not done.

In July, Menon and Burns spent four days negotiating in Washington. The Indian Ministry of External Affairs recorded substantial progress and said the remaining issues would go back to the two governments for final review. Narayanan and Menon also met Vice President Dick Cheney, Rice, Defense Secretary Robert Gates and National Security Adviser Stephen Hadley.

The organizational chart again became part of the story.

Burns and Menon could negotiate.

They could not substitute themselves for their governments.

On July 27, Rice and Mukherjee announced that negotiations on the 123 Agreement had been completed.

Burns briefed reporters that morning.

He described reprocessing as the largest issue in the final phase and the safeguarded facility as the fundamental turning point. He also emphasized something less dramatic: the agreement had been written to remain within American law.

That was the achievement.

Not an American text.

Not an Indian text.

A text both systems could still attempt to carry forward.

The distinction between completing the agreement and completing the deal soon became painfully clear.

The bilateral text still had to survive the IAEA, the Nuclear Suppliers Group and the United States Congress.

Before any of those could finish their work, Indian domestic politics nearly stopped the process.

Singh's coalition government depended on support from Left parties strongly opposed to the agreement and suspicious of a strategic alignment with the United States. The nuclear initiative became entangled in a larger argument about Indian autonomy, American power and the direction of Indian foreign policy.

The agreement stalled.

There was no clever bilateral drafting clause that could solve that problem.

Burns could negotiate with Saran and Menon. Rice could negotiate with Mukherjee. Bush could make commitments to Singh.

None of them possessed the votes in India's Parliament.

This is where the phrase *long game* usually becomes sentimental.

One imagines patience as a personal virtue: the experienced negotiator waits out the difficult moment, refuses to panic, keeps his eye on the horizon.

Sometimes.

But there is another kind of patience in public life.

You leave.

Burns announced that he would step down from government. By the time India broke through its domestic political impasse and moved the agreement into the IAEA and Nuclear Suppliers Group in 2008, another Burns—William J. Burns—held the Under Secretary's job.

The coincidence is almost too neat for a book and therefore requires care.

The two men were not interchangeable. The second Burns did not merely finish the first Burns's negotiation. The work itself had changed.

The bilateral 123 text existed. Now the United States and India had to coordinate internationally to obtain an India-specific safeguards arrangement and persuade the Nuclear Suppliers Group to make an exception to rules that some of its members regarded as central to their nonproliferation identity.

Evan Feigenbaum, who worked on the American side, later remembered the NSG phase as especially difficult. Countries such as Ireland, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Norway and New Zealand did not necessarily share Washington's strategic calculation about India. Some placed exceptional weight on nonproliferation norms.

Again, the opponents were not scenery.

They had interests.

The United States and India had to persuade them.

The IAEA process moved forward. The Nuclear Suppliers Group eventually reached consensus. Congress approved the agreement. On October 10, 2008, Condoleezza Rice and Pranab Mukherjee signed the U.S.-India civilian nuclear cooperation agreement in Washington.

Nicholas Burns was not the serving Under Secretary standing at the end of the process.

That is why the story belongs in this book.

Baseball offers a tempting vocabulary here: innings, relief pitchers, the runner who reaches base but does not score the run himself.

Leave it alone.

The diplomatic reality is better.

Major agreements outlive job titles.

A negotiator can be central without being sovereign. He can spend years constructing a bargain and still depend on presidents, prime ministers, legislatures, regulators, international organizations, other countries and successors. He can solve the hardest bilateral issue and watch a domestic coalition freeze the agreement. He can leave government before the final votes and signatures.

None of that makes the earlier work less consequential.

It makes the work institutional.

Burns helped take a political wager made by Bush and Singh and turn it into a bilateral legal text that the governments could carry into the next arenas. Saran had helped build the first implementation architecture. Menon negotiated the decisive final bilateral phase. Narayanan, Mukherjee, Rice, Hadley, Stratford, Jaishankar, Ronen Sen, David Mulford, legislators, technical officials and many others occupied parts of the same chain.

Then another set of people took over.

That is not a flaw in the heroic narrative of diplomacy.

It is the reason diplomacy can accomplish things larger than one career.

The long game is not knowing that you will be there at the end.

It is building something that can continue when you are not.
