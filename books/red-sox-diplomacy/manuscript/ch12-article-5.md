# Article 5

By the morning of September 12, solidarity had to become procedure.

That is harder than it sounds.

The NATO ambassadors had spent the previous day absorbing an attack whose scale was still becoming visible. They had offered support. Most were prepared to invoke the alliance's collective-defense clause. Some still needed instructions from home.

Nicholas Burns needed something too.

Authority from Washington.

He had finally reestablished contact with the government he represented. In his later telling, he called National Security Adviser Condoleezza Rice early that morning and told her what the allies wanted to do.

They wanted to invoke Article 5.

Burns understood the weight of the request.

The clause had existed since 1949 and had never been used.

It was the sentence behind NATO's credibility. An attack on one would be treated as an attack on all. For more than half a century, everyone had known what the promise was supposed to mean without ever having to discover exactly how it would work.

Now nineteen governments were about to find out.

Burns told Rice he thought he needed presidential authorization before casting the American vote.

This was not because the United States had to be persuaded to accept allied help.

It was because treaty language is not atmosphere.

Governments have constitutions, chains of command, parliaments, military authorities, lawyers, and political consequences. Collective defense is powerful precisely because it is not a spontaneous emotional response. It is an institutional commitment states have agreed in advance to take seriously when fear makes improvisation tempting.

Rice told him to proceed.

Burns would remember one more sentence from the call.

It was good to have friends in the world.

The line survives because it reduces an elaborate alliance system to something almost embarrassingly plain.

Friends.

That word is too soft for some foreign-policy writing. Alliances are supposed to be discussed in terms of deterrence, burden sharing, force posture, strategic depth, access, interoperability, credibility.

All of those terms matter.

On September 12, so did the simpler one.

The North Atlantic Council met again.

What it adopted that day was historic, but not careless.

The statement did not simply declare that any horror suffered by an ally automatically triggered collective defense.

It said that if the attacks had been directed from abroad against the United States, they would be regarded as covered by Article 5.

The condition matters.

It is tempting to strip it away because the cleaner story is emotionally satisfying: America was attacked; NATO immediately declared the attack an attack on all.

The actual story is stronger.

The allies expressed solidarity immediately and still preserved the need to establish what had happened.

That is not hesitation.

It is what a rules-based alliance is supposed to do.

Article 5 was not designed as a machine that transforms grief into war.

It imposed an obligation to assist when an ally had suffered an armed attack within the treaty's scope. The September 12 statement therefore held two ideas at once: the alliance stood with the United States, and the alliance would verify that the attack met the treaty's requirements.

On October 2, after NATO received the results of the American investigation, the condition was satisfied.

The attacks had been directed from abroad.

Article 5 applied.

Then came the harder question.

What did application mean?

Not what people assumed it meant.

Article 5 does not order nineteen countries to do one identical thing.

Its wording is deliberately more flexible. Each ally agrees to assist by taking such action as it deems necessary, individually and in concert with the others. That action can include armed force, but the treaty does not prescribe the same military response for every member.

The flexibility was not a loophole.

It was one reason the commitment could survive contact with reality.

Different governments had different military capabilities, legal structures, parliamentary rules, geographic positions, intelligence resources, and political constraints.

Collective defense did not erase those differences.

It organized them.

By early October, the United States had begun identifying concrete forms of assistance it wanted from NATO.

Burns was again the channel.

At a State Department briefing on October 3, spokesman Richard Boucher identified Burns as the ambassador who had carried the American list of requested measures to the alliance.

The list did not read like a speech.

It read like logistics.

More intelligence sharing.

Better protection for American and allied facilities.

Allies backfilling selected NATO assets that might be pulled toward counterterrorism operations.

Overflight clearances for military aircraft.

Access to ports and airfields.

Naval forces in the eastern Mediterranean.

Airborne early-warning aircraft available to support operations.

This was Article 5 turning from a sentence into infrastructure.

That transformation is the chapter.

Alliances are often discussed at their most theatrical level.

A president at a summit.

Leaders standing shoulder to shoulder.

A communique declaring unity.

Those images matter because deterrence depends partly on visible commitment.

But alliance power is frequently exercised in details too dull for ceremony.

A plane receives permission to cross airspace without negotiating each flight separately.

A ship can enter a port.

An intelligence service shares more than it shared the week before.

A military fills a task another ally can no longer cover.

A surveillance aircraft flies where it is needed.

These things do not look like history while they are happening.

They are often what make history operational.

On October 4, NATO approved eight measures supporting the United States.

Then the alliance crossed the Atlantic in a direction its founders had not imagined.

NATO airborne warning and control aircraft deployed to help patrol American skies.

The symbolism was almost too neat.

For decades, the United States had stationed forces in Europe as tangible proof that an attack on Europe would involve America.

Now multinational NATO crews were flying over the United States because America had been attacked.

The alliance had reversed its traditional flow without abandoning its original principle.

The principle was never actually about geography.

It was about obligation.

Burns would spend years returning to that fact.

He had been trained in a generation of American diplomacy that treated NATO as both inheritance and instrument. The alliance was old enough by 2001 to be taken for granted by people who had never known a Europe without it.

September 11 made the inheritance immediate.

The United States did not need allied sympathy because it was weak.

It needed allied capability because even the strongest state operates in a world of distances, bases, intelligence networks, airspace, law, legitimacy, and shared risk.

This is the distinction between power and reach.

America had enormous power.

Its allies expanded its reach.

They also expanded the political meaning of the response.

The attacks were on New York and Washington, but citizens of many countries died. The campaign against al-Qaeda would cross borders. The military response would depend on access and cooperation far beyond American territory.

Article 5 placed the attack inside a larger political frame.

The United States had not been attacked as a lonely fortress.

It had been attacked as a member of an alliance.

That did not mean the alliance would agree forever.

It did not mean every later American decision would command NATO unity.

The coming years would make that painfully clear.

Afghanistan would test endurance.

Iraq would split allies badly.

Arguments over defense spending, strategy, enlargement, Russia, and American leadership would become fierce.

The fact of later disagreement does not weaken what happened in 2001.

It clarifies it.

An alliance is not meaningful because its members always agree.

It is meaningful because they have agreed in advance about what they owe one another when disagreement is no longer the main problem.

Burns had learned in the briefing room that institutional words acquire force because people believe they mean something.

At NATO he watched the largest version of that principle he would ever encounter.

For fifty-two years, Article 5 had been a sentence no one wanted to test.

On September 12, the allies said they were prepared to use it.

On October 2, they determined that the facts required it.

On October 4, they began turning it into action.

The sequence matters.

Solidarity.

Verification.

Commitment.

Execution.

That is how institutions keep language from becoming theater.

Burns's later memory of Rice's line works because it came before all the machinery.

It was good to have friends in the world.

A month later, some of those friends were helping watch the American sky.
