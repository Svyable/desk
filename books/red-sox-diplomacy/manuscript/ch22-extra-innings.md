# Extra Innings

Diplomatic careers end badly on paper.

There is always a final title.

A final posting.

A final day in office.

A biography can make the sequence look complete because chronology provides a clean edge where life does not.

Nicholas Burns left Beijing in January 2025.

That was an ending.

It was not the end.

A few months later he was back at Harvard, teaching again. He returned to the classroom with another government behind him, another set of decisions to explain, another relationship whose meaning was still changing after he had left it.

In an April 2025 lecture reflecting on China, Burns reached for a relay race rather than baseball. He had carried the American baton in Beijing for a few years, he said in substance, and now he was handing it to his successor.

The image is better than a final score.

A diplomat inherits a relationship already in motion.

He leaves it unfinished.

The next person receives both.

This is obvious administratively and easy to forget narratively. Biographies prefer arcs. Governments prefer continuity. History prefers neither.

The U.S.-China relationship did not become the property of the ambassador who occupied the residence. NATO did not begin when Burns arrived twelve days before September 11. The India nuclear agreement did not belong to one negotiator. Iran did not resolve itself because an Under Secretary left office. Greece did not stop having an argument with America when the embassy changed occupants.

The office is temporary.

The file is not.

That may be the final professional lesson of Burns's career, if a living career can be permitted anything called final.

He had already left government once.

In 2008, after twenty-seven years in the Foreign Service, he moved to Harvard. The move looked like a clean transition from practitioner to teacher until President Joe Biden asked him to return as ambassador to China more than a decade later.

The professor became the representative again.

Then the representative became the professor again.

There is no reason to assume the sequence carries some secret design.

Careers look more intentional in retrospect than they feel while being lived.

What matters is that the returns changed the meaning of the departures.

After China, Burns did not come back to Harvard with theory alone. He came back with recent memories of crisis, constrained channels, prisoners, military risk, arguments over public diplomacy and a relationship he described as the most difficult assignment of his career.

He also came back with outcomes small enough to restore scale.

One of the moments he later emphasized most was the release in late 2024 of Americans who had been detained in China for years. In the sweep of a bilateral relationship involving nuclear weapons, technology competition, Taiwan, trade and the future balance of power, four individuals can sound numerically trivial.

They were not trivial to their families.

Diplomacy has this double scale.

The state scale produces communiqués, deterrence, sanctions, alliances and summits.

The human scale produces a person walking out of a prison.

Both are foreign policy.

The second is easier to lose when writing the first.

Burns's career repeatedly moved between them.

Article 5 is an alliance principle.

It also begins with individual countries deciding whether they will risk individual lives for another member.

The India nuclear agreement was a strategic realignment.

It was also rooms of officials arguing over paragraphs.

Public diplomacy in China was a competition over political space.

It was also whether a student attended a seminar or a musician got to play.

Representation is national.

It is always performed by somebody.

That is why the obvious temptation at the end of this book has to be resisted.

The temptation is verdict.

Was Nicholas Burns a great diplomat?

The question sounds natural and conceals several worse questions inside it.

Compared with whom?

Measured by what?

Agreements signed?

Wars avoided?

Policies defended?

Policies regretted?

Students taught?

Allies reassured?

Adversaries deterred?

Countries persuaded to like the United States?

None of these measures is useless.

None survives as a final statistic.

Diplomatic outcomes decay, reverse, compound and reappear.

A successful agreement can be abandoned by a later government.

A failed negotiation can establish a framework a successor uses.

An alliance can look obsolete until the day it becomes indispensable.

A judgment that appears defensible inside one moment can look badly wrong twenty years later.

This last point became newly explicit in Burns's own public record.

In September 2026, twenty-five years after the attacks of September 11, Burns returned publicly to the period when he had been ambassador to NATO.

He had supported the 2003 invasion of Iraq at the time.

Looking back, he said he regretted that support and believed the decision to invade had been wrong.

That retrospective judgment does not erase the original one.

It should not.

A later admission is not an escape hatch from responsibility.

It is evidence of something else this biography has been circling from the beginning.

Judgment has a half-life.

Officials decide with partial information, institutional pressure, political assumptions and the confidence of the moment. Then history gives them information they did not have—or did not weigh correctly—and asks whether they are willing to revise the story they tell about themselves.

Sometimes they do.

Sometimes they spend the rest of their lives defending the old brief.

There is no rule that experience makes a person wiser.

Experience can make error more sophisticated.

The useful professional habit is not experience alone.

It is revisability.

Harvard gave Burns a place to practice that habit publicly.

Teaching turned former decisions into material that students could question. Interviews with former officials turned memory into a record others could compare. Return to government then exposed the professor's ideas to the pressure of actual authority. Return to teaching exposed the latest exercise of authority to analysis again.

The loop matters.

Practice.

Reflection.

Practice.

Revision.

Not because academia purifies government or government makes academics realistic.

Because both can become self-satisfied without the other.

The same is true of patriotism.

Burns spent a career representing the United States without disguising the fact that representing a country requires seeing where the country is wrong.

That is not anti-American.

It is part of competent representation.

The diplomat who believes the country is always right becomes incapable of reporting inconvenient reality.

The diplomat who believes the country is uniquely corrupt becomes incapable of representing it at all.

The job occupies the less comfortable ground between loyalty and judgment.

Baseball, oddly enough, can return here without being forced.

Not because a diplomatic career is a game.

It is not.

Not because governments get three outs.

They do not.

Not because history provides a scoreboard.

It does not.

The useful thing about extra innings is simpler.

The expected ending has already passed.

The game continues anyway.

For Red Sox fans of Burns's generation, that idea once carried a particular cruelty. Seasons extended only to create another opportunity for disappointment. Then 2004 arrived and permanently changed the emotional grammar of the affiliation.

The fandom remained.

The premise changed.

Burns's public life has undergone something similar, though the stakes and subject are entirely different. The Foreign Service career ended in 2008. The diplomatic work did not. Teaching followed. Public debate followed. Government returned. China followed. Teaching returned again. At seventy-something, Burns was still publicly arguing about alliances, China, intervention and the lessons of September 11.

That is not a victory lap.

It is continued participation.

The distinction matters.

Victory laps imply settled achievement.

Participation permits correction.

This book has resisted calling Burns America's greatest diplomat because greatness is too convenient a word. It collapses craft, judgment, luck, institutions, presidents, colleagues, counterparts and history into a personal ranking nobody can actually compute.

The more interesting claim is smaller.

Burns's career makes visible what diplomacy asks of a person over time.

Learn another country without pretending to become native to it.

Represent your own country without confusing loyalty with infallibility.

Use relationships without imagining relationships erase interests.

Know when language must be exact.

Know when ambiguity is useful.

Understand that allies remember how they are treated.

Understand that adversaries listen too.

Accept that some negotiations end without the result you wanted.

Accept that some agreements will be finished by somebody else.

Leave a file another person can pick up.

Come back years later and be willing to say where you were wrong.

And remain a person while carrying an institution large enough to erase one.

The Red Sox cap was never the answer to any of this.

It was simply evidence.

There was a person under the title.

That person could be from Massachusetts, could care irrationally about baseball, could joke with reporters, could argue with allies, could misjudge a war, could help negotiate an agreement, could teach students, could walk into a prison to visit an American detainee, could return to a classroom and revise what he thought he knew.

Diplomatic institutions need people precisely because institutions have to act before history tells them whether the action was correct.

The people do not make that uncertainty disappear.

They carry it.

Then they hand it off.

That is the part the phrase public service gets right.

The service continues after the public servant leaves the room.

Somewhere another ambassador opens the file.

Another class begins.

Another argument starts.

Another government sends another message that must be interpreted by a person on the other side.

The inning was never extra.

It was only next.
